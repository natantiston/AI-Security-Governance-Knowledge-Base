# 20.09 — AI Vendor Assessment Template

## Part 1 — Foundations, Scope, Supplier Identification, and Third-Party AI Risk

### 1. Introduction to AI Vendor Assessment

An AI Vendor Assessment is a structured process for evaluating the security, privacy, governance, operational resilience, and third-party risks associated with acquiring or using AI products and services supplied by external organizations.

Organizations increasingly depend on external AI providers for foundation models, hosted inference, AI-enabled software, retrieval services, agent platforms, data-processing services, model development, and managed AI operations. These relationships introduce risks that may not be adequately addressed through conventional supplier questionnaires alone.

An AI vendor may process confidential business information, provide models that influence consequential decisions, host critical application components, or supply AI agents capable of interacting with enterprise systems. The vendor's security posture can therefore directly affect the organization's confidentiality, integrity, availability, regulatory obligations, and operational resilience.

An effective AI Vendor Assessment examines both the supplier and the specific AI service being procured. A vendor with a mature corporate security program may still offer a service whose data-handling practices, model capabilities, integration architecture, or contractual limitations are unsuitable for a particular use case.

The assessment should establish whether the supplier can satisfy the organization's security and governance requirements, what risks remain outside the organization's direct control, and what contractual, technical, and operational safeguards are necessary before onboarding and throughout the supplier relationship.

**Core principle:** AI vendor approval should be based on the suitability of the specific service, the evidence supporting the supplier's controls, and the risks created by the intended use—not solely on the vendor's reputation or general security certifications.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Vendor Assessment Template

The purpose of this template is to establish a repeatable, evidence-based method for evaluating third-party AI services before procurement, integration, deployment, and continued use.

The template should help the organization:

- Identify AI-specific risks introduced by external suppliers.
- Evaluate vendor security capabilities and control maturity.
- Determine how organizational data is collected, processed, stored, and retained.
- Assess the security of models, APIs, retrieval systems, and agent capabilities.
- Understand the vendor's role in the AI supply chain.
- Evaluate transparency, assurance evidence, and contractual commitments.
- Identify shared-responsibility boundaries.
- Determine whether the service is suitable for the intended use case.
- Define remediation requirements and compensating controls.
- Support procurement, legal, privacy, security, and business approval decisions.
- Establish monitoring and reassessment requirements for the supplier relationship.
- Preserve evidence for audit, risk management, and future vendor reviews.

The assessment should produce a documented decision rather than a questionnaire-completion score alone.

A vendor may provide satisfactory answers while failing to supply evidence that its controls operate effectively. Conversely, a supplier may have limited documentation for a particular control but provide alternative evidence that supports an informed risk decision.

The assessment methodology should therefore evaluate both the completeness of vendor responses and the credibility of the evidence supporting them.

**Core principle:** The template must support a defensible supplier risk decision, not merely collect information from a vendor.

### 3. AI Vendor Assessment as a Third-Party Risk Management Control

AI Vendor Assessment should form part of the organization's broader Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM) program.

Traditional TPRM evaluates supplier financial stability, information security, contractual compliance, operational resilience, and business continuity. AI-related supplier relationships require these established controls plus additional scrutiny of model behavior, training and inference data, AI-specific attack surfaces, and the degree of control delegated to external services.

The assessment should operate throughout the supplier lifecycle:

1. **Discovery:** Identify the vendor and the AI service being considered.
2. **Initial screening:** Determine whether a formal AI vendor assessment is required.
3. **Risk classification:** Establish the supplier's inherent risk and assessment depth.
4. **Due diligence:** Collect documentation, questionnaire responses, and technical evidence.
5. **Risk analysis:** Evaluate gaps, dependencies, and residual exposure.
6. **Approval:** Determine whether the supplier may be onboarded and under what conditions.
7. **Contracting:** Establish enforceable security and governance obligations.
8. **Integration:** Validate the security of the organization's actual implementation.
9. **Monitoring:** Track supplier changes, incidents, control deficiencies, and performance.
10. **Renewal or exit:** Reassess continued suitability and manage termination securely.

A vendor's initial approval should not be interpreted as permanent authorization. Changes in service functionality, data-processing terms, model versions, ownership, or subcontractors may materially change the supplier's risk profile.

**Core principle:** AI vendor risk management is a lifecycle control, not a one-time procurement questionnaire.

### 4. Scope of the AI Vendor Assessment

The assessment should cover external organizations that provide AI capabilities, AI-related infrastructure, AI-enabled products, or services that materially influence the security of an AI system.

The scope may include:

- Foundation model providers.
- Hosted large language model (LLM) services.
- AI application and software-as-a-service vendors.
- AI agent and orchestration platforms.
- Managed machine learning platforms.
- Model hosting and inference providers.
- AI development and integration consultants.
- Fine-tuning and model customization providers.
- Training data and dataset suppliers.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) providers.
- Vector database and search providers.
- AI security testing and red-teaming suppliers.
- AI monitoring and observability vendors.
- Managed AI operations providers.
- Third-party plugins and tools used by AI agents.
- Cloud infrastructure providers supporting AI workloads.
- Outsourced teams that develop, operate, or maintain AI systems.

The organization should determine scope according to the supplier's actual role and the consequences of supplier failure.

A vendor providing a low-risk productivity feature may require a lighter assessment than a provider operating an AI agent with access to production systems or sensitive customer information.

The assessment should also consider indirect suppliers where a subcontractor or upstream provider can materially affect the security, availability, or integrity of the AI service.

**Core principle:** Assess suppliers according to the AI capabilities, data, privileges, and dependencies they introduce—not merely according to their commercial category.

### 5. Distinguishing AI Vendor Assessment from Related Assessments

AI Vendor Assessment overlaps with several other governance activities, but it serves a distinct purpose.

| Assessment                       | Primary question                                                    | Main focus                                                                             |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Vendor Assessment             | Is this supplier and service suitable for our intended use?         | Supplier controls, assurance, contracts, dependencies, and third-party exposure        |
| AI Security Assessment           | Is the AI system adequately protected against security threats?     | Architecture, attack surfaces, security controls, and technical validation             |
| AI Risk Assessment               | What risks arise from the AI system and how should they be treated? | Risk scenarios, likelihood, impact, controls, and residual risk                        |
| AI Impact Assessment             | What effects may the AI system have on people, groups, and society? | Benefits, harms, distributional effects, and affected stakeholders                     |
| Privacy Impact Assessment / DPIA | What privacy risks arise from the processing of personal data?      | Data processing, privacy risks, and applicable data protection obligations             |
| Procurement Assessment           | Is the purchase commercially and operationally appropriate?         | Cost, business need, supplier capability, and purchasing requirements                  |
| Legal and Contractual Review     | Are the proposed terms and obligations acceptable?                  | Liability, data rights, warranties, intellectual property, and contractual commitments |
| Business Continuity Assessment   | Can the organization withstand disruption of the service?           | Resilience, recovery, continuity, and exit arrangements                                |

These assessments should share relevant evidence without being treated as interchangeable.

For example, a vendor may pass a corporate security review while the proposed integration fails an AI Security Assessment because the application grants excessive tool permissions.

Similarly, a technically secure service may be unsuitable because the vendor's data-retention terms conflict with the organization's obligations.

**Core principle:** Supplier assurance, technical security validation, and use-case risk approval are related but separate decisions.

### 6. Standards, Frameworks, and Regulatory Context

The AI Vendor Assessment should align with the organization's applicable security, AI governance, privacy, procurement, and third-party risk requirements.

Relevant frameworks and standards may include:

- **ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 27002:** Information security management and security controls.
- **ISO/IEC 42001:** AI management system requirements and governance.
- **ISO/IEC 23894:** AI risk management guidance.
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework:** AI risk governance, mapping, measurement, and management.
- **NIST Cybersecurity Framework:** Cybersecurity governance and risk management.
- **NIST SP 800-161:** Cybersecurity supply-chain risk management.
- **SOC 2 reports:** Independent assurance over defined service-provider controls and trust services criteria.
- **OWASP guidance for LLM applications:** Relevant AI application security risks.
- **MITRE ATLAS:** Adversarial threats and attack techniques involving AI systems.
- **Applicable data protection and cybersecurity legislation:** Requirements determined by the organization's activities, jurisdiction, industry, and service use.

The organization should map applicable requirements to specific vendor questions, evidence expectations, contractual clauses, and approval criteria.

The mapping should not imply that a supplier's certification or independent report automatically satisfies every requirement.

For example, an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate can provide evidence of a certified information security management system within its defined scope. It does not independently prove that a particular AI service prevents prompt injection, enforces retrieval authorization, or protects against unsafe agent actions.

Likewise, a SOC 2 report must be examined for its reporting period, service scope, control coverage, exceptions, and complementary user-entity controls.

Regulatory applicability should be determined for the actual organization and use case. A framework reference alone does not establish legal applicability or compliance.

**Core principle:** Use standards and assurance frameworks to structure due diligence, but verify whether the supplier's evidence covers the actual AI service and risk being evaluated.

### 7. Ownership and Responsibilities

AI Vendor Assessment requires coordination across procurement, information security, AI governance, legal, privacy, architecture, business operations, and third-party risk management.

Responsibilities should be assigned before the assessment begins.

| Role                        | Primary responsibility                                                                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business owner              | Defines the business purpose, expected benefits, and consequences of supplier failure |
| Vendor relationship owner   | Coordinates the supplier relationship and ongoing performance monitoring              |
| Procurement                 | Ensures due diligence requirements are completed before contractual commitment        |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Coordinates supplier classification, assessment workflow, and risk tracking           |
| Information Security        | Evaluates security controls, technical assurance, and security-related findings       |
| AI Governance               | Evaluates AI-specific governance, accountability, and lifecycle considerations        |
| Enterprise Risk Management  | Challenges material risk exposure and coordinates risk acceptance where applicable    |
| Privacy / Data Protection   | Reviews personal-data processing and relevant privacy obligations                     |
| Legal / Contract Management | Reviews contractual commitments, liability, data rights, and termination provisions   |
| Enterprise Architecture     | Evaluates technical fit, integration design, dependencies, and security boundaries    |
| Data Governance             | Reviews data classification, provenance, permitted use, and lifecycle controls        |
| Authorized Risk Owner       | Accepts residual risk within delegated authority                                      |
| Internal Audit              | Independently evaluates the effectiveness of the assessment program                   |

The organization should distinguish between the supplier's responsibility to provide accurate evidence and the organization's responsibility to validate that evidence.

Vendor self-attestation may support the assessment, but it should not automatically replace independent assurance or technical verification where those are required.

**Core principle:** Supplier-provided information is an input to the decision; accountability for the organization's risk decision remains with the organization.

### 8. Vendor and Assessment Record Identification

Every AI Vendor Assessment should have a unique identifier and a clear link to the supplier, service, business owner, and associated AI system or use case.

The assessment record should contain, at minimum:

- Assessment ID.
- Vendor legal name.
- Trading name or service brand, where applicable.
- Vendor relationship owner.
- Business owner.
- Service or product name.
- Description of the AI capability.
- Assessment date.
- Assessment methodology version.
- Inherent risk tier.
- Assessment status.
- Relevant AI system inventory identifiers.
- Contract or procurement reference.
- Data classification.
- Hosting and deployment model.
- Supplier assurance evidence references.
- Material findings.
- Residual-risk decision.
- Approval status.
- Review or reassessment date.

The record should distinguish the legal supplier from the product, model, and service being assessed.

A single vendor may provide several AI services with different data-processing practices, hosting models, security features, and levels of autonomy. One supplier-level assessment may therefore need to be supplemented by separate service-level reviews.

**Core principle:** Vendor identity, service identity, and assessed use case must remain distinguishable and traceable.

### 9. Vendor Lifecycle and Assessment Status

The assessment should identify the vendor's current lifecycle stage and the status of the associated AI service.

A practical lifecycle model includes:

| Status                 | Meaning                                                                                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Proposed               | The service is being considered but has not entered formal assessment                      |
| Screening              | Initial risk and applicability checks are underway                                         |
| Due diligence          | Required vendor information and evidence are being collected                               |
| Under review           | Security, governance, privacy, legal, or technical specialists are evaluating the evidence |
| Remediation required   | Material gaps require corrective action or additional safeguards                           |
| Conditionally approved | Use is permitted only under defined and enforceable conditions                             |
| Approved               | Required assessment and authorization criteria have been satisfied                         |
| Restricted             | Use is limited because of specific unresolved risks or conditions                          |
| Suspended              | Use is temporarily prohibited pending investigation or remediation                         |
| Rejected               | The supplier or service is not approved for the proposed use                               |
| Under reassessment     | A material change or scheduled review has triggered renewed evaluation                     |
| Retiring               | The organization is preparing to discontinue the service                                   |
| Closed                 | The relationship has ended and required closure controls have been completed               |

These statuses should have defined entry and exit criteria.

A vendor questionnaire marked complete should not automatically change a supplier's status to approved.

Likewise, conditional approval should identify the permitted scope, restrictions, accountable owners, deadlines, and consequences of failing to meet the conditions.

**Core principle:** Vendor lifecycle status must reflect the actual approval state and conditions of use, not administrative progress alone.

### 10. Vendor Service and AI Capability Description

The assessment should establish exactly what the vendor supplies and how the organization intends to use it.

A generic description such as "AI platform" is insufficient because the security implications vary significantly across model hosting, AI-enabled business software, autonomous agents, data processing, and managed AI operations.

The service description should identify:

- Primary business function.
- AI capabilities provided.
- Model type and deployment arrangement, where known.
- Hosted versus self-managed components.
- Supported integrations.
- Available APIs and administrative interfaces.
- Data inputs and outputs.
- Tool-use or agent capabilities.
- External service dependencies.
- Customization and fine-tuning options.
- Model and configuration update mechanisms.
- Service availability and support model.
- Restrictions or limitations relevant to the intended use.

The assessment should also distinguish between capabilities currently enabled and capabilities that could be enabled later.

A vendor may offer optional agents, plugins, connectors, or data-training features that are not part of the initial deployment but could materially change the security profile if activated.

**Core principle:** Assess the actual service configuration and intended use, while accounting for optional capabilities that could expand the attack surface.

### 11. Intended Use Case and Business Context

The same AI service can create different levels of risk depending on how it is used.

The assessment should therefore record the organization's intended use case rather than evaluating the vendor in isolation.

Relevant questions include:

- What business problem will the service address?
- Who will use the service?
- Which business processes will depend on it?
- What decisions or actions will it support?
- Will it process confidential, personal, regulated, or commercially sensitive information?
- Will its outputs influence consequential decisions?
- Will it interact with production systems?
- Will it execute actions or only generate recommendations?
- What level of human oversight will apply?
- What happens if the service is unavailable, compromised, or produces unsafe outputs?

For example, a hosted language model used to summarize public documents may require a different assessment depth from the same model integrated into a privileged support agent that can retrieve customer records and modify account settings.

The intended use case should be documented precisely enough to support the risk classification and the final suitability decision.

**Core principle:** Vendor risk is determined by the supplier's capabilities in the context of the organization's intended use, data, permissions, and business dependencies.

### 12. Data Classification and Vendor Data Access

The assessment should identify the categories of information the vendor may receive, generate, store, or otherwise process.

Relevant categories may include:

- Public information.
- Internal business information.
- Confidential business information.
- Trade secrets and intellectual property.
- Personal data.
- Sensitive personal data where applicable.
- Customer records.
- Authentication data and secrets.
- Security logs.
- Source code and technical documentation.
- Regulated or contractually restricted information.

The organization should determine whether the vendor's service can technically and contractually support the intended data classification.

The assessment should examine whether the vendor receives only the minimum data necessary, whether access can be restricted, and whether data is retained or reused beyond the intended processing purpose.

The organization should not assume that data is excluded from model training, retained only temporarily, or inaccessible to provider personnel unless the relevant service terms, configuration, and supporting evidence substantiate those claims.

**Core principle:** Data suitability must be established through verified processing terms and technical safeguards, not marketing statements or assumptions about default behavior.

### 13. AI Deployment and Hosting Model

The assessment should identify where the AI capability runs and which parties control the relevant infrastructure.

Common deployment models include:

- Publicly hosted AI APIs.
- AI capabilities embedded in SaaS applications.
- Dedicated or isolated hosted environments.
- Customer-managed cloud deployments.
- Self-hosted models operated by the customer.
- Vendor-managed private deployments.
- Hybrid arrangements combining customer and provider infrastructure.

The hosting model influences the organization's visibility, control, and ability to verify security.

For example, a self-hosted model may provide greater control over infrastructure and data flows, but it also transfers responsibility for patching, hardening, monitoring, and operational resilience to the organization.

A hosted service may reduce infrastructure-management burdens while limiting access to internal implementation details and placing greater reliance on contractual commitments and independent assurance.

The assessment should document the shared-responsibility boundary for each relevant control domain.

**Core principle:** Deployment architecture determines which security controls the vendor operates, which the customer operates, and which require joint implementation.

### 14. Vendor Criticality and Business Dependency

The organization should assess how important the vendor's AI service is to business operations and what would happen if the service became unavailable, compromised, or unsuitable.

Criticality considerations may include:

- Number of dependent business processes.
- Operational importance.
- Availability of alternative providers.
- Time required to migrate or replace the service.
- Volume and sensitivity of processed information.
- Degree of integration into core systems.
- Financial and contractual exposure.
- Regulatory or customer commitments.
- Dependency on proprietary models or formats.
- Concentration of workloads with one provider.

A supplier can be highly critical even if its technical architecture is relatively simple.

For example, an AI service that supports a time-sensitive customer operation may create significant operational dependency if no practical alternative exists.

The organization should distinguish supplier criticality from inherent security risk. A highly critical supplier may require stronger continuity and exit arrangements even where its security controls are otherwise mature.

**Core principle:** Vendor criticality determines the consequences of supplier failure and should influence assessment depth, resilience planning, and exit requirements.

### 15. AI Vendor Inherent Risk Classification

The organization should classify the inherent risk of the proposed vendor relationship before considering the effectiveness of specific mitigating controls.

Inherent risk represents the exposure arising from the nature of the service, data, privileges, business dependency, and operating context before credit is given for mitigating safeguards.

A practical classification model may include:

| Risk tier         | Indicative characteristics                                                                                                                                | Typical assessment approach                                                                                             |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tier 1 — Low      | Limited functionality, public or low-sensitivity data, minimal integration, no privileged actions                                                         | Basic due diligence and baseline security verification                                                                  |
| Tier 2 — Moderate | Internal business information, standard enterprise integration, moderate operational dependency                                                           | Structured questionnaire, assurance review, and selected technical validation                                           |
| Tier 3 — High     | Confidential or personal data, significant integrations, material business dependency, or consequential outputs                                           | Enhanced due diligence, detailed evidence review, contractual safeguards, and specialist assessment                     |
| Tier 4 — Critical | Highly sensitive data, privileged agent capabilities, critical business services, substantial operational concentration, or severe potential consequences | Comprehensive assessment, independent assurance, targeted technical testing, executive-level risk review where required |

These tiers are illustrative internal categories, not universal regulatory classifications.

The organization should establish formal criteria for tier assignment, including mandatory escalation rules and circumstances in which a particular risk characteristic automatically requires enhanced review.

A low inherent-risk classification should not be based solely on a vendor's security certifications, contractual promises, or willingness to complete a questionnaire.

**Core principle:** Classify the supplier relationship based on its exposure before evaluating whether the vendor's controls adequately reduce that exposure.

### 16. AI Vendor Assessment Depth and Proportionality

Assessment depth should be proportional to inherent risk, uncertainty, technical complexity, and the potential consequences of supplier failure.

The organization should determine which evidence and reviews are required for each risk tier.

A proportional assessment may include:

- Initial supplier screening.
- Security and governance questionnaire.
- Independent assurance report review.
- Certification scope validation.
- Contractual security review.
- Data-processing review.
- Architecture and integration review.
- AI-specific threat assessment.
- Technical testing.
- Supplier interview or demonstration.
- Independent specialist review.
- Executive risk escalation.

Higher-risk relationships may require direct evidence of control operation rather than reliance on self-attestation.

Where a vendor does not permit penetration testing or direct inspection of its infrastructure, the organization should consider alternative assurance methods and document any resulting limitations.

Proportionality should reduce unnecessary assessment effort without allowing critical risks to escape review.

**Core principle:** Use the least burdensome assessment that provides sufficient evidence for the risk decision, and increase scrutiny when exposure or uncertainty is material.

### 17. Vendor Security Governance and Accountability

The assessment should evaluate whether the vendor has established security governance appropriate to the service it provides.

Relevant areas include:

- Security leadership and accountability.
- Information security policies.
- Risk management.
- Security control ownership.
- Internal compliance and assurance.
- Vulnerability management.
- Security incident management.
- Access governance.
- Employee and contractor responsibilities.
- Security awareness and training.
- Management oversight.
- Corrective action tracking.

The organization should identify whether the vendor's security program covers the specific AI service and its supporting infrastructure.

A supplier may operate a mature enterprise security program while excluding certain experimental services, acquired products, or subcontracted components from its formal control scope.

The assessment should therefore examine service-specific coverage and identify material exclusions.

**Core principle:** Supplier governance maturity should be evaluated against the service and components that actually support the organization's AI use case.

### 18. AI-Specific Governance and Lifecycle Management

The organization should assess whether the vendor maintains appropriate governance over the AI capabilities it develops, hosts, or supplies.

Relevant areas may include:

- Model and service inventory.
- AI risk management.
- Model release and change control.
- Data provenance and governance.
- Evaluation and testing practices.
- AI security reviews.
- Human oversight mechanisms where applicable.
- Documentation of model limitations.
- Monitoring of deployed AI services.
- Incident response for AI-related failures.
- Retirement and decommissioning processes.

The level of governance expected should depend on the supplier's role.

A provider that develops and trains models may have different responsibilities from a vendor that embeds a third-party model into a business application.

The organization should distinguish between controls the supplier can reasonably demonstrate and activities that remain the customer's responsibility.

**Core principle:** AI governance due diligence should evaluate the vendor's responsibilities across the AI lifecycle without assuming that every supplier performs every lifecycle activity.

### 19. Vendor Security Certifications and Independent Assurance

The organization should evaluate the relevance, scope, currency, and limitations of vendor security certifications and independent assurance reports.

Potential evidence includes:

- ISO/IEC 27001 certification.
- ISO/IEC 27701 certification where relevant.
- SOC 2 Type I or Type II reports.
- Independent penetration testing summaries.
- Cloud security assurance documentation.
- AI management system certifications or assessments where available and relevant.
- Industry-specific assurance reports.
- Independent audit findings and remediation summaries.

The assessment should establish:

- Which legal entity is covered.
- Which products and services are included.
- Which locations and infrastructure are covered.
- Whether subcontractors are included or excluded.
- The reporting or certification period.
- Any material qualifications or exceptions.
- Any complementary customer controls.
- Whether the evidence remains current.

A certificate or report should be considered one source of assurance rather than conclusive proof of suitability.

The organization should determine whether additional evidence is required for AI-specific threats, model behavior, agent permissions, data-processing practices, or the proposed integration.

**Core principle:** Independent assurance is valuable only to the extent that its scope, period, and control coverage support the decision being made.

### 20. Vendor AI Supply-Chain Transparency

The assessment should identify the material upstream dependencies that support the vendor's AI service.

These may include:

- Foundation model providers.
- Cloud infrastructure providers.
- Dataset suppliers.
- Model hosting services.
- Software libraries.
- Open-source components.
- Retrieval and search services.
- External APIs.
- Human review or annotation providers.
- Subprocessors.
- Managed service partners.

The organization should determine whether the vendor can identify material dependencies and explain how it manages the security risks associated with them.

The assessment should also examine whether the vendor can notify the organization of significant changes to upstream providers or components.

Supply-chain transparency may be limited by proprietary arrangements or confidentiality obligations. Such limitations should be recorded and evaluated in terms of their effect on assurance, resilience, and the organization's ability to investigate incidents.

**Core principle:** The assessment should identify material upstream dependencies and determine whether the vendor's supply-chain controls provide sufficient assurance for the intended use.

### 21. Model Provenance and Origin Verification

Where the vendor supplies or operates an AI model, the assessment should determine whether sufficient information is available to establish the model's identity, origin, version, and relevant history.

Relevant information may include:

- Model name and version.
- Model provider.
- Model family or architecture where disclosed.
- Acquisition source.
- Fine-tuning or customization history where relevant.
- Deployment version.
- Release documentation.
- Integrity verification mechanisms.
- Known limitations.
- Security advisories.
- Change-notification arrangements.

The organization should distinguish between a model's publicly documented identity and evidence that the specific model instance used by the service matches the approved version.

For hosted proprietary models, access to model weights or internal training details may be unavailable. In such cases, the organization should evaluate the provider's available release controls, documentation, assurance, and change-management commitments.

**Core principle:** Model provenance should be established to the degree necessary for the use case, with material visibility limitations documented rather than assumed away.

### 22. Vendor Data Collection, Processing, and Retention

The assessment should document how the vendor collects, processes, stores, retains, and deletes information submitted to or generated by the AI service.

The review should cover:

- Data submitted by users.
- Prompt and response processing.
- Uploaded documents and files.
- Retrieved information.
- Service telemetry.
- Security and operational logs.
- Feedback and evaluation data.
- Human review processes.
- Data used for service improvement.
- Backup and recovery copies.

The organization should determine whether the vendor's data practices align with the intended use, internal policies, and applicable contractual or legal requirements.

The assessment should distinguish between processing required to deliver the service and secondary uses such as product improvement, model training, analytics, or human review.

Where data may be reused, the organization should establish the applicable conditions, controls, and permissions.

Retention and deletion claims should be examined for exceptions involving security logs, legal obligations, backups, or provider-specific operational requirements.

**Core principle:** Vendor data handling must be understood across the full processing lifecycle, including secondary uses and copies that may remain after the primary transaction ends.

### 23. Vendor Data Residency and Cross-Border Processing

The assessment should identify where the vendor processes and stores organizational data and whether relevant information may be accessed from other jurisdictions.

The review should consider:

- Primary processing locations.
- Storage locations.
- Backup locations.
- Support and administrative access.
- Subprocessor locations.
- Cross-border transfers.
- Applicable contractual commitments.
- Data localization requirements where relevant.
- Incident investigation access.
- Service continuity arrangements.

The organization should not equate a stated storage region with a guarantee that all processing and administrative access occurs within that region.

For example, data may be stored in a designated region while support personnel, telemetry systems, or subprocessors operate elsewhere.

The organization should coordinate with legal and privacy specialists where jurisdictional or cross-border processing issues are material.

**Core principle:** Data residency assessment must cover storage, processing, support access, and relevant subprocessors—not storage location alone.

### 24. Vendor Use of Customer Data for Model Training

The assessment should establish whether customer data may be used to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve models or services.

The review should identify:

- Which data categories may be used.
- Whether use is enabled by default.
- Whether customer consent or configuration is required.
- Whether the vendor distinguishes between service delivery and model improvement.
- Whether data is aggregated, de-identified, or retained in another form.
- Whether human reviewers may access the data.
- Whether training use can be disabled.
- Whether restrictions apply to particular service tiers.
- Whether contractual commitments cover the relevant service.
- Whether the vendor can demonstrate the configured settings.

The assessment should not assume that an enterprise service excludes training use merely because it is marketed for business customers.

Where training or improvement use is prohibited, the organization should verify the applicable contractual language and technical or administrative configuration.

The organization should also consider whether derived artifacts, evaluation datasets, or other retained representations could preserve sensitive information.

**Core principle:** Training and service-improvement use must be explicitly understood, authorized where appropriate, and consistent with the organization's data governance requirements.

### 25. Vendor Access Control and Personnel Security

The assessment should evaluate how the vendor controls access to systems and information supporting the AI service.

Relevant controls include:

- Workforce identity management.
- Multi-factor authentication for privileged access where appropriate.
- Least-privilege authorization.
- Privileged access management.
- Separation of duties.
- Access review and revocation.
- Personnel screening where lawful and appropriate.
- Security awareness and training.
- Contractor access management.
- Administrative session monitoring.
- Emergency access procedures.
- Protection of customer data during support activities.

The organization should determine whether vendor personnel can access customer prompts, documents, model configurations, logs, or other sensitive information.

Where human access is possible, the assessment should examine authorization, purpose limitation, logging, supervision, and restrictions on copying or exporting information.

**Core principle:** Vendor personnel access should be restricted to legitimate operational needs, independently governed, and supported by appropriate monitoring and accountability.

### 26. Vendor Identity and API Security

The assessment should evaluate the mechanisms used to authenticate and authorize access to the vendor's AI services and related management interfaces.

Relevant controls include:

- API authentication.
- Credential and token management.
- Fine-grained authorization.
- Administrative access restrictions.
- Tenant isolation.
- Rate limiting and usage quotas.
- Secure transport.
- Key rotation and revocation.
- Audit logging.
- Service identity management.
- Protection of integration secrets.
- Restrictions on privileged operations.

The assessment should distinguish between the vendor's security controls and the organization's responsibilities for configuring and using the service securely.

For example, a provider may offer scoped API keys, but the customer must still ensure that its integration uses the minimum permissions required and stores those keys securely.

The organization should also assess whether a compromised credential could expose multiple projects, environments, or customer tenants.

**Core principle:** Vendor API capabilities must be evaluated together with the organization's actual authentication, authorization, and credential-management configuration.

### 27. Vendor Model and Service Change Management

The assessment should establish how the vendor manages changes that may affect the service's security, behavior, availability, or data-processing practices.

Relevant changes include:

- Model version updates.
- Changes to system capabilities.
- New tools or integrations.
- Changes to data retention or training practices.
- Security-control modifications.
- Infrastructure migrations.
- Subprocessor changes.
- API deprecation.
- Changes to service availability or support.
- Material changes to terms and conditions.

The organization should determine whether the vendor provides advance notice, release documentation, testing opportunities, and options to defer or reject certain changes.

The review should also establish how the organization can identify the deployed version and determine whether a change triggers a new security assessment.

Where the vendor controls release timing, the customer may need compensating controls such as monitoring, regression testing, restricted rollout, or contingency arrangements.

**Core principle:** Supplier-controlled changes must be visible and governed well enough to prevent unassessed changes from silently invalidating security assumptions.

### 28. Vendor Security Incident Management and Notification

The assessment should evaluate the vendor's capability to detect, investigate, contain, and recover from security incidents affecting the service or customer data.

Relevant areas include:

- Incident response policies.
- Security monitoring.
- Incident classification.
- Escalation and communication.
- Customer notification commitments.
- Forensic investigation.
- Evidence preservation.
- Containment and recovery.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Corrective action tracking.
- Coordination with customer incident response.
- Post-incident reporting.

The assessment should examine whether contractual notification commitments are sufficiently clear for the organization's operational and legal requirements.

The organization should also determine what information the vendor will provide during an incident, including affected services, data categories, timelines, containment actions, and remediation measures, subject to applicable legal and security constraints.

Vendor incident notification should be integrated into the organization's own security incident response process.

**Core principle:** Supplier incident response must provide a practical path for timely coordination, evidence collection, containment, and risk reassessment.

### 29. Evidence Collection and Assessment Traceability

The AI Vendor Assessment should preserve traceability between vendor responses, supporting evidence, identified gaps, risk conclusions, and approval decisions.

Evidence may include:

- Completed vendor questionnaires.
- Security certifications.
- Independent assurance reports.
- Policies and control descriptions.
- Technical architecture documentation.
- Data-processing documentation.
- Contractual terms.
- Security test summaries.
- Incident response procedures.
- Subprocessor lists.
- Model and service documentation.
- Demonstration or interview records.
- Remediation commitments.

Each evidence item should identify its source, date, scope, version where applicable, and relationship to the assessed service.

The assessor should distinguish between:

1. **Vendor assertion:** A statement supplied by the vendor.
2. **Documentary evidence:** A policy, report, certificate, or contractual document supporting the statement.
3. **Independent assurance:** Evidence produced by a suitably independent assessor or auditor.
4. **Technical validation:** Results from a test or verification of a control or configuration.
5. **Contractual commitment:** An enforceable obligation agreed by the parties.

These evidence types provide different levels and kinds of assurance. A contractual commitment establishes an obligation but does not by itself prove that the related control is technically effective.

The assessment should document material evidence gaps and explain how they affect the final risk decision.

**Core principle:** A vendor's claim should be traceable to appropriate evidence, and the strength of the conclusion should not exceed the strength of that evidence.

### 30. Part 1 Core Principle — Establishing the Basis for AI Vendor Due Diligence

An effective AI Vendor Assessment begins by identifying the exact supplier, service, business use case, data categories, deployment model, and operational dependencies involved.

It then establishes the inherent risk, assigns proportionate assessment requirements, identifies applicable governance expectations, and collects evidence from the vendor and relevant independent sources.

The assessment must preserve several essential distinctions:

- **Vendor identity versus service identity:** One supplier may operate multiple services with different risk profiles.
- **Inherent risk versus residual risk:** Supplier exposure should be classified before mitigation effectiveness is credited.
- **Vendor assertion versus verified evidence:** A completed questionnaire does not prove control effectiveness.
- **Supplier assurance versus use-case suitability:** A mature vendor may still be unsuitable for a particular deployment.
- **Service delivery versus secondary data use:** Processing information to deliver a service is not the same as using it for model improvement.
- **Approval versus ongoing authorization:** Initial approval does not eliminate the need for monitoring and reassessment.

**Core Principle:** AI vendor due diligence must establish a precise, evidence-based understanding of the supplier relationship before the organization decides which controls, contractual commitments, and approval conditions are necessary. The objective is not to prove that a vendor is universally secure, but to determine whether its specific service can be used acceptably within the organization's defined risk tolerance.

# 20.09 — AI Vendor Assessment Template

## Part 2 — Vendor Security Controls, Due Diligence, Evidence Validation, and Risk Treatment

### 31. Vendor Assessment Initiation and Mandatory Screening

The organization should establish a mandatory screening process to identify AI vendors that require formal third-party security and governance assessment before procurement, integration, or use.

Screening should be integrated into procurement, legal review, AI governance, enterprise architecture, and third-party risk management workflows. Business units should not be able to bypass the process simply by purchasing a service using a corporate card, activating a free trial, or integrating an AI capability into an existing software subscription.

The screening process should establish:

- Whether the service contains AI functionality.
- Whether the vendor processes organizational information.
- Whether the service connects to enterprise systems.
- Whether the service can access confidential or regulated data.
- Whether the service can execute actions or invoke tools.
- Whether the vendor introduces material operational dependency.
- Whether the supplier uses subcontractors or upstream AI providers.
- Whether the service is subject to specific contractual or regulatory requirements.
- Whether a formal security assessment is mandatory.
- Which approval authorities must participate.

The screening outcome should be recorded and linked to the relevant procurement or AI inventory record.

A service that does not require a full assessment should still have a documented rationale for the screening decision. Exemptions should follow defined criteria and should not be granted solely because the vendor is well known or the service is described as low risk.

**Required evidence:** Screening record, assessment requirement decision, service identification, exemption rationale where applicable, and workflow audit trail.

### 32. Vendor Assessment Scope Completeness

The assessment should verify that the review covers the complete supplier relationship relevant to the proposed AI use case.

The scope should include the vendor's service, supporting components, material subcontractors, data flows, administrative access, and integration points.

Scope validation should establish whether the assessment includes:

- The exact product or service being procured.
- The applicable service tier and configuration.
- All intended data categories.
- Relevant model and hosting providers.
- API and application integrations.
- Administrative and support access.
- Optional AI capabilities that may be enabled.
- Relevant subcontractors and subprocessors.
- Service continuity and exit dependencies.
- The organization's own integration responsibilities.

The organization should distinguish between a supplier-level assessment and a service-specific assessment. A corporate security report may cover the vendor's general environment but exclude the particular product, region, or AI service under consideration.

Material exclusions should be documented and evaluated for their effect on the assessment conclusion.

**Required evidence:** Approved scope statement, service architecture, dependency inventory, assurance scope review, and documented exclusions.

### 33. Vendor Questionnaire Design and Quality Control

The organization should maintain a structured AI Vendor Security Questionnaire that gathers consistent information while allowing questions to be tailored to the vendor's role and risk tier.

The questionnaire should cover conventional cybersecurity and AI-specific risk domains.

Typical domains include:

- Security governance.
- AI governance and lifecycle management.
- Identity and access management.
- Data security and privacy.
- Model and dataset provenance.
- AI application security.
- Model and API security.
- Agent and tool execution controls.
- Vulnerability management.
- Software supply-chain security.
- Security monitoring.
- Incident response.
- Business continuity.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Independent assurance.
- Contractual commitments.
- Service change management.
- Data deletion and exit arrangements.

Questions should be specific enough to support an evaluable response. A question such as "Is your AI service secure?" provides little actionable information.

A stronger question would ask whether customer data is used for model training, which service configurations affect that behavior, and what contractual or technical evidence supports the answer.

The questionnaire should distinguish mandatory questions from conditional questions and identify which responses require supporting evidence.

**Required evidence:** Approved questionnaire, question-to-control mapping, applicability rules, response validation criteria, and version history.

### 34. Vendor Response Completeness and Consistency

The assessor should review vendor responses for completeness, consistency, specificity, and relevance to the assessed service.

The review should identify:

- Unanswered mandatory questions.
- Ambiguous responses.
- Generic policy statements without service-specific detail.
- Contradictory answers across different sections.
- Unsupported claims.
- Responses that rely on unavailable controls.
- Inconsistent statements about data retention or training.
- Unexplained exclusions.
- Answers that refer to a different product or service tier.

Responses such as "industry-standard security is implemented" or "the service is fully compliant" should not be accepted as sufficient evidence without clarification.

Where answers are unclear, the assessor should request additional information and document the resolution.

The organization should not automatically interpret a blank response as evidence that a control is absent. It should classify the response as unknown or unverified until sufficient information is obtained, then evaluate the resulting uncertainty as part of the risk decision.

**Required evidence:** Completed questionnaire, clarification log, unresolved-question register, and assessor review notes.

### 35. Vendor Security Governance Validation

The organization should evaluate whether the vendor's security governance arrangements are appropriate for the AI service and its supporting infrastructure.

The review should examine:

- Security policy ownership and approval.
- Security risk assessment procedures.
- Control ownership.
- Executive oversight.
- Independent assurance.
- Security exception management.
- Corrective action tracking.
- Employee and contractor security responsibilities.
- Security awareness and training.
- Governance of acquired products and new services.

The assessor should verify that governance arrangements apply to the specific service under review.

For example, a vendor may have an established enterprise information security management system while operating an experimental AI feature outside the scope of its primary assurance program.

The assessment should identify any such gaps and determine whether they create material uncertainty or unacceptable exposure.

**Required evidence:** Security governance documentation, policy scope, assurance reports, responsibility matrix, and relevant corrective action records.

### 36. AI Governance and Lifecycle Control Validation

The assessment should determine whether the vendor has appropriate governance for the AI capabilities it develops, deploys, or operates.

Relevant controls may include:

- AI system inventory.
- Model release management.
- Model evaluation and validation.
- AI risk assessment.
- Data governance.
- Security testing.
- Documentation of intended use and limitations.
- Monitoring of deployed systems.
- Change management.
- Incident handling.
- Retirement and decommissioning.

The assessor should tailor expectations to the vendor's role. A model developer may be expected to provide information about model development and evaluation, whereas a reseller or software integrator may have more limited direct responsibility for the underlying model.

Where the vendor relies on upstream model providers, the assessment should examine how the vendor manages that dependency and what information it can provide about the upstream service.

**Required evidence:** AI governance policies, lifecycle procedures, model documentation, evaluation summaries, and supplier responsibility mapping.

### 37. Independent Assurance Report Validation

The organization should verify that independent assurance reports provide relevant evidence for the supplier relationship.

The assessment should examine:

- Report type and period.
- Auditor or assessor identity.
- Services covered.
- Legal entities and locations covered.
- Control objectives and testing scope.
- Exceptions and identified deficiencies.
- Complementary user-entity controls.
- Subservice organization treatment.
- Remediation statements.
- Changes since the reporting period.

For a SOC 2 report, the assessor should understand whether the report is Type I or Type II, which service and trust services criteria are included, and whether relevant exceptions affect the intended use.

A report may be valid but insufficient if it covers a different service, excludes the relevant AI component, or predates a material architectural change.

Where a reporting gap exists, the organization should request a bridge letter, additional evidence, or other appropriate assurance where available, while recognizing that these measures do not replace all missing audit work.

**Required evidence:** Assurance report, scope analysis, exception review, complementary-control assessment, and documented evidence gaps.

### 38. Security Certification Scope and Validity

The assessment should verify the scope and validity of vendor security certifications rather than recording certification status alone.

The review should establish:

- The certified legal entity.
- The applicable organizational boundaries.
- Included services and locations.
- Certification standard and version.
- Certification validity dates.
- Certification body.
- Relevant exclusions.
- Relationship to the proposed AI service.
- Availability of supporting scope documentation.

A certificate should not be assumed to cover every service offered by the vendor.

Where the AI service is newly launched, acquired, or operated by a separate subsidiary, the organization should verify whether it is included in the certification scope.

The assessor should also distinguish certification of a management system from direct testing of specific AI security controls.

**Required evidence:** Certificate, scope statement, validity verification, relevant audit documentation, and applicability assessment.

### 39. Vendor Control Effectiveness and Evidence Quality

The assessment should evaluate whether the vendor's stated controls are implemented and effective, rather than relying exclusively on descriptions of intended processes.

Evidence strength should be evaluated according to the control's purpose and the level of assurance required.

For example:

| Evidence type                | Typical value                                           | Main limitation                                      |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Vendor questionnaire         | Establishes the vendor's stated position                | May be incomplete or unsupported                     |
| Policy or procedure          | Demonstrates documented requirements                    | Does not prove effective operation                   |
| Configuration evidence       | Demonstrates settings at a particular point in time     | May not establish sustained operation                |
| Independent assurance report | Provides third-party examination within defined scope   | May omit the specific service or control             |
| Technical test result        | Demonstrates observed behavior under defined conditions | May have limited coverage                            |
| Contractual commitment       | Establishes an agreed obligation                        | Does not independently prove technical effectiveness |
| Operational metrics          | Provides evidence of ongoing performance                | Depends on metric quality and completeness           |

The assessor should record what each evidence item demonstrates and what remains unverified.

Where evidence is insufficient for a material control, the organization should request additional validation, introduce compensating safeguards, restrict the proposed use, or decline the service.

**Required evidence:** Evidence assessment notes, control effectiveness conclusions, unresolved gaps, and risk treatment decisions.

### 40. Data Processing and Handling Validation

The organization should validate the vendor's data processing practices against the proposed use case and data classification.

The review should cover:

- Data collection.
- Data transmission.
- Processing locations.
- Storage.
- Access by vendor personnel.
- Logging and telemetry.
- Retention.
- Backup copies.
- Data export.
- Secondary use.
- Deletion.
- Subprocessor access.

The assessor should compare vendor documentation, product configuration, and contractual terms to identify inconsistencies.

For example, the service documentation may describe a short retention period while security logs or support records are retained for a longer period. The assessment should distinguish these data categories rather than treating them as one retention setting.

Where the vendor provides configurable controls, the organization should determine who is responsible for enabling and validating them.

**Required evidence:** Data-flow documentation, processing terms, retention settings, data-handling configuration, and validation results.

### 41. Model Training and Service-Improvement Controls

The organization should verify whether submitted information may be used for model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, analytics, or other service-improvement activities.

The assessment should determine:

- Whether such use occurs.
- Which data categories are eligible.
- Whether the use is enabled by default.
- Whether the customer can opt out.
- Whether the setting varies by service tier.
- Whether human review is involved.
- How the setting is configured and verified.
- Whether contractual restrictions apply.
- Whether derived artifacts may be retained.
- How changes to the practice are communicated.

The organization should distinguish between a vendor's general policy and the specific settings applicable to the customer's account or service.

Where training use is prohibited, the assessor should seek evidence that the applicable contractual and technical restrictions are effective.

If the vendor cannot adequately establish the data-use conditions, the organization should evaluate whether the service can be used with less sensitive data or whether the uncertainty makes the proposed use unacceptable.

**Required evidence:** Data-use terms, account or service configuration, vendor confirmation, contractual provisions, and residual-risk analysis.

### 42. Data Residency and Cross-Border Access Validation

The organization should verify where relevant data is processed, stored, backed up, and accessed by vendor personnel or subprocessors.

The assessment should distinguish:

- Primary storage location.
- Processing location.
- Backup and disaster-recovery locations.
- Support and administrative access.
- Telemetry and logging infrastructure.
- Subprocessor processing.
- Cross-border transfers.

Where a specific geographic restriction is required, the organization should establish which controls and contractual commitments enforce it.

The assessment should not assume that regional storage guarantees regional processing or prevents access from other jurisdictions.

Where data location cannot be independently verified, the organization should document the limitation and obtain legal or privacy review when appropriate.

**Required evidence:** Regional service documentation, data-flow records, contractual commitments, subprocessor locations, and applicable transfer assessments.

### 43. Encryption and Cryptographic Key Management

The assessment should examine how the vendor protects data in transit and at rest, including data processed by the AI service and information held in associated storage systems.

Relevant controls include:

- Encryption in transit.
- Encryption at rest.
- Key generation and protection.
- Key rotation.
- Access to cryptographic keys.
- Separation of duties.
- Backup encryption.
- Key revocation.
- Customer-managed key options where available.
- Protection of secrets used for service integration.

The organization should establish whether the service supports the cryptographic controls required for the intended data classification.

Where customer-managed keys are available, the assessment should determine whether their use is mandatory, optional, or limited to specific service tiers.

The existence of encryption should not be treated as sufficient if vendor personnel or compromised application components can access plaintext data without appropriate authorization.

**Required evidence:** Cryptographic documentation, key-management design, service configuration, and relevant assurance or test results.

### 44. Vendor Identity and Privileged Access Validation

The assessment should examine the vendor's controls for employee, contractor, administrator, and service-account access.

The review should address:

- Identity verification and authentication.
- Privileged access restrictions.
- Multi-factor authentication where appropriate.
- Role separation.
- Just-in-time or time-limited access where supported.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Credential rotation.
- Emergency access.
- Session monitoring.
- Access revocation.
- Support personnel access to customer data.

The organization should determine whether the vendor's personnel can access customer prompts, documents, model configurations, logs, or other sensitive information.

Where privileged access is necessary, the vendor should demonstrate how access is authorized, limited, monitored, and revoked.

**Required evidence:** Access governance documentation, privileged-access procedures, relevant assurance evidence, and customer-data access controls.

### 45. Tenant Isolation and Cross-Customer Data Protection

For multi-tenant AI services, the organization should evaluate how the vendor separates customers' data, identities, configurations, and processing contexts.

The assessment should consider:

- Logical or physical tenant separation.
- Authorization enforcement.
- Storage and retrieval isolation.
- Cache separation.
- Session isolation.
- Administrative access boundaries.
- Cross-tenant API behavior.
- Shared model or service behavior.
- Isolation of customer-specific configuration.
- Handling of support and diagnostic data.

The organization should determine whether shared infrastructure introduces a credible path for unauthorized cross-customer access.

Where the service provides retrieval, memory, or agent capabilities, the assessment should consider whether information from one customer context can become available in another context through incorrect authorization, caching, or configuration.

The depth of technical validation should reflect the sensitivity of the data and the vendor's exposure.

**Required evidence:** Tenant isolation design, assurance documentation, relevant test results, and documented limitations.

### 46. Model and Artifact Integrity Controls

The assessment should evaluate how the vendor protects model artifacts, deployment configurations, adapters, and other components that influence AI service behavior.

Relevant controls include:

- Model provenance.
- Artifact access restrictions.
- Integrity verification.
- Controlled release processes.
- Version identification.
- Protection against unauthorized substitution.
- Change approval.
- Deployment traceability.
- Security testing before release.
- Incident handling for compromised artifacts.

For hosted proprietary models, direct inspection of model weights may not be possible. In such cases, the organization should evaluate available evidence of release governance, integrity protection, change management, and service-level controls.

The organization should also determine whether material model changes are communicated and whether the customer can identify the version or service behavior in use.

**Required evidence:** Model release documentation, artifact governance, integrity controls, version information, and change-notification commitments.

### 47. Vendor AI Application and API Security

The assessment should evaluate the security of the vendor's application interfaces, model endpoints, administrative functions, and supporting APIs.

Controls may include:

- Strong authentication.
- Fine-grained authorization.
- Input validation.
- Rate limiting.
- Resource quotas.
- Secure transport.
- Administrative access restrictions.
- API version management.
- Secure error handling.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Protection against unauthorized data access.
- Vulnerability management.

The review should determine whether the vendor's controls are appropriate for the organization's intended integration.

Where the vendor exposes APIs, the organization should establish whether access can be scoped by project, user, service, or operation and whether credentials can be revoked promptly.

The assessment should also consider the consequences of API abuse, including excessive consumption, unauthorized requests, data extraction, and disruption of service.

**Required evidence:** API security documentation, authentication and authorization evidence, relevant test results, and service configuration.

### 48. Prompt Injection and Untrusted Input Controls

Where the service processes prompts, retrieved documents, uploaded files, external content, or multimodal inputs, the assessment should evaluate how the vendor addresses direct and indirect prompt injection.

The review should consider:

- Treatment of untrusted instructions.
- Separation of system configuration from external content.
- Input validation.
- Retrieval controls.
- Tool permission boundaries.
- Output validation.
- Enforcement of authorization outside the model.
- Human approval for consequential actions.
- Security testing and red teaming.
- Monitoring for suspicious behavior.

The organization should not accept a claim that prompt injection is fully eliminated without evidence supporting the scope and limitations of that claim.

The assessment should focus on whether an injection attempt can produce a meaningful security consequence, such as unauthorized data disclosure, privilege misuse, or unsafe tool execution.

Where the vendor does not provide sufficient evidence, the organization should evaluate whether its own integration can reduce the exposure through restricted permissions, validation, monitoring, and approval gates.

**Required evidence:** AI security documentation, adversarial testing summaries, control descriptions, and residual-risk analysis.

### 49. Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Knowledge-Base Security

For vendors providing RAG, enterprise search, vector databases, or knowledge-grounded AI, the assessment should evaluate how retrieval permissions and data boundaries are enforced.

Relevant controls include:

- Document-level authorization.
- Tenant isolation.
- Source authentication.
- Index integrity.
- Metadata protection.
- Synchronization with source-system permissions.
- Access revocation.
- Secure ingestion.
- Retrieval logging.
- Protection against malicious document content.
- Prevention of unauthorized information disclosure.

The assessment should determine whether the vendor's service enforces access controls before sensitive content enters the model context or whether it relies on downstream filtering.

The organization should also examine how deleted or reclassified documents are removed from indexes and caches.

**Required evidence:** Retrieval architecture, authorization model, indexing procedures, access tests, and evidence of permission synchronization.

### 50. AI Agent and Tool-Execution Security

Where the vendor supplies AI agents or tools capable of executing actions, the assessment should evaluate how the service restricts and validates those actions.

The review should establish:

- Which tools are available.
- Which permissions each tool receives.
- Whether permissions can be scoped narrowly.
- Whether authorization is enforced outside the model.
- How high-impact actions are approved.
- Whether action limits are supported.
- How tool calls are logged.
- Whether execution can be stopped.
- How delegated or chained actions are governed.
- Whether the vendor can demonstrate security testing.

The organization should distinguish between a model that recommends an action and a system that can execute it.

An agent that can send emails, modify records, invoke cloud APIs, or delete information introduces additional risks that require stronger assessment than a text-only assistant with no operational privileges.

The organization should also assess whether the vendor's execution model allows the customer to impose independent restrictions.

**Required evidence:** Agent capability inventory, tool permission model, execution controls, testing results, and customer-configurable safeguards.

### 51. Vendor Software Supply-Chain Security

The assessment should evaluate how the vendor manages vulnerabilities and security risks in the software components supporting the AI service.

Relevant controls include:

- Dependency inventory.
- Vulnerability scanning.
- Secure development practices.
- Code review.
- Software composition analysis.
- Secure build pipelines.
- Artifact integrity.
- Patch management.
- Container security.
- Open-source governance.
- Security advisories.
- Dependency update procedures.

The review should consider AI-specific dependencies such as model-serving frameworks, orchestration libraries, retrieval components, and plugins.

The organization should determine whether the vendor can identify and respond to material vulnerabilities affecting the service and whether relevant incidents or remediation delays are communicated.

Where direct visibility into the vendor's internal software components is limited, independent assurance and documented vulnerability management practices may provide supporting evidence.

**Required evidence:** Secure development documentation, vulnerability management procedures, software supply-chain assurance, and relevant remediation records.

### 52. Vulnerability Disclosure and Remediation Management

The assessment should determine how the vendor identifies, prioritizes, remediates, and communicates vulnerabilities affecting the service.

The review should cover:

- Vulnerability discovery methods.
- Security testing frequency.
- Severity classification.
- Remediation targets.
- Emergency patching.
- Coordinated vulnerability disclosure.
- Customer notification.
- Exploit monitoring.
- Exception management.
- Remediation verification.

The organization should determine whether the vendor's remediation approach is compatible with the criticality of the service and the organization's exposure.

A vendor's published remediation target should not automatically be interpreted as proof that all vulnerabilities are resolved within that period.

Where a material vulnerability remains unresolved, the organization should evaluate the exposure, available mitigations, provider communication, and need for restrictions or contingency measures.

**Required evidence:** Vulnerability management policy, remediation service levels, relevant assurance evidence, and documented material exceptions.

### 53. Security Logging and Monitoring Capabilities

The assessment should evaluate the vendor's ability to detect and investigate security events affecting the service and customer data.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Authentication and authorization logging.
- Administrative activity logging.
- API usage monitoring.
- Data-access logging.
- Model and configuration change records.
- Tool-execution logs.
- Security alerting.
- Log integrity protection.
- Retention controls.
- Customer access to relevant logs.
- Integration with customer security monitoring.

The organization should determine whether the available telemetry is sufficient to investigate suspected unauthorized access, data disclosure, or misuse of the AI service.

The assessment should also consider whether logs may contain sensitive prompts, outputs, personal information, or credentials.

Where the vendor does not provide detailed telemetry, the organization should evaluate alternative monitoring options and document any reduction in detection or investigation capability.

**Required evidence:** Logging documentation, sample event records where available, monitoring capabilities, retention settings, and integration options.

### 54. Incident Response and Customer Notification Validation

The assessment should determine whether the vendor's incident response arrangements support the organization's security and operational requirements.

The review should establish:

- How incidents are detected and classified.
- How customers are notified.
- Which incident categories trigger notification.
- What information is provided.
- How evidence is preserved.
- How containment is coordinated.
- How recovery is validated.
- How root-cause analysis is communicated.
- How corrective actions are tracked.
- How the vendor supports customer investigations.

The organization should compare the vendor's incident notification commitments with applicable legal, contractual, and operational requirements.

Where exact notification timelines are important, the organization should seek explicit contractual terms rather than relying on general statements that customers will be notified promptly.

**Required evidence:** Incident response procedures, notification terms, incident coordination arrangements, and assurance documentation.

### 55. Business Continuity and Service Resilience

The assessment should evaluate the vendor's ability to maintain or restore the AI service following outages, infrastructure failures, cyber incidents, or supplier disruptions.

Relevant controls include:

- Business continuity planning.
- Disaster recovery.
- Backup and restoration.
- Redundancy.
- Recovery-time objectives.
- Recovery-point objectives.
- Dependency management.
- Crisis communication.
- Resilience testing.
- Service availability monitoring.
- Regional or provider failover where supported.

The organization should evaluate the vendor's recovery arrangements in the context of the business process that depends on the AI service.

A service with a published availability target may still be unsuitable if it cannot support the organization's recovery requirements or if the target excludes important dependencies.

The assessment should also determine whether the vendor's recovery approach preserves data integrity and appropriate security controls.

**Required evidence:** Continuity and disaster recovery documentation, availability commitments, recovery test summaries, and relevant contractual provisions.

### 56. Subprocessor and Upstream Provider Risk

The organization should assess the vendor's material subprocessors and upstream providers where their activities affect the security or operation of the AI service.

The review should consider:

- Identity and function of material subprocessors.
- Data categories shared with them.
- Processing locations.
- Access to customer information.
- Security assurance.
- Contractual flow-down obligations.
- Incident notification.
- Change notification.
- Subprocessor replacement.
- Concentration and dependency risk.

The organization should determine whether the vendor maintains sufficient oversight of its supply chain and whether it can provide relevant information when a material upstream dependency changes.

Where a subprocessor creates a significant exposure, the organization should determine whether additional safeguards, contractual commitments, or restrictions are necessary.

**Required evidence:** Subprocessor list, supply-chain governance documentation, applicable contractual provisions, and change-notification arrangements.

### 57. Intellectual Property and Model Output Risk

The assessment should examine the supplier's contractual and operational arrangements concerning customer data, model inputs, generated outputs, and intellectual property.

Relevant questions include:

- Who owns or controls customer-submitted data?
- What rights does the vendor receive to process the data?
- What rights apply to generated outputs?
- Can customer inputs be reused for model improvement?
- What restrictions apply to output reuse?
- How are third-party intellectual property claims handled?
- Are there relevant indemnities or contractual limitations?
- What happens to customer data and outputs when the relationship ends?

The organization should not assume that generated outputs are automatically free of intellectual property, confidentiality, or other legal concerns.

Legal review should determine the applicable contractual and legal position for the service and use case.

From a security perspective, the assessment should also consider whether the vendor can access, retain, or disclose proprietary prompts, source code, technical designs, or other confidential material.

**Required evidence:** Contract terms, data rights provisions, intellectual property clauses, and legal review conclusions.

### 58. Vendor Exit, Portability, and Secure Decommissioning

The assessment should determine whether the organization can terminate the supplier relationship without unacceptable data, operational, or security consequences.

Exit planning should address:

- Data export.
- Data deletion.
- Deletion of derived or cached information where applicable.
- Credential revocation.
- Removal of integrations.
- Transfer of operational knowledge.
- Migration to an alternative provider.
- Preservation of required audit evidence.
- Continuity during transition.
- Termination of subcontractor access.
- Confirmation of closure obligations.

The organization should assess whether proprietary formats, vendor-specific workflows, model dependencies, or specialized integrations create significant lock-in.

Where the vendor cannot guarantee complete deletion from all backup systems immediately, the organization should understand the applicable retention schedule, access restrictions, and final deletion commitments.

**Required evidence:** Exit plan, portability documentation, deletion terms, credential revocation procedure, and closure confirmation requirements.

### 59. Vendor Findings, Remediation, and Corrective Action Tracking

Material deficiencies identified during due diligence should be recorded and managed through a controlled remediation process.

Each finding should include:

- Finding identifier.
- Vendor and service.
- Affected control.
- Evidence supporting the finding.
- Risk description.
- Severity.
- Business consequence.
- Required corrective action.
- Vendor and internal owners.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards.
- Verification requirements.
- Approval or exception reference.

The organization should distinguish between findings that prevent onboarding and findings that can be managed under an approved remediation plan.

For example, an incomplete logging capability may be acceptable for a low-risk service with restricted data access, but unacceptable for a privileged agent handling critical operations if it prevents investigation of consequential actions.

Remediation closure should require appropriate evidence rather than a vendor statement that the issue has been addressed.

**Required evidence:** Vendor findings register, remediation commitments, verification results, and documented closure decisions.

### 60. Residual Vendor Risk and Compensating Controls

The organization should evaluate residual risk after considering the vendor's controls, the customer's own safeguards, contractual protections, and any planned remediation.

Residual-risk analysis should consider:

- Unresolved control deficiencies.
- Limits on vendor transparency.
- Unverified security claims.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Data-handling uncertainty.
- Technical testing limitations.
- Incident response constraints.
- Operational concentration.
- Exit complexity.
- The effectiveness of customer-side safeguards.

Compensating controls may include restricting data classification, disabling optional features, reducing tool permissions, applying additional monitoring, limiting the user population, or maintaining an alternative service.

Compensating controls should address the actual risk. For example, contractual commitments alone may not adequately compensate for an agent that can execute unauthorized privileged operations.

The organization should document who owns the residual risk and which conditions must remain true for the service to continue operating.

**Required evidence:** Residual-risk analysis, compensating-control validation, risk acceptance record, and operating restrictions.

### 61. Vendor Security Approval and Onboarding Gate

The organization should establish a formal approval gate before the AI service is procured, integrated, or deployed in a way that creates material exposure.

Approval criteria should include:

- Completion of mandatory due diligence.
- Review of relevant assurance evidence.
- Resolution or authorized treatment of material findings.
- Completion of required legal and privacy reviews.
- Confirmation of data-processing conditions.
- Review of the proposed integration architecture.
- Approval of residual risk by the authorized owner.
- Completion of required contractual safeguards.
- Definition of ongoing monitoring requirements.
- Confirmation of exit and continuity arrangements where required.

The approval outcome should distinguish between approved, conditionally approved, restricted, rejected, and pending status.

Conditional approval should specify the permitted use, restrictions, responsible owners, deadlines, and consequences of non-compliance.

A vendor should not be treated as approved for every possible AI use case simply because one service has passed assessment.

**Required evidence:** Approval record, onboarding decision, residual-risk acceptance, contractual review, and documented conditions of use.

### 62. Vendor Risk Acceptance and Exception Management

Where a supplier cannot meet a required control or provide sufficient evidence, the organization should use a formal risk acceptance or exception process.

The record should identify:

- The unmet requirement.
- The affected service.
- The reason for the gap.
- The associated threat scenario.
- Potential business consequences.
- Existing and proposed safeguards.
- Available alternatives.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Approval authority.
- Expiry or review date.
- Conditions for remediation or termination.

An exception should not override applicable law, contractual obligations, or non-waivable internal requirements.

The organization should also distinguish between a temporary exception and a permanent decision to operate under a different approved control model.

Where uncertainty is substantial, the organization should evaluate whether restricted use or rejection is more appropriate than accepting the risk.

**Required evidence:** Exception record, approval, compensating-control evidence, review schedule, and closure or renewal decision.

### 63. Vendor Risk Register Integration

Material supplier risks should be linked to the organization's third-party risk register, enterprise risk register, or established GRC repository.

The integration should preserve the distinction between:

- A vendor control deficiency.
- A technical weakness in the AI service.
- A supplier dependency.
- A business risk.
- A contractual obligation.
- A residual-risk acceptance decision.

For example:

- **Finding:** The vendor does not provide sufficient audit logs for agent tool execution.
- **Threat scenario:** Unauthorized tool use cannot be detected or reconstructed reliably.
- **Business risk:** Sensitive information may be disclosed or business records altered without timely detection.
- **Treatment:** Restrict tool permissions, require additional customer-side logging where feasible, obtain contractual commitments, or reject the use case if the residual risk remains unacceptable.

The organization should avoid duplicating risk records unnecessarily while ensuring that vendor risks are visible to the business and technical owners responsible for managing them.

**Required evidence:** Linked risk records, finding-to-risk mapping, assigned owners, treatment plans, and reporting integration.

### 64. Vendor Reassessment and Continuous Monitoring

The organization should establish a risk-based schedule for reassessing AI vendors and a process for triggering reviews when material changes occur.

Reassessment triggers may include:

- Renewal of a critical contract.
- Significant model or service changes.
- Changes to data retention or training practices.
- New subcontractors or upstream providers.
- Material security incidents.
- Newly disclosed vulnerabilities.
- Changes to service hosting or data locations.
- Expansion of tool or agent capabilities.
- New data classifications.
- Deterioration in service availability.
- Expired assurance reports.
- Repeated failure to meet remediation commitments.
- Material changes in business dependency.

The reassessment should determine whether existing controls remain adequate and whether the current approval decision remains valid.

The organization should define how it obtains updated evidence, who evaluates changes, and when the service must be restricted pending review.

**Required evidence:** Monitoring plan, reassessment schedule, trigger catalogue, vendor change records, and completed review decisions.

### 65. Vendor Security Metrics and Management Reporting

The organization should define metrics that provide visibility into the quality and effectiveness of AI vendor risk management.

Potential indicators include:

- Percentage of in-scope AI vendors assessed before onboarding.
- Percentage of critical vendors with current assurance evidence.
- Number of unresolved critical and high-severity vendor findings.
- Age of overdue vendor remediation actions.
- Number of expired risk exceptions.
- Percentage of material vendors with documented exit plans.
- Number of vendors with unresolved data-processing uncertainties.
- Number of supplier incidents affecting AI services.
- Percentage of material vendor changes reassessed within required timeframes.
- Number of critical services dependent on a single provider.

Metrics should have defined owners, data sources, calculation methods, thresholds, and reporting frequency.

A high percentage of completed questionnaires does not establish that supplier risk is adequately managed. Reporting should also show evidence quality, unresolved exposure, remediation progress, and concentration risk.

**Required evidence:** Metric catalogue, management dashboard, trend analysis, and documented actions arising from reported risks.

### 66. Vendor Assessment Quality Assurance

The organization should implement quality checks to ensure that AI Vendor Assessments are consistent, evidence-based, and appropriate for the supplier's risk tier.

Quality review should verify that:

- The correct service and legal supplier were assessed.
- The intended use case is clearly documented.
- The risk tier is supported by defined criteria.
- Mandatory questionnaire responses are complete.
- Assurance evidence covers the relevant service.
- Material subcontractors are considered.
- Data-processing practices are understood.
- AI-specific security risks are addressed.
- Findings are supported by evidence.
- Remediation commitments are verifiable.
- Residual risks are appropriately accepted.
- Contractual requirements are documented.
- Approval conditions are enforceable.
- Reassessment requirements are defined.

High-risk supplier relationships should receive independent review where required by the organization's governance framework.

**Required evidence:** Quality assurance checklist, reviewer comments, remediation records, and final assessment approval.

### 67. Assessment Automation and Procurement Workflow Enforcement

The organization may automate repeatable activities to improve consistency and prevent AI services from bypassing required due diligence.

Suitable automation includes:

- Identifying AI-related procurement requests.
- Triggering mandatory screening.
- Assigning risk tiers based on approved criteria.
- Requesting vendor questionnaires.
- Tracking missing evidence.
- Routing reviews to relevant specialists.
- Linking findings to risk records.
- Tracking remediation commitments.
- Alerting owners to expired assurance evidence.
- Triggering reassessment before contract renewal.
- Preventing unauthorized onboarding when mandatory approvals are missing.

Automation rules should be tested and controlled. Incorrect rules could allow an unassessed supplier to process sensitive information or incorrectly mark an incomplete review as approved.

The organization should also provide a controlled route for emergency procurement, with appropriate authorization and retrospective review where permitted by policy.

**Required evidence:** Workflow design, automation rules, test results, exception procedures, and audit logs.

### 68. Contractual Security Requirements and Control Mapping

The organization should translate material vendor security requirements into contractual obligations where appropriate.

Contractual provisions may address:

- Permitted data processing.
- Restrictions on model training and secondary use.
- Confidentiality.
- Security control obligations.
- Access restrictions.
- Incident notification.
- Vulnerability remediation.
- Audit and assurance rights.
- Subprocessor management.
- Change notification.
- Data retention and deletion.
- Service availability.
- Business continuity.
- Cooperation during investigations.
- Exit assistance.
- Liability and remedies.

The assessment should map material risks to the contractual clauses intended to address them.

The organization should distinguish between an obligation that can be enforced contractually and a technical safeguard that must be implemented in the service or customer integration.

For example, a contract can require the vendor to notify the customer of material security incidents, but it cannot by itself prevent an agent from executing an unauthorized action.

Legal review should confirm that proposed clauses are appropriate for the relevant jurisdiction, service, and negotiating context.

**Required evidence:** Contractual control mapping, approved clauses, legal review, and records of negotiated deviations.

### 69. Vendor Dispute Resolution and Unresolved Assurance Gaps

The organization should establish a process for handling cases where vendor responses, assurance reports, or technical evidence are incomplete, inconsistent, or disputed.

The process should identify:

- The disputed control or statement.
- The evidence already available.
- The information still required.
- The supplier's explanation.
- The internal technical or legal assessment.
- The potential risk of the unresolved issue.
- The available alternative safeguards.
- The final decision authority.

Where the vendor refuses to provide evidence, the organization should not automatically conclude that the control is absent. It should, however, recognize that the lack of evidence may prevent the organization from establishing that the control is effective.

Possible outcomes include requesting alternative evidence, restricting the intended use, applying additional customer-side controls, escalating the issue, accepting residual risk where permitted, or rejecting the service.

**Required evidence:** Clarification record, evidence-gap assessment, vendor correspondence, and authorized decision.

### 70. Vendor Security Incident and Lessons-Learned Integration

Vendor incidents should be linked to the organization's AI security incident management and third-party risk processes.

The organization should determine whether a vendor incident reveals a weakness in:

- Supplier selection.
- Assessment scope.
- Evidence validation.
- Contractual obligations.
- Monitoring.
- Incident notification.
- Shared responsibility.
- Integration security.
- Business continuity.
- Exit planning.

The review should identify affected AI systems and determine whether existing risk assessments remain valid.

Where necessary, the organization should update vendor risk ratings, revise control requirements, initiate remediation, restrict the service, or reassess alternative providers.

Lessons learned should also be incorporated into future questionnaires and assessment procedures so that recurring supplier weaknesses are addressed systematically.

**Required evidence:** Incident report, supplier impact analysis, root-cause review, updated risk records, and corrective action tracking.

### 71. Periodic Testing and Sampling of Vendor Assessments

The organization should periodically test whether its AI Vendor Assessment process operates as designed.

Testing may include sampling supplier relationships across risk tiers, business units, service types, and procurement channels.

Representative procedures include:

- Confirm that selected AI vendors were screened before onboarding.
- Verify that the correct service was assessed.
- Check that assurance evidence covered the assessed service.
- Trace material findings to risk records.
- Verify that risk exceptions remain valid.
- Confirm that contractual safeguards were completed.
- Check that reassessment triggers were followed.
- Verify that terminated services completed required exit controls.
- Confirm that supplier incidents were reflected in risk decisions.
- Test whether management reports accurately reflect underlying records.

Sampling should be risk-based. Critical suppliers, vendors with previous control failures, and services processing sensitive data or performing privileged actions may warrant more intensive review.

**Required evidence:** Sampling plan, test workpapers, findings, remediation assignments, and retest results.

### 72. Vendor Exit and Offboarding Control Validation

When an AI vendor relationship ends, the organization should verify that offboarding controls are completed and that residual access and data exposure are appropriately addressed.

The process should include:

- Revocation of API keys and credentials.
- Removal of user and service accounts.
- Termination of integrations.
- Removal of unnecessary network access.
- Data export where required.
- Data deletion requests.
- Review of retained backups and logs.
- Termination of subprocessor access where applicable.
- Preservation of required assessment evidence.
- Transfer or replacement of business processes.
- Confirmation of contractual closure obligations.

The organization should verify completion rather than relying exclusively on an internal ticket being marked closed.

Where the vendor provides a deletion certificate or confirmation, the organization should assess its scope and limitations.

Offboarding should also include an evaluation of whether the organization remains dependent on vendor-specific configurations, proprietary data formats, or undocumented workflows.

**Required evidence:** Offboarding checklist, credential revocation records, deletion confirmation, integration removal evidence, and closure approval.

### 73. Part 2 Core Implementation Principle

AI Vendor Assessment should convert supplier information into a structured and defensible decision about service suitability, security exposure, contractual requirements, and ongoing oversight.

The organization should evaluate the vendor's stated controls, validate the relevance and strength of available evidence, identify unresolved gaps, and establish enforceable treatment measures before approving the service.

The assessment must distinguish between **a completed questionnaire and validated assurance**, **corporate certification and service-specific coverage**, **contractual obligations and technical enforcement**, **supplier risk and customer integration risk**, and **vendor approval and continued authorization**.

**Core Principle:** An AI vendor should be approved only when the organization has sufficient evidence to evaluate the supplier relationship, has treated material risks within its risk tolerance, and has established contractual, technical, and operational safeguards that remain effective throughout the service lifecycle.

## Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Supplier Oversight, Risk Acceptance, and Lifecycle Management

### 74. AI Vendor Governance Operating Model

An AI vendor governance operating model defines how the organization directs, oversees, approves, monitors, and challenges risks arising from external AI providers. It converts individual vendor assessment results into an organization-wide governance process.

The operating model must connect procurement, cybersecurity, AI governance, enterprise risk management, privacy, legal, compliance, business ownership, and internal audit. These functions should not operate as disconnected approval checkpoints. They must share a common view of supplier criticality, material risks, control deficiencies, contractual obligations, and remediation status.

A mature operating model establishes:

- **Governance authority:** Who can approve a supplier for a defined risk category.
- **Risk ownership:** Who remains accountable for the business consequences of using the supplier.
- **Control ownership:** Who validates technical, operational, privacy, and compliance requirements.
- **Decision escalation:** How unresolved findings reach the appropriate authority.
- **Lifecycle oversight:** How supplier risk is monitored after onboarding.
- **Evidence retention:** How assessment decisions and their supporting evidence remain auditable.

The operating model should distinguish supplier-level risk from use-case-level risk. A vendor may be acceptable for a low-impact internal productivity tool but unsuitable for an autonomous agent processing sensitive data or performing privileged actions.

### 75. Three Lines and Independent Oversight

The organization should establish clear responsibilities for operational ownership, risk oversight, and independent assurance. A Three Lines model can support this separation, although its implementation should reflect organizational size and governance maturity.

| Governance layer                            | AI vendor responsibilities                                                                                          | Examples                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line — business and operations        | Own the supplier relationship, define the business purpose, implement operational controls, and monitor performance | Business owner, procurement relationship manager, system owner         |
| Second line — risk and compliance oversight | Establish requirements, challenge risk assessments, define escalation thresholds, and monitor aggregate risk        | Cybersecurity GRC, AI governance, privacy, compliance, enterprise risk |
| Third line — independent assurance          | Independently evaluate whether governance and controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively         | Internal audit or another suitably independent assurance function      |

The first line must not treat an assessment questionnaire as a substitute for operational accountability. The second line should challenge unsupported supplier claims and inconsistent risk decisions. The third line should evaluate the effectiveness of the governance system rather than assume responsibility for approving individual operational purchases.

Where a small organization cannot maintain fully separate functions, it should document compensating measures, such as independent review by a qualified external assessor or periodic review by an executive who was not responsible for the original decision.

### 76. Executive Accountability for AI Supplier Risk

Executive accountability is necessary when AI suppliers create material exposure involving critical services, regulated decisions, sensitive data, substantial financial commitments, or significant dependencies.

The organization should identify an executive accountable for the overall third-party AI risk program. This executive is responsible for ensuring that supplier governance has appropriate authority, funding, staffing, escalation mechanisms, and management visibility.

Executive accountability does not mean that one executive personally reviews every vendor. Instead, the executive ensures that decisions are delegated appropriately and that high-risk exceptions reach the correct authority.

The governance framework should define:

- The executive sponsor for AI supplier risk.
- The executive accountable for the relevant business service.
- The authority responsible for approving material residual risk.
- The circumstances requiring board or board-committee visibility.
- The conditions under which supplier onboarding, expansion, or renewal must be suspended.

Executive approval must not override non-waivable legal obligations, mandatory security requirements, or regulatory prohibitions. Where a supplier cannot meet a mandatory requirement, the organization must determine whether a lawful alternative exists rather than treating senior approval as an automatic exemption.

### 77. Third-Party AI Governance Committee Charter

A third-party AI governance committee provides coordinated oversight for supplier relationships that exceed predefined risk thresholds or involve cross-functional decisions.

The committee should have a documented charter covering its mandate, membership, decision rights, meeting cadence, quorum, escalation rules, and recordkeeping requirements.

**Recommended committee responsibilities**

1. Review high-risk AI supplier assessments and material unresolved findings.
2. Challenge the adequacy of proposed mitigations and compensating controls.
3. Resolve conflicts between business objectives and risk requirements.
4. Review supplier concentration and critical dependency exposure.
5. Approve decisions only where the committee has explicit delegated authority.
6. Escalate decisions that exceed its authority to the designated executive or risk committee.
7. Review significant AI supplier incidents, systemic weaknesses, and recurring assessment failures.
8. Monitor remediation commitments and overdue actions.

A committee should not become a substitute for accountable decision-makers. Its charter must distinguish recommendations, approvals, risk acceptance, and escalation.

A decision record should identify the supplier, affected use case, decision authority, evidence reviewed, unresolved findings, conditions imposed, decision date, expiry or review date, and accountable owner.

### 78. Vendor Decision Rights and Approval Authority

Supplier governance requires an explicit decision-rights model. A technically complete assessment does not establish who is authorized to approve the resulting risk.

Decision rights should be based on inherent risk, residual risk, service criticality, data sensitivity, autonomy, regulatory obligations, and the consequences of supplier failure.

| Decision                                          | Typical accountable authority                                               | Required consultation                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Approve a low-risk supplier within policy         | Delegated business or procurement authority                                 | Relevant control owners                                      |
| Approve a high-risk AI supplier                   | Designated senior risk authority                                            | Cybersecurity, AI governance, privacy, legal, business owner |
| Accept material residual cyber risk               | Authorized risk owner within delegated limits                               | Cybersecurity risk function                                  |
| Accept privacy-related risk                       | Authority legally and organizationally empowered to make the decision       | Privacy and legal functions                                  |
| Approve a prohibited or legally non-compliant use | No approval permitted where the prohibition is absolute                     | Legal and compliance confirmation                            |
| Approve a material contract deviation             | Authorized commercial or legal authority, with risk approval where required | Procurement, legal, security, business owner                 |
| Suspend a critical supplier                       | Predefined operational or executive authority                               | Incident management, service owner, legal as appropriate     |

The organization should maintain an approval matrix that identifies monetary thresholds, risk thresholds, required control approvals, escalation triggers, and delegation limits.

The matrix must also distinguish approval to purchase a supplier's service from approval to use that service for a particular AI application. These are related but separate decisions.

### 79. Business Owner Accountability

The business owner is accountable for the purpose, expected benefits, operational consequences, and business risks associated with the supplier relationship.

The business owner must understand what the AI service will do, which processes depend on it, what happens if it fails, and what consequences could arise from inaccurate, manipulated, unavailable, or unauthorized outputs.

Required responsibilities include:

- Defining the intended use and approved operating boundaries.
- Confirming that the use case is justified and proportionate.
- Identifying affected business processes and stakeholders.
- Providing accurate information about data, users, integrations, and dependencies.
- Ensuring that required human review and fallback procedures exist.
- Funding remediation and operational safeguards where appropriate.
- Monitoring service performance and changes in business dependency.
- Initiating reassessment when the use case materially changes.
- Ensuring that supplier termination or service disruption can be managed.

The business owner must not delegate all accountability to procurement or cybersecurity. Those functions can provide specialized advice and controls, but they cannot independently determine whether the business purpose remains justified or whether operational dependence is acceptable.

### 80. Independent Supplier Security Review

An independent supplier security review provides challenge beyond the business team's assessment and the supplier's self-attestation.

The depth of review should be proportionate to supplier criticality and the potential consequences of failure. For high-risk providers, the organization may require technical evidence, independent assurance reports, demonstrations, control testing, architecture review, penetration-test summaries, or targeted verification of critical claims.

An independent reviewer should evaluate:

1. Whether the assessment scope matches the actual service and deployment.
2. Whether evidence is current, authentic, and relevant to the service.
3. Whether assurance reports cover the required systems, locations, and control objectives.
4. Whether material exclusions or exceptions undermine the conclusion.
5. Whether AI-specific risks have been addressed rather than inferred from generic cloud security.
6. Whether stated controls operate effectively for the intended use case.
7. Whether the proposed residual risk rating is consistent with the evidence.
8. Whether the recommendation is supported by documented reasoning.

The reviewer should disclose conflicts of interest and should not be the sole approver of their own assessment where meaningful segregation is required.

If independent evidence is unavailable, the assessment must record the limitation and its effect on confidence. Lack of evidence is not proof that a control is absent, but it is also not proof that the control is effective.

### 81. AI Supplier Risk Appetite

Supplier risk appetite defines the types and levels of third-party AI risk the organization is willing to retain while pursuing its objectives.

Risk appetite must be translated into operational criteria. Statements such as "low tolerance for AI risk" are insufficient unless they identify measurable or decision-relevant boundaries.

A supplier risk appetite statement may address:

- Unauthorized disclosure of confidential or regulated data.
- Supplier use of customer data for model training or service improvement.
- Uncontrolled model or agent changes.
- Loss of auditability or insufficient access to relevant evidence.
- Dependence on a single AI provider for a critical business function.
- Inadequate incident notification or cooperation.
- Inability to export data, configurations, records, or required artifacts.
- Supplier practices that conflict with legal or contractual requirements.
- Unacceptable impact on individuals, customers, employees, or other stakeholders.

Risk appetite should distinguish risks that may be accepted within delegated authority from risks that require mandatory remediation or make a use case impermissible.

The risk appetite must also be reviewed when the organization's threat environment, regulatory obligations, business model, or dependence on AI suppliers materially changes.

### 82. Vendor Risk Thresholds and Escalation

Risk thresholds determine when a supplier assessment requires additional scrutiny, higher-level approval, enhanced monitoring, or suspension of onboarding.

Thresholds should combine quantitative indicators with qualitative triggers. A numerical score alone may conceal a critical deficiency.

| Trigger                                                 | Required governance response                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Supplier handles highly sensitive or regulated data     | Enhanced privacy, security, and contractual review                                   |
| AI service supports a critical business process         | Resilience, continuity, exit, and concentration assessment                           |
| Supplier performs autonomous actions                    | Review of delegated authority, action boundaries, and human intervention             |
| Material security control lacks evidence                | Escalate for evidence, independent validation, or risk treatment                     |
| Supplier refuses required audit or incident cooperation | Legal and risk review; restrict or reject where mandatory requirements cannot be met |
| Critical vulnerability remains unresolved               | Document exposure, compensating controls, deadlines, and escalation                  |
| Supplier introduces material model or service changes   | Reassessment before continued use where the change materially alters risk            |
| Multiple critical services depend on the same provider  | Portfolio-level concentration review                                                 |
| Supplier incident affects the organization's service    | Incident response, impact assessment, and reassessment                               |

Each threshold must specify an owner, the required response, a target resolution period, and the authority responsible for closure.

The organization should periodically test whether thresholds produce consistent decisions. If similar suppliers receive materially different outcomes without a documented rationale, the methodology may require recalibration.

### 83. Residual Vendor Risk Acceptance

Residual vendor risk is the risk that remains after applicable controls, mitigations, and contractual safeguards have been considered.

Risk acceptance must be explicit, evidence-based, time-bound where appropriate, and assigned to an authorized risk owner. It must not be inferred from silence, procurement completion, contract signature, or the absence of objections.

A complete risk acceptance record should contain:

- Supplier and affected AI service.
- Intended use case and business dependency.
- Inherent risk rating and residual risk rating.
- Specific findings and the evidence supporting each finding.
- Potential consequences and affected stakeholders.
- Existing controls and proposed compensating controls.
- Reasons remediation is not completed before use.
- Named risk owner and approving authority.
- Conditions, monitoring requirements, and review date.
- Expiry date or a defined event that triggers renewed approval.
- Remediation owner, milestones, and closure evidence.

Risk acceptance does not transfer the supplier's contractual responsibilities to the customer, nor does it remove the organization's own legal obligations.

A risk owner may accept eligible business risk only within the authority assigned by the governance framework. Mandatory legal requirements, prohibited uses, and non-waivable obligations cannot be legitimized through an ordinary risk acceptance process.

### 84. Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties reduces the risk that commercial pressure, conflicts of interest, or incomplete scrutiny result in inappropriate supplier approval.

The organization should separate incompatible responsibilities where practicable, especially for high-risk AI suppliers.

Examples include:

- The business sponsor should not be the sole independent validator of the supplier's security controls.
- The person negotiating commercial terms should not unilaterally approve material security exceptions.
- A reviewer should not provide independent assurance over work for which they are directly accountable without appropriate safeguards.
- The individual recording a risk acceptance should not be able to alter the approval evidence without traceability.
- Procurement should not override a mandatory security or compliance rejection.
- The person closing a critical remediation action should provide evidence that can be independently validated.

In smaller organizations, full separation may be impractical. Compensating measures can include second-person approval, documented executive review, external assurance, restricted system permissions, and periodic retrospective testing.

The control objective is not organizational complexity for its own sake. It is to ensure that consequential decisions receive appropriate challenge and remain traceable.

### 85. Escalation of Critical Supplier Findings

Critical supplier findings require a predefined escalation path because they may prevent safe onboarding or create unacceptable exposure in an existing service.

Examples include confirmed exposure of sensitive customer data, absence of essential tenant isolation, uncontrolled privileged access, exploitable vulnerabilities in critical components, inability to manage material AI incidents, or an agent capable of executing unauthorized high-impact actions.

The escalation procedure should define:

1. **Detection:** Record the finding and identify the affected supplier, service, data, and use case.
2. **Containment:** Determine whether access, deployment, data transfer, or specific capabilities must be restricted immediately.
3. **Notification:** Inform the accountable business owner and designated risk, security, privacy, legal, and executive stakeholders as appropriate.
4. **Risk determination:** Assess the actual exposure, affected assets, exploitability, impact, and available mitigations.
5. **Decision:** Approve remediation conditions, impose restrictions, suspend onboarding, or initiate service suspension or termination.
6. **Tracking:** Assign a remediation owner, due date, interim safeguards, and evidence requirements.
7. **Verification:** Confirm that the corrective action addresses the finding and does not introduce unacceptable secondary risk.
8. **Closure:** Record the closure decision and retain supporting evidence.

Escalation timelines should reflect severity and applicable contractual or legal deadlines. The organization should not wait for a routine committee meeting to respond to an active security incident or an imminent material exposure.

### 86. Remediation Prioritization and Procurement Leverage

Supplier remediation must be prioritized according to actual risk, not simply the order in which findings were reported.

The prioritization model should consider severity, exploitability, data sensitivity, exposure, business criticality, regulatory significance, available compensating controls, and the likelihood that the deficiency could be exploited or cause harm.

Procurement can provide commercial leverage through contractual commitments, renewal conditions, service restrictions, payment milestones where appropriate, and eligibility for future business.

However, commercial leverage must be used within the contract and applicable law. The organization should not assume that it can impose unilateral requirements that were never agreed upon.

Recommended remediation categories include:

| Priority | Typical treatment                                                                                       |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Immediate escalation; contain exposure; determine whether onboarding or continued use must be suspended |
| High     | Formal corrective action plan, senior visibility, defined deadline, and interim controls                |
| Moderate | Scheduled remediation with accountable ownership and periodic progress review                           |
| Low      | Planned improvement, documented rationale, and monitoring appropriate to the risk                       |

The organization should distinguish remediation of an existing control deficiency from enhancement of a control that already meets the required baseline.

If the supplier refuses to remediate a material deficiency, the organization must reassess its options: restrict use, introduce independently verified compensating controls, renegotiate contractual terms, accept eligible residual risk through the proper authority, or exit the relationship.

### 87. Remediation Dependencies and Shared Suppliers

Supplier remediation may depend on upstream cloud providers, model providers, subprocessors, shared software components, or the organization's own technical configuration.

The assessment should distinguish responsibility for correcting the underlying deficiency from responsibility for managing the resulting exposure.

For example, a foundation-model provider may control model-level safeguards, a cloud provider may control infrastructure security, and the customer may control identity configuration, data access, application integration, and agent permissions.

The vendor assessment must document:

- The affected control and its dependency.
- The party responsible for remediation.
- The party responsible for risk mitigation.
- The evidence required to verify resolution.
- The interim exposure while remediation is pending.
- The contractual or operational mechanism for obtaining the correction.
- The escalation route if the responsible upstream provider does not act.

An upstream dependency is not automatically a valid justification for accepting a material risk. The organization must determine whether it can reduce exposure through configuration changes, architectural isolation, alternative providers, reduced functionality, or service replacement.

Where multiple suppliers share a dependency, the organization should assess whether one upstream failure could affect several critical services simultaneously.

### 88. Shared Responsibility and Inherited Controls

AI services frequently operate through layered service arrangements. The vendor may rely on cloud infrastructure, external model APIs, managed databases, retrieval systems, identity services, monitoring platforms, and other third parties.

A shared-responsibility model identifies which party implements, operates, validates, and provides evidence for each relevant control.

| Control area                         | Potential provider responsibility                                                      | Potential customer responsibility                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Physical and infrastructure security | Secure facilities and underlying infrastructure                                        | Validate provider assurance and select appropriate service configuration                            |
| Identity and access                  | Provide identity mechanisms and administrative safeguards                              | Configure roles, federation, least privilege, and access reviews                                    |
| Data protection                      | Provide encryption capabilities and contractual protections                            | Classify data, configure controls, govern access, and validate permitted processing                 |
| Model and service security           | Protect provider-managed components and disclose relevant limitations                  | Secure integrations, constrain use, validate outputs, and monitor application behavior              |
| AI agent permissions                 | Provide supported policy mechanisms where available                                    | Define tool permissions, action limits, approval gates, and execution controls                      |
| Incident response                    | Investigate provider-controlled incidents and meet applicable notification commitments | Detect customer-side incidents, assess impact, and meet the organization's own response obligations |
| Logging and auditability             | Expose relevant service logs and assurance evidence                                    | Enable available logging, retain necessary records, and monitor usage                               |
| Continuity and recovery              | Operate provider recovery capabilities within the agreed service                       | Define business recovery objectives, test dependencies, and maintain viable workarounds             |

The table is illustrative. Actual responsibilities must be established from the specific service, architecture, contract, and deployment configuration.

Inherited controls should not be assumed effective merely because a supplier claims compliance with a standard. The organization must verify the scope of the assurance, applicable service boundaries, exclusions, complementary customer controls, and any relevant exceptions.

### 89. Subcontractor Accountability

AI suppliers may rely on subprocessors that access customer data, operate infrastructure, provide models, or perform essential service functions. These dependencies can introduce risks that are not visible in a direct supplier questionnaire.

The organization should require an appropriate level of transparency regarding relevant subprocessors, including their roles, processing locations, access to data, security responsibilities, and material changes to the supplier chain.

Governance requirements should address:

- Approval or notification mechanisms for material subprocessor changes.
- Flow-down of relevant security, confidentiality, privacy, and incident obligations.
- Responsibility for monitoring subprocessor performance.
- Evidence of upstream control assurance.
- Restrictions on unauthorized access or secondary data use.
- Investigation and notification responsibilities when an upstream incident occurs.
- Replacement or exit options when a subprocessor creates unacceptable exposure.

The direct supplier should remain accountable for obligations assigned to it by the organization's contract, even where delivery depends on another provider. The organization should verify the actual contractual allocation rather than assume that every upstream obligation automatically flows down.

Subprocessor changes should trigger reassessment when they materially alter data access, processing location, service criticality, security controls, or regulatory exposure.

### 90. Vendor and Model Change Governance

AI supplier risk is dynamic. A supplier may change its model, inference endpoint, training practices, safety controls, hosting environment, data retention, subprocessors, or administrative access without changing the commercial product name.

The organization should establish change-notification requirements and define which changes require reassessment before continued use.

Material changes may include:

- Replacing a model or changing model behavior.
- Introducing new data sources or retrieval capabilities.
- Enabling new tool calls or agent autonomy.
- Changing data retention or model-training practices.
- Changing hosting locations or processing jurisdictions.
- Introducing new subprocessors.
- Altering identity, logging, encryption, or tenant-isolation controls.
- Changing the service's incident response or support model.
- Changing contractual terms that affect security or auditability.

The supplier's change-management process should be assessed for transparency, testing, approval, rollback, and customer notification.

The organization should maintain its own change record connecting supplier changes to affected use cases, controls, risk assessments, approvals, and testing outcomes.

Where the supplier cannot provide adequate notice, the organization should consider additional safeguards, such as staged rollout, application-level testing, restricted deployment, independent output monitoring, or contractual remedies where available.

### 91. Agent, Platform Capability, and Delegated Authority Governance

AI platforms increasingly offer capabilities that can materially change the risk profile of a supplier relationship. These include persistent memory, retrieval, connectors, external tool use, code execution, workflow automation, browser access, and autonomous task execution.

The organization should assess not only the capabilities that are currently enabled but also the capabilities the supplier or tenant administrator can activate.

Governance should establish:

- An approved list of permitted capabilities.
- The authority required to enable high-risk functionality.
- The conditions under which new tools or connectors may be introduced.
- The maximum permitted level of autonomy for each use case.
- The approval requirements for consequential or irreversible actions.
- Restrictions on access to sensitive systems and data.
- Monitoring and logging requirements for tool execution.
- Emergency disablement and revocation procedures.
- Reassessment triggers when capability boundaries change.

For agentic systems, the supplier's advertised safety features must not replace application-level authorization. An AI agent should receive only the permissions required for its approved purpose, and consequential actions should be governed by deterministic policy enforcement wherever feasible.

A vendor approval should explicitly identify whether it covers a general platform, a specific model, an enabled capability, a particular deployment, or a defined business use case.

### 92. Human Oversight and AI Provider Responsibility

Human oversight is a governance mechanism that enables qualified people to supervise, intervene in, constrain, or stop an AI-enabled process where required.

The vendor assessment should establish what oversight capabilities the supplier provides and what responsibilities remain with the customer.

Relevant questions include:

- Can the organization configure approval gates for consequential actions?
- Can human reviewers access the information necessary to evaluate AI recommendations?
- Can users reject, reverse, or override an AI-generated result?
- Are confidence indicators and known limitations communicated appropriately?
- Are logs available to reconstruct the sequence of AI actions?
- Can the system be paused or disabled during an incident?
- Does the supplier provide adequate documentation and support for human supervision?
- Are automation boundaries and failure modes clearly described?

Human oversight must be meaningful rather than ceremonial. A reviewer who lacks sufficient time, expertise, information, or authority may not provide effective control.

The assessment must also distinguish between oversight provided by the vendor and oversight implemented by the customer. A provider may offer configurable controls, but the customer may remain responsible for selecting appropriate settings, training users, defining escalation paths, and ensuring operational compliance.

### 93. Risks to Individuals and Impacted Stakeholders

AI supplier risk extends beyond the organization's assets and financial exposure. A supplier's model or service may affect customers, employees, applicants, patients, citizens, business partners, or other individuals.

The organization should assess foreseeable impacts arising from supplier design, data practices, model behavior, and deployment constraints.

Potential impacts include:

- Inaccurate or misleading decisions.
- Discriminatory or unfair outcomes.
- Exposure of personal or confidential information.
- Unexplained denial of services or opportunities.
- Inappropriate profiling or inference.
- Manipulative or misleading AI-generated communications.
- Inadequate accessibility or support for affected users.
- Lack of effective complaint, correction, or appeal mechanisms.
- Unsafe recommendations or actions in high-impact contexts.

The assessment should identify affected groups, plausible harm scenarios, the severity and likelihood of harm, existing safeguards, and available remedies.

Where relevant, the organization should determine whether the supplier can provide sufficient transparency, documentation, testing evidence, and incident cooperation to support its own accountability.

A supplier's general claim that its model is safe or fair is not a substitute for assessing the model's behavior in the intended context, with the organization's data, users, and decision processes.

### 94. Privacy and Data Protection Governance

Privacy and data protection governance must establish whether the supplier's collection, access, use, storage, disclosure, and deletion of data are consistent with applicable legal obligations and the organization's approved purposes.

The assessment should identify the relevant data roles and responsibilities, including whether the supplier acts as a processor, controller, subprocessor, or in another legally relevant capacity. These roles depend on the actual processing arrangements and applicable law.

The review should cover:

- Purpose limitation and permitted processing.
- Data minimization and retention.
- Lawful basis and other applicable legal requirements.
- Special-category or otherwise sensitive data.
- Data-subject rights and support obligations.
- International transfers and remote access.
- Subprocessor disclosure and change management.
- Data protection impact assessment requirements, where applicable.
- Deletion, return, and retention of data after termination.
- Security incidents and personal data breach cooperation.
- Model training, fine-tuning, and service-improvement use.
- Risks of memorization, leakage, inference, and unintended disclosure.

The assessment must not treat a supplier's promise to delete input data as proof that all derived data, logs, backups, embeddings, model artifacts, and downstream copies are also deleted. The actual scope, exceptions, timing, and evidence must be established.

Where legal requirements or the nature of processing require a formal privacy assessment, the vendor assessment should link to that assessment rather than attempting to replace it.

### 95. Regulatory and Contractual Obligations

The organization must determine which regulatory, statutory, contractual, and sector-specific requirements apply to the supplier relationship and the intended AI use case.

Relevant requirements depend on factors such as jurisdiction, organizational role, type of AI system, data processed, sector, deployment context, and the supplier's contractual commitments.

The assessment should identify:

1. The applicable requirement or obligation.
2. The organization or party responsible for meeting it.
3. The supplier capability or contractual commitment required.
4. The evidence needed to demonstrate compliance.
5. Any gaps, exclusions, or limitations.
6. The owner responsible for resolution.
7. The consequences if the obligation cannot be satisfied.

For example, an organization subject to a particular AI regulatory regime may need supplier documentation, risk-management information, logging support, or technical cooperation to meet its own obligations. Whether a specific duty applies must be established from the actual legal context.

Contracts should allocate responsibilities clearly for security, privacy, intellectual property, audit cooperation, incident notification, service continuity, change management, and termination.

A contractual clause does not prove that the associated control operates effectively. Conversely, a technically effective control may still be inadequate if the organization lacks the contractual rights necessary to verify it, obtain timely assistance, or respond to a supplier failure.

### 96. Portfolio Concentration and Supplier Dependencies

Supplier concentration risk arises when the organization depends excessively on one provider, one model family, one cloud platform, one geographic region, or one interconnected group of upstream services.

Concentration can create correlated failures. Multiple applications that appear independent may fail simultaneously if they rely on the same model API, identity service, cloud region, or upstream data provider.

The organization should maintain a portfolio-level view of:

- Critical business services dependent on each supplier.
- Models and platforms shared across multiple use cases.
- Common upstream infrastructure and subprocessors.
- Geographic and jurisdictional concentration.
- Switching costs and technical portability.
- Contractual termination and transition constraints.
- Availability of alternative providers.
- Recovery time and recovery point requirements, where applicable.
- The operational impact of provider suspension, outage, or policy change.

Potential mitigations include multi-provider strategies, portable interfaces, independent data backups, fallback procedures, reduced coupling, and tested manual operating modes.

Multi-provider deployment does not automatically reduce risk. It may increase complexity, create inconsistent security controls, or introduce additional data-transfer exposure. The organization should assess whether the diversification strategy genuinely reduces correlated failure and whether it can operate the alternatives securely.

Concentration risk should be reviewed across the supplier portfolio, not solely within each individual assessment.

### 97. Threat Intelligence and Vendor Advisory Governance

Supplier-related threat intelligence helps the organization detect emerging risks affecting providers, models, platforms, software dependencies, and deployed integrations.

Sources may include vendor security advisories, vulnerability disclosures, incident notifications, industry information-sharing communities, relevant government advisories, and the organization's own monitoring.

The governance process should define:

- Which sources are monitored for critical suppliers.
- Who evaluates the relevance and credibility of an advisory.
- How affected services and use cases are identified.
- How urgency and exposure are determined.
- Who authorizes temporary restrictions or emergency changes.
- How mitigation and remediation are tracked.
- How affected stakeholders are notified.
- How the event influences future supplier assessments.

Supplier advisories must be mapped to the organization's actual architecture. A vulnerability affecting a model gateway, SDK, plugin, agent framework, or integration library may be relevant even if the main AI service remains available.

The organization should distinguish confirmed exposure from theoretical applicability and avoid treating every public vulnerability as equally urgent.

For critical suppliers, the absence of timely or sufficiently detailed advisories may itself be a governance concern if it prevents the organization from evaluating its exposure.

### 98. Vendor Incidents, Near Misses, and Lessons Learned

Supplier incidents and near misses provide evidence about the effectiveness of both supplier controls and the organization's own governance.

The process should integrate vendor incidents with enterprise incident response, third-party risk management, AI governance, privacy, business continuity, and corrective action tracking.

Relevant events include:

- Unauthorized data access or disclosure.
- Service compromise or material availability loss.
- Model manipulation or compromise of supporting components.
- Prompt injection leading to unauthorized tool execution.
- Cross-tenant data exposure.
- Material degradation or unexpected changes in model behavior.
- Compromise of a supplier's software supply chain.
- Unauthorized data use or retention.
- Failure to notify the organization within agreed timelines.
- A near miss revealing a control weakness before material harm occurs.

For each event, the organization should record the supplier, affected services, timeline, business impact, affected data, detection method, response actions, notification obligations, root cause where known, and corrective measures.

The organization should not assume that the supplier's root-cause analysis is complete or independent. It should evaluate whether the evidence supports the conclusions and whether additional verification is necessary.

Lessons learned should feed into risk ratings, control requirements, contract negotiations, reassessment criteria, and the organization's broader supplier risk methodology.

### 99. Organizational Change and Supplier Owner Changes

Supplier risk can increase when internal responsibilities change, even if the supplier's technology remains unchanged.

Examples include a business owner leaving the organization, a service moving to a different department, a merger, a change in data ownership, a new operating jurisdiction, or the transfer of supplier management to a team without appropriate expertise.

The organization should establish a formal handover process that preserves accountability and assessment continuity.

A supplier ownership transfer should confirm:

- The identity of the new business owner and service owner.
- The current approval and risk acceptance status.
- Open findings and overdue remediation actions.
- Contractual commitments and renewal dates.
- Current data flows and technical dependencies.
- Existing exceptions and their expiry dates.
- Monitoring and incident response responsibilities.
- The next scheduled reassessment.
- Any changes in intended use or business criticality.

The new owner must explicitly acknowledge the responsibilities being transferred. An administrative ownership change should not automatically invalidate an otherwise appropriate approval, but it should trigger a review if accountability, use, exposure, or control effectiveness may have changed.

### 100. Methodology Change Control

The AI vendor assessment methodology must remain consistent enough to support comparable decisions while adapting to changes in threats, technology, business requirements, and regulation.

Methodology changes may affect risk scoring, evidence requirements, supplier classification, mandatory controls, approval thresholds, or reassessment frequency.

Every material methodology change should be governed through a controlled process that includes:

1. A documented reason for the change.
2. An assessment of its impact on existing supplier decisions.
3. Review by the methodology owner and relevant control specialists.
4. Approval by the designated governance authority.
5. Version control and an effective date.
6. Communication and training for affected assessors.
7. Migration rules for assessments already in progress.
8. Determination of whether previously approved suppliers require reassessment.
9. Testing to identify inconsistent or unintended results.
10. Retention of the prior methodology and historical decision records.

Changes to a scoring formula must not silently rewrite historical risk decisions. The organization should preserve the methodology version used for each assessment and explain any subsequent reclassification.

A methodology owner should also periodically review whether assessment questions remain relevant and whether the framework adequately captures emerging risks such as agent autonomy, model supply-chain dependencies, and new data-use practices.

### 101. Suspension, Restriction, and Termination Governance

The organization must define the conditions under which an AI supplier relationship may be restricted, suspended, or terminated.

These actions may be necessary when a supplier creates unacceptable risk, fails to meet contractual obligations, suffers a material incident, makes an unauthorized change, refuses essential assurance, or can no longer support the approved use case.

The governance framework should distinguish:

- **Capability restriction:** Disable a specific high-risk feature, connector, model, or agent action.
- **Use-case suspension:** Stop a particular application while preserving unrelated approved services.
- **Supplier suspension:** Temporarily stop new onboarding, data transfers, or use of the supplier pending investigation.
- **Contractual termination:** End the relationship under the applicable contractual and legal process.
- **Emergency disablement:** Immediately stop a capability or service when continued operation creates imminent unacceptable exposure.

Before a planned termination, the organization should consider operational continuity, data return, deletion, legal holds, record retention, portability, replacement services, and transition assistance.

During an active incident, immediate containment may take priority over a complete exit plan. Emergency actions should nevertheless be recorded and reviewed after the immediate threat is controlled.

Termination governance should also verify that residual access has been revoked, credentials and tokens have been disabled, integrations have been removed, data handling obligations have been completed, and evidence of closure has been retained.

### 102. Board-Level Supplier Risk Reporting

Board or board-committee reporting should focus on material exposure, strategic dependencies, risk appetite, and decisions requiring senior oversight rather than routine assessment activity.

The reporting approach should reflect the organization's governance structure and the significance of its AI supplier portfolio.

Potential reporting indicators include:

- Number and proportion of critical AI suppliers.
- Aggregate inherent and residual supplier risk.
- Material exceptions and overdue critical findings.
- Concentration in critical AI models, platforms, or upstream providers.
- Significant supplier incidents and their business impact.
- Suppliers with insufficient assurance or auditability.
- Exposure to material regulatory or contractual obligations.
- Critical services without tested alternatives.
- Significant changes in supplier risk over time.
- Remediation progress and decisions requiring executive intervention.

Board reporting should distinguish current exposure from historical activity. A large number of completed assessments does not necessarily indicate that supplier risk is well controlled.

Metrics should include context, thresholds, trend information, and the management action required. Where possible, reporting should connect supplier risk to business services, financial impact, operational resilience, and strategic objectives.

The board should receive clear explanations of material uncertainties and evidence limitations rather than relying exclusively on a single aggregate score.

### 103. Management Reporting

Management reporting provides operational visibility into assessment status, remediation progress, supplier changes, and emerging exposure.

Unlike board reporting, management reporting should contain enough detail to support assignment of actions and resolution of specific control weaknesses.

A useful management dashboard may include:

| Indicator                                              | Purpose                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessments awaiting completion                        | Identify onboarding bottlenecks and overdue reviews |
| High-risk suppliers without current approval           | Prevent unauthorized or outdated use                |
| Critical findings by age and owner                     | Prioritize remediation and escalation               |
| Risk acceptances nearing expiry                        | Trigger review before authorization lapses          |
| Suppliers with expired or incomplete assurance         | Identify gaps in evidence currency                  |
| Material changes awaiting reassessment                 | Detect risk introduced by provider changes          |
| Open contractual remediation commitments               | Track obligations that require supplier action      |
| Suppliers with unresolved incident actions             | Connect incident response to ongoing risk           |
| Reassessments overdue                                  | Detect degradation in lifecycle governance          |
| Critical supplier dependencies without tested fallback | Highlight resilience and concentration exposure     |

Metrics should have clear definitions, data sources, owners, reporting frequencies, and thresholds.

The organization should validate dashboard data quality. For example, a supplier marked "approved" may have an expired risk acceptance or may be approved for only one of several use cases. Status fields must preserve these distinctions.

### 104. Independent Assurance and Audit Readiness

Audit readiness requires the organization to demonstrate not only that supplier assessments were completed but that decisions were made by authorized individuals, supported by appropriate evidence, and followed by effective remediation and monitoring.

The supplier governance process should maintain a traceable evidence chain linking requirements, assessment results, findings, decisions, contracts, controls, and subsequent reviews.

A typical evidence set includes:

- Approved assessment methodology and version history.
- Supplier inventory and criticality classification.
- Completed assessment and supporting evidence.
- Independent assurance reports and scope analysis.
- Documented findings and remediation plans.
- Risk acceptance records and delegated approval authority.
- Contractual security and AI governance clauses.
- Change notifications and reassessment records.
- Monitoring reports and supplier incident records.
- Committee minutes and escalation decisions.
- Offboarding records and evidence of access revocation.
- Quality assurance results and corrective actions.

Audit procedures should test a sample of supplier decisions, including approved, rejected, restricted, and risk-accepted cases.

Testing should determine whether the evidence supports the risk rating, whether approval authority was appropriate, whether mandatory requirements were satisfied, and whether corrective actions were closed effectively.

The organization should preserve the distinction between a supplier's independent assurance and its own assurance over the supplier relationship. A vendor's certification may provide useful evidence, but it does not independently prove that the customer selected, configured, and governed the service appropriately.

### 105. Continuous Improvement and Governance Effectiveness

Continuous improvement ensures that the AI vendor assessment process adapts to operational experience, changing supplier capabilities, emerging threats, and evolving requirements.

The organization should evaluate governance effectiveness through measurable outcomes rather than relying only on assessment completion rates.

Useful measures include:

- Percentage of critical suppliers with current and complete assessments.
- Percentage of material findings remediated within agreed timeframes.
- Number of repeat findings across assessment cycles.
- Frequency of supplier incidents involving previously identified control gaps.
- Percentage of high-risk suppliers with tested continuity and exit arrangements.
- Number of expired or improperly extended risk acceptances.
- Quality and consistency of risk decisions across comparable suppliers.
- Time required to identify and assess material supplier changes.
- Percentage of high-risk assessment records passing independent quality review.
- Effectiveness of remediation and compensating controls after implementation.

Continuous improvement should include root-cause analysis of recurring deficiencies. If many suppliers cannot provide model provenance information, for example, the organization may need to revise procurement requirements, contract templates, technical architecture, or supplier selection criteria.

The governance team should periodically review the methodology, assessment templates, risk thresholds, evidence standards, training, workflow automation, and committee effectiveness.

Improvements should be version-controlled and their impact on existing suppliers evaluated. A new requirement may justify reassessment of selected suppliers without necessarily requiring every supplier to restart the entire assessment process.

**Part 3 Core Principle — AI Vendor Governance Must Convert Assessment Evidence into Accountable Decisions and Continuous Oversight**

An AI vendor assessment is effective only when its findings influence supplier selection, approval authority, contractual protections, operational controls, remediation, monitoring, and exit decisions.

The organization must preserve clear accountability across business ownership, control oversight, executive decision-making, and independent assurance. It must also distinguish supplier-level risk from use-case-specific risk, inherited controls from customer responsibilities, and eligible residual risk from obligations that cannot be waived.

Governance is not a one-time procurement event. It is a lifecycle capability that continuously connects supplier evidence to organizational decisions and measurable risk reduction.

## Part 4 — Technical Architecture, Implementation, Operational Assurance, and Assessment Deliverables

### 106. AI Vendor Assessment Reference Architecture

The AI vendor assessment reference architecture defines the technical boundaries that must be evaluated when an organization acquires or integrates an external AI service.

The assessment must consider the complete service chain rather than treating the vendor's model endpoint as the entire system. An enterprise AI solution may depend on identity providers, cloud infrastructure, model APIs, retrieval systems, vector databases, application gateways, monitoring services, agent frameworks, and external tools.

A vendor's security controls must therefore be evaluated in relation to the actual architecture, deployment model, data flows, trust boundaries, and operational responsibilities.

```
Enterprise Users and Business Processes
                 |
                 v
       Enterprise Identity Layer
                 |
                 v
      AI Application / Orchestrator
                 |
          +------+------+
          |             |
          v             v
   Policy Gateway   Retrieval Layer
          |             |
          |        Enterprise Data
          |             |
          +------+------+
                 |
                 v
        External AI Vendor API
                 |
                 v
       Vendor Model / AI Platform
                 |
       +---------+---------+
       |         |         |
       v         v         v
  Vendor Cloud  Logging   Subprocessors
  Infrastructure and      / Upstream
                 Audit     Providers
```

This diagram is illustrative. Actual deployments may place retrieval, orchestration, identity, logging, or model hosting inside the enterprise environment, the vendor environment, or a hybrid architecture.

The assessment should identify:

- The system components operated by the supplier.
- The components operated by the organization.
- The interfaces between the two environments.
- The data and control flows crossing trust boundaries.
- The upstream services on which the vendor depends.
- The security responsibilities associated with each component.
- The controls available to constrain or disable the service.

The resulting architecture record should be linked to the vendor inventory, use-case assessment, security assessment, data-flow documentation, and approval record.

### 107. Vendor Trust Boundaries and Data-Flow Analysis

A trust boundary separates components, identities, networks, or organizations with different security assumptions and control responsibilities.

AI vendor assessments should identify every material boundary crossed by prompts, files, retrieved documents, model outputs, tool requests, telemetry, and administrative operations.

The assessment should document the direction of each flow, the data classification, the purpose of transfer, the recipient, the applicable safeguards, and the retention or deletion requirements.

| Data or control flow                      | Principal assessment concern                             | Evidence to request                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| User prompt to vendor                     | Sensitive-data exposure and permitted processing         | Data-flow diagram, API documentation, contractual data-use terms        |
| Enterprise documents to retrieval service | Unauthorized access, indexing scope, and data leakage    | Retrieval architecture, access-control design, data-ingestion controls  |
| Retrieved context to model                | Overexposure of confidential information                 | Authorization enforcement, filtering design, retrieval testing          |
| Model output to user                      | Disclosure, incorrect advice, or harmful output          | Output-control design, validation procedures, testing evidence          |
| Model output to external tool             | Unauthorized action or privilege escalation              | Tool authorization model, policy enforcement, action logs               |
| Vendor telemetry to provider              | Excessive logging or secondary data use                  | Telemetry specification, retention configuration, data-processing terms |
| Administrative access to vendor tenant    | Privileged misuse and unauthorized configuration changes | Access-control evidence, privileged-access procedures, audit logs       |
| Vendor-to-subprocessor transfers          | Uncontrolled onward processing                           | Subprocessor register, transfer details, contractual safeguards         |

The assessment should not assume that encryption alone makes a data flow acceptable. Encryption protects data in transit or at rest under defined conditions, but it does not establish that the recipient is authorized to receive the data or that the recipient's processing is permitted.

### 108. Identity Federation and Access-Control Architecture

Identity integration is a critical control area because an AI vendor may receive access to enterprise accounts, documents, applications, APIs, or administrative interfaces.

The assessment should establish whether the supplier supports appropriate identity federation, multifactor authentication, role-based access control, least privilege, lifecycle management, and privileged-access safeguards.

Required evaluation areas include:

1. Authentication protocols and identity-provider integration.
2. Enforcement of multifactor authentication for privileged and sensitive access.
3. Role design and separation of administrative duties.
4. Service-account and workload-identity management.
5. Token issuance, scope restriction, expiration, and revocation.
6. Joiner, mover, and leaver processes.
7. Administrative access logging and review.
8. Emergency access and break-glass controls.
9. API credential storage and rotation.
10. Restrictions on cross-tenant and cross-customer access.

Where the vendor provides a multi-tenant service, the assessment should establish how tenant identity is bound to each request and how the service prevents unauthorized access across tenant boundaries.

The organization should also validate its own integration. A vendor may support strong identity controls, but an incorrectly configured enterprise application can still expose data or grant excessive permissions.

For high-risk deployments, testing should include account revocation, role changes, expired credentials, unauthorized tenant access, and attempted use of credentials outside their intended scope.

### 109. Network Segmentation and API Security

The AI vendor's API and integration layer should be evaluated for authentication, authorization, traffic control, input validation, abuse prevention, and secure connectivity.

The organization should determine whether the service supports the network and API controls required by the intended deployment, including private connectivity where appropriate, approved egress paths, API gateways, rate limits, and restrictions on administrative endpoints.

Assessment criteria should include:

- Strong API authentication and narrowly scoped authorization.
- Protection against credential exposure and replay where relevant.
- Rate limiting and abuse detection.
- Request-size and payload restrictions.
- Input validation and safe handling of untrusted content.
- Secure transport and certificate validation.
- Network restrictions and private endpoints where supported and required.
- API version control and deprecation notification.
- Separation of production, development, and test environments.
- Monitoring of unusual usage and anomalous request patterns.

The assessment should identify whether prompts, retrieved content, uploaded files, or tool parameters can influence downstream requests.

API gateways and network controls can limit exposure, but they do not independently solve semantic AI threats such as prompt injection, unsafe tool selection, or inappropriate model-generated decisions. Those risks require application-level authorization and validation.

### 110. Data Isolation and Multi-Tenant Security

Multi-tenant AI services require safeguards that prevent one customer from accessing another customer's data, configuration, prompts, files, embeddings, logs, or outputs.

The assessment should determine how tenant isolation is enforced across all relevant layers, including application services, databases, caches, vector stores, model-serving infrastructure, telemetry, support tooling, and administrative operations.

Questions should include:

- How is tenant identity established and propagated?
- Are authorization checks performed server-side for every relevant access?
- Are tenant identifiers protected against manipulation?
- Can retrieval operations return records from another tenant?
- Are caches and temporary files isolated appropriately?
- How are backups and logs segregated?
- Can vendor personnel access customer content, and under what conditions?
- Are administrative support operations logged and controlled?
- What testing validates isolation?
- How are cross-tenant incidents detected, contained, and reported?

The assessment should request appropriate independent assurance or technical evidence for high-risk services.

Where the vendor cannot disclose implementation details, it should provide alternative evidence sufficient to support a reasoned assessment, subject to confidentiality and security constraints.

A contractual statement that customer data is isolated should be treated as one input to the assessment, not conclusive proof that the isolation control is effective.

### 111. Model Provenance, Integrity, and Version Management

Model provenance establishes where a model originated, who developed or modified it, and which versions and artifacts are used by the service.

The assessment should establish whether the vendor can identify the model or model family, provide relevant version information, explain significant modifications, and disclose material dependencies within the limits of legitimate intellectual-property and security restrictions.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Model identity and version identifiers.
- Model source or origin documentation where available.
- Relevant training and fine-tuning provenance disclosures.
- Artifact integrity controls.
- Model release and deployment records.
- Version-change notifications.
- Security testing and evaluation summaries.
- Model rollback or replacement capabilities.
- Documentation of known limitations and intended use.
- Procedures for withdrawing compromised or unsafe versions.

For externally hosted models, the organization may not receive model weights or full training data. The assessment should therefore focus on whether the supplier provides sufficient transparency, integrity assurance, version control, and operational safeguards for the actual risk.

Model provenance is distinct from model performance. A model may perform well on benchmark tests while still presenting unresolved supply-chain, integrity, privacy, or governance risks.

### 112. Model and Service Change Detection

A vendor may change an AI service without changing the customer-facing product name. Changes may affect response quality, refusal behavior, tool selection, context handling, data processing, latency, safety controls, or the service's security characteristics.

The assessment should determine how the supplier communicates changes and whether the organization can identify which model or configuration produced a particular result.

The organization should define change-detection requirements proportionate to the service's criticality.

Recommended controls include:

- Recording model and API version identifiers where available.
- Maintaining a baseline of expected behavior for critical workflows.
- Testing representative inputs before material production changes.
- Monitoring changes in refusal rates, error patterns, output quality, and tool execution.
- Reassessing security and safety assumptions after significant changes.
- Maintaining rollback or fallback options where supported.
- Requiring supplier notification for material changes through contractual or operational mechanisms.
- Recording the relationship between service changes and risk decisions.

Not every variation in a probabilistic model response represents a security incident. Detection rules should distinguish normal output variability from meaningful changes in risk, authorization behavior, data handling, or operational performance.

Where the vendor does not provide advance notification or version pinning, the organization should compensate through additional monitoring, application-level validation, restricted permissions, or a more conservative deployment decision.

### 113. Prompt Injection and Untrusted-Content Controls

Prompt injection occurs when instructions embedded in user input, retrieved content, files, or external data attempt to influence the model in ways that conflict with the application's intended behavior or security requirements.

Vendor assessment should establish what safeguards the provider offers and which protections must be implemented in the customer's application.

Evaluation should cover:

1. Recognition and handling of untrusted input.
2. Separation of system instructions from user-controlled and retrieved content.
3. Restrictions on access to secrets and privileged context.
4. Tool authorization independent of model-generated instructions.
5. Validation of outputs before downstream execution.
6. Isolation of external content from privileged workflows.
7. Monitoring of suspicious instruction patterns and behavior.
8. Testing against direct and indirect prompt injection.
9. Safe failure and human intervention mechanisms.
10. Disclosure of limitations in provider-level protections.

The assessment must avoid assuming that a prompt template, content filter, or vendor safety feature can reliably prevent all prompt-injection attacks.

A robust architecture limits the consequences of successful manipulation. The model should not possess unrestricted access to sensitive resources, and external actions should be authorized by deterministic controls that evaluate the requested operation, identity, permissions, and business policy.

### 114. Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Vector-Store Security

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) connects an AI model to external information sources, allowing responses to be grounded in enterprise documents or other knowledge repositories.

RAG can introduce data exposure, authorization, poisoning, and integrity risks across ingestion, indexing, retrieval, context construction, and output generation.

The vendor assessment should determine:

- Which party operates the retrieval and vector-store components.
- How document permissions are preserved during ingestion and retrieval.
- Whether access controls are applied before content reaches the model.
- How tenant and user identities are propagated through retrieval.
- How document deletion and permission changes affect existing indexes.
- Whether ingestion validates file types and content.
- How malicious or misleading documents are handled.
- Whether source references and provenance are preserved.
- How retrieval logs and embeddings are protected.
- How the service detects and handles unauthorized retrieval.

Embedding vectors should not automatically be treated as harmless or anonymous. Their sensitivity depends on the data, the embedding process, the model, and the feasibility of inference or reconstruction.

The assessment should also distinguish the vendor's responsibility for its managed retrieval infrastructure from the customer's responsibility for source permissions, document classification, indexing scope, and access policy.

Testing should include cross-user access attempts, revoked permissions, deleted documents, malicious documents, and retrieval of sensitive content outside the intended authorization boundary.

### 115. AI Agent and Tool-Execution Security

AI agents can invoke APIs, query databases, modify files, send communications, execute code, or perform business transactions. Their risk depends on both the AI platform and the authority granted by the surrounding application.

Vendor assessment must establish whether agent execution is constrained by enforceable policies rather than relying solely on model-generated decisions.

Required evaluation areas include:

- Tool and connector inventory.
- Permission scope for each tool.
- Identity and authorization of tool requests.
- Isolation of execution environments.
- Approval gates for consequential actions.
- Restrictions on destructive or irreversible operations.
- Time, resource, and transaction limits.
- Protection of credentials and secrets.
- Logging of proposed and executed actions.
- Emergency termination and credential revocation.
- Prevention of unauthorized delegation or recursive execution.
- Validation of results returned by external tools.

The organization should verify that the model cannot grant itself additional privileges merely by requesting a tool call or generating a different instruction.

For high-impact actions, authorization should be enforced outside the model. Human approval may be required for transactions, privilege changes, external communications, or actions that materially affect individuals or critical business operations.

Testing should evaluate malicious instructions, compromised retrieved content, invalid tool arguments, excessive action sequences, unauthorized resources, and attempts to bypass approval requirements.

### 116. Software Supply-Chain and Component Security

An AI service may depend on SDKs, model-serving frameworks, orchestration libraries, plugins, containers, packages, infrastructure components, and third-party APIs.

The assessment should establish how the supplier identifies, protects, updates, and monitors these dependencies.

Evidence may include:

- Software bills of materials where available and appropriate.
- Dependency inventory and version-management procedures.
- Secure development lifecycle documentation.
- Code review and build-pipeline controls.
- Artifact signing and integrity verification.
- Vulnerability scanning and remediation processes.
- Secrets management for build and deployment systems.
- Dependency provenance and repository controls.
- Disclosure of material supply-chain incidents.
- Procedures for replacing compromised components.

For AI-specific components, the review should include model-serving dependencies, agent frameworks, plugin ecosystems, retrieval libraries, and packages capable of executing untrusted content.

The organization should assess whether the supplier can identify affected customers or services when a material dependency vulnerability is discovered.

A supplier's use of reputable upstream components does not eliminate the need for dependency management. Security depends on how components are selected, configured, updated, and integrated into the overall service.

### 117. Secure Development and Deployment Practices

The vendor's development and deployment processes influence the likelihood that vulnerabilities or unsafe changes enter production.

The assessment should evaluate whether the supplier applies risk-appropriate secure development practices across application code, AI integrations, model-serving infrastructure, configuration, and deployment pipelines.

Relevant controls include:

- Security requirements and threat modeling.
- Code review and approval controls.
- Automated security testing.
- Dependency and container scanning.
- Infrastructure-as-code validation.
- Separation of development, testing, and production.
- Restricted deployment permissions.
- Protection of build and release credentials.
- Pre-production testing of material changes.
- Controlled rollout and rollback.
- Production change logging.
- Remediation of discovered vulnerabilities.

For AI-enabled services, development testing should also address model behavior, prompt injection, unauthorized tool use, data leakage, and misuse of integrated retrieval or agent capabilities where applicable.

The assessment should determine whether testing is performed on the actual production-relevant configuration and whether material exceptions are documented.

A mature process connects findings to release decisions. Critical unresolved issues should not be treated as acceptable merely because the release deadline has been reached.

### 118. Vulnerability Management and Penetration Testing

The supplier should maintain a vulnerability management process that identifies weaknesses, prioritizes remediation, validates fixes, and communicates material exposure to affected customers.

The assessment should establish the scope and recency of relevant security testing, including whether it covers the service components and interfaces used by the organization.

Questions should address:

- Vulnerability discovery and intake channels.
- Severity classification and remediation targets.
- Penetration-test scope and testing independence.
- Testing of APIs, tenant isolation, identity, and administrative functions.
- Coverage of AI integrations and tool execution where relevant.
- Remediation verification and retesting.
- Handling of vulnerabilities that cannot be immediately fixed.
- Customer notification and mitigation guidance.
- Restrictions on customer testing and coordinated disclosure.
- Availability of evidence for material findings.

A penetration-test report is useful only when its scope, date, limitations, and findings are understood. Testing a public-facing web interface does not necessarily establish that tenant isolation, model-serving infrastructure, agent execution, or administrative control planes have been adequately evaluated.

Where detailed reports cannot be shared, the organization may consider a suitably scoped independent attestation or a controlled review under confidentiality arrangements.

### 119. Logging, Monitoring, and Auditability

Effective logging and monitoring allow the organization to investigate incidents, detect misuse, reconstruct actions, and demonstrate compliance with relevant requirements.

The assessment should establish what the supplier records, how long records are retained, who can access them, how their integrity is protected, and whether the customer can retrieve the evidence needed for its own oversight.

Relevant events may include:

- Authentication and authorization decisions.
- Administrative configuration changes.
- API requests and service errors.
- Model and service version changes.
- Data access and retrieval events.
- Tool calls and agent actions.
- Policy enforcement and blocked operations.
- Security alerts and incident response activities.
- Data export, deletion, and retention operations.
- Access by supplier support personnel.

Logging requirements must be balanced against privacy, confidentiality, data minimization, and the risk of storing sensitive prompts or outputs unnecessarily.

The assessment should determine whether logs contain sufficient information for investigation without collecting more content than is necessary. Where content-level logging is inappropriate, metadata, identifiers, hashes, or other privacy-conscious records may support some investigative objectives.

The organization should confirm that required events can be correlated with its own identity, application, and security monitoring systems.

### 120. AI Security Testing and Independent Validation

AI security testing evaluates whether the vendor's service and the customer's integration behave safely under expected, adverse, and malicious conditions.

The scope should be proportionate to the intended use, data sensitivity, model capabilities, autonomy, and consequences of failure.

Testing may include:

- Direct and indirect prompt-injection scenarios.
- Unauthorized data retrieval.
- Cross-tenant isolation tests.
- Attempts to bypass tool permissions.
- Unsafe output handling and downstream execution.
- Data leakage and secret exposure scenarios.
- Insecure API and identity configurations.
- Model and service change regression tests.
- Abuse, rate-limit, and resource-exhaustion tests.
- Failure handling and service-degradation tests.
- Logging and incident reconstruction exercises.
- Validation of safety controls under realistic operational conditions.

The organization should distinguish supplier-provided test evidence from tests performed independently by the customer or a qualified third party.

Testing must be authorized and conducted within agreed scope. The assessment should not presume that the customer can perform unrestricted penetration testing against a third-party hosted service.

Findings should be classified, assigned, remediated, retested, and linked to the relevant risk record. Where a finding remains unresolved, the organization should document the residual exposure and determine whether continued use is acceptable.

### 121. Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery Validation

AI suppliers can become critical dependencies when business processes rely on continuous access to model APIs, retrieval services, identity integrations, or agent platforms.

The assessment should establish whether the supplier's continuity arrangements align with the organization's operational requirements.

Required information may include:

- Service availability commitments.
- Recovery time and recovery point objectives where applicable.
- Redundancy and resilience arrangements.
- Geographic and infrastructure dependencies.
- Backup and restoration processes.
- Disaster recovery testing and results.
- Incident communication and status reporting.
- Capacity and rate-limit constraints.
- Service degradation behavior.
- Data portability and restoration options.
- Alternative operating procedures.
- Supplier exit and migration support.

The organization should assess its own ability to continue operations if the AI service is unavailable, degraded, compromised, or suspended.

For critical workflows, continuity may require a manual fallback, a conventional software alternative, a secondary provider, or a reduced-function operating mode.

A contractual service-level agreement does not by itself establish operational resilience. The organization should consider whether the service's actual recovery characteristics, exclusions, and dependencies meet business requirements.

### 122. Data Return, Deletion, and Secure Exit Validation

Supplier exit must be treated as a security control, not solely as a procurement or contract-management activity.

When a relationship ends, the organization should verify that access is revoked, integrations are removed, data is returned or deleted as required, and remaining obligations are documented.

The exit assessment should cover:

1. Export of required customer data in usable formats.
2. Return of relevant configurations, records, and audit evidence.
3. Identification of data held by subprocessors.
4. Deletion of active copies and the treatment of backups.
5. Handling of logs, embeddings, caches, and derived artifacts.
6. Treatment of data used for training or fine-tuning.
7. Revocation of credentials, tokens, service accounts, and delegated permissions.
8. Removal of network routes, connectors, and integrations.
9. Transfer of operational knowledge and continuity responsibilities.
10. Retention of records required by law, contract, or legitimate legal hold.
11. Evidence of completion and authorized exceptions.
12. Confirmation of residual supplier access or dependencies.

The organization should distinguish deletion from active systems from deletion or expiry under backup-retention schedules. It should document any lawful retention exceptions and the safeguards that apply while retained data remains present.

The supplier's deletion certificate or written confirmation may provide evidence, but the organization should evaluate its scope and credibility against the contractual requirements and the service architecture.

Exit planning should begin during supplier selection. If data cannot be exported, model configurations cannot be reproduced, or critical workflows cannot be transferred, these constraints should be reflected in the initial risk and dependency assessment.

A centralized evidence repository provides traceability between supplier claims, assessment findings, approval decisions, contracts, and ongoing monitoring.

The repository may be implemented through a governance, risk, and compliance platform, third-party risk management system, document management service, or controlled internal repository.

The evidence structure should support the following logical relationships:

Each evidence item should record its source, date, scope, owner, applicable control, validity period where relevant, and assessment conclusion.

Evidence reuse may improve efficiency, but it should not be automatic. A report for one product, region, tenant configuration, or service version may not support a conclusion about another.

### 124. Assessment Workflow and Approval Gates

The vendor assessment workflow should connect procurement and technical implementation to risk-based approval decisions.

A standard workflow may include:

```

      |
      v

      |

      |
```

The workflow should support different assessment depths for different risk categories. A low-risk supplier may require a streamlined review, while a critical provider may require detailed architecture analysis, independent assurance, and executive approval.

Mandatory approval gates should prevent the supplier from being used for an unapproved purpose or receiving sensitive data before required conditions are satisfied.

Where emergency procurement is permitted, the exception process must define its scope, duration, minimum safeguards, approving authority, and post-implementation review.

Workflow automation should not automatically approve a supplier solely because a questionnaire is complete or a risk score falls below a threshold. Critical findings, contradictory evidence, mandatory control failures, and legal restrictions require appropriate human review.

### 125. Assessment Quality Assurance and Reviewer Calibration

Quality assurance helps ensure that different assessors reach consistent conclusions when reviewing comparable suppliers and use cases.

The quality process should examine both the assessment record and the reasoning behind the decision.

Review criteria should include:

- Completeness of the supplier and use-case scope.
- Accuracy of the inherent risk classification.
- Relevance and currency of evidence.
- Correct interpretation of supplier assurance.
- Appropriate treatment of findings and compensating controls.
- Consistency between findings and residual risk ratings.
- Compliance with approval authority requirements.
- Traceability of risk acceptance and exceptions.
- Adequacy of remediation ownership and deadlines.
- Completeness of the final recommendation.

Reviewer calibration sessions can compare decisions across representative cases and identify inconsistent interpretations of assessment criteria.

The organization should maintain a documented process for resolving disagreements, correcting assessment errors, and updating guidance where the methodology is ambiguous.

Quality assurance should be risk-based. High-risk assessments and material exceptions warrant more rigorous review than routine low-risk assessments.

The quality function should also examine systemic patterns. Repeated evidence gaps, excessive reliance on self-attestation, or frequent overrides of critical findings may indicate that the methodology or governance model requires improvement.

### 126. AI Vendor Assessment Scoring Model

A scoring model can help classify suppliers and compare risk exposure, provided that it is transparent, consistently applied, and supplemented by mandatory decision rules.

The scoring methodology should distinguish:

- **Inherent risk:** Exposure before considering relevant controls.
- **Control effectiveness:** The extent to which applicable controls are supported by evidence and operate as intended.
- **Residual risk:** Remaining exposure after controls and mitigations are considered.
- **Evidence confidence:** The degree of confidence that the assessment conclusion is justified.
- **Decision status:** Whether the supplier is approved, conditionally approved, restricted, rejected, or pending further evidence.

A sample risk matrix is shown below.

| Dimension             | Example assessment question                                                               |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business criticality  | Would supplier failure materially disrupt a critical business process?                    |
| Data sensitivity      | Does the supplier access personal, confidential, regulated, or restricted information?    |
| AI capability         | Does the service generate consequential outputs or perform autonomous actions?            |
| Exposure              | Is the service internet-facing, integrated with sensitive systems, or broadly accessible? |
| Supplier dependency   | Are critical functions concentrated in this provider or its upstream dependencies?        |
| Control effectiveness | Are material safeguards implemented and supported by credible evidence?                   |
| Regulatory exposure   | Does the use case create material legal or compliance obligations?                        |
| Exit difficulty       | Would replacement or migration be difficult, costly, or operationally disruptive?         |

An illustrative five-level scale may be used:

| Score | Meaning            |
| ----- | ------------------ |
| 1     | Very low exposure  |
| 2     | Low exposure       |
| 3     | Moderate exposure  |
| 4     | High exposure      |
| 5     | Very high exposure |

The organization must define scoring anchors for each dimension rather than assigning values based only on reviewer intuition.

A weighted score may help prioritize review, but it should not override mandatory control failures. For example, a low average score must not conceal an unresolved critical issue involving unauthorized access to highly sensitive data.

The model should be periodically validated against actual incidents, audit findings, remediation outcomes, and observed supplier performance.

### 127. Evidence Confidence and Uncertainty Management

Risk scoring is only as reliable as the evidence supporting it. Two suppliers with identical scores may have different risk profiles if one provides independently validated evidence and the other provides unsupported assertions.

The assessment should record uncertainty separately from the underlying risk rating.

Evidence confidence can be evaluated using factors such as:

- Source independence.
- Scope alignment with the service being assessed.
- Evidence recency.
- Completeness and specificity.
- Technical verifiability.
- Consistency with other available evidence.
- Presence of exclusions or unresolved exceptions.
- Ability to validate operating effectiveness.

A practical confidence classification may use:

| Confidence level | Interpretation                                                                              |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| High             | Relevant, current, sufficiently complete evidence supports the conclusion                   |
| Moderate         | Evidence is useful but contains defined limitations or requires supplementary validation    |
| Low              | Important claims remain unverified, evidence is incomplete, or scope alignment is uncertain |

Low evidence confidence should trigger additional scrutiny, not an automatic assumption that the supplier's controls are absent. However, where evidence is necessary to establish compliance with a mandatory requirement, the absence of sufficient evidence may prevent approval.

The assessment should identify which uncertainties can be resolved through additional evidence, independent testing, contractual commitments, or technical safeguards, and which uncertainties remain unacceptable.

### 128. Conditional Approval and Time-Bound Authorization

Conditional approval allows an organization to proceed with a supplier relationship when specified conditions can be met without violating mandatory requirements or creating unacceptable exposure.

Conditional approval must be more precise than a general statement that the supplier is acceptable subject to remediation.

The authorization should identify:

- The approved supplier, service, and use case.
- The permitted data categories and user groups.
- The enabled features and prohibited capabilities.
- The conditions that must be satisfied before production use.
- The conditions that may be completed after onboarding, where permitted.
- The owner responsible for each condition.
- The target completion date.
- Interim controls and monitoring requirements.
- The authorized decision-maker.
- The expiry date and reassessment trigger.
- The consequences of non-compliance.

Conditions that are prerequisites for safe or lawful operation must be completed before the relevant activity begins. They should not be reclassified as post-approval tasks merely to meet a procurement deadline.

Conditional approval should expire or require renewed authorization when the approved period ends, the use case changes materially, the supplier introduces a significant new capability, or a critical finding invalidates the original decision.

### 129. Contractual Control Requirements and Technical Enforceability

Contractual security obligations are most effective when they align with controls that can be implemented, monitored, and verified.

The vendor assessment should map important contractual requirements to operational mechanisms and supporting evidence.

| Contractual requirement                           | Technical or operational mechanism                                          | Verification evidence                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Restriction on model training using customer data | Enforced provider configuration and documented processing controls          | Contract terms, service settings, supplier confirmation, assurance evidence |
| Access limitation                                 | Role-based access, tenant isolation, and privileged-access controls         | Configuration evidence, access reviews, testing                             |
| Incident notification                             | Defined notification channels, escalation contacts, and incident procedures | Contract clause, incident-response documentation, exercise results          |
| Change notification                               | Supplier release process and customer communication mechanism               | Change records, notification samples, contract terms                        |
| Audit cooperation                                 | Defined assurance rights and evidence access                                | Contractual provisions, assurance reports, audit procedure                  |
| Data deletion at termination                      | Deletion process and handling of backups or derived data                    | Exit plan, deletion confirmation, relevant technical evidence               |
| Subprocessor governance                           | Subprocessor inventory and change-notification process                      | Contract terms, subprocessor register, change records                       |
| Vulnerability remediation                         | Defined remediation commitments and escalation mechanisms                   | Vulnerability policy, remediation evidence, service commitments             |

The assessment should distinguish contractual enforceability from technical effectiveness. A contractual obligation creates a commitment, but it may not prevent an unsafe operation in real time.

Similarly, a technical control may be effective but insufficient if the organization lacks the rights or evidence needed to verify that the supplier continues to operate it appropriately.

Where contract terms conflict with technical realities, the organization should resolve the discrepancy before approving the affected use case or document a lawful, authorized, and risk-proportionate alternative.

### 130. Supplier Monitoring and Reassessment Triggers

Continuous monitoring helps identify changes that invalidate an earlier assessment or increase residual risk.

The monitoring model should combine scheduled reviews with event-driven reassessment.

Potential triggers include:

- A significant security incident or near miss.
- Material changes to the model, platform, or service architecture.
- New data categories or processing purposes.
- Changes to hosting locations or subprocessors.
- Introduction of agentic or tool-execution capabilities.
- Expiry or withdrawal of relevant assurance evidence.
- Material changes in supplier ownership or financial viability.
- Significant deterioration in service reliability.
- New vulnerabilities or threat intelligence affecting the supplier.
- Changes in applicable legal or contractual obligations.
- Repeated failure to meet remediation commitments.
- Changes in the organization's business dependency or risk appetite.

Reassessment should be scoped to the change where possible. A new subprocessor may require focused review of data processing and contractual safeguards, while a major architectural redesign may require a broader reassessment.

The organization should maintain a record of the trigger, affected controls, assessment performed, revised risk conclusion, and resulting decision.

A reassessment process is ineffective if it updates a risk rating without evaluating whether existing approvals, contracts, and operational controls remain valid.

### 131. AI Vendor Exit Strategy and Portability Assessment

Portability determines whether the organization can transfer its data, configurations, workflows, and required operational records to an alternative service without unacceptable loss or disruption.

The assessment should examine technical, contractual, operational, and commercial exit constraints.

Relevant questions include:

- Can customer data be exported in documented, usable formats?
- Can prompts, configurations, policies, and workflow definitions be transferred?
- Are embeddings, indexes, and derived artifacts portable or reproducible?
- Are model-specific dependencies documented?
- Can applications switch to another provider without major redesign?
- Are rate limits or proprietary interfaces likely to obstruct migration?
- Can audit records and evidence be retained after termination?
- Are transition assistance and deletion commitments contractually defined?
- Is a viable replacement provider or fallback process available?
- Has the organization tested its exit assumptions for critical services?

Portability is not simply the ability to download raw data. The organization must consider whether the exported information can be reconstructed into a working, secure, and supportable service.

For model-dependent applications, switching providers may change output quality, safety behavior, latency, cost, and evaluation results. A migration should therefore include functional regression testing, security testing, and reassessment of the new provider.

Exit plans for critical suppliers should identify dependencies, accountable owners, estimated transition activities, acceptance criteria, and conditions for activating the alternative.

### 132. Assessment Exceptions and Compensating Controls

An assessment exception records a defined deviation from an established control requirement or process. It should not be used to disguise a mandatory requirement failure or bypass an approval gate.

The exception process should identify:

1. The requirement that cannot be met.
2. The reason for the deviation.
3. The affected supplier, service, and use case.
4. The resulting threat scenarios and potential impact.
5. The compensating controls proposed.
6. Evidence that those controls are implemented and effective.
7. The authorized approver.
8. The exception start date and expiry date.
9. The remediation owner and target date, where remediation is required.
10. The monitoring and escalation conditions.
11. The event that triggers early revocation.
12. The final closure evidence.

Compensating controls should reduce the specific risk created by the deviation. For example, if a supplier cannot provide a desired network restriction, application-level egress controls or a restricted integration architecture may reduce exposure, provided they are appropriate and independently validated.

A compensating control should not be accepted merely because it exists. Its effectiveness, limitations, and interaction with other controls must be evaluated.

Exceptions should be reviewed periodically, and repeated extensions should trigger escalation. A permanent exception that is repeatedly renewed without reassessing the underlying architecture or supplier choice may indicate a structural governance failure.

### 134. End-to-End AI Vendor Assessment Implementation Procedure

The following procedure integrates the governance and technical controls established throughout this lesson.

**Step 1 — Register the supplier and service.**

Create or update the supplier inventory record. Identify the legal entity, product, service model, upstream dependencies, business sponsor, service owner, and proposed use case.

**Step 2 — Classify the use case and inherent risk.**

Determine the intended purpose, data sensitivity, business criticality, level of autonomy, affected stakeholders, regulatory context, and potential consequences of failure.

**Step 3 — Define the assessment scope.**

Identify applicable security, AI governance, privacy, resilience, contractual, and regulatory requirements. Determine the required assessment depth and evidence standards.

**Step 4 — Collect and validate evidence.**

Request the relevant questionnaire, technical documentation, assurance reports, security testing evidence, data-processing terms, subprocessor information, and contractual commitments. Validate scope, currency, and credibility.

**Step 5 — Evaluate architecture and technical controls.**

Review trust boundaries, identity, API security, data isolation, model provenance, prompt-injection controls, retrieval security, agent permissions, logging, vulnerability management, and operational resilience as applicable.

**Step 6 — Record findings and determine control effectiveness.**

Document control deficiencies, evidence gaps, affected assets, potential consequences, and available mitigations. Distinguish confirmed weaknesses from unresolved uncertainty.

**Step 7 — Determine residual risk.**

Evaluate the effectiveness of existing controls and proposed mitigations. Apply mandatory decision rules and determine whether residual risk is within the organization's risk appetite.

**Step 8 — Obtain the appropriate decision.**

Route the assessment to the authorized approver. Escalate critical findings, material exceptions, and unresolved legal or contractual concerns. Record the decision and its rationale.

**Step 9 — Enforce onboarding conditions.**

Ensure that mandatory conditions are completed before the affected service or capability is used. Apply approved configuration, identity, data-access, logging, and monitoring controls.

**Step 10 — Establish continuous oversight.**

Set reassessment dates, monitoring requirements, change-notification triggers, remediation deadlines, and incident escalation procedures.

**Step 11 — Review renewal or material changes.**

Reassess the supplier when risk conditions change and before material renewals or expansions where required by policy.

**Step 12 — Execute controlled exit when necessary.**

Revoke access, remove integrations, return or delete data as required, preserve necessary records, validate closure, and confirm that critical business processes remain supported.

The procedure should be implemented through a controlled workflow with accountable owners, defined evidence requirements, approval gates, and audit trails.

### 136. AI Vendor Assessment Metrics and Key Risk Indicators

Metrics and key risk indicators (KRIs) provide visibility into the condition of the supplier portfolio and the effectiveness of assessment controls.

The organization should choose indicators that support decisions rather than simply measure activity.

Examples include:

| Metric or KRI                                                                     | Example interpretation                                    |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Percentage of critical AI suppliers with current assessments                      | Measures assessment coverage for high-impact dependencies |
| Percentage of critical suppliers with unresolved high-risk findings               | Indicates material residual exposure                      |
| Number of critical findings past remediation deadlines                            | Signals delayed corrective action                         |
| Percentage of supplier approvals with current risk acceptance                     | Identifies authorization gaps                             |
| Number of material supplier changes not assessed within the defined period        | Measures change-governance effectiveness                  |
| Percentage of critical suppliers with tested exit or fallback arrangements        | Indicates resilience readiness                            |
| Number of material supplier incidents linked to known deficiencies                | Supports root-cause and control-effectiveness analysis    |
| Percentage of assessments failing quality review                                  | Indicates methodology or execution weaknesses             |
| Number of supplier concentration thresholds exceeded                              | Highlights correlated dependency exposure                 |
| Percentage of critical suppliers with verified incident notification arrangements | Measures operational response readiness                   |

Each metric should define its numerator, denominator, data source, owner, reporting frequency, threshold, and required management response.

For example, "percentage of critical suppliers with current assessments" should define what qualifies as critical, what constitutes a current assessment, and how suppliers with multiple services or use cases are counted.

Metrics should be interpreted together. A low number of open findings may indicate strong control performance, or it may reflect weak assessment rigor. Independent quality review and incident outcomes help distinguish these possibilities.

### 137. AI Vendor Assessment Maturity Model

A maturity model helps the organization evaluate how consistently its vendor assessment capability is implemented and where investment would improve risk management.

The following five-level model is illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's size, sector, risk profile, and regulatory context.

| Level | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                                                                                |
| ----- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Initial    | Supplier assessments are informal, inconsistent, and largely reactive                                                                          |
| 2     | Developing | Basic questionnaires, ownership, and risk classifications exist, but coverage and evidence quality vary                                        |
| 3     | Defined    | Standardized methodology, documented approval authority, evidence requirements, and lifecycle processes are established                        |
| 4     | Managed    | Risk-based workflows, continuous monitoring, quality assurance, portfolio reporting, and formal reassessment operate consistently              |
| 5     | Optimized  | Assessment outcomes are measured, integrated with operational risk signals, and continuously improved using validated data and lessons learned |

Maturity should be assessed across distinct dimensions, including governance, methodology, evidence quality, technical depth, workflow integration, monitoring, resilience, and assurance.

An organization may have a mature procurement workflow but limited AI-specific technical assessment capability. A single aggregate maturity score can conceal these differences.

The maturity assessment should result in a prioritized improvement plan with owners, milestones, resources, and measurable outcomes.

### 138. Implementation Roadmap for the AI Vendor Assessment Program

The organization should implement the vendor assessment program in stages, prioritizing critical suppliers and material exposure before expanding coverage across the entire portfolio.

A practical roadmap is:

| Phase               | Primary activities                                                                               | Expected outputs                                           |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Phase 1 — Establish | Define policy, ownership, scope, risk categories, approval authority, and mandatory requirements | Approved methodology and governance model                  |
| Phase 2 — Inventory | Identify AI suppliers, services, use cases, data flows, and upstream dependencies                | Initial AI vendor inventory and criticality classification |
| Phase 3 — Assess    | Apply the assessment template to critical suppliers and validate supporting evidence             | Risk assessments, findings, and remediation plans          |
| Phase 4 — Control   | Enforce onboarding gates, contractual requirements, risk acceptance, and exception processes     | Controlled approval and remediation workflow               |
| Phase 5 — Monitor   | Establish reassessment triggers, supplier incident monitoring, and management reporting          | Monitoring dashboard and lifecycle review schedule         |
| Phase 6 — Optimize  | Test effectiveness, calibrate scoring, analyze recurring findings, and improve automation        | Validated methodology and continuous improvement plan      |

The organization should define measurable completion criteria for each phase. Examples include the proportion of critical suppliers assessed, the percentage of high-risk findings with accountable owners, and the number of critical supplier relationships with verified exit arrangements.

Implementation priorities should reflect risk exposure rather than administrative convenience. Critical providers processing sensitive data or supporting essential business processes should generally receive attention before low-impact suppliers.

The program should also establish a sustainable operating model. Assessment requirements that cannot be supported by available staffing, evidence access, or workflow tooling may create backlogs and inconsistent decisions.

### 139. Part 4 Core Principle — Technical Evidence Must Support the Entire Supplier Lifecycle

An enterprise-grade AI vendor assessment combines technical architecture analysis, evidence validation, risk scoring, approval gates, contractual controls, operational monitoring, and exit readiness.

The organization must understand how the supplier's service interacts with its own identity systems, data, applications, users, models, and business processes. It must also understand which controls are operated by the supplier, which are inherited from upstream providers, and which remain the customer's responsibility.

The assessment should produce an auditable decision, not merely a completed questionnaire. Every material conclusion must connect to relevant evidence, every unresolved finding must have an accountable owner, and every approval must have a defined scope and authority.

**Final lesson principle:** AI vendor risk management is a continuous assurance capability. Supplier selection, technical validation, contractual safeguards, deployment controls, ongoing monitoring, and controlled exit must operate as one connected governance lifecycle.
