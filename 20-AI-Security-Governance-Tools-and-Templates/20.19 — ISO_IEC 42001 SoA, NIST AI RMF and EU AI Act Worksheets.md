# 20.19 — ISO/IEC 42001 SoA, NIST AI RMF and EU AI Act Worksheets

## Part 1 — AI Governance Framework Mapping, Compliance Assessment, and Worksheet Design

### 1. Purpose and Scope of AI Governance Framework Worksheets

AI governance framework worksheets provide a structured way to assess, document, and demonstrate how an organization governs AI-related risks, implements controls, evaluates compliance obligations, and maintains evidence of effective governance.

Organizations adopting AI frequently need to work with several standards, frameworks, and regulatory requirements simultaneously. These sources have different objectives, structures, terminology, and assessment methods. A governance worksheet helps connect their requirements to the organization's AI systems, risks, controls, responsibilities, and evidence.

This lesson develops a practical toolkit for three important AI governance references:

- **ISO/IEC 42001** — a management system standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** — a framework for incorporating trustworthiness considerations into AI risk management through the functions Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage.
- **European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act)** — a legal framework establishing obligations for different AI-related roles and systems, depending on the applicable provisions, risk classification, intended purpose, and circumstances of use.

These references are complementary, but they are not interchangeable.

ISO/IEC 42001 provides management-system requirements. NIST AI RMF provides a risk-management framework. The EU AI Act establishes legally binding obligations within its scope. An organization should therefore avoid treating a completed framework worksheet as automatic proof of compliance with every other framework or applicable law.

#### 1.1 Objectives of the Worksheet Toolkit

The worksheets should enable an organization to:

1. Identify applicable AI governance requirements.
2. Determine which AI systems, business units, and activities are in scope.
3. Map requirements to policies, processes, controls, and accountable owners.
4. Evaluate whether controls are designed and implemented appropriately.
5. Collect evidence supporting governance and compliance conclusions.
6. Identify control gaps, nonconformities, and remediation actions.
7. Establish accountability for implementation and ongoing monitoring.
8. Support internal audits, management reviews, and external assessments.
9. Maintain traceability between risks, requirements, controls, and evidence.
10. Monitor changes to AI systems, organizational processes, and applicable obligations.

#### 1.2 Intended Users

| Role | How the worksheets are used |
|---|---|
| Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) | Reviews AI security governance, control effectiveness, and material exposure |
| Chief AI Officer or AI Governance Lead | Coordinates AI governance requirements, accountability, and oversight |
| Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) Manager | Maintains mappings, assessments, evidence, findings, and remediation tracking |
| AI System Owner | Provides system-specific information and implements applicable requirements |
| AI Risk Owner | Evaluates risk exposure and makes or obtains authorized risk decisions |
| Data Protection Officer (DPO) | Reviews applicable personal-data protection requirements and responsibilities |
| Legal and Compliance | Determines applicable legal obligations and interprets regulatory requirements |
| Internal Audit | Independently tests governance design and operating effectiveness |
| Enterprise Architect | Maps AI systems, integrations, dependencies, and architectural controls |
| Procurement and Third-Party Risk | Assesses AI suppliers, contractual commitments, and external dependencies |
| Engineering and MLOps Teams | Provide technical evidence concerning development, deployment, monitoring, and change management |
| Executive Management | Reviews material risks, resource requirements, and management-system performance |

The worksheet owner should be identified for each assessment. Framework mapping is generally a coordinated GRC activity, but technical, legal, privacy, business, and independent assurance responsibilities should remain appropriately separated.

#### 1.3 Core Worksheet Categories

The toolkit should contain several related worksheet types.

| Worksheet | Primary purpose | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| Framework applicability worksheet | Determine which framework requirements apply | Applicability decisions and rationale |
| ISO/IEC 42001 mapping worksheet | Map management-system requirements and applicable controls | AIMS requirement and control mapping |
| Statement of Applicability (SoA) worksheet | Document applicable Annex A controls, justification, and implementation status | Controlled SoA record |
| NIST AI RMF mapping worksheet | Map AI risk-management activities to Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage | AI RMF implementation assessment |
| EU AI Act applicability worksheet | Determine potentially applicable legal provisions and roles | Legal applicability assessment |
| AI system classification worksheet | Document intended purpose and relevant classification factors | Classification rationale and review record |
| Control implementation worksheet | Assess control design, implementation, and operation | Control assessment results |
| Evidence register | Record supporting evidence and its provenance | Traceable evidence inventory |
| Gap assessment worksheet | Identify deviations from requirements | Gap register |
| Corrective action worksheet | Assign remediation responsibilities and deadlines | Corrective action plan |
| Management review worksheet | Evaluate performance, changes, risks, and improvement needs | Management review inputs and decisions |
| Cross-framework mapping worksheet | Identify relationships among requirements and controls | Reusable control mapping |

These worksheets should share common identifiers and controlled definitions. Otherwise, an organization may assess the same control differently across several workbooks or lose traceability between a compliance gap and its remediation.

### 2. Understanding the Three Governance References

Before creating mappings, the organization must understand the purpose, structure, and limitations of each reference.

#### 2.1 ISO/IEC 42001: AI Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System. It provides a management-system structure for governing AI-related responsibilities, risks, objectives, operational processes, performance evaluation, and continual improvement.

The standard is relevant to organizations that develop, provide, or use AI systems and want a systematic approach to managing their AI-related responsibilities.

An AIMS is not simply a list of technical security controls. It includes organizational governance, leadership, planning, support, operational processes, performance evaluation, and improvement.

The standard's Annex A provides a reference set of AI-related controls. Organizations use the applicable requirements, risk assessments, and the standard's control-selection process to determine which controls are necessary for their circumstances.

**Important distinction:** An organization should not claim ISO/IEC 42001 conformity or certification merely because it has completed a control mapping. Conformity requires assessment against the applicable requirements of the standard, and certification requires the relevant independent certification process.

#### 2.2 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI RMF provides a voluntary framework for incorporating trustworthiness considerations into AI risk management.

Its four core functions are:

| Function | Purpose | Typical governance activities |
|---|---|---|
| Govern | Establish organizational policies, accountability, culture, and oversight | Define roles, policies, risk appetite, accountability, and governance mechanisms |
| Map | Establish context and identify AI risks and affected stakeholders | Document intended purpose, system context, impacts, dependencies, and risk scenarios |
| Measure | Analyze, assess, and monitor AI risks | Evaluate performance, reliability, security, privacy, bias, and other relevant characteristics |
| Manage | Prioritize and respond to identified risks | Select treatment actions, implement controls, monitor risks, and manage residual exposure |

The functions are related and iterative rather than a one-time linear checklist.

For example, a new AI use case may require the organization to revisit its governance requirements, remap affected stakeholders and dependencies, measure new risks, and update its risk treatment decisions.

The AI RMF is outcome-oriented. Organizations may use it to structure their AI risk management even when they also operate an ISO-aligned management system.

#### 2.3 European Union Artificial Intelligence Act

The EU AI Act establishes a legal framework for AI within its defined scope. Its applicability depends on factors including the system's intended purpose, the activities performed by the organization, the organization's role in the AI value chain, the relevant legal provisions, and the circumstances in which the system is placed on the market or put into service or used.

Relevant roles can include provider, deployer, importer, distributor, product manufacturer, and other actors as defined by the applicable legislation.

The legal obligations are not identical for every AI system. Certain prohibited practices are subject to specific restrictions, high-risk AI systems are subject to additional requirements, and other systems may be subject to transparency or other obligations depending on their characteristics and use.

**Important distinction:** The EU AI Act is not simply a voluntary risk-management framework. Where its provisions apply, legal obligations must be evaluated against the legislation itself. An internal risk acceptance decision cannot override a mandatory legal requirement.

Organizations should use the official consolidated legal text, applicable amendments, and relevant authoritative guidance when determining their obligations. They should also confirm the applicable implementation dates and transitional provisions before relying on a compliance timetable.

#### 2.4 Comparative Overview

| Dimension | ISO/IEC 42001 | NIST AI RMF | EU AI Act |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Management system standard | Risk-management framework | Legal regulation |
| Primary objective | Establish and improve an AIMS | Manage AI risks and support trustworthy AI | Establish legally binding rules and obligations within scope |
| Main structure | Management-system clauses and Annex A controls | Govern, Map, Measure, Manage | Legal provisions addressing defined AI practices, roles, and risk categories |
| Risk approach | Risk-based management system | Contextual, lifecycle-oriented risk management | Obligation-specific, with requirements dependent on applicable provisions and classifications |
| Control approach | Organizational requirements and reference controls | Risk-management outcomes and practices | Legally prescribed obligations where applicable |
| Evidence | Management-system records, assessments, controls, and audit evidence | Evidence demonstrating implementation of risk-management activities | Evidence required to demonstrate applicable legal compliance |
| Assessment approach | Internal evaluation and management-system audit; certification may be pursued | Organizational assessment against relevant outcomes and practices | Legal applicability and compliance assessment |
| Main limitation | Does not automatically establish compliance with every applicable law | Does not replace legal obligations | Does not replace an organization's broader governance and risk-management needs |

An organization may use all three references within a common governance architecture. However, the mapping must preserve the distinct authority and purpose of each source.

### 3. Framework Applicability and Scoping Worksheet

A framework applicability assessment determines which requirements should be included in the organization's governance program and why.

Without documented scoping, organizations may omit relevant obligations, assess irrelevant controls, or incorrectly assume that an entire framework applies uniformly to every AI system.

The scoping exercise should cover both the organizational management system and individual AI systems or use cases.

#### 3.1 Scope Dimensions

| Scope dimension | Questions to answer | Expected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Organizational scope | Which legal entities, departments, and business units are included? | Organizational structure, scope statement |
| Geographic scope | In which jurisdictions does the organization operate or provide AI-related services? | Business footprint, service locations |
| AI lifecycle | Does the scope include design, development, acquisition, deployment, use, monitoring, and retirement? | Lifecycle procedures, system inventory |
| AI system population | Which AI systems and use cases are included? | AI system inventory |
| Business processes | Which processes depend on AI outputs or decisions? | Process maps, service documentation |
| Data scope | What data types are processed, and which data sources are involved? | Data inventory, data-flow diagrams |
| Technology scope | Which platforms, models, APIs, infrastructure, and integrations are involved? | Architecture diagrams, technical inventory |
| Supplier scope | Which external providers supply models, data, infrastructure, or AI services? | Supplier register, contracts |
| Regulatory scope | Which legal obligations may apply? | Legal register, applicability assessment |
| Certification scope | If certification is intended, what AIMS boundaries will be included? | Proposed certification scope and boundary documentation |

The scope should be sufficiently precise to identify the systems, functions, and organizational activities covered. Broad statements such as "all AI activities" should be supported by an inventory and documented boundary decisions.

#### 3.2 Applicability Decision Fields

A reusable applicability worksheet should contain the following fields:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Unique identifier for the scoping assessment |
| Framework | ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act, or another reference |
| Requirement reference | Specific clause, control, function, or legal provision |
| Requirement summary | Concise description of the requirement |
| Organizational scope | Relevant entity, department, or function |
| AI system or use case | Affected system or business application |
| Applicability status | Applicable, not applicable, conditional, or pending assessment |
| Decision rationale | Explanation supporting the applicability decision |
| Supporting evidence | Documents or records supporting the decision |
| Assessor | Person responsible for the assessment |
| Reviewer | Person responsible for review, where required |
| Assessment date | Date the decision was made |
| Review trigger | Event that requires the decision to be reconsidered |
| Approval status | Draft, reviewed, approved, or another controlled status |

For regulatory requirements, an internal applicability worksheet should not be treated as a substitute for qualified legal analysis where the interpretation is uncertain or material.

#### 3.3 Recommended Applicability Statuses

| Status | Meaning | Required handling |
|---|---|---|
| Applicable | The requirement applies within the defined scope | Assess implementation and evidence |
| Not Applicable | The requirement does not apply under the documented scope and criteria | Record the justification and supporting evidence |
| Conditional | Applicability depends on a condition that must be evaluated | Identify the condition, owner, and decision deadline |
| Pending Assessment | Insufficient information exists to determine applicability | Assign an owner and complete the assessment |
| Applicable — Gap Identified | The requirement applies but is not fully implemented | Record the gap and corrective action |
| Implemented — Evidence Pending | Implementation is reported but sufficient evidence has not yet been verified | Obtain and validate the evidence |
| Under Review | The applicability decision is being reconsidered | Track the review to completion |

These statuses should not be confused with a control's operating-effectiveness rating. Applicability and implementation are different assessment dimensions.

### 4. ISO/IEC 42001 Statement of Applicability Worksheet

The Statement of Applicability (SoA) is an important component of an ISO/IEC 42001 implementation. It documents the necessary controls, the rationale for their inclusion, whether they are implemented, and the justification for excluding Annex A reference controls.

The SoA should be derived from the organization's AI risk assessment and control-selection process. It should not be generated by copying a generic list and marking every control as applicable without analysis.

The organization should follow the exact requirements of its controlled edition of ISO/IEC 42001 when preparing the SoA and determining its required content.

#### 4.1 Purpose of the SoA

The SoA supports the organization in:

- Establishing a documented view of selected AI management controls.
- Connecting risk assessment outcomes to control selection.
- Explaining why controls are necessary or why reference controls are excluded.
- Tracking control implementation status.
- Supporting internal audits and management reviews.
- Identifying gaps between planned and actual implementation.
- Maintaining traceability between risks, controls, and evidence.

The SoA is not a replacement for the AI risk assessment, risk treatment plan, or supporting implementation records. It summarizes control-selection decisions and implementation status within the management system.

#### 4.2 Recommended SoA Worksheet Structure

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| SoA Record ID | Unique record identifier |
| Control Reference | Reference to the applicable Annex A control |
| Control Name | Official control title from the controlled standard |
| Control Objective or Purpose | Concise description of the control's intended outcome |
| Inclusion Status | Included or excluded |
| Inclusion Rationale | Why the control is necessary or selected |
| Exclusion Justification | Why an excluded reference control is not considered necessary, where applicable |
| Related AI Risk IDs | Risks addressed by the control |
| Related Requirements | Internal, contractual, or regulatory requirements supported by the control |
| Implementation Status | Planned, in progress, implemented, or another controlled value |
| Control Owner | Person accountable for implementation and operation |
| Implementation Evidence | Reference to supporting documentation or technical evidence |
| Effectiveness Assessment | Results of design or operating-effectiveness evaluation |
| Gap or Finding ID | Reference to an identified deficiency |
| Corrective Action ID | Reference to the remediation plan |
| Last Review Date | Date of the most recent review |
| Next Review Date | Date of the next scheduled review |
| Approver | Authorized person responsible for approval, where required |

The official control reference and title should be taken from the organization's licensed or otherwise authorized copy of the applicable standard. Avoid reproducing large portions of copyrighted standard text in internal or public knowledge-base materials.

#### 4.3 SoA Inclusion and Exclusion Logic

The organization should evaluate whether each reference control is necessary in light of its AI risk assessment, applicable requirements, organizational context, and control-selection decisions.

A simplified decision model is:

#### 4.4 Illustrative SoA Records

The following examples illustrate worksheet design only. They are not official control determinations or authoritative interpretations of particular Annex A controls.

| Control reference | Control subject | Inclusion decision | Rationale | Implementation status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Official reference to be verified | AI policy and governance | Illustrative: included | The organization requires formal governance and documented accountability for its AI activities | Implemented — evidence review pending |
| Official reference to be verified | AI risk assessment | Illustrative: included | The organization needs a consistent process to identify and evaluate AI-related risks | In progress |
| Official reference to be verified | AI data governance | Illustrative: included | The AI use cases depend on controlled data acquisition, processing, and quality | Planned |
| Official reference to be verified | AI supplier relationships | Illustrative: included | The organization uses externally provided models or AI services | Implemented |
| Official reference to be verified | A reference control evaluated as unnecessary | Illustrative: exclusion under review | Applicability must be justified against organizational context and risk assessment | Pending approval |

The control titles and reference identifiers should be replaced with verified references from the applicable edition of ISO/IEC 42001 before the worksheet is used as an actual SoA.

#### 4.5 SoA Quality Checks

Before approving the SoA, confirm that:

- [ ] The AIMS scope is documented and approved.
- [ ] The AI risk assessment has been completed to the required extent.
- [ ] Control selection is traceable to the risk assessment and applicable requirements.
- [ ] Every reference control has a documented inclusion or exclusion decision.
- [ ] Exclusions have a defensible rationale.
- [ ] The SoA implementation status agrees with actual implementation evidence.
- [ ] Control owners are assigned.
- [ ] Known gaps are recorded and linked to corrective actions.
- [ ] Evidence references are valid and accessible to authorized reviewers.
- [ ] The SoA has been reviewed and approved in accordance with the organization's governance process.
- [ ] Changes in risks, systems, or obligations trigger appropriate reassessment.

An auditor should challenge unexplained exclusions, unsupported implementation claims, and inconsistencies between the SoA, risk treatment plan, and actual operating controls.

### 5. NIST AI RMF Govern Function Mapping Worksheet

The Govern function establishes the organizational structures, policies, accountability mechanisms, and oversight practices needed to manage AI risks.

Governance should not be confined to a central AI committee. It must influence business decisions, system development, procurement, deployment, monitoring, and incident response.

The NIST AI RMF Core organizes Govern into categories and subcategories. When creating a detailed mapping worksheet, use the exact identifiers and wording from the selected official version of the NIST AI RMF rather than relying on paraphrased category labels alone.

#### 5.1 Govern Assessment Objectives

The worksheet should evaluate whether the organization has established:

- Clear accountability for AI-related decisions.
- Defined AI risk management policies and procedures.
- Appropriate risk culture and management oversight.
- Processes for identifying applicable legal and organizational requirements.
- Governance arrangements for third-party AI services.
- Workforce competence and training.
- Processes for managing AI risks across the lifecycle.
- Documentation and accountability for significant decisions.
- Monitoring and review of governance effectiveness.
- Mechanisms for addressing concerns, incidents, and emerging risks.

These objectives should be mapped to the relevant official NIST AI RMF categories and subcategories during implementation.

#### 5.2 Govern Mapping Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Mapping ID | Unique worksheet record identifier |
| NIST AI RMF reference | Official category or subcategory identifier |
| Official requirement or outcome | Text or controlled summary from the authorized source |
| Governance objective | Organizational outcome being assessed |
| Organizational process | Policy, workflow, committee, or operating process |
| Responsible owner | Accountable function or role |
| Supporting control | Internal control or procedure |
| Related ISO/IEC 42001 reference | Relevant management-system requirement or control, if applicable |
| Related AI Act provision | Potentially relevant legal requirement, subject to legal applicability assessment |
| Implementation status | Not started, planned, in progress, implemented, or another controlled value |
| Evidence reference | Supporting document or record |
| Assessment result | Effective, partially effective, ineffective, or not assessed |
| Identified gap | Description of the deficiency |
| Corrective action | Planned remediation |
| Action owner | Person responsible for remediation |
| Target date | Agreed completion date |
| Reviewer | Person responsible for independent or management review, where applicable |

#### 5.3 Example Govern Assessment

Consider an organization deploying a generative AI assistant for customer service.

| Governance area | Assessment question | Illustrative evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Accountability | Is a business owner accountable for the AI use case? | Approved ownership record |
| Policy | Are approved policies available to relevant personnel? | AI governance and acceptable-use policies |
| Risk oversight | Are AI risks evaluated before deployment and after material changes? | Risk assessments and review records |
| Legal obligations | Are applicable legal requirements identified and assigned? | Legal applicability register |
| Third parties | Are model-provider risks evaluated? | Supplier assessments and contracts |
| Competence | Have relevant personnel received role-appropriate training? | Training records and competency assessments |
| Monitoring | Are AI risks reviewed during operation? | Monitoring reports and governance dashboards |
| Incident handling | Are AI-related incidents subject to defined escalation procedures? | Incident response procedures and incident records |
| Management review | Does management review material AI risks and program performance? | Meeting records, decisions, and action logs |

These are practical assessment questions, not a substitute for mapping each applicable NIST AI RMF subcategory.

#### 5.4 Govern Maturity Assessment

Organizations may use an internal maturity scale to prioritize improvement. The scale below is an optional assessment technique, not an official NIST AI RMF maturity model.

| Level | Description | Evidence characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — Not Established | No consistent governance process exists | No reliable evidence of defined ownership or controls |
| 1 — Ad Hoc | Activities occur inconsistently | Informal decisions and fragmented documentation |
| 2 — Defined | Policies, roles, and procedures are documented | Approved procedures and assigned owners |
| 3 — Implemented | Governance processes operate across the defined scope | Records demonstrate consistent execution |
| 4 — Measured | Performance and effectiveness are monitored | Defined metrics, reviews, and corrective actions |
| 5 — Optimized | Governance is continuously improved based on evidence and changing risk | Trend analysis, lessons learned, and demonstrable improvement |

The organization should document how the maturity scale is applied and avoid equating a maturity score with regulatory compliance.

#### 5.5 Govern Gap Analysis

For each gap, the organization should document:

1. The applicable NIST AI RMF reference.
2. The expected governance outcome.
3. The current implementation state.
4. Evidence supporting the assessment.
5. The nature and potential consequences of the gap.
6. The corrective action required.
7. The accountable owner.
8. The target date and interim safeguards, where appropriate.
9. The validation method.
10. The final closure evidence.

A gap should be closed only when the organization has sufficient evidence that the required improvement has been implemented and, where appropriate, is operating effectively.

**Core Principle**

AI governance framework worksheets must preserve the distinction between management-system conformity, risk-management implementation, and legal compliance. ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, and the EU AI Act can support a coordinated governance architecture, but each must be assessed according to its own purpose, requirements, and authority.

A defensible worksheet records more than a control name and a compliance status. It establishes scope, applicability, accountability, implementation evidence, assessment results, and corrective action. The objective is to create traceable governance decisions that can be verified, maintained, and improved as AI systems and their operating environments evolve.

## Part 2 — ISO/IEC 42001 Control Mapping, NIST AI RMF Map and Measure Worksheets, and AI Risk Assessment Traceability

### 6. ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A Control Mapping and Implementation Assessment

ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A provides a reference set of controls intended to support organizations in addressing AI-related organizational, lifecycle, data, information, and other governance considerations. The organization determines which controls are necessary based on its context, AI risk assessment, applicable requirements, and control-selection process.

The objective of the control mapping worksheet is to establish a traceable relationship between the AI management system's requirements, identified risks, selected controls, implementation evidence, and corrective actions.

The worksheet must not be treated as a generic checklist where every control is automatically considered applicable or implemented. Each decision requires a documented basis.

#### 6.1 Control Mapping Objectives

The control mapping process should establish whether:

1. Applicable ISO/IEC 42001 requirements have been identified.
2. Relevant Annex A reference controls have been evaluated.
3. Selected controls address identified AI risks and organizational requirements.
4. Control owners and implementation responsibilities are assigned.
5. Control implementation status is supported by evidence.
6. Gaps are recorded and incorporated into the risk treatment process.
7. Controls are evaluated for design and operating effectiveness.
8. Control relationships with other frameworks are documented without assuming equivalence.
9. Changes to AI systems or risks trigger reassessment where appropriate.
10. Management receives reliable information about control implementation and effectiveness.

#### 6.2 Recommended Control Mapping Worksheet

| Field | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Control Mapping ID | Unique mapping identifier | MAP-AI-001 |
| Framework | Source framework | ISO/IEC 42001 |
| Requirement Reference | Exact clause or control reference | Verified reference from the controlled standard |
| Control Name | Official control title | Transcribed from the authorized standard |
| Control Objective | Intended governance or risk-reduction outcome | Establish consistent AI risk management |
| Related AI Risk ID | Risk addressed by the control | AIR-023 |
| Control Owner | Person accountable for operation | AI Governance Lead |
| Control Operator | Team performing the activity | AI Platform Operations |
| Implementation Status | Current implementation state | Implemented |
| Design Assessment | Whether the control design addresses the requirement | Effective / Partially Effective / Ineffective |
| Operating Effectiveness | Whether the control operates as intended | Effective / Partially Effective / Ineffective / Not Tested |
| Evidence Reference | Supporting records | EVD-AI-145 |
| Related NIST AI RMF Reference | Relevant category or subcategory | Verified Govern or Manage reference |
| Related Legal Requirement | Potentially relevant legal obligation | Subject to legal applicability assessment |
| Gap ID | Identified deficiency | GAP-AI-011 |
| Corrective Action ID | Remediation reference | CAP-AI-019 |
| Last Assessment Date | Date of latest assessment | YYYY-MM-DD |
| Next Review Date | Next planned review | YYYY-MM-DD |

Use controlled identifiers and authoritative source references. The illustrative IDs above are examples and do not refer to official framework identifiers.

#### 6.3 Distinguishing Implementation from Effectiveness

A common assessment error is to treat a documented policy as proof that the relevant control is effective.

The organization should distinguish between the following assessment dimensions.

| Assessment dimension | Question | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Design adequacy | Is the control appropriately designed to address the requirement or risk? | Control design review, documented procedure |
| Implementation | Has the control actually been put in place? | Configuration, approved workflow, assigned owner |
| Operating effectiveness | Does the control operate consistently as intended? | Sampled records, logs, testing results |
| Sustainability | Is the control maintained over time? | Periodic reviews, monitoring reports, corrective actions |
| Traceability | Can the organization connect the control to its risks and requirements? | Risk register, control mapping, evidence register |

For example, an AI risk assessment procedure may be approved and published, but the organization may not consistently perform assessments before deploying AI systems. The procedure exists, but operating effectiveness is not demonstrated.

#### 6.4 Control Assessment Decision Matrix

| Design assessment | Implementation status | Operating evidence | Suggested conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Effective | Implemented | Sufficient evidence of consistent operation | Potentially effective, subject to assessment scope |
| Effective | Implemented | No operating evidence | Effectiveness not demonstrated |
| Partially Effective | Implemented | Evidence identifies design limitations | Improvement required |
| Ineffective | Implemented | Control fails to address the risk | Significant corrective action may be required |
| Effective | In Progress | Implementation incomplete | Not fully implemented |
| Not Assessed | Unknown | Evidence unavailable | Assessment incomplete |

The final conclusion should follow the organization's documented assessment methodology. An auditor should not assign an effectiveness rating merely because the control owner has selected a status in the worksheet.

#### 6.5 Annex A Mapping Quality Checklist

- [ ] The applicable edition of ISO/IEC 42001 has been identified.
- [ ] Control references have been verified against the authorized standard.
- [ ] The AIMS scope is documented.
- [ ] Control selection is supported by the organization's risk assessment and applicable requirements.
- [ ] Inclusion and exclusion decisions are documented in the SoA where required.
- [ ] Every selected control has an accountable owner.
- [ ] Implementation status is supported by evidence.
- [ ] Design and operating effectiveness are assessed separately.
- [ ] Identified gaps are recorded and linked to corrective actions.
- [ ] Related framework mappings have been reviewed for accuracy.
- [ ] The mapping is reviewed when material changes occur.

### 7. ISO/IEC 42001 Risk Treatment Plan and Control Traceability Worksheet

The risk treatment plan translates AI risk assessment results into decisions about how risks will be addressed. It provides a bridge between risk analysis, control selection, implementation activities, and residual-risk decisions.

The organization should establish traceability from each material AI risk to the selected treatment approach and the controls intended to implement that treatment.

A risk treatment plan should not merely list controls. It should explain what will be done, why it is appropriate, who is accountable, when it will be completed, and how implementation will be verified.

#### 7.1 Risk Treatment Options

Organizations commonly evaluate whether to:

- Modify the risk by implementing or strengthening controls.
- Avoid the risk by not undertaking the relevant activity or by changing its design.
- Share or transfer appropriate aspects of the risk through contractual or other arrangements, recognizing that accountability and legal obligations may remain with the organization.
- Retain the risk through an authorized decision where permitted and justified.

The exact treatment approach should follow the organization's approved risk management methodology.

Risk acceptance must not be used to authorize conduct prohibited by law or to bypass mandatory obligations.

#### 7.2 Risk Treatment Plan Worksheet

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Treatment Plan ID | Unique identifier for the treatment record |
| AI System ID | Identifies the affected AI system |
| Risk ID | Links to the authoritative risk register |
| Risk Description | Describes the risk scenario |
| Inherent Risk Rating | Risk before relevant controls |
| Existing Controls | Current safeguards |
| Residual Risk Rating | Risk after existing controls |
| Treatment Decision | Modify, avoid, share or transfer where appropriate, or retain |
| Treatment Rationale | Explains why the selected approach is suitable |
| Planned Controls | Controls selected to address the risk |
| Control References | Internal and framework control identifiers |
| Implementation Tasks | Specific activities needed |

### 9. NIST AI RMF Measure Function Assessment Worksheet

The Measure function evaluates and monitors AI risks using appropriate methods, metrics, tests, and evidence.

Measurement should address the risks that matter for the particular AI system rather than rely on a universal checklist of technical metrics. The required methods depend on the model, intended purpose, deployment context, potential harms, and applicable requirements.

For example, a generative AI assistant connected to internal business systems may require testing for prompt injection, unauthorized retrieval, data leakage, and unsafe tool execution. A predictive model used for a high-impact decision may require different performance, validity, fairness, and monitoring assessments.

#### 9.1 Measurement Objectives

The worksheet should determine whether the organization has:

1. Defined measurable objectives for relevant AI risks.

5. Evaluated model limitations and uncertainty.
6. Assessed relevant trustworthiness characteristics.
7. Documented test environments, assumptions, and limitations.
8. Recorded measurement results and supporting evidence.
9. Defined thresholds for escalation and corrective action.
10. Established monitoring requirements for deployed systems.

#### 10.5 Evidence Management Controls

The organization should establish controls to ensure that evidence remains reliable, accessible, and appropriately protected.

Recommended practices include:

- Assign unique evidence identifiers.
- Link evidence to specific requirements and controls.
- Record the period covered by each artifact.
- Preserve versions and approval history.
- Restrict access according to sensitivity.
- Protect relevant records against unauthorized alteration.
- Define retention and disposal rules.
- Validate that evidence is current before relying on it.
- Record the person responsible for collection and review.
- Avoid collecting unnecessary personal, confidential, or security-sensitive data.
- Retain sufficient information to reproduce or review important assessment conclusions.
- Reassess evidence when the underlying AI system or control changes materially.

Evidence must be relevant to the claim being made. A training attendance record, for example, can demonstrate that training was recorded, but it does not independently demonstrate that the participant can apply the required security practices.

- [ ] Each mapped requirement uses a verified reference.
- [ ] The relationship between requirements is documented.
- [ ] Shared controls are evaluated against each requirement's actual criteria.
- [ ] Partial mappings and remaining gaps are explicitly identified.
- [ ] Legal obligations are assessed independently where necessary.
- [ ] Evidence is linked to controls and requirements.
- [ ] Evidence is current, reliable, and appropriately protected.
- [ ] Findings are traceable to the relevant control and requirement.
- [ ] Corrective actions have owners and target dates.
- [ ] Mappings are reviewed after relevant framework, legal, or system changes.

**Core Principle**

Cross-framework governance is effective when an organization can trace each applicable requirement to a documented decision, an accountable owner, an implemented control, and reliable evidence.

ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, and the EU AI Act can be integrated into a common governance architecture, but their differences must remain explicit. Shared controls and evidence can reduce duplication; they cannot justify treating management-system conformity, risk-management outcomes, and legal compliance as equivalent.

A defensible mapping demonstrates not only where requirements overlap, but also where additional controls, separate assessments, or legal interpretation remain necessary.

### 11. NIST AI RMF Map Function: Context, Intended Purpose, and Stakeholder Impact Worksheet

The Map function establishes the context needed to identify and understand AI risks. It helps an organization determine what an AI system is intended to do, how it operates, who may be affected, and which conditions could influence its performance or consequences.

The context assessment should not be a one-time administrative exercise. It should be revisited when the system's intended purpose, data sources, model, deployment environment, user population, or operating conditions change.

The organization should also distinguish between the AI model itself and the broader AI system. An AI system may include models, retrieval services, databases, application interfaces, external APIs, human decision-makers, and automated actions. Risks can arise from interactions among these components even when the model performs as expected.

#### 11.2 Intended-Purpose and Out-of-Scope Assessment

The intended purpose should be specific enough to guide design decisions, testing, access restrictions, and operational monitoring.

A vague purpose such as "improve productivity using AI" is insufficient to define meaningful controls. A more useful purpose identifies the task, user group, information sources, permitted outputs, and limits on automated actions.

| Assessment dimension | Weak description | Stronger description |
|---|---|---|
| Business objective | Improve customer service | Assist authorized representatives in resolving customer inquiries |
| Data access | Access customer data | Retrieve only records permitted for the authenticated user and approved task |
| Output use | Generate answers | Draft responses using approved sources for representative review |
| Automation | Perform customer-service tasks | Execute only explicitly authorized actions through approved APIs |
| Human oversight | Human in the loop | Representatives must review specified high-impact or externally communicated outputs |
| Prohibited behavior | Avoid misuse | Do not disclose unauthorized records, bypass access controls, or execute unapproved transactions |

The purpose statement should be consistent with the system's actual capabilities. If the system can execute transactions, change records, or trigger external actions, the assessment must account for those capabilities even if the business documentation describes it primarily as an advisory assistant.

| Suppliers | Commercial opportunities | Contractual disputes or dependency risks | Assurance, service commitments, and exit planning |

The organization should document how significant stakeholder concerns were identified and addressed. Consultation methods may include interviews, workshops, complaint analysis, user research, impact assessments, or reviews by subject-matter experts.

#### 11.4 Context Change and Reassessment Triggers

A previously acceptable risk assessment may become unreliable when the AI system changes materially.

Recommended triggers include:

- A change in intended purpose or authorized use.
- Introduction of a new model or model version.
- Changes to training, fine-tuning, retrieval, or inference data.
- New access permissions or tool integrations.
- Expansion to a new user population or geographical market.
- Changes to the degree of automation or human oversight.
- Material security incidents or unexpected model behavior.
- Changes in applicable legal or contractual requirements.
- Significant changes to the supplier or hosting arrangement.
- Evidence that the original risk assumptions are no longer valid.

**Assessment record:** Every reassessment should identify the trigger, affected system components, risk changes, control changes, responsible reviewer, and approval decision where required.

### 12. NIST AI RMF Measure Function: AI Trustworthiness and Risk Measurement Worksheet

The Measure function supports the analysis, assessment, benchmarking, and monitoring of AI risks. It helps organizations determine whether identified risks are understood well enough to support appropriate decisions and whether safeguards perform as intended.

The measurement process should be proportionate to the AI system's intended purpose, potential impact, technical characteristics, and operating environment.

An organization should not assume that a single accuracy score, security test, or model benchmark demonstrates that an AI system is trustworthy. Different risk dimensions require different assessment methods.

For example, a model may perform well on an accuracy benchmark while still disclosing sensitive information, responding incorrectly to adversarial inputs, or executing unauthorized tool calls.

#### 12.1 Trustworthiness Measurement Dimensions

The following dimensions can inform an organization's measurement strategy. They are useful assessment categories, not a requirement that every dimension must be measured identically for every system.

| Dimension | Assessment objective | Possible method |
|---|---|---|
| Validity and reliability | Determine whether the system performs its intended task consistently and appropriately | Evaluation against representative test cases |
| Safety | Evaluate the possibility of harmful outcomes | Hazard analysis, scenario testing, and safety evaluations |
| Security and resilience | Assess resistance to malicious activity and operational disruption | Threat-based testing, penetration testing, and resilience exercises |
| Accountability and transparency | Determine whether decisions, outputs, and responsibilities can be appropriately understood and reviewed | Documentation review, logging assessment, and traceability testing |
| Explainability and interpretability | Evaluate whether relevant stakeholders can understand outputs to the degree needed for the use case | Explanation-quality assessment and user studies |
| Privacy enhancement | Assess whether privacy risks are appropriately controlled | Privacy testing, data-flow review, and access-control testing |
| Fairness with harmful bias managed | Evaluate relevant differences in outcomes and potential discriminatory effects | Appropriate subgroup analysis and impact evaluation |
| Robustness | Determine whether the system continues to perform acceptably under relevant variations | Perturbation testing and robustness evaluations |

The relevance and interpretation of each dimension depend on the system. An organization should document why a measurement approach is appropriate and what it cannot establish.

#### 12.2 Measurement Planning Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Measurement ID | Unique measurement record |
| AI System ID | System under assessment |
| Risk ID | Associated risk register entry |
| Measurement Objective | Question the assessment must answer |
| Risk Dimension | Security, safety, reliability, privacy, fairness, or another relevant dimension |
| Evaluation Method | Test, benchmark, review, simulation, or other method |
| Test Environment | Environment in which the evaluation occurs |

| Prompt injection in retrieved content | Introduce malicious instructions into controlled retrieval documents | The system does not follow instructions that bypass approved policies or authorization controls | Adversarial test results |
| Sensitive-data leakage | Test whether restricted data appears in responses | No prohibited disclosure occurs in the defined scenarios | Data leakage assessment |
| Unauthorized tool execution | Attempt to invoke actions beyond the user's permitted scope | Unauthorized actions are rejected by enforcement controls | API and application logs |
| Unsupported factual statements | Compare outputs against approved source material | Results meet the organization's documented factuality criteria | Evaluation dataset and scoring report |
| Model or provider change | Repeat selected tests after a material update | Regression results satisfy approved release criteria | Version comparison and regression report |
| Monitoring failure | Generate controlled security events | Required alerts are produced and routed according to policy | Alert test evidence |

The acceptance criteria in this table are examples, not universal thresholds. The organization must define thresholds that reflect its risk appetite, business context, legal requirements, and technical constraints.

#### 12.4 Interpreting Measurement Results

Measurement results should be interpreted alongside the limitations of the evaluation.

A test result may be misleading if:

- The test dataset does not represent real operating conditions.
- The sample size is too small to support the conclusion.
- The evaluation excludes important user groups or attack scenarios.
- The test environment differs materially from production.
- The assessment measures average performance while ignoring severe failures.
- The model or system changes after the evaluation.
- The evaluation method has not been independently reviewed where independence is required.

Organizations should distinguish between a failure to meet a defined threshold and a lack of sufficient evidence to determine whether the threshold is met. An inconclusive result must not automatically be recorded as a pass.

#### 12.5 Measurement and Evidence Quality Checklist

- [ ] Each measurement has a defined objective.
- [ ] The method is appropriate to the identified risk.
- [ ] Acceptance criteria are defined and justified.
- [ ] Test datasets and scenarios are documented.
- [ ] The system version and test environment are recorded.
- [ ] Results and limitations are documented.
- [ ] Severe or low-frequency failures are considered where relevant.
- [ ] Inconclusive results are distinguished from successful results.
- [ ] Findings are linked to risk and corrective-action records.
- [ ] Material system changes trigger appropriate regression testing.

### 13. NIST AI RMF Manage Function: AI Risk Treatment and Prioritization Worksheet

The Manage function focuses on allocating resources and taking action to address identified AI risks. It uses information gathered through governance, context mapping, and measurement to determine which risks require treatment, what actions are appropriate, and how those actions will be monitored.

Risk treatment should be linked to the organization's risk appetite and applicable requirements. A risk that is technically manageable may still be unacceptable if it conflicts with legal obligations, contractual commitments, or approved organizational policies.

The Manage function should also account for the fact that AI risk changes over time. A treatment decision that was reasonable before deployment may need to be revisited when the system's behavior, data, users, or environment changes.

#### 13.1 Risk Prioritization Criteria

The organization may use a documented prioritization method that considers:

- Severity of potential harm.
- Likelihood or plausibility of the risk scenario.
- Scale of exposure and number of affected stakeholders.
- Difficulty of detecting the failure before harm occurs.
- Reversibility of the outcome.
- Exposure of sensitive information.
- Degree of automation and system authority.
- Dependence on external providers.
- Applicable legal or contractual requirements.
- Existing control effectiveness.
- Uncertainty in the available evidence.
- Time required to implement treatment.

The method should be consistent with the organization's risk management policy. A scoring model is a decision-support tool; it should not replace professional judgment or mandatory legal controls.

The following is an illustrative internal decision-support model. It is not an official NIST AI RMF scoring system.

| Priority | Illustrative condition | Expected management response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Credible potential for severe harm, major unauthorized action, or a conflict with a mandatory requirement | Immediate escalation; restrict, suspend, or prevent the relevant operation where necessary |

A low priority must not be used to override a legal prohibition or mandatory requirement. Where risk ratings conflict with applicable law or policy, the organization must follow the relevant mandatory obligation.

#### 13.4 Risk Treatment Decision Record

Each material risk should have a decision record that explains:

1. What the risk is and who may be affected.
2. What evidence supports the assessment.
3. Which treatment options were considered.
4. Why the selected option is appropriate.
5. Which controls or operational changes will be implemented.
6. Who is responsible for implementation.
7. How effectiveness will be verified.
8. What residual risk is expected.
9. Who must approve the decision.
10. When the decision must be reviewed.

A treatment decision should not be considered complete merely because an action has been marked as finished. The organization should verify that the control has been implemented and that the residual risk has been reassessed.

### 14. NIST AI RMF Profiles and Organizational Gap Analysis Worksheet

NIST AI RMF Profiles help organizations apply the framework in a particular context. A profile can reflect an organization's sector, use case, risk tolerance, business objectives, regulatory environment, or intended application of AI.

Organizations may develop a Current Profile describing their present risk management practices and a Target Profile describing desired outcomes. Comparing the two helps identify gaps and prioritize improvement activities.

A profile is not automatically a certification, legal compliance statement, or assurance that the AI system is safe. It is a structured way to organize desired and current risk management outcomes.

#### 14.1 Current and Target Profile Definitions

**Current Profile:** Documents the organization's present AI risk management practices and the extent to which relevant outcomes are achieved.

**Target Profile:** Describes the outcomes the organization intends to achieve based on business objectives, risk appetite, legal obligations, stakeholder expectations, and available resources.

**Gap Analysis:** Compares the current and target states to identify missing practices, weaknesses, evidence gaps, and improvement priorities.

Organizations should record the rationale for selecting profile outcomes. Not every outcome needs the same implementation approach, but decisions to omit or defer relevant outcomes should be documented and justified.

#### 14.2 Profile Gap Analysis Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Profile ID | Unique profile identifier |
| Organization or Business Unit | Scope of the profile |
| AI System or Portfolio | Systems covered |
| Framework Version | Selected NIST AI RMF version |
| Profile Type | Current, Target, or comparison |
| Function | Govern, Map, Measure, or Manage |
| Category | Verified category reference |
| Subcategory | Verified subcategory reference |
| Desired Outcome | Intended risk management outcome |
| Current Practice | Description of present implementation |
| Current Evidence | Evidence supporting the current-state assessment |
| Current Status | Achieved, partially achieved, not achieved, or not assessed |
| Target State | Desired implementation outcome |
| Gap Description | Difference between current and target states |
| Gap Priority | Priority assigned using the approved method |
| Improvement Action | Activity required to close the gap |
| Accountable Owner | Person responsible for the outcome |
| Target Date | Planned completion |
| Resource Requirement | Budget, personnel, or technical dependency |
| Verification Method | How achievement will be assessed |
| Review Date | Date for reassessment |

The status labels above are suggested internal values, not official NIST AI RMF maturity ratings.

#### 14.3 Example Current-to-Target Gap Analysis

Consider an organization deploying generative AI across several business units.

| Outcome area | Current state | Target state | Identified gap | Improvement action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI governance | AI ownership varies by business unit | Defined enterprise accountability and escalation | Responsibilities are inconsistent | Approve an enterprise AI governance charter |
| AI inventory | Major AI platforms are recorded, but embedded AI is incompletely identified | Inventory covers material AI systems and dependencies | Incomplete visibility | Implement inventory discovery and owner attestation |
| Risk assessment | Assessments occur only for selected projects | Risk assessment is integrated into AI lifecycle gates | Inconsistent assessment coverage | Introduce a risk-based assessment workflow |
| AI security testing | Testing focuses mainly on conventional application security | Relevant AI-specific threats are included | AI threat scenarios are not systematically tested | Establish an AI security evaluation standard |
| Supplier oversight | Standard supplier reviews are performed | AI-specific provider and model dependencies are assessed | Model behavior, data use, and change processes receive limited review | Extend third-party assessment criteria |
| Monitoring | Operational availability is monitored | Material AI risks and control failures are also monitored | Limited risk-focused monitoring | Define AI risk indicators and escalation thresholds |
| Incident management | General incident process exists | AI-specific incident scenarios and evidence are addressed | AI incidents are not consistently classified | Update incident taxonomy and response procedures |

The example is illustrative. The organization's actual profile should use verified NIST AI RMF outcomes and evidence from its own operations.

#### 14.4 Gap Prioritization

A profile gap should not be prioritized solely by how easy it is to close.

The organization should consider:

- Whether the gap relates to a legal or contractual obligation.
- The severity of the associated AI risk.
- The number of systems or business units affected.
- Whether the gap undermines other controls.
- The availability and reliability of current evidence.
- Dependencies on suppliers or technology changes.
- The resources and time required for remediation.
- The potential consequences of leaving the gap unresolved.

Where a gap involves a mandatory obligation, the organization should apply the relevant compliance process rather than defer the issue solely because its internal priority score is low.

#### 14.5 Profile Review Checklist

- [ ] The profile scope and intended use are documented.
- [ ] The selected framework version is recorded.
- [ ] Relevant categories and subcategories use verified references.
- [ ] Current-state assessments are supported by evidence.
- [ ] Target outcomes are linked to business and risk objectives.
- [ ] Gaps are clearly described.
- [ ] Improvement actions have accountable owners.
- [ ] Dependencies and resources are documented.
- [ ] Progress is measured against defined criteria.
- [ ] The profile is reviewed when the AI portfolio or operating context changes.

### 15. NIST AI RMF Implementation Assessment and Continuous Monitoring Worksheet

AI risk management requires more than a completed initial assessment. Organizations should establish ongoing processes to evaluate whether risk management practices remain suitable and whether controls continue to operate effectively as AI systems, threats, and business conditions evolve.

Continuous monitoring should combine technical signals, control assessments, operational events, user feedback, and governance reviews where appropriate. It should be designed around the risk characteristics of the system rather than implemented as a generic dashboard of activity metrics.

For example, counting the number of AI models deployed does not establish that those models are secure. A meaningful monitoring program connects metrics to defined risks, thresholds, accountable owners, and required actions.

#### 15.5 Monitoring Governance Requirements

The organization should define:

- Which AI risks require ongoing monitoring.
- Which signals and metrics provide meaningful information.
- Who reviews monitoring results.
- Which thresholds require investigation or escalation.
- How false positives and false negatives are handled.
- How incidents and control failures are documented.
- How changes to models, data, and integrations affect monitoring.
- How monitoring evidence is protected and retained.
- How corrective actions are tracked and verified.
- How monitoring results are reported to governance bodies.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the risk. Systems with significant potential consequences or extensive authority to access data and execute actions may require stronger safeguards and more frequent review than low-impact internal productivity tools.

**Core Principle**

The NIST AI RMF should be applied as an iterative risk management process, not as a one-time compliance checklist. The Map function establishes context; Measure evaluates risks and relevant evidence; Manage supports decisions and treatment; and Govern provides the organizational structures, accountability, and oversight that enable these activities to operate coherently.

A defensible implementation assessment distinguishes between documented procedures, actual implementation, demonstrated effectiveness, and ongoing monitoring. An organization should claim that an outcome has been achieved only when the available evidence supports that conclusion under its documented assessment methodology.
