# 20.07 — AI Impact Assessment Template

## Part 1 — AI Impact Assessment Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Impact Assessment

An AI Impact Assessment is a structured process for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, documenting, and managing the potential effects of an AI system on individuals, groups, organizations, and society.

It examines not only whether an AI system is technically secure or operationally reliable, but also what the system may do to people, their rights, their opportunities, their privacy, their safety, and their ability to make informed decisions.

Within an enterprise AI Security Governance framework, the assessment provides a documented basis for determining whether a proposed AI use case is appropriate, what safeguards are necessary, and whether the organization should approve, restrict, redesign, defer, or reject the activity.

The assessment should be proportionate to the AI system's intended purpose, deployment context, potential severity of harm, scale of exposure, and degree of autonomy.

Typical impact areas include:

- Fundamental rights and human dignity.
- Privacy and personal data protection.
- Health, physical safety, and psychological well-being.
- Fairness, discrimination, and equal opportunity.
- Human autonomy and meaningful human oversight.
- Transparency, explainability, and contestability.
- Information security and resilience.
- Economic, employment, and organizational effects.
- Environmental and societal consequences.
- Legal, regulatory, contractual, and reputational exposure.

An AI Impact Assessment should be completed early enough to influence system design and deployment decisions. An assessment conducted only after a system has entered production may identify risks without leaving sufficient time or authority to prevent harm.

The assessment is not merely a form to complete. It is a decision-support mechanism that connects potential impacts to evidence, safeguards, accountable owners, and explicit approval conditions.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Impact Assessment Template

The AI Impact Assessment Template standardizes how an organization evaluates the consequences of introducing, modifying, procuring, or operating an AI system.

Its primary purpose is to establish a repeatable method for answering six questions:

1. What AI system or use case is being assessed?
2. Who or what may be affected, and how?
3. What adverse or beneficial impacts could occur?
4. How significant are the identified impacts?
5. What safeguards and mitigation measures are required?
6. Who has the authority to decide whether the proposed activity may proceed?

The template should enable assessors to document the reasoning behind their conclusions rather than merely select predefined ratings.

A complete assessment should ordinarily contain:

| Assessment component        | Purpose                                                  |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessment identification   | Establishes the record, owner, scope, and status         |
| System and use-case context | Explains the proposed AI activity                        |
| Stakeholder analysis        | Identifies affected individuals and groups               |
| Impact identification       | Documents potential beneficial and adverse effects       |
| Impact analysis             | Examines severity, likelihood, scale, and context        |
| Risk and obligation mapping | Connects impacts to relevant risks and requirements      |
| Safeguard assessment        | Evaluates existing and proposed protections              |
| Mitigation plan             | Assigns actions, owners, and deadlines                   |
| Residual impact evaluation  | Determines the impact remaining after safeguards         |
| Governance decision         | Records approval, restrictions, escalation, or rejection |
| Monitoring and review       | Defines how impacts will be detected and reassessed      |
| Evidence and sign-off       | Preserves the basis for the decision                     |

The template should be adaptable to different AI technologies and business contexts without losing its mandatory governance requirements.

### 3. AI Impact Assessment as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI Impact Assessment should operate as a preventive governance control within the organization's AI lifecycle.

It provides a formal checkpoint before material commitments are made, including procurement, development, integration, pilot testing, production deployment, or expansion into a new use case.

The assessment should connect with existing enterprise processes rather than operate as an isolated AI-specific document.

```

The initial screening decision must itself be documented. A decision that a full assessment is unnecessary should identify the screening criteria, relevant evidence, accountable reviewer, and rationale.

Where the assessment identifies impacts that cannot be adequately mitigated, the organization should escalate the matter to the appropriate decision authority rather than treating completion of the template as evidence that the use case is acceptable.

### 4. Scope of the AI Impact Assessment

The scope should be defined according to the AI system's intended purpose, actual operating context, affected stakeholders, and reasonably foreseeable uses or misuse.

The assessment may cover:

- Internally developed AI systems.
- Third-party AI products and platforms.
- Foundation models and externally hosted models.
- Generative AI applications.
- AI-enabled business processes.
- Automated or semi-automated decision systems.
- AI agents capable of taking actions.
- AI features embedded in existing enterprise applications.
- Material changes to an approved AI system.
- New populations, jurisdictions, or purposes for an existing system.

The scope should include relevant dependencies, including data sources, model providers, APIs, external tools, downstream applications, and human decision-makers.

For example, an AI assistant used to summarize internal meeting notes may present limited direct impact in one context. The same assistant, when used to produce employee performance assessments or recommend employment decisions, may create materially different impacts.

The organization should therefore assess the use case, not just the model or product name.

A complete scope statement should identify:

- The system and use case.
- The business objective.
- The intended users.
- The affected individuals and groups.
- The deployment environment.
- The data and external dependencies.
- The decisions or actions influenced by the AI.
- The lifecycle stages covered.
- The exclusions and their rationale.
- The assessment boundaries and assumptions.

### 5. Distinction Between AI Impact Assessment, AI Risk Assessment, DPIA, and AI Security Assessment

These assessment types overlap, but they do not have identical objectives or scopes.

| Assessment                                                           | Primary focus                                                                                          | Typical question                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Impact Assessment                                                 | Broad beneficial and adverse effects of AI on people, organizations, and society                       | What consequences could this AI use case create?                                |
| AI Risk Assessment                                                   | Identification, analysis, evaluation, and treatment of AI-related risks                                | What could go wrong, how significant is it, and how should the risk be managed? |
| Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)                             | Risks to individuals arising from personal data processing, where applicable under data protection law | Could this processing create a high risk to people's rights and freedoms?       |
| AI Security Assessment                                               | Threats, vulnerabilities, abuse cases, and security control effectiveness                              | How could the AI system or its dependencies be compromised or abused?           |
| AI System Impact Assessment under a specific regulation or framework | Requirements defined by that particular regime                                                         | Which prescribed impacts, rights, obligations, and safeguards must be assessed? |

An AI Impact Assessment may incorporate inputs from an AI Risk Assessment, DPIA, security assessment, safety analysis, or human rights assessment. However, combining the records does not automatically satisfy every applicable legal or framework-specific requirement.

For example, an AI Impact Assessment may identify that a hiring system could disadvantage certain applicants. The AI Risk Assessment may record discriminatory outcomes as a risk scenario, the DPIA may examine the processing of applicants' personal data, and the security assessment may examine unauthorized manipulation of applicant profiles or model outputs.

These assessments should share evidence and identifiers where appropriate, while retaining their distinct legal, technical, and governance purposes.

### 6. Regulatory and Standards Context

The assessment methodology should be mapped to the laws, regulations, standards, and organizational commitments applicable to the specific system and deployment context.

Potential reference points include:

- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, commonly known as the EU AI Act.
- Regulation (EU) 2016/679, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
- ISO/IEC 42001, AI management systems.
- ISO/IEC 23894, AI risk management.
- Applicable human rights, consumer protection, employment, product safety, and sector-specific requirements.
- Internal AI governance policies and risk appetite statements.

The applicability of each source must be assessed rather than assumed. Different obligations may depend on the system's intended purpose, provider or deployer role, risk classification, processing activities, sector, jurisdiction, and date of application.

The EU AI Act includes specific impact-assessment requirements for certain circumstances, including a fundamental rights impact assessment for specified deployers of certain high-risk AI systems under Article 27. GDPR Article 35 establishes DPIA requirements for processing likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

A general enterprise AI Impact Assessment should not be presented as a substitute for either requirement. The organization should identify applicable legal triggers and complete the prescribed assessment where required.

The template should therefore include a regulatory applicability section that records the relevant provision, applicability determination, accountable reviewer, evidence, and resulting obligations.

### 7. AI Impact Assessment Ownership and Accountability

The assessment requires clear ownership across the business, AI governance, technology, risk, privacy, security, legal, and compliance functions.

The business owner should remain accountable for the purpose and consequences of the proposed AI use case. Specialist functions provide independent or expert review within their respective areas of responsibility.

Representative responsibilities include:

| Role                                                 | Core responsibility                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business or use-case owner                           | Defines purpose, expected benefits, operating context, and affected stakeholders |
| AI system owner                                      | Provides system design, capability, dependency, and lifecycle information        |
| Assessment lead                                      | Coordinates the assessment and maintains its completeness                        |
| AI governance function                               | Ensures consistency with the AI governance framework                             |
| Cybersecurity                                        | Evaluates security-related impacts and safeguards                                |
| Privacy or Data Protection Officer, where applicable | Reviews personal data implications and applicable DPIA requirements              |
| Legal and compliance                                 | Evaluates relevant legal and regulatory obligations                              |
| Human resources or domain specialist                 | Evaluates domain-specific impacts where relevant                                 |
| Affected stakeholder representatives                 | Contribute contextual knowledge and perspectives where appropriate               |
| Risk acceptance authority                            | Makes decisions within formally delegated authority                              |
| Internal audit or independent assurance              | Provides independent evaluation of the process and evidence, as applicable       |

The person completing the assessment should not automatically be authorized to approve every identified impact or accept all residual exposure.

Where impacts are material, the organization should require appropriate independent challenge and escalation.

### 8. AI Impact Assessment Record Identification

Every formal assessment should have a unique, persistent identifier that enables traceability across the AI governance lifecycle.

A suggested naming convention is:

`AIA-[BUSINESS_UNIT]-[YEAR]-[SEQUENCE]`

Example:

`AIA-HR-2026-0042`

The identifier is illustrative; organizations may use their existing GRC or document management conventions.

The assessment record should include:

- Assessment ID.
- Assessment title.
- AI system ID.
- Use-case ID.
- Business unit.
- Assessment owner.
- Date initiated.
- Assessment version.
- Assessment type.
- Current status.
- Planned completion date.
- Review date.
- Related risk register IDs.
- Related security and privacy assessment IDs.
- Approval or decision reference.

The assessment ID should remain stable when the assessment is updated. Material changes should be recorded through versioning or linked reassessment records rather than creating ambiguity about which assessment supported a particular decision.

### 9. AI Impact Assessment Lifecycle Status

The template should define controlled statuses so stakeholders can determine the assessment's current state.

A practical status model includes:

| Status                   | Meaning                                                                          |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Draft                    | Initial record is being prepared                                                 |
| Screening                | Scope and assessment requirements are being determined                           |
| In Progress              | Impact identification and analysis are underway                                  |
| Pending Evidence         | Required information or supporting evidence is outstanding                       |
| Under Review             | Specialist or governance review is underway                                      |
| Remediation Required     | Safeguards or mitigation actions must be completed or addressed                  |
| Pending Decision         | Required approval or escalation decision is outstanding                          |
| Approved with Conditions | The use case may proceed subject to documented conditions                        |
| Approved                 | The use case has received the required approval                                  |
| Restricted               | Operation is permitted only within defined boundaries                            |
| Rejected                 | The proposed activity is not authorized                                          |
| Superseded               | A newer assessment has replaced this record for current decisions                |
| Closed                   | The assessment lifecycle has been formally completed under the defined procedure |

Status changes should be controlled by workflow rules and recorded with timestamps, responsible actors, and decision references.

An assessment marked "Approved" should not imply that every impact has been eliminated. The record must state the approved conditions, residual impacts, and monitoring obligations.

### 10. AI System and Use-Case Context

The assessment must describe the actual use case in enough detail for reviewers to understand what the AI system does, how it is used, and whose interests may be affected.

The system description should identify:

- Business purpose and intended outcomes.
- Model or AI technology type.
- Provider and deployment arrangement.
- Input data and relevant data sources.
- Output types and intended recipients.
- Decisions or actions influenced by outputs.
- Degree of automation and human involvement.
- External tools and connected systems.
- Intended users and affected stakeholders.
- Deployment location and operating context.
- Expected scale and frequency of use.
- Known limitations and foreseeable misuse.

A vague description such as "AI for HR efficiency" is insufficient.

A more useful description would state that an AI-enabled recruitment tool analyzes submitted applications, extracts experience and qualifications, ranks candidates against specified criteria, and presents recommendations to recruiters who make final selection decisions.

The second description identifies the decision pathway, affected population, and potential impact areas that require assessment.

### 11. Assessment Objectives and Intended Benefits

The assessment should document the intended benefits of the AI use case alongside its potential adverse impacts.

Potential benefits may include improved accessibility, faster service delivery, more consistent processing, reduced administrative burden, improved detection of safety issues, or better access to information.

The organization should specify the intended outcomes and the evidence that will be used to evaluate whether they are achieved.

Representative fields include:

- Business objective.
- Expected benefits.
- Beneficiary groups.
- Baseline process or outcome.
- Expected performance improvement.
- Benefit measurement method.
- Potential unintended consequences.
- Assumptions underlying the benefit case.
- Conditions required to realize the benefits.

Benefits should not be assumed to outweigh harms merely because they are commercially attractive.

For example, faster recruitment processing may benefit an employer and applicants, but the benefit does not automatically justify discriminatory ranking, inaccessible application procedures, or inadequate opportunities to challenge incorrect outcomes.

The assessment should consider who receives the benefits, who bears the burdens, and whether the distribution of outcomes is reasonable and defensible.

### 12. Stakeholder Identification and Mapping

Stakeholder mapping identifies individuals, groups, and organizations that may experience, influence, or be responsible for the consequences of the AI system.

Stakeholders may include:

- Direct users.
- Individuals whose data is processed.
- People subject to AI-supported decisions.
- Employees and contractors.
- Customers and service users.
- Children or other potentially vulnerable groups.
- People with disabilities.
- Suppliers and downstream service providers.
- Regulators and oversight bodies.
- Communities indirectly affected by deployment.
- Internal business functions responsible for decisions and outcomes.

The assessment should consider both direct and indirect effects.

For example, a workforce scheduling system may directly affect employees whose shifts are assigned by the AI and indirectly affect their families through changes in working hours, income stability, or caregiving arrangements.

The stakeholder map should record the nature of each group's relationship to the system, potential impacts, relevant vulnerabilities, and appropriate methods of engagement.

Stakeholder identification should not rely exclusively on the views of the system owner or intended users. People who experience the consequences may have different priorities and may identify impacts that are not visible from the developer's perspective.

### 13. Affected Stakeholder Characteristics and Vulnerability

The assessment should consider whether specific stakeholder characteristics increase the likelihood or severity of harm.

Relevant factors may include age, disability, language proficiency, access to technology, economic dependence, power imbalance, exposure to consequential decisions, and limited ability to challenge an AI-supported outcome.

The analysis should be context-sensitive and should avoid collecting sensitive personal information without a justified purpose and appropriate safeguards.

Questions to consider include:

- Can affected individuals understand how the system influences decisions?
- Can they challenge or correct inaccurate outputs?
- Do they have a realistic alternative to using the service?
- Could an incorrect output materially affect their rights, income, safety, or opportunities?
- Are certain groups more exposed to errors or adverse outcomes?
- Are accessibility and language requirements adequately addressed?
- Could the system amplify existing inequalities?
- Are additional safeguards required for vulnerable groups?

The organization should document the evidence supporting its conclusions and acknowledge uncertainty where stakeholder characteristics or effects are not sufficiently understood.

### 14. AI Impact Identification Taxonomy

A standardized taxonomy helps assessors identify impact categories systematically and supports consistent reporting across business units.

A suggested taxonomy is:

| Impact category                 | Examples                                                                        |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fundamental rights              | Interference with protected rights or freedoms                                  |
| Privacy and data protection     | Excessive processing, disclosure, or loss of control over personal data         |
| Fairness and discrimination     | Unequal outcomes or unjustified differential treatment                          |
| Human autonomy                  | Undue influence, manipulation, or reduced ability to make independent decisions |
| Transparency and contestability | Inability to understand, challenge, or correct consequential outputs            |
| Health and safety               | Physical injury, psychological harm, or unsafe recommendations                  |
| Financial and economic          | Loss of income, financial exclusion, or erroneous financial decisions           |
| Employment and education        | Unfair recruitment, evaluation, access, or progression outcomes                 |
| Information security            | Data leakage, unauthorized actions, model manipulation, or service disruption   |
| Operational and service quality | Incorrect outputs, service denial, or failure of essential processes            |
| Societal and community          | Scaled misinformation, exclusion, or erosion of trust                           |
| Environmental                   | Resource consumption or other material environmental effects                    |
| Legal and regulatory            | Breach of applicable obligations or enforceable commitments                     |
| Reputational and trust          | Loss of stakeholder confidence resulting from harmful or irresponsible use      |

The taxonomy should be adapted to the organization's sector and operating context.

A single scenario may map to multiple categories. For example, a manipulated AI-generated medical recommendation may create safety, privacy, security, legal, and reputational impacts.

The organization should allow primary and secondary classifications without forcing a complex scenario into only one category.

### 15. Positive, Negative, Direct, and Indirect Impacts

Impact identification should include beneficial and adverse outcomes, as well as direct and indirect effects.

These distinctions help avoid an overly narrow assessment focused only on immediate technical outputs.

| Dimension         | Definition                                          | Example                                                       |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Positive impact   | A beneficial consequence                            | Improved access to information                                |
| Negative impact   | An adverse consequence                              | Incorrect advice causing harm                                 |
| Direct impact     | An effect arising directly from system operation    | An erroneous decision recommendation                          |
| Indirect impact   | An effect mediated through another process or actor | A manager relying on a flawed recommendation                  |
| Intended impact   | An expected consequence of the use case             | Reduced processing time                                       |
| Unintended impact | An outcome not intended by the system owner         | Overreliance on recommendations or exclusion of certain users |
| Individual impact | An effect on a person                               | An inaccurate assessment affecting eligibility                |
| Collective impact | An effect on a group or community                   | Systematic exclusion from a service                           |

The assessment should consider how effects may propagate through downstream decisions and organizational behavior.

For example, an AI system may not make a final employment decision, but a recruiter may consistently follow its rankings. The resulting effect on applicants can still be material even when a human formally retains the final decision.

The presence of a human decision-maker does not automatically remove the need to assess downstream impacts.

### 16. AI Impact Scenario Construction

Each material impact should be expressed as a concrete scenario that explains how the system could create a beneficial or adverse outcome.

A useful scenario structure is:

**System or activity + causal condition + affected stakeholder + resulting impact.**

For adverse impacts, the scenario should describe a plausible pathway from the AI system's behavior or operating context to the consequence.

Example:

- **System:** AI-enabled recruitment screening.
- **Causal condition:** Training data and ranking criteria inadequately represent certain applicant groups.
- **Affected stakeholders:** Applicants whose qualifications are evaluated by the system.
- **Impact:** Qualified applicants may be disproportionately excluded from further consideration.

The scenario should be specific enough to support meaningful analysis and mitigation.

Weak scenario: "AI may be biased."

Stronger scenario: "The recruitment ranking model may systematically assign lower suitability scores to qualified applicants whose career histories differ from patterns overrepresented in the training data, reducing their likelihood of progressing to interviews."

A useful scenario identifies what could happen, under what conditions, to whom, and with what consequence. It should not present an unverified hypothesis as an established fact.

### 17. Cause, Event, and Impact Distinction

The assessment should distinguish the cause of a scenario, the event that occurs, and the resulting impact.

This structure improves the selection of safeguards because controls should address the actual causal pathway.

| Element | Meaning                                            | Example                                                                        |
| ------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cause   | Condition or weakness contributing to the scenario | Inadequate representation of relevant applicant groups in evaluation data      |
| Event   | What happens                                       | The model produces systematically lower rankings for some qualified applicants |
| Impact  | Resulting consequence                              | Reduced access to employment opportunities                                     |

A cause may be technical, organizational, human, environmental, or related to the deployment context.

An event may arise from model behavior, data quality, a configuration error, misuse, an external attack, or inappropriate human reliance.

An impact describes the consequence for people, the organization, or society—not merely the technical malfunction.

For instance, "incorrect output" may describe an event, while "a patient delays seeking urgent care because of the recommendation" describes a possible impact.

The distinction is essential when designing controls. Improving output validation may address one cause, while escalation procedures and human review may reduce the severity of the resulting impact.

### 18. AI Impact Severity Assessment

Severity assessment estimates how serious an identified impact could be if it occurs.

The assessment should consider the nature of the harm and its consequences, rather than relying solely on financial loss or technical system criticality.

Relevant factors may include:

- Magnitude of harm.
- Duration and reversibility.
- Number of affected people.
- Degree of interference with rights or opportunities.
- Physical or psychological consequences.
- Financial or economic consequences.
- Effects on vulnerable stakeholders.
- Ability to detect and correct the impact.
- Consequences of repeated or cumulative exposure.
- Potential for systemic or widespread effects.

A practical qualitative scale is:

| Rating         | Description                                                                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1 — Negligible | Minimal adverse effect with limited practical consequences                     |
| 2 — Minor      | Limited harm that is readily corrected                                         |
| 3 — Moderate   | Material adverse effect requiring substantive intervention                     |
| 4 — Major      | Serious harm or significant interference with rights, safety, or opportunities |
| 5 — Severe     | Catastrophic, irreversible, widespread, or exceptionally serious harm          |

The scale should be calibrated to the organization's domain and the type of impact being assessed.

A single numerical scale may not adequately represent all impact categories. Severe rights-related or safety-related impacts should not be downgraded simply because their direct financial cost is low.

The assessor should record the rationale, supporting evidence, assumptions, and uncertainty associated with the severity rating.

### 19. AI Impact Likelihood and Plausibility

The assessment should evaluate how plausible an impact scenario is within the intended operating context and reasonably foreseeable conditions.

Likelihood analysis may consider:

- Historical incidents and observed failures.
- Model and system evaluation results.
- Known limitations.
- Data quality.
- Frequency and scale of use.
- Exposure to adversarial inputs or misuse.
- Human behavior and reliance patterns.
- Environmental and operational conditions.
- Control effectiveness.
- Uncertainty about system behavior.
- Changes in the deployment context.

Likelihood should not be assessed solely from the frequency of documented past incidents. Emerging AI systems may have limited operational history, and a lack of recorded events does not establish that an impact is unlikely.

The organization should distinguish evidence-based estimates from expert judgment and explicitly document where data is insufficient.

Where likelihood cannot be estimated reliably, the assessor should record the uncertainty and determine whether precautionary safeguards, additional testing, restricted deployment, or further evidence are required.

### 20. Scale, Scope, and Distribution of Impact

The assessment should evaluate how widely an impact may extend and how its effects may be distributed among affected stakeholders.

Three important dimensions are:

- **Scale:** The number of people, transactions, decisions, or systems that may be affected.
- **Scope:** The range of consequences, rights, services, or activities affected.
- **Distribution:** Whether the impact is concentrated among particular groups or distributed unevenly.

An AI system used for a small number of internal administrative tasks may have a different impact profile from one used across millions of customer interactions.

However, a small population does not automatically imply low severity. A system affecting a single person's safety or fundamental rights may still create a major impact.

The assessment should consider both aggregate effects and concentrated harm.

For example, an automated eligibility system may perform acceptably on average while producing disproportionately harmful outcomes for a particular group. Aggregate accuracy alone would not establish that the system's impacts are acceptable.

### 21. Reversibility and Remediability of Impact

The assessment should determine whether an adverse impact can be prevented, corrected, compensated for, or meaningfully reversed after it occurs.

Relevant questions include:

- Can the affected person be identified?
- Can the incorrect output or decision be corrected?
- Can the affected person appeal or request human review?
- Can financial or other losses be remedied?
- Can disclosed information be recovered or its further use stopped?
- Can a safety-related consequence be reversed?
- Can repeated harm be prevented?
- Is the remediation process accessible and timely?

Some impacts are difficult or impossible to reverse. Examples include irreversible physical injury, disclosure of sensitive information that has been copied, or missed opportunities that cannot realistically be restored.

Low reversibility should influence severity analysis and safeguard design.

Where harm cannot be fully reversed, preventive controls, pre-deployment evaluation, and effective intervention mechanisms become particularly important.

### 22. Cumulative and Systemic Impact

The assessment should consider impacts that emerge through repeated decisions, large-scale deployment, or interactions among multiple AI systems.

A single output may appear low impact while repeated outputs create substantial aggregate consequences.

Examples include:

- Repeatedly disadvantaging a group in automated screening.
- Gradual erosion of human decision-making skills through overreliance.
- Repeated exposure to misleading generated content.
- Multiple AI systems relying on the same flawed data source.
- Large-scale propagation of an incorrect recommendation.
- Concentration of essential services among a small number of AI providers.

Systemic impacts may be difficult to identify through testing of individual transactions alone.

The assessment should consider monitoring at both the individual-case level and the aggregate population or portfolio level.

Where relevant, it should evaluate feedback loops, correlated failures, shared dependencies, and the possibility that several individually acceptable systems collectively produce unacceptable outcomes.

### 23. Uncertainty, Assumptions, and Evidence Limitations

AI Impact Assessments frequently involve incomplete information, uncertain model behavior, evolving technology, and limited operational evidence.

The template should explicitly document uncertainty rather than creating false precision.

Representative fields include:

- Key assumptions.
- Evidence available.
- Evidence missing.
- Confidence in findings.
- Uncertainty about affected populations.
- Limitations of testing.
- Known model limitations.
- Unverified provider claims.
- Alternative plausible scenarios.
- Additional evidence required.
- Conditions for reassessment.

A rating supported by extensive independent evaluation should be distinguished from one based primarily on supplier statements or limited pilot data.

Where uncertainty is material, the organization should determine whether the use case can proceed under restricted conditions or whether further assessment is necessary.

The absence of sufficient evidence should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of low impact.

### 24. Existing Safeguards and Mitigation Measures

The assessment should identify safeguards that prevent impacts, reduce their likelihood, limit their severity, improve detection, or support remediation.

Safeguards may include:

- Data quality and representativeness controls.
- Model evaluation and validation.
- Human review and escalation.
- Access restrictions.
- Output validation.
- Privacy and security controls.
- Fairness testing.
- Accessibility measures.
- Transparency notices.
- Appeal and correction mechanisms.
- Operational monitoring.
- Incident response.
- Deployment restrictions.
- User training.
- Independent oversight.

The assessment must distinguish between existing and proposed safeguards.

A safeguard should not be treated as effective merely because it appears in a policy or technical design document. The assessor should consider whether it is implemented, operating as intended, supported by evidence, and appropriate to the identified scenario.

Where a safeguard is planned but not implemented, the assessment should record the dependency and the conditions that must be satisfied before the system proceeds.

### 25. Residual Impact Evaluation

Residual impact is the impact exposure that remains after considering the safeguards and mitigation measures that are implemented and demonstrably effective.

The assessment should document the expected residual impact and explain why the remaining exposure is considered acceptable, requires additional action, or prevents the proposed use.

Residual impact analysis should consider whether safeguards:

- Address the identified causal pathway.
- Reduce the likelihood of the event.
- Reduce the severity of the consequence.
- Protect affected stakeholders.
- Enable timely detection and intervention.
- Support correction, appeal, or remediation.
- Operate reliably at the expected scale.
- Remain effective under foreseeable misuse or change.

The assessment should not automatically reduce an impact rating because controls have been proposed.

The residual assessment should reflect verified or adequately substantiated safeguards, with explicit treatment of any remaining uncertainty.

If material impacts remain, the organization should document the required decision authority, any operating restrictions, additional mitigation, and monitoring conditions.

### 26. AI Impact Assessment Decision Outcomes

The assessment should lead to an explicit and documented decision.

Possible outcomes include:

| Outcome                 | Meaning                                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Approve                 | The use case satisfies the defined requirements and may proceed                                |
| Approve with conditions | Deployment is permitted only after or subject to specified safeguards and restrictions         |
| Restrict                | The use case may proceed within defined limits, populations, purposes, or operating conditions |
| Defer                   | A decision is postponed until required evidence or mitigation is available                     |
| Redesign                | The use case or system must be materially changed before reconsideration                       |
| Reject                  | The proposed activity is not authorized                                                        |
| Escalate                | The decision requires a higher or specialist authority                                         |

The decision record should identify the approving authority, rationale, assessment version, conditions, unresolved impacts, and required follow-up actions.

Approval must not be inferred from silence, completion of the assessment, or technical readiness.

Where applicable law prohibits the proposed activity or imposes requirements that have not been met, internal risk acceptance cannot override those legal constraints.

### 27. AI Impact Assessment Review and Reassessment Triggers

An approved assessment should remain subject to review when material changes could alter the impact profile.

Reassessment triggers may include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- A change in affected stakeholder groups.
- Introduction of a new model or provider.
- Material changes to training or input data.
- Changes in automation or human oversight.
- Expansion to new jurisdictions.
- Increased deployment scale.
- New external integrations or agent capabilities.
- Significant incidents or near misses.
- Evidence of unequal or unexpected outcomes.
- Material changes in law or applicable standards.
- Loss or failure of a key safeguard.
- A change in the operating environment.

The organization should define which changes require a full reassessment, a targeted review, or a documented determination that the existing assessment remains valid.

A reassessment should preserve the previous decision history while documenting the new evidence, revised impact analysis, and resulting approval conditions.

### 28. AI Impact Assessment Evidence and Auditability

The assessment must preserve sufficient evidence to demonstrate how impacts were identified, analyzed, mitigated, and approved.

Evidence may include:

- System and use-case documentation.
- Stakeholder maps.
- Data and model documentation.
- Evaluation and testing results.
- Fairness and accessibility analyses.
- Privacy and security assessments.
- Expert review records.
- Consultation feedback.
- Mitigation plans.
- Control testing evidence.
- Decision records.
- Monitoring plans.
- Approval and exception records.

The organization should record the source, date, owner, version, and relevance of material evidence.

Assessment conclusions should be traceable to the information and reasoning that support them.

A completed form without sufficient evidence may be administratively complete but substantively inadequate.

### 29. AI Impact Assessment Quality Assurance

The organization should establish quality checks to ensure that assessments are complete, consistent, evidence-based, and suitable for governance decisions.

Quality assurance should examine whether:

- The scope accurately reflects the use case.
- Relevant stakeholder groups have been considered.
- Impact scenarios are specific and plausible.
- Cause, event, and consequence are distinguished.
- Severity and likelihood rationales are documented.
- Uncertainty and evidence limitations are visible.
- Existing and proposed safeguards are distinguished.
- Residual impacts are justified.
- Applicable legal assessment requirements have been considered.
- Material impacts have been escalated appropriately.
- Decision authority is clear.
- Conditions and actions have accountable owners.
- Review and monitoring triggers are defined.

The organization should apply additional review rigor to high-impact use cases and situations involving potentially severe, irreversible, or widespread harm.

Quality assurance should evaluate the substance of the assessment rather than relying only on mandatory-field completion.

### 30. AI Impact Assessment Core Implementation Principle

The AI Impact Assessment Template should establish a repeatable and evidence-based process for understanding how an AI use case may affect individuals, groups, organizations, and society.

Its value lies in connecting system context, stakeholder perspectives, impact scenarios, severity analysis, safeguards, residual exposure, and accountable governance decisions.

The assessment must distinguish between **an AI product and its specific use case**, **an identified impact and a quantified risk score**, **a proposed safeguard and an effective safeguard**, **technical accuracy and acceptable human impact**, and **assessment completion and authorization to deploy**.

**Core Principle:** An AI Impact Assessment is effective when it identifies material consequences before they occur, evaluates those consequences in context, establishes proportionate and verifiable safeguards, and ensures that decisions to proceed are made by authorized stakeholders using documented evidence.

## Part 2 — Assessment Controls, Evaluation Criteria, and Mitigation Requirements

### 31. Assessment Initiation and Mandatory Screening Control

The organization should establish a formal initiation control to ensure that proposed AI use cases are screened before procurement, development, deployment, or material expansion.

The screening process determines whether an AI Impact Assessment is required, what assessment depth is appropriate, and which specialist reviews must be completed.

The control should define:

- Events that trigger screening.
- Roles authorized to initiate an assessment.
- Minimum information required for screening.
- Criteria for determining assessment depth.
- Escalation requirements for uncertain cases.
- Documentation requirements for screening decisions.
- Conditions under which work may proceed before assessment completion.

Screening should be integrated with existing processes such as procurement, project approval, architecture review, privacy assessment, change management, and production release.

A business unit should not be able to avoid an assessment merely by describing an AI-enabled capability as a productivity tool, pilot, proof of concept, or third-party feature.

**Required control evidence:** Screening record, applicability rationale, assessment-depth decision, reviewer identity, and timestamp.

### 32. Assessment Depth and Proportionality Control

The organization should use a documented methodology to determine the depth of assessment required for each AI use case.

Proportionality should reflect the potential consequences of the system, not simply its technical complexity or purchase price.

Assessment depth may be determined by factors such as:

- Potential severity of harm.
- Number and characteristics of affected stakeholders.
- Influence over consequential decisions.
- Degree of automation or autonomy.
- Sensitivity of data processed.
- Reversibility of potential impacts.
- Scale and frequency of deployment.
- Uncertainty about model behavior.
- Exposure to misuse or adversarial manipulation.
- Applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

A practical tiering model may use three levels:

| Tier                 | Indicative characteristics                                                        | Assessment expectation                                                                                    |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tier 1 — Limited     | Low-consequence use, narrow scope, limited affected population                    | Standardized screening and proportionate impact review                                                    |
| Tier 2 — Material    | Meaningful effects on users, business processes, or stakeholder outcomes          | Detailed impact scenarios, specialist review, and documented mitigation                                   |
| Tier 3 — High impact | Potential for serious, widespread, difficult-to-reverse, or rights-sensitive harm | Enhanced assessment, independent challenge, senior decision authority, and explicit deployment conditions |

These tiers are an internal governance design, not a substitute for legally prescribed classifications. A system categorized as Tier 1 internally may still trigger a specific legal assessment requirement.

**Required control evidence:** Tiering methodology, completed classification, supporting rationale, and approval of any departure from the standard tier.

### 33. Mandatory Assessment Fields and Completeness Validation

The assessment workflow should prevent formal approval when mandatory fields or required evidence are missing.

Mandatory information should include, at minimum:

- Assessment ID and version.
- AI system and use-case identifiers.
- Business owner and assessment lead.
- Intended purpose and deployment context.
- Affected stakeholder groups.
- Material impact scenarios.
- Severity and likelihood rationale.
- Applicable legal and policy requirements.
- Existing safeguards and proposed mitigation.
- Residual impact evaluation.
- Outstanding actions and dependencies.
- Governance decision and approval authority.
- Review date and reassessment triggers.

Completeness validation should distinguish between a missing field and a field that has been assessed and determined to be not applicable.

A "not applicable" entry should require a reason where the field concerns a material assessment requirement.

Automated validation may check required fields, evidence references, owner assignments, and approval workflow states. It should not be treated as a replacement for substantive review.

**Required control evidence:** Validation rules, completed assessment, exception records, and workflow audit trail.

### 34. Impact Scenario Quality Control

Each material impact scenario should be sufficiently specific to support evaluation and mitigation.

A scenario should identify:

1. The AI system or activity involved.
2. The condition or cause that could produce the outcome.
3. The affected stakeholder or group.
4. The resulting beneficial or adverse consequence.
5. Relevant operating conditions and assumptions.
6. Available evidence and uncertainty.

The assessment reviewer should challenge generic statements that do not establish a plausible causal pathway.

| Weak scenario                     | Improved scenario                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI could create privacy problems. | A support assistant may disclose personal information from conversation history to an unauthorized user because identity verification is not enforced consistently across connected services. |
| AI could be biased.               | A candidate-ranking model may assign systematically lower scores to qualified applicants whose experience is underrepresented in the evaluation data.                                         |
| AI could make mistakes.           | An AI-generated maintenance recommendation may omit a critical warning, leading a technician to perform an unsafe procedure.                                                                  |

The objective is not to predict every possible event. It is to identify material, reasonably foreseeable outcomes that can be evaluated and managed.

**Required control evidence:** Scenario register, reviewer comments, supporting evidence, and documented scenario acceptance or revision.

### 35. Impact Taxonomy and Classification Consistency

The organization should maintain a common impact taxonomy to enable consistent assessment and enterprise-level reporting.

The taxonomy should define categories, subcategories, rating guidance, and examples relevant to the organization's operating environment.

It should support classification across areas such as:

- Fundamental rights and human dignity.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Fairness and discrimination.
- Safety and well-being.
- Human autonomy and oversight.
- Transparency and contestability.
- Information security.
- Financial and economic consequences.
- Employment and education.
- Operational service delivery.
- Environmental and societal effects.
- Legal and regulatory exposure.

Assessors should be allowed to assign multiple categories to a scenario where appropriate.

The organization should govern taxonomy changes through version control and communicate changes to assessment owners. Historical assessments should retain their original classifications or include a documented mapping to the revised taxonomy.

**Required control evidence:** Approved taxonomy, definitions, version history, mapping rules, and periodic classification-quality reviews.

### 36. Stakeholder Identification and Coverage Control

The assessment process should require systematic identification of stakeholders who may experience direct or indirect effects.

The assessor should consider not only intended users but also individuals subject to AI-supported decisions, people whose information is processed, downstream recipients of AI outputs, and groups indirectly affected by deployment.

The coverage review should ask:

- Have affected individuals and groups been identified?
- Are potentially vulnerable groups considered?
- Could effects differ across populations?
- Are indirect and cumulative impacts relevant?
- Have relevant internal and external stakeholders been considered?
- Is stakeholder engagement necessary?
- If engagement is not undertaken, is the rationale documented?

Stakeholder identification should be proportionate to the use case but should not be limited to groups that are easiest to consult or measure.

Where a stakeholder group cannot be consulted directly, the assessor should identify alternative evidence sources and document the limitation.

**Required control evidence:** Stakeholder map, coverage checklist, consultation record where applicable, and documented limitations.

### 37. Stakeholder Consultation and Engagement Control

For material or high-impact use cases, the organization should determine whether consultation with affected stakeholders or their legitimate representatives is necessary.

Consultation can identify impacts that internal technical and business teams may overlook.

Depending on context, engagement may include interviews, workshops, usability testing, accessibility testing, surveys, representative panels, or consultation through established stakeholder bodies.

The process should define:

- Who should be consulted.
- The purpose and scope of engagement.
- The information participants receive.
- Accessibility and language arrangements.
- Confidentiality and data-handling safeguards.
- How feedback is recorded and analyzed.
- How disagreements are handled.
- How the assessment reflects stakeholder input.

Consultation should be meaningful rather than ceremonial. Feedback should be evaluated and linked to assessment findings, mitigation decisions, or documented reasons for not adopting a recommendation.

**Required control evidence:** Engagement plan, participant or stakeholder-group records where appropriate, feedback analysis, resulting actions, and rationale for disputed findings.

### 38. Impact Severity Rating Control

The organization should define a consistent method for rating the severity of identified impacts.

Severity criteria should reflect the nature and consequence of harm, including its magnitude, duration, reversibility, and implications for affected stakeholders.

The rating methodology should include:

- Defined rating levels.
- Category-specific guidance where needed.
- Examples for consistent interpretation.
- Escalation thresholds.
- Guidance for uncertain or disputed ratings.
- Requirements for independent review of material impacts.

A common rating scale may range from negligible to severe, but the organization should not assume that every impact category can be represented adequately by a single universal measure.

A serious impact on a person's rights or physical safety should not be downgraded because its direct financial cost is limited.

**Required control evidence:** Severity methodology, scenario-level rating rationale, supporting evidence, and reviewer approval.

### 39. Likelihood and Plausibility Rating Control

The organization should define how assessors evaluate the likelihood or plausibility of impact scenarios.

The methodology should account for available empirical evidence, testing results, deployment conditions, model limitations, human behavior, and control effectiveness.

Where numerical estimates are not defensible, qualitative ratings may be more appropriate.

The assessment should distinguish:

- Observed occurrence.
- Evidence-supported probability estimates.
- Expert judgment.
- Hypothetical but plausible scenarios.
- Scenarios whose likelihood cannot currently be estimated.

A lack of incident history should not automatically justify a low rating, particularly for newly deployed systems or systems with limited exposure.

Where uncertainty is material, the assessor should document the uncertainty and consider additional testing, restricted deployment, or precautionary safeguards.

**Required control evidence:** Likelihood methodology, evidence references, assumptions, uncertainty statements, and approval of material judgment calls.

### 40. Scale, Scope, and Distribution Evaluation Control

The assessment should evaluate the potential reach and distribution of each material impact.

The analysis should consider the number of affected individuals, frequency of exposure, geographic reach, downstream dependencies, and the concentration of effects among particular groups.

The organization should not rely exclusively on average system performance or aggregate outcome metrics.

For example, an AI service may achieve high overall accuracy while consistently performing poorly for a smaller stakeholder group. The assessment should examine whether the concentrated impact is material and whether additional safeguards are required.

Where practical, the organization should document the assumptions used to estimate the affected population and the expected duration of exposure.

**Required control evidence:** Scale estimates, affected-population analysis, distributional findings, and supporting assumptions.

### 41. Reversibility and Remediation Evaluation Control

The assessment should explicitly evaluate whether potential impacts can be prevented, corrected, or meaningfully remedied.

The review should consider the availability and effectiveness of appeal mechanisms, correction procedures, human intervention, compensation where appropriate, and restoration of affected services or opportunities.

Impacts that are difficult to reverse require particular attention because corrective action after deployment may be insufficient.

Examples include irreversible physical injury, disclosure of information that cannot realistically be recalled, and missed opportunities that cannot be restored.

The organization should consider reversibility when determining assessment depth, safeguards, deployment restrictions, and escalation requirements.

**Required control evidence:** Reversibility analysis, remediation design, escalation rationale, and identified limitations.

### 42. Cumulative and Systemic Impact Control

The assessment should consider whether repeated use, scale, feedback loops, or shared dependencies could create cumulative or systemic effects.

The review should consider:

- Repeated adverse decisions affecting the same individuals or groups.
- Correlated errors across multiple AI systems.
- Common data or model dependencies.
- Feedback loops that reinforce existing patterns.
- Large-scale reliance on a single AI provider.
- Gradual loss of human expertise or independent judgment.
- Impacts that emerge only after extended deployment.

The organization should determine whether individual-case evaluation is sufficient or whether population-level and portfolio-level analysis is also required.

For material cumulative impacts, the monitoring plan should include suitable aggregate indicators and escalation thresholds.

**Required control evidence:** Cumulative-impact analysis, dependency map where relevant, monitoring design, and documented assumptions.

### 43. Benefit Assessment and Distributional Analysis

The organization should evaluate expected benefits using defined outcomes and, where possible, measurable indicators.

The assessment should identify who benefits, how benefits are expected to arise, and whether benefits are distributed differently across stakeholder groups.

Potential benefits should not be used to dismiss material adverse impacts without a documented and legitimate decision process.

The analysis should distinguish:

- Expected benefits.
- Demonstrated benefits.
- Benefits dependent on unverified assumptions.
- Benefits accruing to the organization.
- Benefits accruing to affected individuals or society.
- Potential trade-offs between groups.

For example, a customer-service AI may reduce handling times for the organization while making service less accessible to customers who need language assistance or human support.

The assessment should determine whether the benefit case remains valid after considering these trade-offs.

**Required control evidence:** Benefit statement, baseline, proposed measures, beneficiary analysis, assumptions, and validation plan.

### 44. Existing Safeguard Validation Control

The assessment should not assume that an existing safeguard is effective merely because it is documented or implemented in principle.

Safeguards should be evaluated against the impact scenario they are intended to address.

The reviewer should determine whether the safeguard:

- Addresses a relevant cause or consequence.
- Is implemented in the actual deployment environment.
- Operates consistently at expected scale.
- Has been tested or independently reviewed where appropriate.
- Has a clear owner.
- Is monitored for degradation.
- Has known limitations.
- Depends on other controls or external parties.

For example, a human-review requirement is not necessarily effective if reviewers lack sufficient time, information, authority, or training to challenge the AI output.

The assessment should record the difference between design effectiveness and operating effectiveness where this distinction is relevant.

**Required control evidence:** Control description, owner, implementation status, test results, limitations, and effectiveness conclusion.

### 45. Proposed Safeguard and Mitigation Planning Control

The assessment should require each material impact needing treatment to be linked to one or more proposed mitigation actions.

The mitigation plan should specify:

- The impact or scenario being addressed.
- The action or safeguard required.
- The accountable owner.
- Required resources and dependencies.
- Target completion date.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Validation method.
- Required evidence.
- Consequences of incomplete implementation.

Mitigation may involve redesigning the system, limiting its purpose, changing the data or model, introducing human review, improving transparency, restricting access, or declining deployment.

The organization should prioritize measures that prevent or reduce harm at its source rather than relying exclusively on warnings or post-event remediation.

**Required control evidence:** Mitigation plan, accountable owner assignments, target dates, acceptance criteria, and validation records.

### 46. Mitigation Effectiveness and Residual Impact Control

The assessment should require a reassessment of material impacts after mitigation measures have been implemented and validated.

The residual assessment should reflect the safeguards that are actually in place and supported by appropriate evidence.

It should not assume that all safeguards eliminate risk or that residual impact can be reduced to zero.

The reviewer should examine whether:

- The original impact pathway remains plausible.
- The likelihood or severity has changed.
- New impacts have been introduced by mitigation.
- Dependencies remain unresolved.
- Evidence supports the revised conclusion.
- Additional restrictions are necessary.
- The remaining exposure falls within approved governance boundaries.

Where a safeguard is incomplete, untested, or dependent on future work, the assessment should identify the limitation and avoid presenting the intended future state as the current state.

**Required control evidence:** Mitigation validation, revised impact ratings, residual-impact rationale, and decision record.

### 47. Human Oversight Effectiveness Control

Where human oversight is used to prevent or reduce impacts, the assessment should evaluate whether the oversight mechanism is meaningful and effective.

The presence of a human in the workflow does not by itself establish that the system is adequately controlled.

The assessment should consider whether the human reviewer has:

- Appropriate competence and training.
- Access to relevant evidence.
- Sufficient time to review outputs.
- Authority to reject or override recommendations.
- Clear escalation procedures.
- An understanding of system limitations.
- Protection against inappropriate pressure to accept AI outputs.
- A means of recording decisions and disagreements.

The review should also consider automation bias, alert fatigue, workload, and the possibility that reviewers may defer to the AI despite having formal decision authority.

**Required control evidence:** Oversight design, role definitions, training records, review procedures, override mechanisms, and testing or evaluation results.

### 48. Transparency, Explainability, and Contestability Control

The assessment should evaluate whether affected stakeholders receive information appropriate to the consequences of the AI system.

Relevant questions include:

- Are stakeholders informed when AI materially influences an interaction or decision, where required or appropriate?
- Can decision-makers understand the system's intended purpose and limitations?
- Can affected individuals obtain an explanation suitable to the context?
- Can incorrect information be corrected?
- Can a consequential outcome be challenged?
- Is there a documented escalation path?
- Are transparency mechanisms accessible to intended users?

The required level of transparency depends on the use case and applicable obligations. A technical explanation intended for an AI engineer may not be sufficient for an individual seeking to understand an adverse decision.

Transparency should support meaningful understanding and action rather than merely disclose that AI is being used.

**Required control evidence:** Transparency requirements, user-facing materials, explanation procedures, appeal mechanisms, and validation records.

### 49. Fairness and Discrimination Impact Control

Where an AI system may influence opportunities, access to services, treatment, or resource allocation, the assessment should evaluate potential unequal outcomes.

The evaluation should consider whether relevant groups may experience different error rates, treatment, access, or consequences.

Potential evidence includes:

- Data representativeness analysis.
- Evaluation results across relevant groups.
- Outcome and error-rate comparisons.
- Domain-expert review.
- Analysis of historical or structural inequalities.
- Feedback from affected stakeholders.
- Procedures for investigating adverse patterns.

The selection of groups and metrics should be justified by the use case and applicable legal requirements. Sensitive characteristics should not be collected or inferred without an appropriate legal basis and safeguards.

A fairness assessment should not assume that one metric establishes fairness in every context. Different fairness objectives may conflict, and the chosen approach should be documented.

**Required control evidence:** Fairness assessment, metric rationale, relevant evaluation results, limitations, mitigation actions, and approval of material residual concerns.

### 50. Privacy and Data Protection Impact Control

The assessment should identify privacy-related impacts arising from the AI system's data collection, use, retention, inference, disclosure, and integration with other systems.

Relevant considerations include:

- Personal data and sensitive data categories.
- Purpose limitation and data minimization.
- Lawful basis where applicable.
- Transparency and individual rights.
- Data accuracy and correction.
- Retention and deletion.
- Access and disclosure.
- Re-identification or inference risks.
- Use of data for training or model improvement.
- International transfers and third-party processing where relevant.

The assessment should identify whether a separate DPIA or other formal privacy review is required and track its completion.

An AI Impact Assessment may incorporate privacy findings, but it should not be assumed to satisfy a DPIA merely because privacy is included as an impact category.

**Required control evidence:** Data-flow information, privacy review, applicable DPIA decision or record, mitigation actions, and approval references.

### 51. Security-Related Impact Control

The assessment should evaluate how cybersecurity weaknesses, malicious activity, or misuse could create adverse impacts for individuals, organizations, or society.

Relevant scenarios may include:

- Unauthorized disclosure of personal or confidential information.
- Prompt injection leading to unintended actions.
- Manipulation of AI outputs or recommendations.
- Abuse of connected tools or privileges.
- Compromise of model or data integrity.
- Denial of essential AI-enabled services.
- Unauthorized access to connected systems.
- Supply-chain compromise.
- Abuse of AI capabilities by authorized users.

The assessment should connect security scenarios to their resulting impacts rather than treating the presence of a technical vulnerability as the final impact.

For example, an unauthorized tool call may be a security event; the impact may be unauthorized disclosure, financial loss, disruption of a service, or harm to an affected individual.

Security findings should be linked to the AI Security Assessment and enterprise risk register as appropriate.

**Required control evidence:** Relevant threat scenarios, security assessment references, control status, mitigation plan, and residual-impact evaluation.

### 52. Safety, Well-Being, and Consequential Decision Control

For use cases that could affect physical safety, mental well-being, essential services, or other consequential outcomes, the assessment should evaluate the potential severity and timing of harm.

The analysis should consider:

- Whether incorrect outputs could cause injury or delay necessary intervention.
- Whether the system may be used outside its validated conditions.
- Whether users can recognize unreliable outputs.
- Whether escalation to a qualified person is available.
- Whether the system fails safely.
- Whether emergency or fallback procedures exist.
- Whether monitoring can detect unsafe behavior promptly.
- Whether foreseeable misuse could create serious consequences.

The organization should determine whether domain-specific safety engineering, clinical review, product safety analysis, or other specialist assessment is necessary.

A generic impact rating should not replace specialist safety analysis where the domain requires it.

**Required control evidence:** Safety scenario analysis, domain-specific review, validation results, fallback design, and deployment restrictions where applicable.

### 53. Accessibility and Inclusion Impact Control

The assessment should evaluate whether the AI system can be used by its intended population and whether it may create barriers for people with disabilities, language needs, limited digital access, or other relevant circumstances.

Considerations may include:

- Compatibility with assistive technologies.
- Availability of alternative interaction methods.
- Language coverage and quality.
- Accessibility of explanations and appeal mechanisms.
- Ability to access human assistance.
- Performance across relevant user groups.
- Risks of excluding people who cannot use the default interface.

Accessibility should be assessed in the context of the actual service and its consequences.

For example, a voice-based service may create barriers for people with speech impairments, while a text-only service may be unsuitable for some users who rely on audio interfaces.

The organization should define reasonable acceptance criteria and document any known limitations or alternative service arrangements.

**Required control evidence:** Accessibility requirements, testing results, stakeholder feedback where available, remediation plan, and documented limitations.

### 54. Labor, Organizational, and Economic Impact Control

Where an AI system affects employment, work allocation, performance evaluation, staffing, or organizational decision-making, the assessment should consider the implications for affected workers and business operations.

Potential impacts include:

- Changes to job responsibilities.
- Increased monitoring or surveillance.
- Inaccurate performance evaluations.
- Unequal access to training or opportunities.
- Work intensification or reduced autonomy.
- Overreliance on automated recommendations.
- Changes in decision accountability.
- Unequal distribution of economic benefits or burdens.

The assessment should consider whether workers or their representatives need to be consulted under applicable law, agreements, or organizational processes.

It should distinguish between documented impacts and speculative long-term possibilities, while recording material uncertainties.

**Required control evidence:** Workforce impact analysis, relevant consultation records, operational change plan, mitigation actions, and accountable owner.

### 55. Third-Party and Supply-Chain Impact Control

The assessment should consider impacts arising from external AI providers, models, datasets, APIs, hosting services, plugins, and other dependencies.

The organization should identify whether it has sufficient information to evaluate the system's behavior, limitations, data handling, and changes over time.

Relevant concerns include:

- Limited visibility into model development or evaluation.
- Unclear data-use or retention practices.
- Changes to model behavior or provider terms.
- External service outages.
- Cross-border data handling.
- Supplier security weaknesses.
- Limited audit or assurance rights.
- Dependency on a single provider.
- Inadequate exit or migration options.

Third-party claims should be evaluated in light of available contractual commitments, technical documentation, independent evidence, and the organization's own testing.

The business owner remains responsible for ensuring that the organization has adequate grounds to approve the intended use, even when the underlying model is externally supplied.

**Required control evidence:** Supplier assessment, contract and data-use review, dependency record, available assurance evidence, and residual-impact analysis.

### 56. Legal and Regulatory Applicability Control

The assessment should include a documented determination of potentially applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, and sector-specific requirements.

The determination should consider the intended purpose, deployment context, affected stakeholders, jurisdiction, and organizational role.

Where relevant, the assessment should identify:

- EU AI Act applicability and role classification.
- Whether a specific AI Act impact-assessment requirement applies.
- GDPR and other applicable data protection requirements.
- Employment, consumer, safety, and sector-specific obligations.
- Relevant contractual commitments.
- Required notifications, consultations, or approvals.
- Applicable recordkeeping and monitoring duties.

Legal applicability should be reviewed by appropriately qualified personnel where necessary.

The assessment should not equate internal impact tiers with regulatory risk categories or infer that an internal approval authorizes an otherwise prohibited activity.

**Required control evidence:** Applicability matrix, legal review, documented conclusions, required assessment references, and outstanding compliance actions.

### 57. Mitigation Prioritization and Action Tracking Control

The organization should prioritize mitigation actions according to the significance of the impact, urgency of intervention, feasibility, dependencies, and applicable legal requirements.

The process should prevent low-priority administrative work from delaying controls needed to address serious or irreversible harm.

Each action should include:

- Action ID.
- Linked assessment and impact scenario.
- Description of the required change.
- Priority and rationale.
- Accountable owner.
- Target date.
- Dependencies.
- Completion criteria.
- Validation evidence.
- Status and escalation path.

The organization should define how overdue or blocked actions are escalated.

An action should not be considered complete solely because the owner reports that work has finished. Where the action is a critical safeguard, the organization should validate implementation and effectiveness before relying on it in the residual-impact assessment.

**Required control evidence:** Mitigation action register, status history, validation evidence, overdue-action reports, and escalation records.

### 58. Deployment Gate and Conditional Approval Control

The organization should establish a formal deployment gate that connects the assessment outcome to the release or operational authorization process.

The gate should verify that:

- Required assessments are complete.
- Material findings have been addressed or escalated.
- Critical safeguards are implemented.
- Residual impacts have been evaluated.
- Required approvals have been obtained.
- Outstanding conditions have accountable owners.
- Monitoring and incident-response arrangements are ready.
- Any restrictions are reflected in the operating environment.

Conditional approval should specify which conditions must be met before deployment and which obligations apply during operation.

Conditions that are prerequisites for safe or lawful operation should not be treated as optional post-deployment tasks.

**Required control evidence:** Deployment-gate checklist, assessment decision, condition register, safeguard validation, and release authorization.

### 59. Assessment Change Management and Version Control

The assessment should be maintained as a controlled record that reflects material changes in the AI system or its operating context.

The organization should define when a change requires:

- A targeted assessment update.
- A new specialist assessment.
- A full impact reassessment.
- Reapproval by the original decision authority.
- Escalation to a higher authority.
- Suspension of deployment pending review.

Version history should preserve previous findings and decisions so that the organization can establish what was known and approved at a given point in time.

Changes to the assessment methodology, impact taxonomy, or rating criteria should also be governed and documented.

**Required control evidence:** Version history, change record, impact analysis, reassessment decision, and updated approval references.

### 60. Assessment Access, Integrity, and Confidentiality Control

The organization should protect impact assessment records against unauthorized access, modification, disclosure, and deletion.

Access should reflect business need, assessment responsibilities, confidentiality requirements, and applicable data protection obligations.

The control should address:

- Role-based access.
- Approval and review permissions.
- Protection of sensitive stakeholder feedback.
- Integrity of assessment findings and decisions.
- Audit logging.
- Secure evidence references.
- Retention and disposal.
- Restrictions on unnecessary personal data.
- Access by external assessors or suppliers.

Assessment records may contain sensitive information about individuals, business processes, security weaknesses, and unresolved governance concerns. Their protection should therefore be considered part of the assessment control environment.

**Required control evidence:** Access-control configuration, permission reviews, audit logs, retention rules, and documented handling procedures.

### 61. Evidence Quality and Traceability Control

The assessment should ensure that material conclusions are supported by evidence appropriate to the nature and significance of the impact.

Evidence should be traceable to the relevant scenario, safeguard, rating, mitigation action, or approval decision.

The reviewer should consider:

- Source reliability.
- Evidence date and version.
- Applicability to the deployed system.
- Independence of the evidence where relevant.
- Completeness and limitations.
- Whether the evidence supports the specific conclusion.
- Whether new evidence invalidates earlier findings.

Supplier documentation may be useful but should not automatically be treated as independent validation.

Where evidence is unavailable, the assessment should record the gap and determine whether the gap prevents approval, requires a restriction, or can be managed through a documented action.

**Required control evidence:** Evidence index, source metadata, scenario-to-evidence mapping, limitation records, and review findings.

### 62. Assessment Quality Review and Independent Challenge

The organization should define when independent review is required and how reviewers should challenge assessment conclusions.

Independent challenge is particularly important where impacts may be severe, difficult to reverse, concentrated among vulnerable groups, or subject to significant uncertainty.

Reviewers should examine whether:

- The scope is sufficiently broad.
- Stakeholder groups are adequately represented.
- Impact scenarios are plausible and specific.
- Rating decisions are justified.
- Mitigation addresses underlying causes.
- Residual impacts are not understated.
- Legal obligations are correctly identified.
- Evidence supports key conclusions.
- Approval authority is appropriate.
- Outstanding actions are clearly defined.

Reviewers should document disagreements and how they were resolved.

Where a material disagreement remains unresolved, the assessment should be escalated under the organization's governance procedures rather than silently adopting the least restrictive interpretation.

**Required control evidence:** Review checklist, reviewer comments, challenge resolution, escalation records, and final review conclusion.

### 63. Exception Management for Assessment Requirements

Exceptions may be needed when a standard assessment requirement cannot be met within the normal process. However, exceptions must not override applicable legal requirements or bypass mandatory safeguards.

The organization should define:

- Which internal requirements may be excepted.
- Who may request an exception.
- Who may approve it.
- What justification and evidence are required.
- Whether temporary restrictions are necessary.
- How long the exception remains valid.
- What compensating measures are required.
- When the exception must be reviewed or closed.

Exceptions should be recorded separately or linked to the assessment so that they remain visible to reviewers and management.

A missing assessment caused by an urgent business deadline should not automatically qualify for an exception.

**Required control evidence:** Exception request, rationale, risk and impact analysis, approval, compensating measures, expiry date, and closure evidence.

### 64. Assessment Closure and Record Retention Control

The organization should define the conditions under which an assessment may be closed, superseded, or archived.

Closure should not erase unresolved impacts, outstanding obligations, or relevant decision history.

The closure record should identify:

- Final assessment status.
- Final decision and approval reference.
- Remaining conditions or obligations.
- Related open mitigation actions.
- Monitoring responsibilities.
- Superseding assessment, if any.
- Retention classification.
- Closure rationale and date.

Retention periods should be determined according to applicable law, regulatory requirements, contractual obligations, litigation or investigation needs, and the organization's records management policy.

The organization should ensure that archived assessments remain retrievable for authorized audit, investigation, and governance purposes.

**Required control evidence:** Closure checklist, final decision record, archive reference, retention rule, and outstanding-action handover.

### 65. Assessment Integration with the AI System Inventory

The AI Impact Assessment should be linked to the authoritative AI System Inventory.

The inventory should identify whether an assessment is required, its current status, the latest approved version, and the relevant review date.

The relationship should support:

- Traceability between systems and assessments.
- Identification of systems with missing or expired assessments.
- Visibility of deployment restrictions.
- Tracking of material changes.
- Reporting of high-impact use cases.
- Reconciliation between inventory and assessment records.

A system should not be marked as fully governed solely because an assessment record exists. Its status should reflect whether the required assessment is complete, current, and approved.

**Required control evidence:** Inventory linkage, reconciliation reports, missing-assessment exceptions, and status-validation results.

### 66. Assessment Integration with the AI Risk Register

Material impact scenarios should be linked to the AI Risk Register when they represent risks requiring enterprise risk treatment or ongoing monitoring.

The relationship should preserve the distinction between an impact, a risk scenario, and a risk rating.

For example, an identified impact on applicants may lead to a risk-register entry describing the conditions under which discriminatory outcomes could occur, the likelihood and severity of the scenario, the controls in place, and the required treatment.

The integration should avoid creating unnecessary duplicate records. A single risk may relate to several impact scenarios, and one impact scenario may contribute to multiple risk records.

The organization should define rules for ownership, identifiers, synchronization, and change propagation.

**Required control evidence:** Impact-to-risk mapping, linked record identifiers, ownership rules, reconciliation results, and change history.

### 67. Assessment Integration with Privacy, Security, and Other Specialist Reviews

The AI Impact Assessment should coordinate with relevant specialist assessments to reduce duplication while preserving their distinct purposes.

Integration points may include:

- DPIA and privacy review.
- AI security assessment.
- Safety and reliability assessment.
- Vendor and supply-chain assessment.
- Architecture review.
- Model validation.
- Human rights review.
- Legal and regulatory compliance review.

The assessment should reference the authoritative specialist record rather than copying conclusions without context.

Where specialist reviews reach conflicting conclusions, the organization should document the conflict, determine which authority is responsible for resolution, and ensure that the final decision reflects all material findings.

**Required control evidence:** Cross-assessment references, dependency tracking, conflict-resolution records, and consolidated decision documentation.

### 68. Assessment Monitoring and Post-Deployment Review Control

The assessment should define how the organization will determine whether actual impacts differ from expected impacts after deployment.

Monitoring may include:

- Complaints and appeals.
- Error and override patterns.
- Unequal outcome indicators.
- Safety incidents and near misses.
- Security incidents.
- Unexpected data use.
- Changes in affected populations.
- User feedback.
- Provider or model changes.
- Degradation of safeguard effectiveness.
- Evidence of cumulative or systemic impacts.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the use case and should include clear thresholds for investigation, escalation, restriction, or suspension.

The organization should assign responsibility for interpreting monitoring results. Collecting metrics without a defined response process is not sufficient.

**Required control evidence:** Monitoring plan, indicator definitions, thresholds, owner assignments, review records, and actions resulting from observed impacts.

### 69. Assessment Metrics and Governance Reporting

The organization should define metrics that measure both the operation of the assessment process and the management of material impacts.

Potential process indicators include:

- Percentage of registered AI use cases screened.
- Percentage of required assessments completed before deployment.
- Percentage of assessments with current approvals.
- Number of overdue material mitigation actions.
- Average time to complete assessments.
- Percentage of high-impact assessments independently reviewed.
- Number of exceptions and overdue exceptions.
- Percentage of assessments with documented stakeholder analysis.
- Number of material post-deployment impact findings.
- Time taken to investigate and resolve material findings.

Metrics should be interpreted carefully. A high assessment-completion rate does not prove that the organization is preventing harm, and a low incident count does not necessarily prove that impacts are absent.

Reporting should combine process measures with outcome indicators, evidence quality, significant findings, and management actions.

**Required control evidence:** Metric definitions, data sources, reporting cadence, trend analysis, and management review records.

### 70. Assessment Deficiency and Corrective Action Control

The organization should maintain a formal process for addressing deficiencies identified through assessment, review, monitoring, audit, or incident investigation.

Deficiencies may include incomplete stakeholder analysis, unsupported ratings, ineffective safeguards, missing approvals, outdated assessments, or unaddressed material impacts.

Each deficiency should include:

- Deficiency identifier.
- Related assessment and impact scenario.
- Description and evidence.
- Significance and priority.
- Root cause where appropriate.
- Corrective action.
- Accountable owner.
- Target date.
- Validation criteria.
- Closure evidence.

Material deficiencies should be escalated according to defined governance thresholds.

Closure should require evidence that the corrective action has addressed the deficiency sufficiently for the intended purpose. Administrative completion alone should not be treated as proof of effectiveness.

**Required control evidence:** Deficiency register, root-cause analysis where applicable, corrective-action plan, validation results, and closure approval.

### 71. Periodic Control Testing and Sampling

The organization should periodically test whether the AI Impact Assessment process operates as designed.

Testing may examine a sample of assessments across business units, impact tiers, system types, and deployment stages.

The test should evaluate both process compliance and substantive quality.

Examples of testing procedures include:

- Verify that applicable use cases were screened.
- Confirm assessment completion before required decision gates.
- Trace selected impact ratings to supporting evidence.
- Verify that material stakeholder groups were considered.
- Confirm that critical safeguards were validated.
- Check that approvals came from authorized roles.
- Verify that restrictions were reflected in deployment conditions.
- Trace post-deployment findings to reassessment decisions.
- Confirm that exceptions have valid approvals and expiry dates.

Sampling should be risk-based. Higher-impact systems and processes with prior deficiencies may require more intensive review.

**Required control evidence:** Test plan, sample selection rationale, workpapers, findings, remediation assignments, and retest results.

### 72. Assessment Automation and Workflow Enforcement

The organization may automate assessment initiation, workflow routing, completeness checks, reminders, evidence collection, approval gates, and reporting.

Automation should improve consistency and traceability without replacing professional judgment in material impact analysis.

Suitable automated controls include:

- Triggering screening when an AI use case is registered.
- Routing assessments based on impact tier.
- Requiring specialist reviews for defined scenarios.
- Preventing release when mandatory approvals are missing.
- Generating reminders for overdue actions.
- Linking assessments to risk and inventory records.
- Maintaining version history and audit logs.
- Flagging changes that may require reassessment.

Automated rules should be tested, version-controlled, and reviewed periodically. Incorrect workflow logic can create systematic governance failures, such as allowing high-impact use cases to bypass review.

**Required control evidence:** Workflow configuration, rule definitions, test results, access controls, audit logs, and change approvals.

### 73. Part 2 Core Implementation Principle

Assessment controls should make the AI Impact Assessment process consistent, evidence-based, traceable, and enforceable across the AI lifecycle.

The organization should ensure that screening, impact identification, stakeholder coverage, rating decisions, safeguards, mitigation, residual impact evaluation, and deployment approval operate as a connected control system.

The assessment must distinguish between **process completion and substantive adequacy**, **impact severity and likelihood**, **existing safeguards and proposed safeguards**, **human presence and effective human oversight**, and **automated workflow enforcement and accountable human judgment**.

**Core Principle:** An AI Impact Assessment is a reliable governance control only when its requirements are consistently applied, its conclusions are supported by evidence, its mitigation actions are validated, and its findings directly influence deployment and operating decisions.

## Part 3 — Governance, Decision Rights, and Enterprise Accountability

### 74. AI Impact Assessment Governance Operating Model

The AI Impact Assessment process should operate within a formally defined governance model that establishes accountability, decision rights, independent challenge, escalation pathways, and ongoing oversight.

The operating model should ensure that impact assessments are not treated as isolated documents completed by project teams solely to satisfy administrative requirements. Instead, assessment findings must influence business decisions, system design, deployment authorization, operational monitoring, and lifecycle management.

The governance model should define how the following functions interact:

- Business ownership and use-case accountability.
- AI system ownership and technical delivery.
- AI governance policy and methodology management.
- Enterprise risk management.
- Cybersecurity and technology risk.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Human resources, safety, and other domain specialists.
- Executive decision-making and risk acceptance.
- Independent assurance and internal audit.

The organization should define the authority of each function and ensure that material impacts cannot be dismissed solely by the business unit seeking to deploy the system.

A practical governance structure is:

```
Board / Executive Leadership
            |
            v
Enterprise Risk and AI Governance Committees
            |
            v
AI Governance Function
            |
            +--------------------+
            |                    |
            v                    v
Business / Use-Case       Specialist Review
Owners                    Functions
            |                    |
            +----------+---------+
                       |
                       v
             Impact Assessment
                       |
                       v
              Decision Authority
                       |
                       v
             Deployment Conditions
                       |
                       v
             Monitoring and Review
                       |
                       v
             Assurance and Reporting
```

The precise structure may differ by organization size, regulatory environment, and risk profile. The essential requirement is that responsibilities and decision authority remain explicit, traceable, and enforceable.

### 75. Three Lines and Independent Oversight

The organization may align its AI Impact Assessment governance with the Three Lines Model to clarify management responsibility, risk oversight, and independent assurance.

The model should be adapted to the organization's existing risk management structure rather than implemented as a separate bureaucracy.

| Line        | Typical role in impact assessment                                                          | Accountability                                                                                    |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line  | Business owners, product owners, AI system owners, and operational teams                   | Identify impacts, implement safeguards, maintain evidence, and operate within approved conditions |
| Second line | AI governance, risk management, privacy, compliance, and cybersecurity oversight functions | Define standards, challenge assessments, monitor compliance, and escalate material concerns       |
| Third line  | Internal audit or another appropriately independent assurance function                     | Independently evaluate the design and effectiveness of the governance process                     |

Specialist functions may have different organizational placements. For example, cybersecurity or privacy may perform operational activities while also providing independent oversight within a defined mandate.

The organization should therefore document functional responsibilities, independence requirements, and potential conflicts rather than assume that a department's name determines its governance role.

Internal audit should not become the operational owner of the assessments it is expected to audit independently.

**Governance requirement:** The organization must be able to demonstrate who performed the assessment, who challenged it, who approved the decision, and who independently evaluated the process where required.

### 76. Executive Accountability for AI Impacts

Executive leadership should establish the conditions under which AI systems may be developed, acquired, deployed, and operated.

Executives should ensure that the organization has sufficient resources, authority, expertise, and reporting mechanisms to identify and manage material impacts.

Executive responsibilities may include:

- Approving the AI governance policy and related risk appetite.
- Defining escalation thresholds for material impacts.
- Assigning accountability for high-impact use cases.
- Ensuring that material risks and impacts receive appropriate attention.
- Resolving significant resource or ownership conflicts.
- Reviewing trends in impact findings and remediation.
- Ensuring that prohibited or unacceptable activities are not authorized.
- Supporting suspension or withdrawal when conditions are no longer acceptable.

Executive accountability does not mean that executives must personally complete technical impact analyses. They must ensure that appropriate assessments are performed and that decisions are made by authorized individuals using reliable evidence.

The organization should define which decisions require executive approval and which may be delegated to an AI governance committee, business executive, or specialist authority.

**Required governance evidence:** Approved policy, delegated authority matrix, executive reporting, decision records, and documented responses to material findings.

### 77. AI Impact Assessment Governance Committee Charter

Where the organization's scale or risk profile justifies it, an AI Governance Committee or equivalent body should oversee material impact assessments and resolve cross-functional issues.

The committee charter should define its mandate, membership, decision rights, meeting cadence, quorum, escalation procedures, and reporting obligations.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Reviewing high-impact or disputed assessments.
- Challenging residual-impact conclusions.
- Reviewing proposed exceptions and deployment restrictions.
- Resolving conflicts between business objectives and governance requirements.
- Monitoring overdue mitigation actions.
- Reviewing recurring impact patterns.
- Determining whether additional specialist analysis is required.
- Escalating matters beyond delegated authority.
- Reviewing material post-deployment impact findings.

Committee membership may include business leadership, AI governance, enterprise risk, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, and relevant domain specialists.

Membership should reflect the nature of the decision. A healthcare use case, for example, may require clinical and patient-safety expertise that would not be necessary for a low-impact internal drafting assistant.

The committee should not approve assessments merely because the required documentation exists. Its review should challenge the evidence, assumptions, proposed safeguards, and acceptability of residual impacts.

**Required governance evidence:** Approved charter, membership and authority records, meeting minutes, decision logs, conflict-of-interest declarations where appropriate, and action tracking.

### 78. Decision Rights and Delegated Authority

The organization should define which roles may approve, restrict, defer, reject, or escalate an AI use case based on its impact profile.

Decision authority should reflect the significance of the potential consequences and the organization's approved risk governance arrangements.

A decision-rights matrix may include:

| Decision type                                     | Typical authority                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Routine, low-impact assessment                    | Delegated business or product authority                      |
| Material impact with manageable residual exposure | Business executive with required specialist concurrence      |
| High-impact or rights-sensitive use case          | Designated senior authority or AI governance committee       |
| Significant unresolved disagreement               | Escalation to a higher governance body                       |
| Proposed exception to internal policy             | Authority explicitly delegated under the exception framework |
| Activity prohibited by applicable law             | Cannot be authorized through internal risk acceptance        |

The organization should distinguish between a recommendation, a specialist concurrence, and the final authorization decision.

A privacy specialist may determine that a particular privacy control is inadequate, while the designated decision authority determines whether the use case must be deferred or redesigned. That decision authority cannot override a binding legal prohibition.

Delegations should identify their scope, limits, conditions, and expiry where appropriate.

**Required governance evidence:** Decision-rights matrix, approval workflow, delegated authority records, and documented escalation outcomes.

### 79. Business Ownership and Accountability for Impact Outcomes

The business owner should remain accountable for the purpose, intended benefits, operational context, and consequences of the AI use case.

Responsibility should not be transferred entirely to the AI governance team, technology provider, or technical system owner.

The business owner should ensure that:

- The use case has a legitimate and clearly defined purpose.
- Expected benefits are documented.
- Affected stakeholders are identified.
- The assessment is initiated and maintained.
- Required safeguards are implemented.
- Operating restrictions are followed.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Monitoring findings receive appropriate action.
- Complaints and adverse outcomes are addressed.
- The system is suspended or withdrawn when required.

The business owner should also ensure that operational staff understand their responsibilities and have a route for reporting unexpected impacts.

Where ownership changes, the organization should formally transfer accountability and confirm that the new owner understands outstanding obligations.

**Required governance evidence:** Named owner, documented responsibilities, acceptance of ownership, operational procedures, and handover records.

### 80. Independence and Objectivity of Assessment Review

The organization should establish review arrangements that reduce the risk of assessors understating impacts because of commercial pressure, delivery deadlines, reputational concerns, or personal involvement in the project.

Independence should be proportionate to the significance of the use case.

Potential safeguards include:

- Independent review of high-impact assessments.
- Separation between assessment preparation and final approval.
- Specialist review of relevant impact categories.
- Escalation of unresolved disagreements.
- Conflict-of-interest declarations where appropriate.
- Direct access to the designated governance authority.
- Protection against inappropriate pressure to alter findings.

Complete organizational separation is not always necessary. A small organization may use an external specialist or a senior reviewer from another function to provide appropriate challenge.

The critical requirement is that material findings can be reported accurately and considered without improper interference.

**Required governance evidence:** Review assignment, reviewer independence rationale, documented challenge, conflict disclosures where applicable, and resolution records.

### 81. Enterprise AI Impact Appetite

The organization should define its general willingness to accept different types and levels of AI-related impact, consistent with its legal obligations, business objectives, ethical commitments, and risk management framework.

Impact appetite should not be expressed solely as a financial loss threshold.

Relevant considerations include:

- Protection of fundamental rights.
- Physical safety and well-being.
- Privacy and confidentiality.
- Fair access to opportunities and services.
- Human autonomy and meaningful oversight.
- Reliability of consequential decisions.
- Acceptable levels of operational disruption.
- Reputational and stakeholder trust.
- Environmental or societal consequences where material.

The organization may establish different expectations for different impact categories. For example, it may accept limited inconvenience arising from a low-impact internal assistant while maintaining a very low tolerance for serious physical harm or unlawful discrimination.

Impact appetite should guide assessment criteria and escalation. It should not replace scenario-level analysis.

Where a proposed use case conflicts with a non-negotiable legal or policy requirement, an appetite statement cannot be used to authorize the conflict.

**Required governance evidence:** Approved appetite statement, category-specific thresholds, decision guidance, and review records.

### 82. Impact Thresholds and Escalation Criteria

The organization should define thresholds that determine when an impact requires specialist review, senior approval, deployment restriction, or rejection.

Thresholds should reflect the characteristics of the impact rather than rely exclusively on a combined numerical score.

Escalation criteria may include:

- Potential for severe or irreversible harm.
- Material effects on fundamental rights.
- Significant effects on vulnerable populations.
- Unresolved discrimination concerns.
- Insufficient evidence for a consequential decision.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Material privacy or security exposure.
- Unresolved legal applicability questions.
- Safeguards that cannot be validated before deployment.
- Significant disagreement between reviewers.
- Potential for widespread or systemic effects.

The organization should define how a single severe impact is handled, even when the average or aggregate assessment score appears low.

Thresholds should be reviewed periodically to determine whether they remain appropriate as technology, regulation, business practices, and organizational experience evolve.

**Required governance evidence:** Threshold methodology, escalation rules, examples, calibration records, and documented threshold-triggered decisions.

### 83. Governance of Residual Impact Acceptance

Residual-impact acceptance should be a controlled decision based on documented evidence, not an automatic consequence of completing mitigation activities.

The decision authority should consider:

- The nature and severity of remaining impacts.
- The likelihood and scale of adverse outcomes.
- The effectiveness of implemented safeguards.
- The uncertainty in the assessment.
- The ability to detect and remedy harm.
- Applicable legal and regulatory constraints.
- The interests of affected stakeholders.
- The availability of safer alternatives.
- The conditions required for continued operation.

The organization should record why the remaining exposure is considered acceptable or why further action is required.

Acceptance should include conditions where appropriate, such as limited deployment, additional human review, enhanced monitoring, restrictions on affected decisions, or mandatory reassessment after a defined period.

Internal acceptance must not be used to waive a legal obligation, excuse a prohibited practice, or bypass a mandatory assessment.

**Required governance evidence:** Residual-impact decision, supporting analysis, approval authority, conditions, review date, and linked risk acceptance record where applicable.

### 84. Segregation of Duties and Conflict Management

The organization should separate responsibilities where combining them could weaken assessment quality or compromise decision integrity.

Potential conflicts include a project sponsor approving their own high-impact assessment without independent challenge, or a supplier controlling both the evidence and the conclusion regarding its own system's impacts.

Segregation requirements may include:

- Different individuals preparing and approving material assessments.
- Independent validation of critical safeguards.
- Specialist review of sensitive impact categories.
- Separate approval for policy exceptions.
- Independent review of disputed findings.
- Clear boundaries between delivery ownership and assurance.

The level of segregation should be proportionate to the organization's size and risk profile.

Where full segregation is impractical, the organization should document compensating safeguards, such as external review, senior oversight, or retrospective assurance.

**Required governance evidence:** Role assignment, conflict assessment, approval workflow, compensating controls, and documented review.

### 85. Escalation of Material or Unresolved Impacts

The organization should define a formal escalation process for material impacts that cannot be adequately addressed at the assessment team's level.

Escalation triggers may include:

- Potential severe or irreversible harm.
- Unresolved disagreement over impact significance.
- Inadequate or conflicting evidence.
- Failure to implement a critical safeguard.
- A proposed exception beyond delegated authority.
- A material legal or regulatory uncertainty.
- A significant incident or unexpected impact.
- A decision that could affect many stakeholders.
- A finding that conflicts with organizational policy or appetite.

The escalation process should specify the receiving authority, required evidence, expected response time, interim restrictions, and documentation requirements.

Pending resolution, the organization should determine whether work may continue, must be restricted, or must be suspended.

The urgency of escalation should reflect the potential consequences and timing of exposure.

**Required governance evidence:** Escalation criteria, case record, interim decision, authority response, and final resolution.

### 86. Governance of Mitigation Prioritization

The organization should ensure that mitigation priorities reflect the significance of impacts and the urgency of preventing harm.

Prioritization should not be determined solely by cost, delivery convenience, or the ease of completing an action.

Relevant factors include:

- Severity and reversibility of impact.
- Number and characteristics of affected stakeholders.
- Likelihood and duration of exposure.
- Strength of existing safeguards.
- Legal or contractual deadlines.
- Dependencies between mitigation actions.
- Availability of safer alternatives.
- Potential for harm before completion.

Where multiple actions compete for limited resources, the responsible governance authority should document the prioritization rationale.

Mitigation that is essential to lawful or safe operation should be treated as a deployment prerequisite, not merely assigned a high project-management priority.

**Required governance evidence:** Prioritization criteria, action register, resource decisions, overdue-action escalation, and approval records.

### 87. Governance of Mitigation Dependencies

Some mitigation actions depend on other teams, suppliers, platforms, or organizational decisions.

The assessment should identify dependencies that could delay implementation or undermine the effectiveness of the proposed safeguard.

Examples include:

- A human-review control that depends on hiring and training staff.
- A privacy safeguard that depends on a supplier changing its data-retention practices.
- An access restriction that depends on identity-management integration.
- A fairness evaluation that depends on obtaining appropriate and lawful evaluation data.
- A monitoring control that depends on reliable logging.
- A safe fallback procedure that depends on maintaining an alternative service.

The organization should identify the owner of each dependency, its status, and the consequences if it is not delivered.

Where a critical dependency remains unresolved, the governance authority should determine whether deployment must be delayed or restricted.

**Required governance evidence:** Dependency register, owner assignments, delivery commitments, validation criteria, and decision records for unresolved dependencies.

### 88. Shared AI Platforms and Common Impact Dependencies

The organization should evaluate whether a shared AI platform, model, data source, or service could create similar impacts across multiple use cases.

Shared dependencies can increase the scale of harm and make several business units vulnerable to the same failure.

Examples include:

- A shared model producing systematically misleading outputs.
- A central retrieval system exposing confidential information.
- A common data pipeline introducing inaccurate records.
- A shared identity integration permitting excessive access.
- A central provider changing model behavior across multiple services.

Governance should determine which impacts are assessed centrally and which require use-case-specific evaluation.

A platform-level assessment can identify common risks, but it does not eliminate the need to assess the distinct stakeholders, decisions, and consequences associated with each deployment.

**Required governance evidence:** Shared-dependency inventory, platform-level assessment, use-case mappings, common control ownership, and cross-system escalation procedures.

### 89. Third-Party Provider Accountability

The organization should define how accountability is maintained when AI capabilities are provided by an external supplier.

Provider contracts and assurance evidence may support the assessment, but they do not automatically transfer the organization's responsibilities for its own deployment decisions.

Governance arrangements should address:

- Provider documentation and limitations.
- Intended and prohibited uses.
- Data processing and retention.
- Changes to models and services.
- Security and incident notification.
- Audit and assurance rights where available.
- Subprocessor and supply-chain dependencies.
- Performance and impact monitoring.
- Remediation responsibilities.
- Service exit and migration.

The organization should determine what evidence it needs from the supplier and what validation it must perform independently.

Where a provider cannot supply sufficient information, the organization should document the resulting uncertainty and determine whether the use case can proceed with restrictions or should be rejected.

**Required governance evidence:** Supplier assessment, contractual review, assurance documentation, provider-change records, and approved limitations.

### 90. Governance of Model and Provider Changes

Changes to a model or provider may alter the AI system's outputs, stakeholder effects, and safeguard requirements.

The organization should define which changes require notification, review, reassessment, and reapproval.

Potential triggers include:

- A model version update.
- A change in model hosting.
- New training or fine-tuning data.
- Changes to system prompts or guardrails.
- Changes to retrieval sources.
- New external tools or integrations.
- Changes to data retention or processing.
- Material changes in model behavior.
- Changes to contractual or service conditions.

The governance process should distinguish routine changes from those capable of materially altering impacts.

Where provider updates occur without adequate notice or documentation, the organization should consider additional monitoring, controlled rollout, regression testing, restrictions, or alternative-provider arrangements.

**Required governance evidence:** Change notifications, impact analysis, regression results, reassessment records, and reapproval decisions.

### 91. Governance of Agentic AI and Delegated Actions

AI agents may plan tasks, invoke tools, interact with external services, and execute multi-step actions. Their impact assessment should therefore consider both individual outputs and the consequences of delegated actions.

Governance should establish boundaries for:

- Permitted goals and tasks.
- Available tools and permissions.
- Human authorization requirements.
- Transaction and spending limits.
- Access to sensitive data.
- Actions affecting individuals or external parties.
- Logging and action traceability.
- Emergency stop and revocation.
- Recovery from unintended actions.
- Escalation when the agent encounters uncertainty.

The assessment should consider how a sequence of individually reasonable actions could produce an unacceptable aggregate outcome.

For example, an agent authorized to update customer records might make a series of changes that collectively deny service to a customer, even if each individual action appears technically valid.

The organization should require stronger restrictions and oversight when an agent can create external effects that are difficult to reverse.

**Required governance evidence:** Agent capability and permission inventory, action boundaries, approval rules, test results, logs, and emergency intervention procedures.

### 92. Human Oversight Governance and Accountability

The organization should define who is responsible for human oversight and what authority that person or function possesses.

Oversight responsibilities should not be assigned generically to "the business" or "the human operator" without identifying the relevant role.

The governance model should specify:

- Which outputs require review.
- Which decisions must remain with an authorized person.
- Required competence and training.
- Access to information needed for review.
- Authority to override or stop the system.
- Escalation procedures.
- Documentation requirements.
- Monitoring of override patterns and review quality.

The organization should assess whether the assigned oversight model is feasible in practice, including staffing levels, workload, and operational pressures.

Where human review is a critical safeguard, the organization should monitor whether it is performed effectively rather than merely confirming that a review step exists in the workflow.

**Required governance evidence:** Oversight charter or procedure, role assignments, training records, review metrics, override records, and effectiveness evaluation.

### 93. Governance of Fairness and Stakeholder Harm

The organization should define who is accountable for reviewing material fairness concerns and how decisions are made when outcomes differ across stakeholder groups.

The process should require documented analysis of the affected groups, relevant evidence, potential causes, and the consequences of unequal outcomes.

Governance should address:

- Selection of relevant fairness measures.
- Legal and contextual considerations.
- Review of data and model limitations.
- Stakeholder consultation where appropriate.
- Escalation of material disparities.
- Approval of mitigation measures.
- Monitoring after deployment.
- Reassessment when the population or context changes.

A decision to accept a disparity should not be made solely by the technical team or by the business owner whose objectives depend on deployment.

The organization should ensure that fairness conclusions are reviewed by appropriately qualified personnel and are consistent with applicable law and organizational policy.

**Required governance evidence:** Fairness review record, selected measures and rationale, findings, mitigation decisions, and authorized approval.

### 94. Governance of Privacy and Data Protection Impacts

The organization should establish clear responsibility for privacy-related findings within the AI Impact Assessment.

Privacy governance should coordinate with the Data Protection Officer or other designated privacy function where applicable, while preserving the independence and responsibilities established by law.

Governance should address:

- Identification of personal data processing.
- Lawful basis and purpose limitation.
- Data minimization and retention.
- Transparency and individual rights.
- Privacy risk mitigation.
- DPIA screening and completion where required.
- Supplier and cross-border processing issues.
- Escalation of unresolved privacy concerns.
- Monitoring of changes in data use.

Where a DPIA is legally required, its completion and findings should be tracked as a distinct obligation even when its analysis is coordinated with the broader AI Impact Assessment.

The governance authority should not treat a general AI approval as proof that all privacy obligations have been fulfilled.

**Required governance evidence:** Privacy review, DPIA applicability decision, relevant assessment records, mitigation status, and privacy sign-off or consultation record where appropriate.

### 95. Governance of Security-Related Impacts

The organization should ensure that security-related impact scenarios are reviewed by qualified cybersecurity personnel and linked to the relevant security and enterprise risk processes.

Governance should distinguish between the technical severity of a vulnerability and the consequences that exploitation could create for affected stakeholders.

For example, a vulnerability in an AI agent's tool authorization mechanism may enable unauthorized actions. The associated impact could include financial loss, disclosure of confidential information, disruption of services, or harm to individuals.

The governance process should establish:

- Security review requirements.
- Criteria for escalation of material findings.
- Ownership of remediation.
- Conditions for deployment authorization.
- Treatment of unresolved critical findings.
- Incident and near-miss reporting.
- Reassessment after material security changes.

Security acceptance should follow the organization's approved risk authority and applicable legal requirements.

**Required governance evidence:** Security assessment references, remediation decisions, risk linkage, deployment restrictions, and approval records.

### 96. Governance of Regulatory Obligations and Impact Assessments

The organization should establish a formal process for determining whether the AI use case triggers specific legal assessment, consultation, documentation, or approval requirements.

The process should identify the relevant legal regime, the organization's role, the applicable trigger, the accountable reviewer, and the required evidence.

For EU deployments, relevant considerations may include:

- The applicable provisions of the EU AI Act.
- Whether the use case falls within a regulated AI-system category.
- Whether a specific fundamental rights impact assessment is required.
- GDPR DPIA obligations.
- Sector-specific rules and other applicable legal requirements.

The organization should not assume that every AI use case is subject to the same regulatory assessment requirements.

For example, Article 27 of the EU AI Act establishes a fundamental rights impact assessment requirement for specified deployers of certain high-risk AI systems. Its applicability depends on the relevant legal conditions; it should not be treated as a universal requirement for every AI deployment.

Where a specific assessment is required, the organization should ensure that the appropriate process is completed and that its results inform the overall deployment decision.

**Required governance evidence:** Legal applicability analysis, role classification, assessment references, required approvals, and compliance tracking.

### 97. Enterprise Impact Concentration and Portfolio Governance

The organization should assess whether its portfolio of AI systems creates concentrated or correlated impacts that are not visible when each use case is reviewed separately.

Portfolio-level analysis may identify:

- Several systems affecting the same stakeholder group.
- Multiple use cases relying on the same model or provider.
- Shared data-quality weaknesses.
- Common failures in human oversight.
- Concentrated dependence on AI-enabled essential services.
- Repeated adverse outcomes across business units.
- Aggregate exposure to a single technology or supplier.

The organization should establish a mechanism for aggregating material findings from individual assessments.

Portfolio governance should distinguish between common causes, correlated scenarios, and independent impacts, because each may require a different response.

Where common dependencies create significant concentration, management may need to strengthen shared controls, diversify providers, limit deployment, or prioritize remediation across multiple systems.

**Required governance evidence:** Portfolio impact reports, dependency analysis, concentration indicators, common remediation plans, and management decisions.

### 98. Governance of Emerging and Previously Unidentified Impacts

AI systems may produce impacts that were not anticipated during initial assessment because of changing user behavior, new integrations, evolving model capabilities, or previously unavailable evidence.

The organization should establish mechanisms for identifying and escalating emerging impacts.

Potential information sources include:

- User complaints.
- Appeals and disputed decisions.
- Incident and near-miss reports.
- Operational monitoring.
- Model evaluation and red-team findings.
- Supplier notices.
- External research and relevant regulatory guidance.
- Feedback from affected stakeholders.
- Findings from other business units.

The process should distinguish genuinely new impact scenarios from changes in the likelihood or severity of previously identified scenarios.

Material new findings should trigger reassessment, mitigation, and governance review as appropriate.

The organization should also ensure that lessons learned from one AI system are shared with other systems that may have similar architectures, data, stakeholders, or operating conditions.

**Required governance evidence:** Emerging-impact register, source records, triage decisions, reassessment triggers, and documented lessons learned.

### 99. Governance of Incidents, Near Misses, and Lessons Learned

AI-related incidents and near misses should feed directly into the impact assessment lifecycle.

An incident may reveal that an assumed safeguard is ineffective, that an impact scenario was incomplete, or that actual consequences are more serious than previously estimated.

The governance process should define how incident findings influence:

- Impact scenario descriptions.
- Severity and likelihood ratings.
- Control effectiveness conclusions.
- Residual-impact evaluations.
- Deployment restrictions.
- Monitoring thresholds.
- Assessment methodology.
- Similar use cases and shared platforms.

Near misses should also be examined because they may reveal weaknesses before significant harm occurs.

The organization should preserve the distinction between incident investigation and impact assessment. Incident investigation establishes what happened and why; the assessment evaluates what the event means for the current and future acceptability of the use case.

**Required governance evidence:** Incident linkage, root-cause findings, updated impact analysis, corrective actions, and records of decisions affecting deployment.

### 100. Governance of Organizational and Operational Change

The organization should assess whether changes in people, processes, responsibilities, or operating conditions alter the expected impacts of an AI system.

Relevant changes may include:

- New business owners.
- Restructuring of operational teams.
- Changes to human review arrangements.
- Reduced staffing or training.
- New user populations.
- Expansion into new jurisdictions.
- Changes in service delivery.
- Changes to the fallback process.
- Changes in accountability for decisions.

A system may retain the same model and technical architecture while its impact profile changes materially because the surrounding process has changed.

For example, a human-review safeguard may become ineffective if workload increases and reviewers can no longer examine recommendations adequately.

The organization should therefore link impact reassessment to organizational change management, not only to technical release management.

**Required governance evidence:** Change records, impact screening, updated ownership and operating procedures, and reassessment decisions.

### 101. Governance of Assessment Methodology Changes

The organization should control changes to the assessment methodology, rating scales, impact taxonomy, evidence requirements, and approval thresholds.

Methodology changes may be necessary when the organization learns from incidents, adopts new standards, enters new markets, or encounters emerging forms of AI.

The change process should define:

- The reason for the change.
- The approving authority.
- The effective date.
- The affected assessments and business units.
- Training and communication requirements.
- Migration or remapping rules.
- Treatment of assessments completed under earlier versions.
- The need for retrospective reassessment.

Historical assessments should remain interpretable. A methodology update should not silently change the meaning of a previous rating or approval.

Where a new methodology identifies previously unrecognized material impacts, the organization should determine whether affected systems require reassessment.

**Required governance evidence:** Methodology version history, change approval, impact analysis, communications, and reassessment decisions.

### 102. Governance of Suspension, Withdrawal, and Retirement

The organization should establish criteria for suspending, restricting, withdrawing, or retiring an AI use case when its impact profile becomes unacceptable or its required conditions are no longer met.

Potential triggers include:

- A material incident.
- Failure of a critical safeguard.
- Discovery of a previously unidentified severe impact.
- Loss of a required legal or operational condition.
- Significant provider or model change.
- Unresolved harmful outcomes.
- Inability to monitor material impacts.
- Expiry of a required authorization.
- A governance decision that the use case is no longer acceptable.

The process should identify who may order suspension, how affected stakeholders are protected, how the system is placed into a safe state, and how services continue where necessary.

Retirement should include consideration of residual data, records, integrations, model access, and outstanding stakeholder obligations.

**Required governance evidence:** Suspension criteria, decision authority, incident or reassessment reference, transition plan, and closure record.

### 103. Board-Level Reporting on AI Impacts

The organization should provide senior governing bodies with reporting appropriate to their oversight responsibilities.

Board-level reporting should focus on material exposures, systemic patterns, significant decisions, and the effectiveness of governance—not merely the number of forms completed.

Potential reporting elements include:

- Number and distribution of material AI use cases.
- High-impact assessments and unresolved findings.
- Significant residual impacts.
- Material incidents and near misses.
- Overdue critical mitigation actions.
- Significant stakeholder or fairness concerns.
- Exceptions and restrictions.
- Major provider or platform dependencies.
- Changes in regulatory exposure.
- Material decisions to defer, restrict, or reject deployment.
- Trends in impact outcomes and governance maturity.

Reporting should identify matters requiring board attention or decisions.

The organization should avoid relying on aggregate scores that conceal severe individual impacts or concentrated harm to particular groups.

**Required governance evidence:** Board or committee reports, minutes, decision records, escalation logs, and follow-up actions.

### 104. Management Reporting and Operational Oversight

Management reporting should provide sufficient detail for accountable owners to operate the assessment process and remediate identified deficiencies.

Operational reporting may include:

- Assessments awaiting evidence or approval.
- Mitigation actions approaching their deadlines.
- Overdue high-priority actions.
- Assessments requiring reassessment.
- Systems operating under restrictions.
- Repeated findings across business units.
- Material changes in stakeholder outcomes.
- Exceptions approaching expiry.
- Missing or stale assessment records.
- Outstanding specialist reviews.

Management should use these reports to assign resources, resolve dependencies, escalate blocked actions, and confirm that deployment conditions are being followed.

The reporting cadence should reflect the significance and rate of change of the organization's AI portfolio.

**Required governance evidence:** Management dashboard, action-tracking records, meeting decisions, and evidence of follow-up.

### 105. Independent Assurance and Continuous Improvement

The organization should periodically evaluate whether its AI Impact Assessment governance model is appropriately designed and operating effectively.

Assurance should examine more than procedural compliance. It should assess whether the process identifies material impacts, produces defensible conclusions, leads to effective safeguards, and influences deployment decisions.

Assurance activities may include:

- Independent assessment sampling.
- Review of high-impact decisions.
- Testing of workflow enforcement.
- Examination of mitigation effectiveness.
- Analysis of incident and complaint trends.
- Review of exception management.
- Assessment of stakeholder engagement quality.
- Verification of reassessment triggers.
- Comparison of documented assumptions with observed outcomes.

Findings should be assigned owners and tracked to closure.

The organization should use assurance results to improve assessment criteria, governance responsibilities, training, technical controls, and decision processes.

Continuous improvement should preserve accountability: a recurring process deficiency should lead to a controlled change in the operating model rather than repeated reliance on individual assessors to compensate for a weak system.

**Required governance evidence:** Assurance plan, testing results, findings, corrective actions, retest records, and approved methodology or governance updates.

### Part 3 Core Implementation Principle

AI Impact Assessment governance must connect the assessment process to organizational authority, independent challenge, legal accountability, deployment decisions, and ongoing oversight.

The governance model should make it clear who owns the use case, who evaluates material impacts, who may approve the decision, who can escalate concerns, and who verifies that the process remains effective.

The organization must distinguish between **business ownership and independent oversight**, **specialist advice and final decision authority**, **risk appetite and legal permissibility**, **documented approval and effective safeguards**, and **individual-system assessment and enterprise-wide impact governance**.

**Core Principle:** AI Impact Assessment governance is effective when material impacts cannot be bypassed through unclear ownership, commercial pressure, or procedural completion, and when every consequential decision is made by an authorized party using evidence, accountable safeguards, and transparent escalation.

### 107. System-of-Record Design

The organization should designate an authoritative system of record for AI Impact Assessments.

The system of record may be an enterprise GRC platform, a dedicated AI governance application, or a controlled internal solution. The choice should reflect assessment volume, integration requirements, regulatory obligations, security needs, and operational maturity.

The system of record should provide:

- Unique assessment identifiers.
- Version-controlled assessment records.
- Controlled lifecycle statuses.
- Relationship mapping to AI systems and use cases.
- Evidence references.
- Approval and decision history.
- Mitigation action tracking.
- Reassessment history.
- Access control and audit logging.
- Retention and archival support.

If the organization uses multiple tools, it must explicitly identify which system owns each data element. For example, the AI inventory may own the system identifier, while the impact assessment platform owns the assessment decision and version history.

The architecture should avoid uncontrolled duplication of authoritative records. Copies used for reporting should be identifiable as derived records and should not become competing sources of truth.

**Acceptance criteria:** Every approved assessment can be uniquely identified, retrieved, traced to its evidence, and associated with the correct AI system and decision history.

### 110. Assessment Taxonomy and Rules Engine

The architecture should provide a controlled mechanism for maintaining impact categories, rating criteria, escalation thresholds, and assessment-routing rules.

The rules engine may determine whether a use case requires additional review based on characteristics such as:

- Impact tier.
- Intended purpose.
- Affected stakeholder groups.
- Potential severity.
- Degree of automation.
- Relevant data-processing activities.
- Regulatory applicability.
- Unresolved uncertainty.
- Required specialist reviews.

Rules should be explicit, version-controlled, tested, and approved by the appropriate governance authority.

The system should distinguish between a deterministic workflow rule and an analytical recommendation. For example, a configured rule may require privacy review when specified conditions are met, while an AI-generated suggestion about an impact category should remain subject to appropriate human validation.

A rules engine must not automatically infer that a legally required assessment is unnecessary unless the relevant applicability determination is made through an authorized and sufficiently reliable process.

**Acceptance criteria:** Every enforced rule has a documented purpose, owner, version, test result, and approval record.

### 111. Impact Rating and Scoring Architecture

The scoring architecture should support consistent assessment without implying that complex human impacts can always be reduced to a single numerical score.

The system may store separate dimensions such as severity, likelihood, scale, reversibility, confidence, and uncertainty.

Where the organization uses a composite score, it should document the formula, assumptions, thresholds, limitations, and intended use.

For example:

```

A composite score should not conceal a severe individual impact behind a favorable average. The architecture should support mandatory escalation for defined impact conditions regardless of the overall score.

Where ratings are based on expert judgment rather than empirical estimates, the system should record the basis and confidence of that judgment.

**Acceptance criteria:** Ratings are reproducible under the same methodology, exceptions are traceable, and the scoring mechanism cannot silently bypass defined escalation thresholds.

### 113. Integration Architecture and Data Exchange

The assessment platform should integrate with enterprise systems that initiate, inform, approve, or monitor AI use cases.

Potential integrations include:

- AI System Inventory.

Integrations should support error handling, reconciliation, retries where appropriate, and traceable updates. A failed integration must not silently create the appearance that an assessment has been updated when the authoritative record remains unchanged.

### 114. API Security and Integration Integrity

APIs and service integrations should be protected against unauthorized access, data manipulation, replay, and unintended disclosure.

The security design should address:

- Strong service authentication.
- Least-privilege authorization.
- Encryption in transit.
- Input validation.
- Schema validation.
- Rate limiting where appropriate.
- Secure secret management.
- Audit logging.
- Error handling that avoids sensitive information disclosure.
- Integrity and consistency of assessment updates.
- Monitoring of anomalous integration activity.

Write operations affecting ratings, approvals, or decision status should receive stronger authorization controls than ordinary reporting queries.

Where asynchronous integration is used, the platform should detect duplicate, delayed, or out-of-order events and reconcile them safely.

**Acceptance criteria:** Integration tests demonstrate that unauthorized updates are rejected, sensitive data is appropriately protected, and failed or inconsistent exchanges can be detected and resolved.

### 115. Identity, Access Control, and Segregation of Duties

The platform should enforce role-based or attribute-based access according to the organization's identity and access management standards.

Access should distinguish among activities such as:

- Creating an assessment.
- Editing impact scenarios.
- Reviewing specialist findings.
- Approving mitigation.
- Accepting residual impact.
- Approving exceptions.
- Changing rating methodologies.
- Managing platform configuration.
- Viewing sensitive evidence.
- Exporting assessment data.

The architecture should prevent users from acquiring approval authority merely because they can edit an assessment.

Privileged administrative access should be restricted, logged, and periodically reviewed.

Where an individual has multiple roles, the platform should enforce applicable segregation-of-duties rules and require compensating review when full separation is impractical.

**Acceptance criteria:** Access tests confirm that users can perform only authorized actions, approval permissions are controlled, and sensitive administrative activities are auditable.

### 116. Evidence Repository and Provenance Architecture

The platform should maintain traceable links between assessment conclusions and supporting evidence.

Evidence may be stored directly in the assessment platform or in an approved document repository. Where evidence is stored externally, the assessment should reference a stable identifier and preserve sufficient metadata to establish which version supported the decision.

Evidence metadata should include, where appropriate:

- Evidence ID.
- Source system or document.
- Evidence owner.
- Creation or collection date.
- Version.
- Relevant assessment or scenario.
- Classification and access restrictions.
- Validation status.
- Retention requirement.
- Supersession or expiry status.

The system should distinguish between evidence that supports an assertion and evidence that merely describes the system.

For example, a provider statement that a model was tested for fairness is not equivalent to receiving the relevant evaluation results and determining whether they apply to the intended deployment.

**Acceptance criteria:** Material conclusions can be traced to the supporting evidence, and reviewers can identify the version and provenance of evidence used in an approval decision.

### 117. Versioning and Historical Decision Traceability

The assessment architecture should preserve the history of material changes to the assessment, including changes to impact scenarios, ratings, evidence, safeguards, and approval decisions.

Version control should support the reconstruction of the assessment state at the time a decision was made.

The platform should record:

- Previous and current values for material changes.
- The identity of the person or service making the change.
- Timestamp.
- Change rationale.
- Related change request.
- Approval where required.
- Impact on the assessment decision.

An updated assessment should not overwrite the only record of a previous approved version.

Where a methodology changes, the system should preserve the methodology version used for each assessment and explain how historical results relate to later versions.

**Acceptance criteria:** Authorized reviewers can reconstruct prior assessment versions and determine which evidence and findings supported each formal decision.

### 118. Privacy-by-Design and Data Minimization

The assessment platform itself should follow privacy-by-design principles because it may contain stakeholder feedback, sensitive impact descriptions, and personal or confidential information.

The implementation should determine what personal information is necessary to document impacts and avoid collecting details that are not required for the assessment purpose.

Controls should address:

- Data minimization.
- Purpose limitation.
- Role-based visibility.
- Separation of identifying information where appropriate.
- Retention and deletion.
- Secure sharing.
- Access logging.
- Handling of stakeholder consultation records.
- Data-subject rights where applicable.
- Restrictions on secondary use.

The platform should not automatically collect sensitive personal data merely because the assessment concerns fairness or vulnerable groups. Any such collection must be justified and handled under applicable legal and organizational requirements.

**Acceptance criteria:** Data fields are justified, access is controlled, retention rules are defined, and privacy review findings have been addressed.

**Acceptance criteria:** Recovery tests demonstrate that essential records and workflow configurations can be restored within defined objectives and that recovered data preserves integrity and traceability.

### 122. Retention, Archival, and Secure Disposal Architecture

The platform should enforce approved retention and archival rules for assessment records and supporting evidence.

The system should distinguish between active assessment records, superseded versions, closed assessments, and records subject to legal or investigative preservation.

Retention should not be indefinite by default, nor should records be deleted merely because the associated AI system has been retired.

**Acceptance criteria:** Retention and disposal rules are approved, tested, auditable, and consistently applied to both primary records and linked evidence.

**Acceptance criteria:** Automated workflows are tested against normal, exceptional, and failure scenarios, and their outputs remain subject to appropriate governance controls.

Monitoring should be proportionate and privacy-preserving. The organization should not collect excessive personal data merely to create a larger monitoring dataset.

**Acceptance criteria:** Material monitoring signals can trigger investigation, reassessment, or escalation, and resulting actions are traceable to the relevant assessment record.

- Recovery and restoration testing.

Representative test cases should include both expected behavior and negative scenarios.

For example, a test should verify not only that an authorized reviewer can approve an assessment, but also that an unauthorized user cannot approve it by modifying a request or bypassing the user interface.

Testing should be repeated after material changes to the platform, methodology, workflow, or integration architecture.

**Acceptance criteria:** Test results are documented, material defects are resolved or formally addressed, and required approval is obtained before production use.

### 127. DevSecOps and Configuration Change Management

Where the assessment platform is developed or configured internally, changes should be managed through a controlled delivery process.

The process should include:

- Version control for application code and configuration.
- Peer review.
- Automated testing.
- Security testing.
- Controlled deployment.
- Environment separation.
- Change approval.
- Rollback capability.
- Configuration drift monitoring.
- Release documentation.

Changes to assessment rules, impact taxonomies, scoring logic, or approval workflows should receive governance review in addition to technical review.

A technically successful release may still create a governance defect if it weakens a mandatory approval gate or changes the interpretation of existing assessment data.

**Acceptance criteria:** Production changes are traceable, tested, authorized, and reversible where technically feasible, with governance-sensitive changes subject to the appropriate additional approval.

### 128. Scalability, Performance, and Resilience

The platform should support expected assessment volumes, evidence sizes, user concurrency, integration throughput, and reporting demands.

Scalability considerations include:

- Growth in AI system registrations.
- Multiple assessments per use case.
- Large evidence repositories.
- Increased specialist-review workload.
- Cross-region or multi-business-unit deployments.
- Integration with enterprise GRC platforms.
- Audit and reporting queries.
- High-volume reassessment triggers.

Performance problems can create governance weaknesses if they delay review, discourage evidence capture, or encourage teams to bypass formal workflows.

The organization should establish performance targets based on actual requirements and test the platform under representative workloads.

Resilience should include monitoring, failure recovery, and safe handling of unavailable dependencies.

**Acceptance criteria:** Performance and resilience tests demonstrate that the platform supports the approved operating scale without weakening required governance controls.

### 129. Implementation Cost and Resource Model

The organization should estimate the total cost of implementing and operating the assessment capability.

The cost model should include:

- Platform licensing or development.
- Configuration and integration.
- Data migration.
- Security and privacy controls.
- Specialist review capacity.
- Training and change management.
- Evidence storage.
- Monitoring and reporting.
- Ongoing maintenance.
- Assurance and testing.
- Regulatory or methodology updates.

The organization should also estimate the internal effort required to complete assessments at different impact tiers.

A low-cost platform may still be operationally expensive if the assessment process requires extensive manual coordination. Conversely, sophisticated automation may not be justified for a small portfolio with limited assessment volume.

The design should optimize total lifecycle cost without compromising mandatory assessment requirements or the quality of material impact decisions.

**Implementation deliverable:** Cost model, resource assumptions, operating model, funding approval, and periodic cost review.

### 130. Implementation Roadmap and Maturity Stages

The organization should implement the AI Impact Assessment capability in controlled stages.

A practical roadmap is:

**Implementation deliverable:** Approved roadmap, milestone plan, dependency register, pilot results, and go-live criteria.

### 131. Key Performance Indicators and Key Risk Indicators

The organization should establish measures that assess both the operation of the assessment process and the outcomes it is intended to support.

**Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** may include:

- Percentage of required assessments completed before deployment.
- Average time to complete an assessment by tier.
- Percentage of assessments with complete evidence references.
- Percentage of required specialist reviews completed.
- Percentage of mitigation actions completed by their due dates.
- Percentage of reassessments completed within policy deadlines.

**Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** may include:

- Number of high-severity impacts without validated safeguards.
- Number of systems operating with overdue reassessments.
- Number of material impact-related incidents.
- Number of repeated adverse findings across use cases.
- Number of expired exceptions.
- Number of unresolved critical mitigation actions.
- Number of systems with material uncertainty that exceeds defined thresholds.

The organization should define each metric's calculation, data source, owner, reporting cadence, and escalation threshold.

Metrics should not encourage teams to minimize reported impacts merely to improve performance indicators. Reporting quality and the appropriate escalation of material concerns should be recognized as positive governance behavior.

**Acceptance criteria:** Metrics are defined consistently, supported by reliable data, reviewed periodically, and linked to management action.

### 137. Continuous Improvement and Maturity Assessment

The organization should periodically assess the maturity and effectiveness of its AI Impact Assessment capability.

The maturity review should consider:

- Coverage of AI use cases.
- Consistency of assessment quality.
- Stakeholder engagement effectiveness.
- Reliability of impact ratings.
- Safeguard implementation and validation.
- Timeliness of mitigation.

Improvement initiatives should be prioritized according to material deficiencies and expected governance benefit.

**Acceptance criteria:** Periodic maturity reviews produce documented findings, assigned improvement actions, target dates, and follow-up validation.

### 138. Final Implementation Acceptance Criteria

The AI Impact Assessment capability should be accepted for operational use only when the organization can demonstrate that the process is governed, secure, traceable, and operationally effective.

Final acceptance should confirm that:

2. Ownership and decision rights are approved.
3. Impact scenarios and rating criteria are consistently defined.
4. Specialist review requirements are operational.
5. Material findings can be escalated.
6. Mitigation actions are tracked and validated.

Acceptance should be based on demonstrated control effectiveness, not merely the delivery of documentation or successful completion of a software implementation project.

**Required deliverable:** Formal implementation acceptance record identifying the approved scope, test evidence, outstanding limitations, authorized signatories, and conditions for continued operation.

### 139. Final Architecture and Governance Principle

The AI Impact Assessment capability should function as an integrated enterprise governance mechanism rather than a standalone questionnaire or document repository.

Its architecture must connect stakeholder analysis, impact scenarios, evidence, safeguards, residual-impact decisions, deployment conditions, and post-deployment monitoring while preserving clear ownership and independent oversight.

The implementation must distinguish between **workflow completion and governance authorization**, **a documented safeguard and a validated safeguard**, **a numerical score and a defensible impact judgment**, **a technical platform and an effective governance capability**, and **initial approval and continuing authorization under changing conditions**.

**Core Principle:** A mature AI Impact Assessment capability is successful when the organization can demonstrate not only that impacts were assessed, but also that material findings changed decisions where necessary, safeguards were validated, affected stakeholders were considered, and the system remains subject to accountable oversight throughout its lifecycle.
