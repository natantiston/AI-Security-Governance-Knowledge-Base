# 20.01 — AI Governance Policy Template

## Part 1 — AI Governance Policy Template Foundations

### 1. Purpose of the AI Governance Policy Template

The AI Governance Policy establishes the organization's mandatory governance requirements for the responsible, secure, compliant, and risk-based use and management of Artificial Intelligence (AI).

The policy should provide the authoritative governance foundation for:

- AI system development;
- AI acquisition;
- AI deployment;
- AI operation;
- AI monitoring;
- AI risk management;
- AI security;
- AI privacy;
- AI compliance;
- AI third-party management;
- AI incident management;
- AI assurance; and
- AI system retirement.

The policy should establish **what the organization requires**, while standards, procedures, guidelines, and technical controls define **how those requirements are implemented**.

### 2. Purpose of an AI Governance Policy

An AI Governance Policy should ensure that AI is managed through a consistent enterprise framework rather than through independent decisions made by individual business units, developers, or technology teams.

The policy should establish:

- Governance authority
- Accountability
- Decision rights
- Risk ownership
- AI lifecycle requirements
- Control expectations
- Compliance responsibilities
- Security responsibilities
- Oversight mechanisms
- Assurance requirements
- Exception management

The policy should apply regardless of whether AI is developed internally, acquired commercially, consumed through an API, embedded within another product, or provided through a third-party service.

### 3. AI Governance Policy as an Enterprise-Level Control

The AI Governance Policy should function as a top-level governance control.

It should establish the minimum requirements from which subordinate controls and operational processes are derived.

```text id="aig20p1-01"
Enterprise Strategy
        │
        ▼
AI Governance Policy
        │
        ├── AI Governance Standard
        ├── AI Security Standard
        ├── AI Risk Management Standard
        ├── AI Privacy Requirements
        ├── AI Lifecycle Procedures
        ├── AI Assessment Procedures
        └── AI Technical Controls
                │
                ▼
          AI Operations
```

The policy should therefore be formally approved, communicated, periodically reviewed, and enforced.

### 4. AI Governance Policy Scope

The policy scope should define which AI activities, systems, people, processes, technologies, and third parties are covered.

The scope may include:

- Traditional machine-learning systems
- Generative AI
- Large language models
- Foundation models
- AI-enabled applications
- AI agents
- Predictive analytics
- Computer vision
- Natural-language processing
- Recommendation systems
- Automated decision systems
- AI APIs
- Internally developed AI
- Third-party AI services
- Embedded AI functionality

The scope should cover AI across development, testing, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement.

### 5. Organizational Applicability

The policy should apply to all organizational functions that develop, acquire, use, operate, govern, secure, assess, or oversee AI.

Potentially applicable functions include:

- Executive management
- Business units
- IT
- Cybersecurity
- Data management
- Legal
- Compliance
- Privacy
- Risk management
- Internal audit
- Procurement
- Human resources
- Engineering
- Data science
- AI development teams
- Product management
- Third-party management

The organization should explicitly identify whether contractors, consultants, temporary workers, and external service providers are subject to applicable policy requirements.

### 6. AI Governance Policy Authority

The policy should identify the authority under which it is issued.

Authority may derive from:

- Board oversight
- Executive management
- Enterprise risk governance
- Information security governance
- Legal and regulatory obligations
- Corporate governance requirements
- Organizational policies
- Contractual obligations

The policy should specify the authority responsible for approving the policy and the authority responsible for interpreting it.

### 7. AI Governance Policy Ownership

A designated policy owner should be accountable for maintaining the AI Governance Policy.

The policy owner may be:

- Chief Information Security Officer
- Chief Risk Officer
- Chief Data Officer
- Chief AI Officer
- AI Governance Officer
- Responsible AI function
- Enterprise Risk function

The organization should avoid ambiguous ownership.

The policy owner should be responsible for ensuring that the policy remains aligned with:

- Business strategy
- AI risk
- Regulatory requirements
- Security requirements
- Privacy requirements
- Enterprise governance
- Technology changes

### 8. AI Governance Accountability

Policy ownership does not mean that the policy owner is responsible for every AI decision.

Accountability should be distributed across the organization.

Typical accountability may include:

- Executive leadership — enterprise accountability
- AI governance body — governance oversight
- Business owner — use-case accountability
- AI system owner — system accountability
- Risk owner — risk accountability
- Security — security oversight
- Privacy — privacy oversight
- Legal/compliance — regulatory interpretation
- Internal audit — independent assurance

The policy should distinguish **accountability**, **responsibility**, **consultation**, and **approval authority**.

### 9. AI Governance Decision Rights

The policy should establish who has authority to make material AI governance decisions.

Decision rights may include:

- Approving AI use cases
- Approving high-risk AI
- Approving AI exceptions
- Accepting residual risk
- Approving production deployment
- Approving material model changes
- Approving third-party AI providers
- Suspending AI systems
- Authorizing emergency AI use
- Approving policy exceptions

Decision rights should be explicit and documented.

### 10. AI Governance Committee

The organization should establish an appropriate governance body for AI oversight.

Depending on organizational size and complexity, this may be:

- AI Governance Committee
- Responsible AI Committee
- AI Risk Committee
- AI Security and Governance Council
- Enterprise AI Steering Committee

The committee should have sufficient authority, expertise, and organizational representation to make or escalate material AI decisions.

### 11. AI Governance Committee Responsibilities

The governance body may be responsible for:

- Reviewing AI risk
- Approving governance standards
- Reviewing high-risk AI
- Reviewing material AI incidents
- Monitoring compliance
- Reviewing exceptions
- Reviewing assurance results
- Monitoring emerging AI risks
- Escalating significant issues
- Reviewing AI governance metrics
- Approving major governance decisions

The committee should not replace operational ownership.

### 12. AI Governance Principles

The policy should establish a concise set of enterprise AI governance principles.

Typical principles include:

1. Accountability
2. Risk proportionality
3. Security by design
4. Privacy by design
5. Transparency
6. Explainability where appropriate
7. Human oversight
8. Fairness and non-discrimination
9. Data governance
10. Regulatory compliance
11. Third-party accountability
12. Continuous monitoring
13. Auditability
14. Resilience
15. Responsible innovation

The principles should be translated into enforceable requirements.

### 13. Risk-Based AI Governance

AI governance should be proportional to the risk presented by each AI system or use case.

Risk factors may include:

- Intended purpose
- Business criticality
- Regulatory classification
- Impact on individuals
- Decision-making authority
- Data sensitivity
- Security exposure
- Model capability
- Autonomy
- External connectivity
- Geographic deployment
- Third-party dependency

Higher-risk AI should receive stronger governance requirements.

### 14. AI Risk Classification

The organization should establish a standardized AI risk-classification methodology.

A classification model may include:

- Low risk
- Moderate risk
- High risk
- Critical risk

The classification should be based on defined criteria rather than subjective judgment alone.

Risk classification should determine the level of:

- Assessment
- Approval
- Security control
- Human oversight
- Monitoring
- Assurance
- Documentation
- Review frequency

### 16. AI Use-Case Registration

All organizational AI use cases should be registered through an approved process.

Registration should capture sufficient information to determine:

- Business purpose
- AI capability
- Business owner
- System owner
- Data involved
- Intended users
- Risk classification
- Regulatory relevance
- Third-party involvement
- Deployment environment

Unregistered AI should not be permitted to operate as an undisclosed enterprise capability.

### 17. AI System Inventory Requirement

The organization should maintain an authoritative inventory of AI systems.

The inventory should include, where applicable:

- AI system identifier
- System name
- Business owner
- Technical owner
- Purpose
- AI type
- Model
- Provider
- Data classification
- Risk classification
- Regulatory classification
- Deployment status
- Geographic scope
- Lifecycle status
- Assessment status
- Approval status

The inventory should be periodically reconciled against the actual technology environment.

### 18. AI Governance Registration Boundary

The policy should define what constitutes an AI system or AI use case that requires registration.

The boundary should consider:

- Internally developed AI
- Embedded AI
- SaaS features containing AI
- Public AI services
- AI APIs
- Experimental AI
- Personal productivity AI
- Shadow AI
- AI agents
- AI-enabled automation

A narrow definition can create significant governance blind spots.

### 19. Shadow AI Governance

The policy should address unauthorized or unmanaged AI usage.

Shadow AI may arise when employees use:

- Public generative AI tools
- Unapproved AI APIs
- Consumer AI applications
- Personal AI accounts
- AI browser extensions
- AI-enabled SaaS functionality
- Unapproved autonomous agents

The policy should establish requirements for:

- Discovery
- Reporting
- Risk evaluation
- Restriction
- Approval
- Monitoring
- Remediation

### 20. AI Approval Requirement

AI systems should require appropriate approval before entering defined lifecycle stages.

Approval requirements should be risk-based.

For example:

Approval should be documented and traceable.

### 21. AI Prohibited Use

The policy should identify AI activities that are prohibited or restricted.

Prohibited activities may include:

- Unauthorized processing of sensitive information
- Circumvention of security controls
- Unauthorized automated decisions
- Unapproved external disclosure
- Use of unapproved AI services for restricted information
- Deployment without required assessment
- Deliberate bypass of governance requirements
- Use of AI contrary to applicable law

Prohibited-use requirements should be clear enough to be operationally enforceable.

### 22. AI Restricted Use

Some AI activities may not be absolutely prohibited but should require additional controls.

Examples include:

- Processing sensitive information
- High-impact decision support
- Autonomous actions
- External-facing AI
- Regulated use cases
- AI involving minors or vulnerable populations
- AI with significant financial impact
- AI connected to privileged systems

Restricted use should trigger enhanced governance requirements.

### 23. AI Human Oversight Requirement

The policy should establish when human oversight is required.

Human oversight should be proportional to:

- AI risk
- Decision impact
- AI autonomy
- Potential harm
- Regulatory requirements
- Reversibility of decisions
- Availability of human intervention

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely nominal.

### 24. AI Accountability for Automated Decisions

Where AI contributes to decisions affecting individuals, organizations, customers, employees, or critical business processes, the organization should establish clear accountability for the resulting decision.

The policy should define:

- Decision owner
- AI role
- Human role
- Escalation process
- Review mechanism
- Override capability
- Documentation requirements
- Appeal or challenge mechanism where applicable

AI should not become an accountability gap.

### 25. AI Documentation Requirements

AI systems should maintain documentation proportionate to their risk and complexity.

Documentation may include:

- Business purpose

### 26. AI Governance Evidence

AI governance activities should generate sufficient evidence to demonstrate compliance with policy requirements.

Evidence may include:

- Approvals

- Testing results
- Monitoring records
- Audit reports
- Exception approvals
- Risk acceptance
- Incident records
- Remediation evidence

### 27. AI Security Governance Requirement

AI governance should integrate security requirements throughout the AI lifecycle.

Security governance should address:

- Identity and access
- Data security
- Model security
- Application security
- Infrastructure security
- API security
- Supply-chain security
- Prompt security
- AI-specific threats
- Monitoring
- Incident response
- Resilience

Security should be treated as an integral AI governance requirement rather than a separate downstream activity.

### 28. AI Privacy Governance Requirement

Where AI processes personal or sensitive information, privacy requirements should be integrated into governance.

Requirements may include:

- Data minimization
- Purpose limitation
- Lawful processing
- Data classification
- Retention
- Access control
- Privacy impact assessment
- Data subject rights
- Data protection measures
- Cross-border transfer controls

Privacy requirements should be established before material AI deployment.

### 29. AI Regulatory Compliance Requirement

The organization should identify and manage applicable AI regulatory obligations.

The governance process should consider:

- Applicable jurisdictions
- Regulatory classification
- AI-specific legislation
- Data protection requirements
- Sector-specific requirements
- Consumer protection
- Employment requirements
- Contractual obligations

Regulatory applicability should be documented and periodically reassessed.

### 30. AI Third-Party Governance

Third-party AI services should be subject to appropriate governance requirements.

The organization should evaluate:

- Provider risk
- Service capabilities
- Security controls
- Privacy practices
- Data use
- Data retention
- Model dependencies
- Subprocessors
- Contractual obligations
- Assurance evidence
- Incident obligations
- Change notification

Third-party use does not transfer ultimate organizational accountability.

### 31. AI Procurement Governance

AI procurement should include governance requirements before contracts are finalized.

Procurement processes should consider:

- AI functionality
- Security requirements
- Privacy requirements
- Regulatory obligations
- Audit rights
- Data ownership
- Data retention
- Model training use
- Subprocessors
- Incident notification
- Business continuity
- Exit requirements

AI governance should begin during procurement rather than after implementation.

### 32. AI Development Governance

Internally developed AI should be subject to defined governance requirements.

Development governance may include:

- Approved development environments
- Secure development practices
- Data governance
- Model governance
- Security testing
- Privacy assessment
- Documentation
- Peer review
- Model validation
- Approval gates

Development teams should not independently determine all governance requirements.

### 33. AI Deployment Governance

Production deployment should require confirmation that applicable governance requirements have been satisfied.

Deployment gates may verify:

- Risk assessment
- Security assessment
- Privacy assessment
- Testing
- Approval
- Monitoring
- Documentation
- Incident response
- Human oversight
- Operational ownership

Deployment should be blocked when mandatory requirements have not been satisfied unless an authorized exception exists.

### 34. AI Change Governance

Material changes to AI systems should trigger appropriate reassessment.

Material changes may include:

- Model replacement
- Model retraining
- Major prompt changes
- Dataset changes
- Architecture changes
- Provider changes
- New integrations
- New jurisdictions
- New use cases
- Increased autonomy
- New sensitive data

The organization should define thresholds for determining materiality.

### 35. AI Continuous Monitoring

AI governance should include continuous monitoring proportionate to risk.

Monitoring may include:

- Model performance
- Security events
- Access activity
- Data-quality issues
- Policy violations
- AI incidents
- Control failures
- Regulatory changes
- Provider changes
- Material system changes

Monitoring should feed governance decisions rather than merely produce operational dashboards.

### 36. AI Incident Governance

The policy should establish governance requirements for AI incidents.

An AI incident may involve:

- Security compromise
- Privacy breach
- Harmful output
- Significant model failure
- Unauthorized AI action
- Data leakage
- Regulatory violation
- Material bias
- Service disruption
- AI supply-chain compromise

The policy should define escalation, reporting, investigation, remediation, and lessons-learned requirements.

### 37. AI Exception Management

Exceptions to AI governance requirements should be formally controlled.

Each exception should document:

- Requirement being waived
- Business justification
- Risk assessment
- Compensating controls
- Exception owner
- Approval authority
- Expiration date
- Review frequency
- Residual risk

Permanent undocumented exceptions should not become informal policy.

### 38. AI Risk Acceptance

Residual AI risk should only be accepted by an authorized risk owner.

Risk acceptance should identify:

- Risk
- Cause
- Potential impact
- Likelihood
- Existing controls
- Residual risk
- Acceptance rationale
- Risk owner
- Approval authority
- Expiration or review date

Risk acceptance should not eliminate the underlying risk or automatically establish regulatory compliance.

### 39. AI Assurance and Auditability

The AI governance framework should support independent audit and assurance.

AI systems should maintain sufficient evidence to allow authorized assessors to evaluate:

- Governance compliance
- Control effectiveness
- Risk management
- Security
- Privacy
- Regulatory compliance
- Operational performance
- Incident management
- Third-party controls

Auditability should be designed into AI governance rather than added after deployment.

### 40. Core Principle of the AI Governance Policy Template

The AI Governance Policy should establish the authoritative enterprise requirements for governing AI throughout its lifecycle.

The policy should create a clear chain of accountability:

```text id="aig20p1-04"
AI Strategy
    │
    ▼
AI Governance Policy
    │
    ▼
AI Governance Requirements
    │
    ▼
Risk Classification
    │
    ▼
Assessment
    │
    ▼
Approval
    │
    ▼
Deployment
    │
    ▼
Monitoring
    │
    ▼
Change / Reassessment
    │
    ▼
Retirement
```

The fundamental governance separations remain:

- **Policy ≠ Standard**
- **Policy ≠ Procedure**
- **Governance ≠ Operations**
- **Accountability ≠ Responsibility**
- **Risk ownership ≠ Control ownership**
- **Approval ≠ Implementation**
- **AI registration ≠ AI governance**
- **Documentation ≠ Effective governance**
- **Monitoring ≠ Assurance**
- **Risk acceptance ≠ Risk elimination**
- **Third-party responsibility ≠ Organizational accountability**
- **Human oversight ≠ Human involvement without authority**
- **Compliance claim ≠ Demonstrated compliance**
- **Exception approval ≠ Permanent exemption**

**Core Principle:**

> **The AI Governance Policy should establish clear, enforceable, risk-based requirements that define who is accountable for AI, what governance controls are mandatory, when approval is required, how AI risks are managed, and how governance remains effective throughout the AI lifecycle.**

> ## Part 2 — AI Governance Policy Template Controls

### 41. AI Governance Policy Control Framework

The AI Governance Policy should be supported by a defined control framework that translates governance principles into mandatory, testable requirements.

Each policy requirement should identify:

- Control objective
- Control requirement
- Control owner
- Applicable AI systems
- Risk addressed
- Evidence requirement
- Control frequency
- Monitoring method
- Exception process
- Assurance requirement

The control framework should allow policy compliance to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

### 42. AI Governance Policy Approval Control

The policy should be formally approved by an authorized governance authority before becoming effective.

Approval should establish:

- Approver
- Approval date
- Effective date
- Policy version
- Review date
- Policy owner
- Approval authority
- Superseded version

Draft policy content should not be treated as an enforceable governance requirement.

### 43. AI Governance Policy Version-Control Control

Every policy version should have a unique identifier and controlled revision history.

Version control should capture:

- Version number
- Change date
- Change description
- Change owner
- Reviewer
- Approver
- Effective date
- Superseded version

Historical versions should remain available where required for audit and regulatory purposes.

### 44. AI Governance Policy Review Control

The policy should be reviewed periodically and when significant changes occur.

Review triggers may include:

- New AI regulation
- Major organizational change
- New AI technologies
- Material AI incidents
- Major audit findings
- Changes to enterprise risk
- Changes to AI strategy
- Changes to security requirements
- Changes to privacy requirements

Periodic review should not replace event-driven review.

### 45. AI Governance Policy Communication Control

The organization should communicate the policy to all relevant personnel.

Communication should be targeted according to role and responsibility.

Relevant personnel may include:

- AI developers
- Business owners
- System owners
- Security teams
- Risk teams
- Procurement
- Legal
- Privacy
- Compliance
- Executives
- Contractors

Communication records should be retained where appropriate.

### 46. AI Governance Policy Acknowledgment Control

Personnel with material AI responsibilities should acknowledge applicable governance requirements.

Acknowledgment mechanisms may include:

- Policy acceptance
- Annual attestation
- Role-based training
- Governance certification
- Electronic acknowledgment

Acknowledgment demonstrates awareness but does not by itself demonstrate compliance.

### 47. AI Governance Training Control

The organization should provide role-appropriate AI governance training.

Training should address relevant requirements for:

- AI users
- AI developers
- AI administrators
- AI owners
- Risk owners
- Security personnel
- Privacy personnel
- Procurement personnel
- Executives
- Governance committee members

Training should be updated when material policy changes occur.

### 48. AI Governance Competency Control

Individuals performing specialized AI governance activities should possess appropriate competency.

Competency requirements may apply to:

- AI risk assessment
- AI security assessment
- Model risk
- Privacy assessment
- Regulatory analysis
- AI auditing
- AI assurance
- AI architecture
- AI incident management

Competency should be evaluated based on role requirements rather than job title alone.

### 49. AI Governance Role Definition Control

Roles involved in AI governance should be formally defined.

The governance framework should identify:

- Role
- Responsibilities
- Authority
- Decision rights
- Escalation responsibilities
- Required competencies
- Independence requirements

Ambiguous roles create governance gaps.

### 50. AI Governance RACI Control

The organization should maintain a RACI or equivalent responsibility model for material AI governance activities.

The RACI should distinguish:

- Responsible
- Accountable
- Consulted
- Informed

Activities may include:

- AI registration
- Risk classification
- Security assessment
- Privacy assessment
- Vendor assessment
- Approval
- Deployment
- Monitoring
- Incident response
- Exception approval
- Risk acceptance
- Audit
- Assurance
- Retirement

### 51. AI Governance Segregation-of-Duties Control

Material AI governance decisions should maintain appropriate segregation of duties.

The same individual should not unnecessarily control the entire chain from:

     ↓

     ↓

Where separation is impractical, compensating controls should be documented.

### 52. AI Governance Conflict-of-Interest Control

Personnel participating in material AI governance decisions should disclose relevant conflicts of interest.

Potential conflicts may arise when an individual:

- Owns the AI business outcome
- Approves their own controls
- Performs independent assurance over their own work
- Has financial interests in a provider
- Has conflicting organizational responsibilities

Conflicts should be evaluated and managed.

### 53. AI Governance Escalation Control

The policy should establish defined escalation paths for material AI governance issues.

Escalation triggers may include:

- High-risk AI
- Significant security incidents
- Material privacy events
- Regulatory violations
- Major control failures
- Unresolved exceptions
- Unacceptable residual risk
- Significant model failures
- Material provider incidents

Escalation should specify both the trigger and the destination.

### 54. AI Governance Decision-Record Control

Material AI governance decisions should be documented.

Decision records should include:

- Decision
- Decision date
- Decision maker
- Relevant AI system
- Risk considered
- Evidence reviewed
- Alternatives considered
- Conditions imposed
- Residual risk
- Follow-up requirements

This creates an auditable governance trail.

### 55. AI Risk Assessment Control

AI systems should undergo documented risk assessment before defined lifecycle milestones.

The assessment should consider:

- Business impact
- Security risk
- Privacy risk
- Regulatory risk
- Operational risk
- Model risk
- Third-party risk
- Human impact
- Reputational risk

Risk assessments should be updated following material changes.

### 56. AI Risk Classification Validation Control

AI risk classifications should be reviewed and validated by appropriately authorized personnel.

Validation should confirm:

- Classification criteria were applied
- Relevant risks were considered
- Regulatory factors were evaluated
- Material impacts were identified
- Classification is consistent with organizational methodology

High-risk classifications should receive enhanced governance.

### 57. AI Inventory Accuracy Control

The organization should periodically validate the completeness and accuracy of its AI inventory.

Validation may compare the inventory against:

- Cloud environments
- Application inventories
- Procurement records
- SaaS inventories
- Model registries
- Data platforms
- API usage
- Security telemetry
- Development repositories

Inventory discrepancies should be investigated.

### 58. AI Inventory Ownership Control

Every registered AI system should have an identified owner.

Ownership should include:

- Business owner
- Technical owner
- Risk owner where applicable
- Security responsibility
- Lifecycle responsibility

An AI system without accountable ownership should not be considered adequately governed.

### 59. AI Use-Case Approval Control

AI use cases should undergo documented approval before entering defined stages of implementation.

Approval should confirm:

- Business purpose
- Risk classification
- Applicable controls
- Regulatory considerations
- Data considerations
- Security requirements
- Required oversight
- Ownership

### 60. AI High-Risk Approval Control

High-risk AI should require enhanced approval.

Enhanced approval may include review by:

- AI Governance Committee
- Risk function
- Security
- Privacy
- Legal
- Compliance
- Independent assurance

Approval criteria should be documented.

### 61. AI Production Deployment Control

AI systems should not enter production unless mandatory governance requirements have been satisfied.

Deployment authorization should verify:

- Required assessments completed
- Risks addressed
- Controls implemented
- Monitoring enabled
- Ownership established
- Incident procedures available
- Required approvals obtained

Deployment exceptions should be formally approved.

### 62. AI Change Assessment Control

Material AI changes should trigger reassessment.

The change-control process should determine whether a change affects:

- Risk classification
- Security controls
- Privacy requirements
- Regulatory classification
- Model behavior
- Data processing
- Human oversight
- Third-party dependencies

### 63. AI Model Change Control

Model changes should be subject to controlled approval where they may materially affect risk.

Relevant changes include:

- New model
- New model version
- Retraining
- Fine-tuning
- Parameter changes
- Model provider change
- Model architecture change

The change record should identify the assurance impact.

### 64. AI Data Change Control

Material changes to AI data should be governed.

Changes may include:

- New datasets
- Dataset replacement
- New data sources
- Data classification changes
- Significant distribution changes
- New personal data
- New geographic sources
- New retention requirements

The organization should determine whether the change requires reassessment.

### 65. AI Prompt and Configuration Change Control

For generative AI and agentic systems, material changes to prompts, system instructions, policies, configurations, tools, or guardrails should be governed where they can affect system behavior or risk.

The organization should define materiality thresholds.

### 66. AI Provider Change Control

Changes to an AI provider or material provider configuration should trigger governance review.

The assessment should consider:

- Provider risk
- Data processing
- Security controls
- Privacy impact
- Model changes
- Subprocessors
- Contractual requirements
- Assurance evidence

### 67. AI Regulatory Change Control

The organization should maintain a process for identifying changes in applicable AI regulation.

Regulatory changes should be evaluated for impact on:

- AI systems
- Policies
- Controls
- Risk classifications
- Assessments
- Contracts
- Documentation
- Assurance requirements

Regulatory changes should be connected to governance action.

- **Control oversight ≠ control operation**

- **Assurance ≠ governance**
- **Policy ownership ≠ enterprise accountability**
- **Committee oversight ≠ operational decision-making**
- **Executive sponsorship ≠ control ownership**
- **Regulatory interpretation ≠ regulatory compliance**
- **Policy approval ≠ policy effectiveness**
- **Policy communication ≠ policy implementation**
- **Policy acknowledgment ≠ policy compliance**
- **Framework alignment ≠ effective control implementation**
- **Governance maturity ≠ governance effectiveness**

The governance model is effective only when:

- Authority is clearly established
- Decision rights are explicit
- Accountability is assigned
- Risk ownership is defined
- Control ownership is defined
- Governance bodies have appropriate authority
- The three lines remain appropriately separated
- Regulatory obligations are incorporated
- Business and technical governance are integrated
- Material decisions are documented
- Exceptions are controlled
- Risk acceptance is authorized
- Assurance remains independent
- Governance performance is measured
- Material AI changes trigger governance reassessment
- Governance continuously adapts to AI technology, business strategy, regulation, and risk

**Core Principle:**

> **AI governance should operate as an integrated enterprise management system in which authority, accountability, risk ownership, control ownership, regulatory obligations, independent challenge, and executive oversight are clearly defined and continuously coordinated throughout the AI lifecycle.**

### 248. AI Governance Policy as an Architectural Enforcement Layer

The AI Governance Policy should not be limited to communicating expectations.

Where technically feasible, policy requirements should be translated into enforceable architectural controls.

Examples include:

- Blocking deployment without approval
- Preventing use of unregistered AI
- Requiring current assessments
- Restricting high-risk data
- Detecting unauthorized AI services
- Requiring reassessment after material changes
- Automatically escalating expired exceptions

This creates a transition from:

**Policy → Manual Interpretation**

to:

**Policy → Control → Automated Enforcement**

### 249. AI Governance Architecture Anti-Patterns

The organization should avoid architectures where:

- The policy exists without operational controls
- The AI inventory is maintained manually without reconciliation
- Governance depends entirely on spreadsheets
- Risk assessments are disconnected from AI systems
- Approvals cannot be traced
- Exceptions never expire
- Governance decisions lack evidence
- Policy rules are implemented inconsistently
- Automated governance rules are not tested
- Reporting data is manually manipulated
- Assurance cannot reconstruct historical decisions
- Governance platforms have excessive privileges
- Provider governance exists outside enterprise risk management

These patterns create an appearance of governance without effective governance.

### 251. Enterprise AI Governance Architecture Principle

The AI Governance Policy should be implemented as an integrated enterprise governance architecture connecting strategy, policy, risk, controls, AI inventory, assessments, approvals, evidence, monitoring, incidents, compliance, assurance, and continuous improvement.

The architecture should ensure that governance requirements are not merely documented but are translated into enforceable and auditable mechanisms throughout the AI lifecycle.

The fundamental architectural separations remain:

- **Policy ≠ Architecture**
- **Policy ≠ Control**
- **Governance platform ≠ Governance effectiveness**
- **AI inventory ≠ Complete AI discovery**
- **Automation ≠ Governance judgment**
- **Risk classification ≠ Risk treatment**
- **Approval workflow ≠ Effective approval**
- **Evidence repository ≠ Evidence reliability**
- **Monitoring ≠ Assurance**
- **Dashboard ≠ Governance**
- **Third-party assurance ≠ Enterprise assurance**
- **Policy-as-code ≠ Complete governance**
- **System integration ≠ Data accuracy**
- **Data availability ≠ Data quality**
- **Historical record ≠ Current governance state**
- **Compliance mapping ≠ Compliance**
- **Architecture resilience ≠ Governance resilience**
- **AI registration ≠ AI accountability**

The architecture is effective only when:

- Every material AI system can be identified
- Every material AI system has accountable ownership
- Risk determines governance requirements
- Policy requirements map to controls
- Controls produce reliable evidence
- Governance decisions are traceable
- Approval authority is explicit
- Exceptions are controlled and time-bound
- Material changes trigger reassessment
- Regulatory changes can propagate into governance requirements
- Third-party responsibilities are clearly defined
- Governance systems are secure and resilient
- Governance data is accurate and authoritative
- Independent assurance can reconstruct material decisions
- Historical governance states remain auditable
- Governance continuously adapts to changes in AI technology, business strategy, regulation, and risk

**Core Principle:**

> **AI Governance Policy architecture should transform governance requirements into an integrated, enforceable, evidence-driven, and auditable operating capability that continuously connects AI strategy, risk, controls, decisions, technology, compliance, and assurance throughout the AI lifecycle.**
