# 20.14 — AI Model Risk Assessment

## Part 1 — Model Risk Foundations, Assessment Scope, Model Inventory, and Risk Classification

### 1. Introduction to the AI Model Risk Assessment

An **AI Model Risk Assessment** is a structured process for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and documenting risks arising from the design, development, selection, deployment, use, and ongoing management of an AI model.

The assessment examines whether a model is appropriate for its intended purpose, whether its limitations are understood, whether its outputs can be relied upon within defined boundaries, and whether the organization has implemented appropriate controls to manage potential harm.

AI model risk extends beyond conventional cybersecurity risk. A model may operate securely at the infrastructure level while still producing inaccurate, biased, unstable, misleading, or otherwise unsuitable outputs. Conversely, a model with acceptable performance characteristics may introduce serious security risks when connected to sensitive enterprise data or privileged business operations.

The assessment must therefore consider model characteristics alongside the application, data, users, integrations, business processes, and governance arrangements in which the model operates.

#### 1.1 Objectives of the assessment

The AI Model Risk Assessment should establish whether:

- The model has a clearly defined and authorized business purpose.
- The model is appropriate for the task and operating environment.
- The training, validation, and evaluation evidence is sufficient for the intended use.
- Relevant data quality, representativeness, provenance, and integrity risks are understood.
- Model performance and limitations have been evaluated.
- Potential bias, fairness, privacy, security, and safety risks have been considered.
- Model outputs are subject to suitable validation and oversight.
- The model's integration into business processes does not create unacceptable downstream risks.
- Model ownership, monitoring, change control, and incident response responsibilities are established.
- Residual risks are documented and handled by authorized decision-makers.

The assessment should produce a defensible risk determination rather than merely a checklist of model characteristics.

#### 1.2 Scope of model risk

The assessment should distinguish risks originating in the model itself from risks arising in the broader AI system.

| Risk Domain | Primary Concern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Model performance | Whether the model performs adequately for its intended task | A classification model incorrectly categorizes customer transactions |
| Data quality | Whether the data used to develop or operate the model is accurate and suitable | Training data contains outdated or inconsistent records |
| Bias and fairness | Whether model behavior creates unjustified differences between relevant groups | A hiring model systematically disadvantages qualified applicants from a particular group |
| Robustness | Whether model behavior remains acceptable under expected variations or disturbances | Small changes in input data cause unstable predictions |
| Explainability and transparency | Whether relevant stakeholders can understand the model's behavior sufficiently for the intended purpose | A high-impact decision cannot be adequately explained to affected individuals |
| Privacy | Whether personal or confidential information is improperly collected, inferred, retained, or disclosed | A model output exposes sensitive information from its training or inference context |
| Security | Whether the model or its associated assets can be manipulated, compromised, or misused | An attacker manipulates inputs to influence a security-sensitive prediction |
| Safety | Whether model behavior can contribute to physical, operational, or other serious harm | An AI-enabled industrial process receives an unsafe recommendation |
| Operational reliability | Whether the model remains available and functions consistently under expected conditions | A model service fails during a critical business process |
| Governance | Whether ownership, oversight, accountability, and change management are adequate | A model is deployed without an accountable business owner |
| Legal and regulatory compliance | Whether the model's use meets applicable obligations | An AI system is used in a regulated context without required assessments |
| Third-party dependency | Whether reliance on an external model provider creates unacceptable exposure | A provider changes a model or its service terms without sufficient notice |

These domains overlap. For example, poor training-data quality may create performance, fairness, compliance, and operational risks simultaneously.

#### 1.3 Model risk versus AI system risk

A model risk assessment should not be confused with a complete AI system risk assessment.

| Dimension | AI Model Risk Assessment | AI System Risk Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Model behavior, suitability, limitations, and lifecycle | Complete AI-enabled solution and its operating environment |
| Typical scope | Model, training and evaluation data, parameters, performance, and model governance | Model, application, infrastructure, identities, data flows, APIs, users, tools, and business processes |
| Performance concerns | Accuracy, calibration, robustness, generalization, and other task-specific metrics | End-to-end business outcomes and operational behavior |
| Security concerns | Model manipulation, model extraction, poisoning, and model-specific weaknesses where applicable | Identity, access, infrastructure, APIs, data exposure, integrations, and model-specific threats |
| Accountability | Model owner and relevant technical or scientific stakeholders | Business owner, system owner, security, operations, and other responsible stakeholders |
| Typical output | Model risk profile and recommendations for model acceptance or restrictions | System risk profile and recommendations for architecture, deployment, and operational controls |

Both assessments may be necessary. The required scope depends on the model's role, business impact, deployment architecture, and applicable governance requirements.

#### 1.4 Expected assessment outputs

A complete assessment may produce:

1. Model inventory and identification record.
2. Intended-purpose and use-context statement.
3. Model risk classification.
4. Data suitability and provenance assessment.
5. Model performance and limitation review.
6. Bias, fairness, privacy, security, and safety analysis.
7. Risk register with inherent and residual risk ratings.
8. Control requirements and remediation actions.
9. Model acceptance criteria and operational restrictions.
10. Monitoring and reassessment requirements.
11. Formal review and approval record.
12. Supporting evidence register.

**Core Principle:** A model should not be accepted solely because it produces plausible outputs or performs well on a benchmark. Its suitability must be demonstrated for the intended purpose, relevant operating conditions, and potential consequences of failure.

### 2. Scope and Applicability of the Model Risk Assessment

The assessment process should apply to models according to their risk, intended purpose, and organizational requirements.

Not every model requires the same depth of assessment. A low-impact internal text-classification model may need a proportionate review, while a model influencing credit decisions, recruitment, healthcare, critical infrastructure, or autonomous business actions may require substantially more rigorous evaluation.

A risk-based approach helps organizations allocate assessment effort according to the likelihood and consequences of model failure.

#### 2.1 Models within scope

The assessment may cover the following categories.

| Model Category | Description | Illustrative Use Case |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional machine learning | Models trained to classify, predict, cluster, or estimate outcomes | Fraud detection or demand forecasting |
| Deep learning | Models using multilayer neural networks | Image classification or speech recognition |
| Large language models | Models designed to process and generate language | Enterprise knowledge assistant |
| Multimodal models | Models processing combinations of text, images, audio, video, or other modalities | Document analysis or visual inspection |
| Computer vision models | Models interpreting visual information | Manufacturing defect detection |
| Speech and audio models | Models processing or generating speech and audio | Speech transcription or voice-based customer support |
| Recommendation models | Models ranking or recommending items or actions | Product recommendations |
| Anomaly detection models | Models identifying deviations from expected patterns | Network anomaly detection |
| Predictive analytics models | Models forecasting future values or events | Operational forecasting |
| AI agents using models | Systems in which models support planning or tool selection | AI agent that interacts with enterprise applications |
| Third-party hosted models | Models supplied or operated by external providers | External foundation model accessed through an API |
| Fine-tuned or adapted models | Models modified for a specific domain or task | Fine-tuned support assistant |
| Open-weight models | Models whose weights are made available under applicable license terms | Self-hosted internal AI service |

The assessment scope should reflect the model's actual function. A system may contain several models, each with different risk characteristics.

#### 2.2 Lifecycle applicability

Model risk can change throughout the model lifecycle.

| Lifecycle Stage | Key Assessment Concern |
|---|---|
| Use-case proposal | Is the intended purpose legitimate, necessary, and appropriately scoped? |
| Model selection | Is the selected model suitable for the task and risk level? |
| Data acquisition | Are data sources appropriate, authorized, and sufficiently documented? |
| Development or adaptation | Are training, fine-tuning, or adaptation practices governed? |
| Validation | Has performance been evaluated against suitable criteria? |
| Pre-deployment review | Are material risks understood and acceptable for the intended use? |
| Deployment | Are model configurations, access controls, and operational safeguards appropriate? |
| Production operation | Is the model performing within established limits? |
| Model or data change | Could the change invalidate earlier evaluation results or assumptions? |
| Incident management | Has unexpected or harmful behavior occurred? |
| Retirement | Are model dependencies, retained data, access permissions, and records handled appropriately? |

An assessment completed before deployment should be revisited when material changes alter the model, its data, its intended purpose, or the consequences of its outputs.

#### 2.3 In-scope and out-of-scope decisions

The assessment must explicitly document its boundaries.

**Potentially in scope:**
- The model and its version.
- The intended use case.
- Training, validation, and evaluation data.
- Model-specific security and performance characteristics.
- Model configuration and deployment parameters.
- Human oversight arrangements.
- Downstream uses of model outputs.
- Model-provider dependencies.
- Monitoring and change-management processes.

**Potentially addressed through related assessments:**
- Full application penetration testing.
- Enterprise infrastructure security.
- Detailed privacy impact assessment.
- Supplier security assessment.
- AI system impact assessment.
- Business continuity and disaster recovery.
- Legal analysis of the intended use.
- Organizational AI governance and policy compliance.

These related assessments should not be excluded from the overall governance process merely because they fall outside the model assessment's immediate scope. Where appropriate, the model assessment should reference their findings and identify unresolved dependencies.

#### 2.4 Scope definition template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Unique assessment reference |
| Model Name | Official model name |
| Model ID | Identifier from the model inventory |
| Model Version | Version, release, or provider identifier |
| Intended Purpose | Approved function of the model |
| Intended Users | User groups permitted to use the model |
| Deployment Context | Development, testing, production, or other |
| Hosting Model | SaaS, API, private cloud, self-hosted, or other |
| Model Owner | Accountable model owner |
| Business Owner | Accountable owner of the intended business use |
| Assessment Trigger | New model, change, periodic review, incident, or other |
| Assessment Scope | Components and risk domains included |
| Exclusions | Excluded items and justification |
| Related Assessments | References to privacy, security, impact, or supplier assessments |
| Assessment Date | Date the assessment was performed |
| Reviewer | Responsible assessor or team |
| Approval Authority | Role authorized to make the relevant decision |

**Core Principle:** Assessment scope must be explicit and proportionate. Excluding an activity from the model assessment does not eliminate the organization's responsibility to manage the associated risk elsewhere.

### 3. Model Inventory and Identification

An accurate model inventory is the foundation of effective model risk management.

An organization cannot consistently assess, monitor, or govern models it does not know it is using. Models may enter the enterprise through centrally managed AI platforms, third-party SaaS applications, embedded software features, departmental experiments, open-source deployments, or employee use of external services.

A model inventory should provide a reliable record of the models in use, the business processes they support, the parties responsible for them, and their risk classifications.

#### 3.1 Why model inventory matters

A model inventory supports:

- Identification of models that require assessment.
- Assignment of ownership and accountability.
- Visibility into business-critical AI dependencies.
- Tracking of model versions and configuration changes.
- Identification of duplicate or unauthorized model deployments.
- Monitoring of third-party and supplier dependencies.
- Identification of models processing personal or confidential information.
- Prioritization of high-impact assessments.
- Evidence collection for audits and governance reviews.
- Controlled retirement of models that are no longer required.

The model inventory should be connected to the enterprise AI system inventory where one exists. The system inventory describes the overall solution, while the model inventory records the specific models and their relevant lifecycle information.

#### 3.2 Model inventory versus AI system inventory

| Attribute | Model Inventory | AI System Inventory |
|---|---|---|
| Primary record | Individual model or model instance | Complete AI-enabled system or application |
| Main purpose | Track model identity, ownership, versions, and risk | Track system purpose, architecture, ownership, and lifecycle |
| Example | A specified foundation model version | An enterprise assistant using that model, a vector database, and CRM integration |
| Model versioning | Central attribute | May reference multiple model versions |
| System integrations | Relevant dependencies | Complete integration landscape |
| System-level risks | Captured when relevant to the model | Primary focus for end-to-end system risk |
| Typical owner | Model owner or AI platform owner | Business or system owner |

A single AI system may use several models. Conversely, the same model may be used by multiple systems with different risk profiles.

For example, a foundation model used to summarize public information may create a different level of risk from the same model connected to customer records and authorized to initiate account changes.

The assessment must therefore consider both the model's intrinsic characteristics and the context in which it is deployed.

#### 3.3 Model inventory template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Model ID | Unique identifier |
| Model Name | Model name |
| Model Family | Model type or family |
| Provider or Developer | Internal team or external provider |
| Model Version | Version or release identifier |
| Model Type | Classification, regression, LLM, multimodal, or other |
| Model Status | Proposed, development, validation, approved, production, suspended, or retired |
| Intended Purpose | Approved business purpose |
| Business Process | Process supported by the model |
| AI System ID | Associated AI system identifier |
| Business Owner | Accountable business owner |
| Model Owner | Accountable model owner |
| Technical Owner | Responsible technical team |
| Deployment Model | API, SaaS, self-hosted, or other |
| Hosting Environment | Relevant platform or environment |
| Data Categories | Data processed by or associated with the model |
| Data Classification | Highest applicable classification |
| External Data Transfer | Whether information is sent to external services |
| User Population | Intended users or affected groups |
| Output Consumers | People, systems, or processes consuming outputs |
| Autonomy Level | Advisory, human-reviewed execution, or other defined category |
| Risk Tier | Organizational model risk classification |
| Assessment Status | Not started, in progress, complete, or overdue |
| Last Assessment Date | Date of latest assessment |
| Next Review Date | Scheduled reassessment date |
| Material Dependencies | Relevant datasets, services, providers, and tools |
| Retirement Plan | Planned decommissioning or replacement approach |

#### 3.4 Model identification and version control

Model identity must be sufficiently precise to support reproducibility and accountability.

Depending on the model and deployment method, the inventory may need to record:

- Provider and model family.
- Exact model identifier.
- Version, release, or deployment revision.
- Model artifact hash or integrity reference where available.
- Fine-tuning or adaptation details.
- Relevant configuration parameters.
- Evaluation dataset and test-suite versions.
- Prompt templates or system configuration versions where they materially influence behavior.
- Deployment date.
- Change history.
- Known limitations.
- Provider update and deprecation arrangements.

For externally hosted models, the provider may not disclose all internal technical details. In such cases, the organization should document the information available, identify the limitations, and evaluate the resulting uncertainty.

A provider's model alias may refer to an automatically updated service rather than a fixed model version. The organization should establish whether changes can occur without its direct control and what notification, testing, or reassessment arrangements are available.

#### 3.5 Example: Inventorying a model used by a telecom operator

Consider a telecommunications company using a language model to support customer-service employees.

The model summarizes customer interactions, retrieves relevant policy information, and proposes responses. A separate application integrates the model with a CRM platform.

A model inventory record might include:

| Field | Illustrative Value |
|---|---|
| Model ID | MOD-CRM-001 |
| Model Name | Customer Service Language Model |
| Model Type | Large language model |
| Intended Purpose | Summarization and response assistance |
| AI System ID | SYS-CRM-AI-001 |
| Business Owner | Customer Operations |
| Model Owner | AI Platform Team |
| Technical Owner | Enterprise Applications |
| Data Categories | Customer interactions and approved knowledge content |
| Data Classification | To be determined under the company's classification policy |
| Deployment Model | External model API |
| Output Consumers | Customer-service employees |
| Autonomy Level | Human-reviewed assistance |
| Primary Risks | Disclosure, inaccurate summaries, prompt injection, and inappropriate recommendations |
| Assessment Status | Pending assessment |
| Review Trigger | New model deployment |

The record illustrates how the model inventory connects technical identity to business use and risk ownership.

The organization should not infer that the model is approved merely because it has been inventoried. Inventory registration is an identification and governance activity, not a security approval.

**Core Principle:** Every material model should have a unique, traceable inventory record linking its identity, version, owner, intended purpose, associated AI system, and risk assessment status.

### 4. Model Risk Classification and Assessment Prioritization

Model risk classification determines the depth of assessment, level of oversight, evidence requirements, and approval authority appropriate to a model.

A model's risk cannot be determined solely by its technical complexity, size, or provider reputation. The same model may create different risks depending on the decisions it influences, the people affected, the information it processes, and the degree of autonomy granted to the surrounding application.

Classification should therefore be based on the model's intended purpose and actual deployment context.

#### 4.1 Risk classification dimensions

| Dimension | Assessment Question | Risk-Increasing Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Business impact | What happens if the model produces an incorrect or misleading result? | Significant financial, operational, or strategic consequences |
| Human impact | Can outputs affect people's rights, opportunities, safety, or access to services? | Decisions with substantial effects on individuals |
| Decision authority | Does the model advise, recommend, or directly influence decisions? | Automated or highly influential decisions |
| Autonomy | Can the model trigger actions without human confirmation? | Independent execution of consequential actions |
| Data sensitivity | What information does the model process or reveal? | Sensitive personal, confidential, financial, or regulated data |
| Security exposure | Can the model or surrounding application be manipulated or misused? | External access, broad integrations, or privileged tools |
| Performance uncertainty | How predictable and reliable is model behavior for the intended task? | Limited validation, unstable behavior, or significant uncertainty |
| Explainability | Can the organization understand and investigate material outputs? | Limited ability to explain consequential results |
| Scale | How many people, transactions, or systems can be affected? | Large-scale or rapid propagation of errors |
| Reversibility | Can harmful outcomes be corrected? | Irreversible or difficult-to-reverse actions |
| Regulatory exposure | Does the use fall under specific legal or regulatory obligations? | Applicable high-impact or regulated use |
| Third-party dependence | How much control does the organization have over the model and its lifecycle? | Opaque provider updates or limited assurance evidence |

A risk assessment should consider interactions among these dimensions. A model with moderate individual risk characteristics may create significant overall exposure when it is connected to sensitive systems and operates at scale.

#### 4.2 Illustrative risk tiers

The following classification is a reusable organizational model. It is not a universal regulatory classification and should be aligned with the organization's approved methodology.

| Tier | Description | Example | Typical Assessment Requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Low | Limited-impact use with constrained data access and minimal consequences of failure | Internal categorization of non-sensitive documents | Basic inventory, purpose review, and proportionate testing |
| Tier 2 — Moderate | Model supports business activities where errors may create manageable operational or financial consequences | Internal knowledge assistant for routine employee questions | Documented risk assessment, performance evaluation, access-control review, and monitoring |
| Tier 3 — High | Model influences significant business decisions or processes sensitive information | Customer-service model processing confidential customer records | Detailed assessment, stronger validation, security and privacy review, formal approval, and enhanced monitoring |
| Tier 4 — Critical | Model failure or misuse could cause severe, widespread, safety-related, or difficult-to-reverse consequences | AI-enabled control of a critical process or highly consequential automated decision | Highest applicable assurance, specialized testing, strict authorization, independent review, and formal governance decisions |

The tier should be assigned according to the organization's defined criteria, not selected solely from the examples.

#### 4.3 Example risk-tier comparison

Consider three deployments using a similar underlying language model.

| Deployment | Context | Illustrative Risk Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Use Case A | Summarizes publicly available product information for internal employees | Limited data sensitivity and low consequence if an output is incorrect |
| Use Case B | Summarizes customer complaints containing personal information | Confidentiality, privacy, accuracy, and operational risks |
| Use Case C | Initiates customer-account changes through connected enterprise APIs | Authorization, financial or service impact, prompt injection, and unauthorized execution risks |

The model family may be identical, but the assessment depth should differ because the information, permissions, and potential consequences differ.

#### 4.4 Inherent risk versus residual risk

The model risk assessment should distinguish inherent risk from residual risk.

**Inherent risk** is the risk assessed before accounting for the effectiveness of the relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the risk that remains after considering implemented controls and their demonstrated effectiveness.

For example, a model connected to a customer database may initially present a material risk of unauthorized disclosure. Strong retrieval authorization, least-privilege access, output controls, and monitoring may reduce that risk.

However, the existence of these controls does not automatically establish a low residual risk. The reviewer must evaluate whether they are implemented, effective, appropriately scoped, and supported by evidence.

| Assessment Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Risk scenario | The model discloses a customer's confidential information to an unauthorized user |
| Inherent risk factors | Sensitive data, broad retrieval capability, and external model processing |
| Existing controls | Identity-based access, retrieval filtering, context minimization, and output restrictions |
| Control evidence | Configuration records, authorization tests, and controlled disclosure tests |
| Residual risk considerations | Test coverage, failure modes, provider behavior, and remaining access paths |
| Risk owner | Designated business or system risk owner |
| Treatment decision | Remediate, mitigate, avoid, transfer where appropriate, or accept where permitted |

#### 4.5 Risk scoring methodology

Organizations may use qualitative, semi-quantitative, or quantitative scoring, provided the methodology is documented and consistently applied.

A simple qualitative model may combine likelihood and impact.

| Impact / Likelihood | Rare | Unlikely | Possible | Likely | Almost Certain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Severe | Medium | High | Critical | Critical | Critical |
| Major | Medium | Medium | High | Critical | Critical |
| Moderate | Low | Medium | Medium | High | Critical |
| Minor | Low | Low | Medium | Medium | High |
| Negligible | Low | Low | Low | Medium | Medium |

This matrix is illustrative. An organization should define its own thresholds, scoring rules, escalation criteria, and risk acceptance authority.

Likelihood should reflect the relevant threat conditions and operating environment. Impact should consider consequences such as data disclosure, financial loss, service disruption, unfair treatment, safety effects, and regulatory exposure.

Where several impacts apply, the methodology should define how they are combined rather than relying on an undocumented average.

#### 4.6 Assessment prioritization rules

The organization should prioritize assessment where one or more of the following conditions apply:

- The model supports a high-impact or regulated use.
- The model processes sensitive or confidential information.
- The model has broad access to enterprise systems.
- The model can execute actions with limited human oversight.
- The model is externally exposed.
- The model is provided by a third party with limited transparency.
- The model has limited validation evidence.
- The model has experienced material incidents or unexpected behavior.
- The model has undergone a significant change.
- The model affects a large population or critical business process.

**Core Principle:** Risk classification should reflect the consequences and context of model use, not merely the model's technical sophistication or reputation.

### 5. Model Purpose, Intended Use, and Prohibited Use

The intended purpose defines what the model is expected and authorized to do. It is a foundational input to model risk assessment because performance, safety, fairness, and control requirements cannot be evaluated meaningfully without a clearly defined use case.

A model that is suitable for drafting internal text may be unsuitable for making final employment decisions. A model that can summarize customer records may not be suitable for independently changing account permissions or issuing financial approvals.

The assessment should document the boundaries of acceptable use and identify uses that are prohibited or require additional authorization.

#### 5.1 Intended-purpose statement

A complete intended-purpose statement should identify:

- The business problem the model addresses.
- The task the model performs.
- The users and affected parties.
- The information the model receives.
- The outputs it generates.
- The business process in which outputs are used.
- The degree of human oversight.
- The decisions or actions the model may influence.
- The operating conditions and assumptions.
- The expected benefits and measurable success criteria.
- The known limitations.
- The boundaries of permitted use.

#### 5.2 Intended-use template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Use-Case ID | Unique use-case reference |
| Business Problem | Problem or opportunity being addressed |
| Intended Purpose | Specific function the model performs |
| Target Users | Authorized user population |
| Affected Parties | Individuals or groups potentially affected |
| Input Data | Data supplied to the model |
| Output Type | Prediction, classification, summary, recommendation, generated content, or action proposal |
| Business Process | Process receiving the output |
| Decision Role | Advisory, decision-support, or automated role |
| Human Oversight | Required review, approval, or intervention |
| Success Criteria | Measurable criteria for acceptable performance |
| Operating Conditions | Assumptions and supported use conditions |
| Known Limitations | Documented limitations and uncertainty |
| Prohibited Uses | Activities outside the authorized purpose |
| Approval Owner | Role accountable for approving the intended use |

#### 5.3 Intended use versus actual use

A model may be approved for one purpose but gradually be used for another.

For example, a customer-service assistant may initially be approved to summarize support tickets. Employees may later begin using it to recommend refunds, assess customer eligibility, or make decisions that affect access to services.

These additional uses may introduce different performance, fairness, privacy, security, and compliance risks.

The organization should monitor for use-case expansion and require reassessment when actual use departs materially from the approved purpose.

#### 5.4 Prohibited-use register

| Prohibited Use ID | Prohibited Activity | Rationale | Preventive Control | Monitoring or Detection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PU-01 | Using the model to make decisions outside its approved scope | Prevents unauthorized expansion of decision authority | Application-level use restrictions | Use-case monitoring and periodic review |
| PU-02 | Supplying data the user is not authorized to process | Protects confidential and restricted information | Data-access controls and input restrictions | Access logging and incident monitoring |
| PU-03 | Allowing the model to execute privileged operations without required authorization | Prevents unauthorized business or technical actions | Independent authorization and approval gates | Tool-call monitoring |
| PU-04 | Treating unvalidated output as authoritative for a consequential decision | Reduces reliance on unsupported or inaccurate outputs | Human review and defined acceptance criteria | Quality checks and exception monitoring |
| PU-05 | Using the model for a newly introduced high-impact purpose without assessment | Prevents unreviewed risk expansion | Change control and reassessment | Periodic use-case review |

These examples should be adapted to the organization's policies, applicable obligations, and actual model capabilities.

A prohibited-use policy should not rely solely on the model refusing certain prompts. Where the restriction is essential, it should be enforced through application permissions, workflow rules, data controls, or other appropriate mechanisms.

#### 5.5 Use-case change control

A proposed change to the intended use should trigger a structured assessment.

The reviewer should determine whether the change affects:

1. The population using or affected by the model.
2. The categories or sensitivity of processed data.
3. The decisions influenced by model outputs.
4. The consequences of inaccurate or misleading outputs.
5. The level of autonomy or action authority.
6. The applicable legal or regulatory obligations.
7. The performance and validation criteria.
8. The security architecture and access model.
9. Required human oversight.
10. Existing approval conditions and residual-risk decisions.

Material changes should be approved through the applicable governance process before they are introduced into production.

**Core Principle:** Model approval is purpose-specific. Approval for one task does not automatically authorize a different use, a more consequential decision, or a broader level of autonomy.

### 6. Model Data Provenance, Quality, and Suitability

Data is a major determinant of model behavior and risk.

Depending on the model type, data may include training datasets, fine-tuning data, validation datasets, evaluation datasets, inference inputs, retrieval content, feedback records, and operational monitoring data.

The assessment should determine whether the relevant data is appropriate for the intended purpose and whether its origin, quality, integrity, representativeness, and permitted use are sufficiently understood.

The data requirements differ by model type. A conventional supervised-learning model may depend heavily on labeled training data, while an externally hosted foundation model may provide limited visibility into its original training corpus. The assessment should account for these differences explicitly.

#### 6.1 Data categories relevant to model risk

| Data Category | Purpose | Key Risk Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Training data | Used to fit model parameters | Quality, provenance, representativeness, integrity, and permitted use |
| Fine-tuning data | Used to adapt a pretrained model | Label quality, domain suitability, leakage, and unintended behavior |
| Validation data | Used during model-development decisions | Suitability, independence, and leakage into training |
| Evaluation data | Used to assess model performance | Coverage, representativeness, and independence from development data |
| Inference input | Supplied when the model is used | Authorization, accuracy, sensitivity, and manipulation |
| Retrieval data | Supplied to a model from external sources at inference time | Source integrity, access authorization, freshness, and prompt injection |
| Feedback data | Captured from users or operational outcomes | Bias, manipulation, representativeness, and consent or other applicable requirements |
| Monitoring data | Used to detect changes in behavior or data characteristics | Privacy, integrity, retention, and coverage |

#### 6.2 Data provenance

Data provenance describes the origin and relevant processing history of a dataset or information asset.

For model risk management, provenance may include:

- Original source or provider.
- Collection method.
- Collection date and relevant time period.
- Data owner or accountable source authority.
- Applicable permissions, licenses, or contractual restrictions.
- Processing and transformation history.
- Labeling methodology, where applicable.
- Quality checks and known limitations.
- Data lineage and dependencies.
- Relevant retention and deletion requirements.

A model may produce apparently accurate results while relying on data that is outdated, poorly documented, unauthorized for the intended use, or unrepresentative of the population on which it will operate.

The organization should document provenance gaps and assess whether they prevent adequate assurance.

#### 6.3 Data quality dimensions

| Dimension | Meaning | Example Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Data correctly represents the underlying fact or event | Incorrect customer status in the input dataset |
| Completeness | Required values or records are present | Important transaction categories are missing |
| Consistency | Values follow compatible definitions and formats | Different systems use conflicting customer identifiers |
| Timeliness | Data reflects the relevant period | A forecasting model relies on obsolete demand patterns |
| Representativeness | Data adequately reflects the intended operating population or conditions | Evaluation data excludes a significant user group |
| Integrity | Data has not been improperly altered | Training records are modified without authorization |
| Label quality | Labels accurately represent the target outcome | Historical decisions contain systematic labeling errors |
| Relevance | Data is appropriate for the intended task | Training data reflects a different business process |
| Uniqueness | Duplicate records are controlled appropriately | Duplicate records distort model evaluation |
| Lineage | Data transformations and sources are traceable | The team cannot identify which dataset produced a result |

Not every dimension applies equally to every model. The reviewer should select the relevant dimensions according to the model type, purpose, and risk.

#### 6.4 Data leakage

Data leakage occurs when information that should not be available to the model during training or evaluation influences model development or performance measurement.

For example, a model intended to predict whether a transaction is fraudulent may inadvertently receive a feature created only after fraud has been confirmed. The resulting test performance may appear strong but will not reflect the information available at the time of actual prediction.

Potential consequences include:

- Overstated model performance.
- Poor generalization to production conditions.
- Misleading model acceptance decisions.
- Unreliable business outcomes.
- Inadequate risk estimates.

The assessment should evaluate whether training, validation, and test datasets are appropriately separated and whether features are available at the intended prediction time.

For externally provided models, complete verification of original training data may not be possible. The reviewer should document the available evidence and consider alternative evaluation methods appropriate to the use case.

#### 6.5 Data suitability assessment template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Dataset ID | Unique identifier |

#### 6.6 Data suitability review questions

The assessor should establish:

1. Is the data suitable for the model's intended purpose?
2. Can its origin and relevant processing history be established?
3. Are quality and representativeness limitations understood?
4. Are labels sufficiently reliable for the intended task?
5. Are training and evaluation data appropriately separated?
6. Could data leakage distort the reported performance?
7. Are applicable permissions and usage restrictions understood?
8. Does the dataset contain sensitive information requiring additional controls?
9. Are transformations and derived features documented?
10. Can relevant data-quality failures be detected during operation?

**Core Principle:** Model performance claims are only as reliable as the data and evaluation methods supporting them. Where data provenance or suitability cannot be adequately established, the resulting uncertainty must be reflected in the model risk decision.

### 7. Model Performance, Validation, and Acceptance Criteria

Model performance assessment determines whether the model meets the requirements of its intended purpose under defined conditions.

The appropriate evaluation method depends on the task. A classifier, forecasting model, recommendation system, and generative language model require different metrics and test methods.

The assessment should avoid relying on a single aggregate score when that score could conceal important weaknesses across user groups, input categories, operating conditions, or failure types.

#### 7.1 Performance evaluation principles

A credible model performance assessment should establish:

- The task and expected model behavior.
- The evaluation dataset and its suitability.
- The selected performance metrics.
- The acceptance thresholds and rationale.
- The relevant operating conditions.
- Known limitations and failure modes.
- The consequences of false positives and false negatives.
- Performance differences across relevant groups or conditions.
- The model's behavior when inputs are incomplete, ambiguous, or outside the supported scope.
- The relationship between evaluation results and the intended business decision.

Acceptance criteria should be defined before final evaluation wherever practicable. This reduces the risk of selecting metrics or thresholds merely because they make the observed results appear acceptable.

#### 7.2 Example metrics by model type

| Model Type | Possible Metrics | Important Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Binary classifier | Precision, recall, F1 score, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC | False-positive and false-negative consequences |
| Multiclass classifier | Per-class precision and recall, macro-F1, confusion matrix | Performance across individual classes |
| Regression model | MAE, RMSE, error distribution | Magnitude and direction of prediction errors |
| Forecasting model | Forecast error, bias, interval coverage | Seasonality, drift, and forecast horizon |
| Ranking or recommendation model | Precision at K, recall at K, ranking metrics | Relevance, exposure, and recommendation quality |
| Anomaly detection | Detection rate, false-alarm rate | Rare events and operational alert burden |
| Generative language model | Task-specific correctness, groundedness, refusal behavior, human evaluation | Context sensitivity, hallucinations, and evaluation limitations |
| Retrieval-augmented system | Retrieval precision and recall, source relevance, answer support | Retrieval quality and authorization boundaries |
| Computer vision model | Precision, recall, task-specific error measures | Image quality, environmental variation, and relevant subgroups |
| Speech model | Word error rate and task-specific measures | Accents, noise, language coverage, and recording conditions |

These metrics are examples, not universal acceptance requirements. The assessor should select measures that meaningfully reflect the risks of the actual task.

#### 7.3 False positives and false negatives

For many predictive models, the relative consequences of false positives and false negatives are important to risk evaluation.

Consider a fraud-detection model:

- A false positive may incorrectly flag a legitimate transaction.
- A false negative may allow a fraudulent transaction to proceed.

The organization should assess both error types and establish thresholds consistent with the business purpose, operational constraints, and impact on affected parties.

For other use cases, the relevant error categories will differ. In a medical screening model, for example, missed detections may have materially different consequences from unnecessary follow-up examinations.

#### 7.4 Generative model evaluation

Generative models require task-specific evaluation because fluent language does not guarantee factual correctness, completeness, or suitability.

Depending on the use case, evaluation may examine:

- Factual correctness.
- Grounding in authorized source material.
- Completeness of relevant information.
- Unsupported claims or hallucinations.
- Consistency across repeated or varied inputs.
- Appropriate handling of ambiguous requests.
- Refusal or redirection behavior for prohibited tasks.
- Disclosure of sensitive information.
- Robustness to adversarial inputs.
- Correctness of structured output.
- Performance across relevant languages and user groups.
- Safe handling of tool results and downstream actions.

For RAG applications, the assessor should distinguish retrieval quality from answer quality. A model may generate a plausible answer from incomplete retrieval results, or it may fail to answer correctly despite retrieving the appropriate sources.

Evaluation should therefore examine the complete workflow where end-to-end behavior matters.

| Metric or Test | Measure used to evaluate performance |
| Target Threshold | Required result |
| Rationale | Why the threshold is appropriate |
| Evaluation Dataset | Dataset or test-suite reference |
| Evaluation Conditions | Environment, configuration, and assumptions |
| Results | Observed performance |
| Subgroup or Scenario Analysis | Relevant breakdowns |

| Reviewer | Responsible assessor |

| Required Remediation | Corrective action if criteria are not met |

#### 7.6 Acceptance decisions

A model should not be accepted solely because its average performance meets a threshold.

The assessor should also consider whether:

- Material failure modes remain untested.
- Evaluation data reflects expected production conditions.
- Performance is adequate for the highest-impact use of the model.
- Relevant user groups experience materially different error rates.
- The model fails safely when confidence is low or inputs are unsupported.
- The surrounding application can detect or limit unacceptable outputs.
- The organization can monitor performance after deployment.
- Any limitations are reflected in permitted-use restrictions.

Where performance is insufficient, options may include improving the model, changing the use case, adding independent validation, introducing human oversight, restricting deployment, or rejecting the proposed use.

**Core Principle:** Model acceptance requires evidence that performance is appropriate for the intended task and its consequences. A high aggregate score does not, by itself, establish that the model is suitable or safe for deployment.

### 8. Model Bias, Fairness, and Impact on Affected Parties

Models may produce different outcomes for different individuals or groups because of data characteristics, labeling practices, feature selection, model design, deployment context, or feedback loops.

Bias and fairness risks must be assessed in relation to the intended purpose, affected population, applicable obligations, and potential consequences.

A model may perform well overall while producing materially worse results for a relevant subgroup. Aggregate performance metrics can conceal these differences.

#### 8.1 Sources of bias

| Source | Description | Illustrative Example |
|---|---|---|
| Historical bias | Training data reflects existing inequalities or past practices | A model reproduces patterns from historical hiring decisions |
| Representation bias | Some relevant populations are insufficiently represented | A speech model performs poorly for certain accents |
| Measurement bias | The recorded variables do not accurately represent the intended concept | A proxy measure is used for a complex human outcome |
| Label bias | Labels reflect inconsistent or unfair judgments | Historical decisions are treated as objective ground truth |
| Sampling bias | The dataset is not representative of the intended population | Evaluation data excludes important customer segments |
| Aggregation bias | A single model or rule performs differently across groups | One model performs poorly across distinct operating populations |
| Deployment bias | The model is used in a context different from the one for which it was evaluated | A support model is used for final eligibility decisions |
| Feedback-loop bias | Model outputs influence future data in ways that reinforce previous behavior | Recommendations alter future user behavior and training data |
| Automation bias | Users over-rely on model recommendations | Employees accept inaccurate recommendations without sufficient review |

These categories are analytical aids. Multiple sources may contribute to the same observed disparity.

#### 8.2 Fairness assessment considerations

The reviewer should establish:

- Which individuals or groups may be affected.
- Which outcomes matter for the intended purpose.
- Whether relevant group-level performance differences should be evaluated.
- Whether the available data supports meaningful evaluation.
- Whether the chosen fairness criteria fit the use case.
- Whether model errors could create disproportionate harm.
- Whether human reviewers have the competence and authority to challenge outputs.
- Whether the model's outputs can be monitored for emerging disparities.
- Whether applicable legal requirements restrict the use of particular data or decision methods.
- Whether the organization has defined remediation and escalation procedures.

Fairness metrics may conflict with one another, particularly where base rates or error characteristics differ across groups. Selecting a metric is therefore a governance decision that should be justified in the context of the intended use and applicable obligations.

#### 8.3 Human oversight assessment

Human oversight is not automatically effective merely because a person is present in the workflow.

The assessment should examine whether reviewers:

- Understand the model's intended purpose and limitations.
- Receive enough information to evaluate the output.
- Have sufficient time and competence to perform the review.
- Can reject or override the recommendation.
- Are not improperly penalized for challenging model outputs.
- Know when escalation is required.
- Record material overrides and exceptions where appropriate.
- Are monitored for inappropriate over-reliance on model recommendations.

For consequential decisions, the organization should determine whether human review is meaningful and proportionate to the risk.

**Core Principle:** Fairness and impact must be assessed in the context of the model's intended use and affected population. Aggregate performance alone may conceal material harm to specific groups or individuals.

### 9. Model Security and Adversarial Risk

Model security assessment examines threats that may compromise model integrity, confidentiality, availability, or the reliability of its outputs.

The relevant threats depend on the model type, access conditions, development process, deployment architecture, and surrounding application.

The model assessment should connect these threats to the broader AI security architecture review rather than treating model-specific risks as isolated technical issues.

#### 9.1 Model security threat categories

| Threat | Description | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Data poisoning | Training or adaptation data is manipulated to influence model behavior | Reduced integrity or targeted misbehavior |
| Model tampering | Model artifacts or deployment configuration are altered without authorization | Compromised model behavior |
| Model extraction | An attacker attempts to reproduce a model through repeated queries or other means | Intellectual-property exposure or increased attack capability |
| Membership inference | An attacker attempts to determine whether specific records were included in training data | Privacy exposure |
| Model inversion | An attacker attempts to infer sensitive characteristics or training information from model behavior | Information disclosure |
| Adversarial input manipulation | Inputs are crafted to cause incorrect or undesirable predictions | Reliability or security failure |
| Prompt injection | Malicious instructions influence a generative model or connected application | Policy bypass, disclosure, or unauthorized actions |
| Model denial of service | Excessive or crafted requests degrade model availability | Service disruption and cost escalation |
| Unauthorized model access | Model endpoints or artifacts are exposed beyond permitted scope | Confidentiality or integrity compromise |
| Supply-chain compromise | A dependency, model artifact, or provider is compromised | Compromised behavior or broader system exposure |
| Insecure model loading | Untrusted model artifacts or unsafe serialization mechanisms are processed | Code execution or system compromise, depending on the implementation |
| Excessive model permissions | Model-connected components have more access than necessary | Expanded impact if another control fails |

Not every threat applies to every model. The assessor should identify applicable threats based on the actual implementation and credible attack scenarios.

#### 9.2 Security controls to evaluate

The model risk assessment may evaluate whether the organization has implemented appropriate controls for:

- Model artifact integrity and provenance.
- Restricted access to model files and configuration.
- Authentication and authorization for inference endpoints.
- Request limits and abuse prevention.
- Secure handling of model dependencies.
- Protection of training and adaptation pipelines.
- Data access restrictions.
- Secret management.
- Logging and detection of suspicious behavior.
- Isolation of model execution environments where appropriate.
- Controlled model updates and rollback.
- Security testing and vulnerability remediation.
- Incident containment and recovery.

The required control set should be proportionate to the threat model and deployment context.

#### 9.3 Model security versus application security

A model may be protected against unauthorized artifact modification while the application using it remains vulnerable to excessive permissions or insecure API handling.

Similarly, an application may enforce authentication correctly while exposing a model to adversarial inputs that undermine the intended task.

The assessment should therefore distinguish:

- Model artifact and configuration security.
- Model endpoint security.
- Training and adaptation pipeline security.
- Application and API security.
- Data and retrieval security.
- Agent and tool execution security.
- Operational monitoring and response.

This separation helps assign findings to the correct owners while preserving an end-to-end understanding of risk.

#### 9.4 Model security assessment template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Threat ID | Unique threat reference |
| Threat Category | Relevant model security threat |
| Affected Asset | Model, dataset, endpoint, artifact, or connected service |
| Attack Surface | Interface or process through which the threat may occur |
| Threat Actor | Relevant actor or source of threat |
| Preconditions | Conditions required for the scenario |
| Potential Impact | Confidentiality, integrity, availability, or business consequence |
| Existing Controls | Current preventive, detective, and recovery measures |
| Evidence | Configuration, testing, or operational records |
| Likelihood | Rating under the approved methodology |
| Impact Rating | Rating under the approved methodology |
| Inherent Risk | Risk before considering controls |
| Residual Risk | Risk remaining after evaluating controls |
| Treatment | Remediate, mitigate, avoid, transfer where appropriate, or accept where permitted |
| Owner | Accountable person or team |
| Review Status | Open, in progress, verified, or otherwise defined |

#### 9.5 Example: Model endpoint exposed through an application

An enterprise deploys a language model behind an API. The application authenticates users, but the endpoint permits excessively large requests and has insufficient rate limiting.

An attacker repeatedly submits resource-intensive requests. The model service becomes unavailable to legitimate users, and operating costs increase.

The risk is not limited to model quality. It involves endpoint exposure, resource consumption, abuse controls, service availability, and business continuity.

The assessment should examine:

- Whether access is restricted to authorized clients.
- Whether request size and execution time are bounded.
- Whether usage limits are appropriate for the business purpose.
- Whether anomalous consumption can be detected.
- Whether capacity and cost thresholds trigger alerts.
- Whether service degradation has a documented response procedure.
- Whether the system can recover safely from excessive demand.

**Core Principle:** Model security must be evaluated in the context of the model's actual attack surface and deployment architecture. Security claims require evidence that relevant threats are addressed by appropriate controls.

### 10. Core Principle — Model Risk Management Must Be Evidence-Based and Lifecycle-Oriented

An effective AI Model Risk Assessment establishes a traceable relationship between the model's intended purpose, its technical and data characteristics, its potential consequences, and the controls required to manage those consequences.

The organization should be able to explain why a model is suitable for a particular use, what evidence supports that conclusion, which limitations remain, and who is authorized to decide whether the residual risk is acceptable.

The assessment should be proportionate to the model's risk while maintaining clear accountability and documented decision criteria.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Model risk vs. AI system risk | Model risk concerns model behavior and suitability; system risk covers the complete AI-enabled solution |
| Model inventory vs. AI system inventory | A model inventory records individual models; a system inventory records the wider application and its dependencies |
| Model capability vs. approved purpose | A model's technical ability to perform a task does not mean the organization has authorized that use |
| Inherent risk vs. residual risk | Inherent risk is assessed before controls; residual risk remains after evaluating controls |
| Data provenance vs. data quality | Provenance concerns origin and processing history; quality concerns fitness for the intended task |
| Training data vs. evaluation data | Training data supports model development; evaluation data provides evidence of performance under defined conditions |
| Performance vs. suitability | A performance score is a measurement; suitability is a broader judgment based on intended purpose and consequences |
| Accuracy vs. fairness | Overall accuracy does not establish that outcomes are equitable or acceptable for relevant groups |
| Human presence vs. effective oversight | Human oversight is effective only when reviewers can meaningfully evaluate, challenge, and influence outcomes |
| Model security vs. application security | Model security addresses model-specific assets and threats; application security addresses the software and interfaces through which the model is used |
| Inventory registration vs. model approval | Recording a model identifies it for governance; approval requires an appropriate assessment and authorized decision |
| Assessment completion vs. ongoing assurance | Completing an assessment records a point-in-time conclusion; ongoing assurance requires monitoring and reassessment |

**Completion criterion:** Part 1 establishes the foundations for the AI Model Risk Assessment, including its purpose, scope, inventory requirements, risk classification, intended-use boundaries, data suitability, performance validation, fairness, and model security. The assessment should use these foundations to establish a documented and risk-proportionate basis for evaluating a model before relying on it in an enterprise context.

### 11. Model Validation Framework and Independent Review

Model validation establishes whether a model is fit for its intended purpose and whether the evidence supporting its use is sufficiently reliable.

Validation should go beyond confirming that a model executes successfully or achieves an acceptable benchmark score. It should examine the appropriateness of the model's design, the reliability of its evaluation methods, the limitations of the available evidence, and the consequences of failure in the intended operating environment.

For enterprise AI governance, validation is an assurance activity that supports a model risk decision. It does not automatically establish that a model is secure, legally compliant, fair, or suitable for every possible application.

#### 11.1 Model testing versus model validation

Model testing and model validation are related but distinct activities.

| Dimension | Model Testing | Model Validation |
|---|---|---|
| Primary objective | Determine whether the model or a specific component behaves as expected under defined tests | Determine whether the model is suitable for its intended purpose and risk context |
| Typical scope | Specific functions, metrics, scenarios, and technical behaviors | Model assumptions, methodology, performance, limitations, controls, and use context |
| Activities | Unit tests, benchmark tests, adversarial tests, and regression tests | Review of methodology, evaluation evidence, assumptions, limitations, and acceptance criteria |
| Main output | Test results and identified defects | Validation conclusion and documented limitations |
| Decision supported | Whether specific tests have passed | Whether the model can be accepted, restricted, conditionally approved, or rejected |
| Independence | May be performed by the development team | Independence should be proportionate to the model's risk and organizational requirements |

Testing is an input to validation. A collection of successful tests does not, by itself, demonstrate that the model is appropriate for the intended purpose.

#### 11.2 Validation independence

The degree of independence required should reflect the model's risk and the consequences of failure.

For low-risk models, validation may be performed within the development or platform team, provided responsibilities and review criteria are clear.

For high-impact or critical models, the organization should consider independent review by personnel who were not responsible for developing the model or making the original performance claims.

Independent review may be required to evaluate:

- Whether the selected methodology is appropriate.
- Whether the evaluation dataset is sufficiently independent.
- Whether the reported metrics accurately represent model behavior.
- Whether relevant failure scenarios were omitted.
- Whether acceptance thresholds are justified.
- Whether limitations have been adequately disclosed.
- Whether the model's intended use exceeds the available evidence.
- Whether the proposed controls are sufficient for the identified risks.
- Whether the approval recommendation is supported by the evidence.

Independence should be documented rather than assumed from job titles.

#### 11.3 Validation evidence requirements

| Evidence Category | Examples | Validation Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Model documentation | Model description, architecture, intended use, and known limitations | Establishes what is being assessed |
| Data documentation | Dataset descriptions, provenance, quality checks, and lineage | Supports assessment of data suitability |
| Evaluation methodology | Test design, datasets, metrics, and thresholds | Establishes how performance was evaluated |
| Test results | Benchmark results, error analysis, and scenario testing | Demonstrates observed model behavior |
| Robustness evidence | Perturbation tests, stress tests, and out-of-distribution evaluation | Supports assessment of reliability under variation |
| Security evidence | Threat assessments, adversarial tests, and artifact-integrity checks | Supports assessment of relevant security risks |
| Fairness evidence | Group-level metrics and impact analysis where applicable | Identifies potential disparities |
| Operational evidence | Monitoring plans, incident records, and deployment constraints | Supports assessment of production readiness |
| Governance evidence | Review records, approval conditions, and risk acceptance decisions | Establishes accountability and decision authority |

The assessor should record evidence gaps and determine whether they are acceptable, require remediation, or prevent a reliable validation conclusion.

#### 11.4 Validation outcome categories

An organization may define the following outcome categories.

| Outcome | Meaning | Required Follow-Up |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | Available evidence supports the model's use within the defined scope | Record approval and operating conditions |
| Approved with conditions | The model may be used only under specified restrictions or after defined actions | Assign owners, deadlines, and verification requirements |
| Pending remediation | Material gaps remain that must be resolved before approval | Track corrective actions and repeat relevant validation |
| Inconclusive | Available evidence is insufficient to reach a defensible conclusion | Obtain additional evidence or narrow the intended use |
| Rejected | The model does not meet required criteria or presents unacceptable risk | Do not authorize the proposed use |
| Suspended | An already deployed model is temporarily prevented from operating because of a material concern | Contain the risk, investigate, and determine whether use can resume |

These outcomes should be aligned with the organization's formal governance process. In particular, conditional approval must not become an informal way to bypass mandatory security, legal, or safety requirements.

### 12. Model Robustness, Generalization, and Distribution Shift

Model robustness concerns the extent to which a model continues to behave acceptably when inputs, environmental conditions, data distributions, or operating assumptions vary.

A model may perform well on its original evaluation dataset but fail when exposed to new customers, different languages, changed business conditions, unusual inputs, or data that differs from its development environment.

The assessment should determine whether the model's expected operating conditions are sufficiently defined and whether relevant deviations have been evaluated.

#### 12.1 Generalization

Generalization is the model's ability to perform adequately on data or conditions beyond those used directly to fit its parameters.

A model that memorizes characteristics of its training data may achieve strong training performance while producing poor results on previously unseen examples.

Potential indicators of inadequate generalization include:

- A substantial difference between training and evaluation performance.
- Unstable results across different evaluation datasets.
- Performance degradation for specific input categories.
- Reliance on spurious correlations.
- Poor performance when data collection methods change.
- Sensitivity to irrelevant features.
- Weak performance in the actual production environment.

The assessment should determine whether evaluation conditions reasonably represent the intended use and whether important differences remain untested.

#### 12.2 Distribution shift

Distribution shift occurs when the data or conditions encountered during operation differ from those represented in model development or evaluation.

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Covariate shift | The distribution of input features changes | Customer behavior changes after a new service launch |
| Label shift | The distribution of outcome classes changes | Fraud becomes less common or more common than before |
| Concept drift | The relationship between inputs and outcomes changes | Previously reliable indicators no longer predict customer churn |
| Operational shift | The environment or process changes | A model moves from a controlled pilot to a high-volume production environment |
| Population shift | The model is used with a different population | A model developed for one language group is used with another |
| Temporal shift | The underlying data patterns change over time | Historical demand patterns become unreliable after a major market change |

These categories are not mutually exclusive. A single event may cause several forms of shift.

#### 12.3 Robustness testing methods

The appropriate tests depend on the model type and use case.

| Method | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Holdout evaluation | Test performance on data not used for model fitting | Evaluate a classifier on an independent dataset |
| Temporal validation | Test performance on a later period | Train on historical data and evaluate on a subsequent period |
| Cross-validation | Examine performance across multiple data partitions | Evaluate a predictive model using repeated folds |
| Perturbation testing | Assess sensitivity to relevant input changes | Introduce realistic variations in image brightness |
| Stress testing | Evaluate behavior under extreme but credible conditions | Test a forecasting model during demand spikes |
| Out-of-distribution testing | Evaluate inputs that differ from the expected distribution | Test a document model on unfamiliar document formats |
| Subgroup evaluation | Identify performance differences across relevant groups | Compare error rates across language groups |
| Scenario testing | Evaluate complete, realistic operating scenarios | Test how a model handles incomplete customer records |
| Metamorphic testing | Evaluate whether related inputs produce logically consistent outputs | Check whether irrelevant formatting changes materially alter a prediction |
| Regression testing | Detect unintended degradation after changes | Compare performance before and after a model update |

The test plan should explain why the selected scenarios are relevant to the model's risks.

#### 12.4 Robustness acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria should identify:

- Expected operating conditions.
- Relevant input ranges and constraints.
- Minimum acceptable performance.
- Maximum acceptable degradation under defined scenarios.
- Conditions under which the model should abstain, escalate, or fail safely.
- Material differences across relevant groups or environments.
- Triggers for reassessment.
- The owner responsible for reviewing test results.

A model need not perform identically under all circumstances. The objective is to determine whether its behavior remains acceptable within the approved operating envelope and whether it handles unsupported conditions appropriately.

#### 12.5 Robustness assessment template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Test ID | Unique test reference |
| Model ID and Version | Exact model under evaluation |
| Operating Assumption | Condition the model is expected to satisfy |
| Shift Scenario | Distribution or environmental change being tested |
| Test Dataset | Dataset used for the test |
| Baseline Result | Performance under reference conditions |
| Stress Result | Performance under changed conditions |
| Acceptance Threshold | Required result |
| Observed Degradation | Difference from baseline |
| Failure Behavior | Model response when conditions are unsupported |
| Risk Implication | Potential operational or business consequence |
| Required Action | Remediation, restriction, or additional monitoring |
| Test Owner | Responsible person or team |
| Evidence Reference | Report or test artifact |

#### 12.6 Example: Customer churn model

A telecommunications operator uses a model to predict which customers are likely to terminate their subscriptions.

The model was trained using historical behavior before a major change to the company's pricing structure.

After the pricing change, customer behavior changes and the model's predictions become less reliable.

The original model may still execute correctly, but its assumptions are no longer sufficiently representative of current conditions.

The assessment should consider:

1. Whether input distributions have changed.
2. Whether the relationship between customer behavior and churn has changed.
3. Whether prediction quality has degraded.
4. Whether the business decisions influenced by the model remain appropriate.
5. Whether retraining, recalibration, or restricted use is necessary.
6. Whether new evaluation evidence is required before continued reliance.

**Core Principle:** A model's previous performance does not guarantee future reliability. Robustness must be evaluated against credible changes in data, populations, and operating conditions.

### 13. Model Explainability, Interpretability, and Transparency

Explainability, interpretability, and transparency support the organization's ability to understand model behavior, investigate errors, communicate limitations, and exercise appropriate oversight.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable.

- **Interpretability** concerns how readily a person can understand a model's internal logic or the relationship between inputs and outputs.
- **Explainability** concerns the methods used to provide an account of why a model produced a particular result or behaved in a particular way.
- **Transparency** concerns the availability of relevant information about the model, its intended purpose, development, limitations, and operating conditions.

The level of explanation required depends on the model, the intended use, the affected parties, and applicable obligations.

#### 13.1 Why explainability matters

Explainability can support:

- Investigation of incorrect or unexpected outputs.
- Review of consequential recommendations.
- Identification of inappropriate input dependencies.
- Detection of possible data or model defects.
- Communication of model limitations to users.
- Auditability of model-related decisions.
- Human oversight and escalation.
- Investigation of complaints and incidents.
- Governance review of changes to model behavior.

An explanation does not automatically establish that a model is correct, fair, or secure. Explanation methods may themselves have limitations, and the assessor should evaluate whether the explanation is suitable for the decision being supported.

#### 13.2 Explainability methods

| Method | Description | Potential Use |
|---|---|---|
| Feature importance | Estimates the contribution or importance of input features under a defined method | Understanding influential variables in a predictive model |
| Local explanation | Explains a particular prediction or decision | Investigating an individual model output |
| Global explanation | Describes general model behavior across a dataset or population | Understanding broad patterns in a model |
| Surrogate model | Uses a simpler model to approximate a more complex model's behavior | Supporting analysis when the original model is difficult to interpret |
| Counterfactual explanation | Identifies how changes to selected inputs could alter an outcome | Examining decision sensitivity |
| Attention analysis | Examines attention-related signals in models that use attention mechanisms | Exploring certain aspects of model processing, with significant interpretive limitations |
| Example-based explanation | Uses representative examples or similar cases to illustrate behavior | Supporting review of classifications or recommendations |
| Natural-language rationale | Provides a textual explanation associated with a generated answer or decision | Helping users understand a recommendation, subject to reliability limits |
| Provenance and citation | Links an output to relevant source material | Supporting verification of retrieval-based responses |

No single method is universally appropriate. The organization should assess whether the method provides useful and reliable information for the intended audience.

#### 13.3 Explainability limitations in generative AI

A language model may produce a convincing explanation that does not faithfully describe the internal processes responsible for its output.

Similarly, a generated rationale may contain unsupported claims even when the final answer is correct.

For this reason, the assessment should distinguish:

- An explanation generated by the model.
- Evidence showing which source material was retrieved.
- Evidence showing which data or features influenced a prediction.
- A verified record of the system's actions.
- A human analyst's independently validated conclusion.

These forms of evidence serve different purposes.

For example, an enterprise assistant may state that it recommended a refund because of a particular customer policy. The organization should verify that the relevant policy was actually retrieved, that the customer met the policy's criteria, and that the recommendation followed the authorized business rules.

The explanation alone is insufficient evidence that the decision was correct.

#### 13.5 Explainability review questions

The assessor should determine:

1. What level of explanation is necessary for the intended use?
2. Can reviewers identify the relevant input data or source material?
3. Can important model limitations be communicated clearly?
4. Can incorrect outputs be investigated?
5. Are explanations distinguishable from verified facts?
6. Are the records required for audit and incident investigation available?
7. Can the organization explain the role of the model in a consequential decision?
8. Are explanation methods evaluated for reliability and suitability?
9. Can affected users obtain appropriate information where required?
10. Are there use cases for which the available explainability is insufficient?

**Core Principle:** Explanations support understanding and oversight, but they are not proof of correctness. The organization must retain appropriate evidence of model behavior and the decisions made using its outputs.

### 14. Privacy Risk Assessment for AI Models

AI models may introduce privacy risks during data collection, training, fine-tuning, inference, retrieval, evaluation, monitoring, and retirement.

Privacy assessment should examine the complete processing context rather than focusing only on whether personal data appears in the model's input.

Depending on the model and deployment architecture, risks may arise from excessive collection, unauthorized processing, retention of sensitive information, memorization, unintended disclosure, inference of sensitive attributes, or inadequate handling of deletion and access requests.

The assessment should be coordinated with the organization's privacy and data-protection functions where applicable.

#### 14.1 Privacy risk categories

| Risk Category | Description | Illustrative Scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Excessive data collection | More personal information is collected than necessary for the purpose | A support assistant receives complete customer records when only a ticket summary is required |
| Unauthorized processing | Data is used outside the permitted purpose or authority | Customer information is sent to an external model service without the necessary assessment or authorization |
| Training-data memorization | A model retains information in a way that may permit later disclosure | Sensitive information from training data appears in a model response |
| Membership inference | An attacker attempts to determine whether a person's record was used in training | A model reveals signals associated with the inclusion of a particular record |
| Sensitive attribute inference | A model or connected system infers sensitive information | Behavioral data is used to infer a characteristic that was not directly provided |
| Inference-time disclosure | Personal information is exposed through model inputs or outputs | A generated response includes another customer's confidential details |
| Excessive retention | Personal data or prompts are retained longer than necessary or permitted | Detailed inference logs are kept without an appropriate retention basis |
| Inadequate deletion handling | Deletion obligations or requests are not appropriately addressed | Removing a source record does not remove related copies or derived records where removal is required |
| Cross-context leakage | Information from one user or tenant becomes available to another | Retrieval filtering fails to enforce tenant boundaries |
| Privacy monitoring exposure | Monitoring systems collect more personal information than necessary | Debug logs retain complete conversations containing sensitive data |

These risks require different controls. For example, access control may help prevent cross-tenant disclosure, while data minimization may reduce the amount of information exposed if a disclosure occurs.

#### 14.2 Data minimization and purpose limitation

The assessor should determine whether the model receives only the information necessary for its approved task.

Relevant questions include:

- Can sensitive fields be removed before inference?
- Can data be masked or pseudonymized where appropriate?
- Is the model's use of personal data consistent with the approved purpose?
- Are external model providers involved in processing?
- Are provider retention and training practices sufficiently understood?
- Are prompts, outputs, and monitoring records subject to suitable controls?
- Can the organization restrict access to personal data used for retrieval?
- Are data retention and deletion requirements defined?
- Are derived datasets and embeddings included in the data-flow analysis?
- Are privacy risks reassessed when the model's purpose or data sources change?

Pseudonymization and masking may reduce exposure but do not necessarily make information anonymous. The assessment should consider whether individuals could still be identified directly or indirectly.

#### 14.3 Privacy-enhancing techniques

| Technique | Potential Benefit | Important Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Data minimization | Reduces unnecessary personal-data processing | Requires a clear understanding of task requirements |
| Masking or redaction | Removes selected sensitive fields from processing | Context may still permit re-identification |
| Pseudonymization | Replaces direct identifiers with substitute values | Information may remain personal data if re-identification is possible |
| Access-controlled retrieval | Restricts access to information according to authorization rules | Does not by itself prevent every form of output disclosure |
| Differential privacy | Can provide formal privacy guarantees under a defined implementation and threat model | Requires careful parameter selection and does not automatically protect every processing stage |
| Federated learning | Allows certain training approaches without centrally collecting all raw training data | Model updates and participating systems can still create privacy and security risks |
| Encryption | Protects information against certain unauthorized access scenarios | Does not prevent authorized components from misusing decrypted information |
| Synthetic data | Can reduce reliance on real records for some development and testing activities | Synthetic data may still leak information or reproduce unwanted patterns |
| Output filtering | Helps prevent selected sensitive content from being returned | May be incomplete and should not replace upstream data and access controls |

The assessor should document the purpose, implementation, limitations, and verification evidence for any privacy-enhancing technique relied upon as a risk control.

|---|---|
| Privacy Risk ID | Unique reference |
| Model and Use Case | Model and intended purpose |
| Data Subjects | Individuals whose data may be processed |
| Personal Data Categories | Relevant data types |
| Processing Stage | Collection, training, inference, retrieval, monitoring, or retirement |

#### 14.5 Privacy risk example

An organization uses a hosted language model to summarize customer-service conversations.

The conversations contain names, contact details, account identifiers, and occasional payment-related information.

The model provider's service terms, retention behavior, and data-processing arrangements have not yet been fully evaluated.

The assessor should determine whether:

1. The model needs all the information included in the conversations.
2. Sensitive fields can be removed before inference.
3. The provider's processing conditions are suitable for the use case.
4. The organization has an appropriate legal and governance basis for the processing.
5. Data transfers and storage locations have been evaluated where relevant.
6. Prompt and response logging is adequately controlled.
7. Retention and deletion arrangements are documented.
8. The system prevents one customer's information from appearing in another customer's results.
9. The residual privacy risk is acceptable under the organization's approval process.

The model should not be treated as privacy-safe simply because the provider describes its service as enterprise-grade.

**Core Principle:** Privacy risk must be evaluated across the model's complete data lifecycle. Data minimization, access control, provider assurance, retention management, and appropriate technical safeguards should be supported by evidence rather than assumed.

### 15. Model Safety, Harm Analysis, and Failure Containment

Model safety assessment examines whether model behavior can contribute to harmful outcomes in the intended operating environment.

The term *safety* may refer to different concerns depending on the use case. In one context, it may concern physical harm. In another, it may involve harmful recommendations, unsafe operational decisions, inappropriate content, or failures that cause serious financial or organizational consequences.

The assessor should define the relevant harm categories instead of relying on a generic safety label.

#### 15.1 Potential harm categories

| Harm Category | Description | Illustrative Scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Physical harm | Model output contributes to injury or unsafe physical operation | An industrial AI system recommends an unsafe operating parameter |
| Financial harm | Model behavior causes financial loss or inappropriate financial decisions | A model generates incorrect financial recommendations that are acted upon |
| Operational harm | Model failure disrupts essential business processes | A model generates incorrect routing decisions during a service incident |
| Individual harm | A model negatively affects a person's opportunities, treatment, or access to services | A model produces a misleading recommendation about an individual |
| Security harm | Model behavior contributes to unauthorized disclosure or action | An agent reveals restricted information |
| Reputational harm | Model outputs damage trust or create public controversy | A customer-facing model repeatedly generates inappropriate responses |
| Legal or regulatory harm | Model behavior contributes to noncompliance | A model is used beyond its approved purpose in a regulated process |
| Environmental or infrastructure harm | Model-supported decisions contribute to environmental damage or infrastructure disruption | An AI-enabled process recommends an unsafe resource allocation |

The assessment should evaluate severity, likelihood, exposure, detectability, and reversibility using the organization's approved risk methodology.

#### 15.2 Hazard identification

A hazard is a condition or behavior that could contribute to harm.

The assessor should identify:

- The hazardous model output or behavior.
- The circumstances in which it could occur.
- The system components involved.
- The people, processes, or assets that could be affected.
- The potential severity of the outcome.
- The controls intended to prevent or limit the hazard.
- The conditions under which those controls may fail.
- The mechanisms for detecting and containing the failure.

For high-impact applications, the assessment may require specialist safety engineering, domain expertise, or formal hazard-analysis methods.

#### 15.3 Safety controls

Potential controls include:

- Defining a restricted operating envelope.
- Preventing the model from independently performing prohibited actions.
- Requiring independent validation of safety-critical outputs.
- Enforcing deterministic business rules for critical constraints.
- Introducing human approval for consequential actions.
- Using independent monitoring to detect unsafe conditions.
- Establishing fail-safe behavior when required information is missing.
- Providing a controlled fallback process.
- Testing foreseeable misuse and failure scenarios.
- Maintaining incident escalation and recovery procedures.
- Defining conditions that require suspension of the model.

A generated statement such as “I cannot perform that action” should not be treated as a sufficient safety control where the surrounding application can still execute the action through another path.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Hazard ID | Unique reference |
| Model ID | Model being assessed |
| Intended Use | Approved application |
| Hazardous Behavior | Behavior that could contribute to harm |
| Trigger Conditions | Conditions under which the behavior may occur |
| Exposed Population or Asset | Potentially affected parties or systems |
| Consequence | Description of potential harm |
| Severity | Rating under the approved methodology |
| Likelihood | Rating under the approved methodology |
| Existing Safeguards | Controls intended to prevent or limit harm |
| Failure Conditions | Circumstances in which safeguards may fail |
| Detection Method | How unsafe behavior will be detected |

| Residual Risk | Risk remaining after controls |
| Risk Owner | Accountable person or function |
| Decision | Accepted, restricted, remediated, or rejected |

#### 15.5 Example: AI model supporting a critical business process

A model generates recommendations for handling network-service incidents. Operations staff use the recommendations to prioritize troubleshooting actions.

The model occasionally produces recommendations that are inconsistent with the organization's approved operating procedures.

The assessment should examine whether:

- Recommendations are grounded in current, approved technical information.
- The model is prevented from bypassing mandatory operational controls.
- High-impact actions require qualified human approval.
- Unsupported recommendations can be detected.
- Operators can identify when the model is uncertain or operating outside its supported scope.
- A documented fallback process exists.
- Unsafe recommendations can be reported and investigated.
- Model suspension is possible without disrupting the entire incident-management process.

The objective is not to assume that model errors can be eliminated. It is to establish whether foreseeable errors can be detected, controlled, and managed within acceptable limits.

**Core Principle:** Safety must be assessed in terms of potential consequences and the system's ability to prevent, detect, and contain harmful behavior. Model-generated assurances are not substitutes for enforceable safeguards.

### 16. Model Drift, Production Monitoring, and Performance Degradation

Model risk does not end when the model is approved for production.

A model may become less reliable because of changes in input data, business conditions, user behavior, external dependencies, or the model itself. Monitoring helps determine whether the model continues to operate within its approved conditions.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the model's risk and should distinguish between technical availability, data changes, model performance, and harmful outcomes.

#### 16.1 Types of production monitoring

| Monitoring Category | What Is Monitored | Example Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Whether the model service is accessible | Error rate or service availability |
| Latency | Whether responses meet operational requirements | Response-time percentiles |
| Resource utilization | Whether processing demand remains manageable | Compute usage or request volume |
| Input-data quality | Whether production inputs meet defined requirements | Missing fields or invalid values |
| Data drift | Whether input distributions change | Population Stability Index or another suitable drift measure |
| Performance drift | Whether observed outcomes deteriorate | Change in task-specific performance metrics |
| Output quality | Whether generated or predicted outputs remain acceptable | Verified error rate or human-review findings |
| Fairness indicators | Whether relevant group-level outcomes change | Differences in error rates across relevant groups |
| Security signals | Whether suspicious or unauthorized behavior occurs | Abnormal requests or access attempts |
| Privacy signals | Whether sensitive information may be exposed | Confirmed or suspected disclosure events |
| Cost behavior | Whether model consumption remains within expected limits | Cost per request or daily consumption |
| Human oversight | Whether required reviews and interventions occur | Override rates or missed approval steps |

Monitoring metrics must be selected according to the model's actual risks. A drift indicator is not automatically evidence that the model has become harmful, and the absence of detected drift does not prove that model performance remains acceptable.

#### 16.2 Monitoring thresholds

The organization should define thresholds and escalation rules before relying on monitoring as a control.

| Threshold Level | Meaning | Expected Response |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | Metrics remain within approved operating ranges | Continue routine monitoring |
| Warning | A metric approaches a defined limit or exhibits a concerning trend | Investigate and determine whether additional testing is required |
| Breach | An approved threshold is exceeded | Initiate the defined escalation and corrective-action process |
| Critical | Evidence indicates unacceptable harm, material compromise, or severe degradation | Consider immediate containment, restricted operation, or suspension |

Thresholds should be justified and reviewed. A threshold suitable for one model may be inappropriate for another.

For example, a small performance decline in a low-impact recommendation model may warrant investigation, while a smaller decline in a high-impact detection model may require immediate intervention.

#### 16.3 Ground-truth limitations

Some model outcomes cannot be verified immediately.

A fraud model may require investigation results before its predictions can be evaluated. A forecasting model may not have a known outcome until the forecast period has elapsed. A generative assistant may require human review to determine whether an answer was correct and appropriately grounded.

The monitoring plan should document:

- Which outcomes can be observed directly.
- Which outcomes require delayed verification.
- How feedback is collected and validated.
- Whether human reviewers are sufficiently qualified.
- How incomplete or biased feedback is handled.
- How monitoring limitations affect confidence in the risk assessment.

The organization should not assume that user satisfaction, engagement, or acceptance rates are equivalent to correctness.

### 17. Model Change Management, Reassessment, and Revalidation

Changes to a model or its operating context can invalidate earlier assessment conclusions.

A change may affect performance, data suitability, security exposure, privacy risk, fairness, operational reliability, or the authority granted to the surrounding application.

The organization should therefore establish a controlled process for identifying, evaluating, approving, testing, and documenting material model changes.

#### 17.1 Types of model changes

| Change Type | Example | Potential Risk Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Model-version change | Replacing one provider model version with another | Different behavior, performance, or limitations |
| Fine-tuning change | Updating a model using new domain-specific data | New bias, data leakage, or unexpected behavior |
| Configuration change | Changing inference parameters or output constraints | Different output characteristics or control effectiveness |
| Prompt or instruction change | Revising instructions used by a generative model | Changed task behavior, refusal behavior, or interpretation |
| Data-source change | Adding a new training or retrieval source | New provenance, quality, privacy, or injection risks |
| Integration change | Connecting the model to a new enterprise application | Expanded data access and attack surface |
| Permission change | Granting additional tool or API access | Increased impact of model errors or manipulation |
| Intended-use change | Using the model for a new business purpose | Different performance, fairness, and compliance requirements |
| Deployment change | Moving from a managed service to self-hosting | New infrastructure, operations, and security responsibilities |
| Monitoring change | Modifying metrics or alert thresholds | Reduced ability to detect material degradation |
| Provider-service change | Provider changes retention, hosting, or service behavior | New contractual, privacy, or operational risks |

A change may be material even if it does not involve changing the underlying model weights. Altering the model's permissions or intended use can significantly increase risk without changing the model itself.

#### 17.2 Change classification

The organization may classify changes into three categories.

**Minor change:** A change with a demonstrated, limited effect on the model's approved purpose, behavior, or risk profile.

**Material change:** A change that may affect performance, data processing, security, fairness, operational behavior, or existing approval conditions.

**Critical change:** A change that could substantially alter the model's risk profile, expand consequential decision authority, affect a safety-critical process, or invalidate a key approval assumption.

The classification must follow documented organizational criteria. A developer's informal judgment should not be the sole basis for bypassing reassessment.

Emergency changes may require a separate expedited process, but the organization should still document the reason, authorized decision, compensating controls, and required retrospective review.

#### 17.4 Revalidation triggers

A reassessment or revalidation should be considered when:

- The model's intended purpose changes.
- The model version changes materially.
- New training or fine-tuning data is introduced.
- A provider changes model behavior or service conditions.
- New data sources are added.
- The model receives broader permissions.
- A new integration creates additional attack paths.
- Monitoring identifies significant drift.
- A security, privacy, fairness, or safety incident occurs.
- A new regulatory obligation becomes applicable.
- A previous assumption is found to be incorrect.
- A periodic review date is reached.

The required depth of reassessment should reflect the significance of the change and the risk it introduces.

### 18. Core Principle — Model Assurance Must Continue Throughout the Lifecycle

Model risk management is not a one-time activity completed before deployment. It is a lifecycle discipline that connects initial evaluation to production monitoring, controlled change, incident response, and periodic reassessment.

A model that was appropriate at deployment may become unsuitable because its data changes, its intended purpose expands, its provider modifies the service, or its operating environment evolves.

The organization should maintain an evidence trail showing how the model was evaluated, what limitations were identified, which controls were implemented, and how subsequent changes were governed.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Testing vs. validation | Testing evaluates defined behaviors; validation determines fitness for purpose based on broader evidence |
| Independent review vs. developer self-review | Independent review introduces appropriate separation from development and original claims |
| Generalization vs. memorization | Generalization concerns performance beyond development data; memorization may produce misleadingly strong training results |
| Distribution shift vs. model defect | Distribution shift concerns changed data or conditions; a model defect concerns a flaw in implementation or behavior, although both can coexist |
| Robustness vs. accuracy | Accuracy measures performance under defined conditions; robustness concerns behavior under relevant variation and stress |
| Explainability vs. correctness | An explanation can help interpret behavior but does not establish that the output is correct |
| Transparency vs. interpretability | Transparency concerns the availability of relevant information; interpretability concerns how readily behavior can be understood |
| Privacy control vs. privacy guarantee | A control may reduce a specific exposure; a guarantee requires clearly defined assumptions and evidence |
| Privacy risk vs. security risk | Privacy concerns the appropriate processing and potential exposure of personal information; security concerns protection against unauthorized access, alteration, disruption, or misuse |
| Safety safeguard vs. model refusal | A safeguard enforces or supports safe operation; a model refusal is only one behavior and may not prevent other execution paths |
| Drift detection vs. confirmed degradation | A drift signal identifies a change; further analysis may be needed to establish its effect on model performance |
| Monitoring vs. remediation | Monitoring detects or measures behavior; remediation changes the system or its operating conditions |
| Revalidation vs. routine monitoring | Revalidation reassesses suitability after relevant changes or triggers; routine monitoring observes ongoing behavior |
| Model update vs. approved change | A technical update becomes an approved change only through the required governance process |
| Conditional approval vs. risk acceptance | Conditional approval imposes defined requirements; risk acceptance is an authorized decision about residual risk under the organization's rules |

**Completion criterion:** Part 2 establishes the assurance mechanisms used to evaluate model validity, robustness, explainability, privacy, safety, production behavior, and change management. A defensible model risk decision should rely on suitable evidence, explicitly documented limitations, proportionate oversight, and lifecycle controls that remain effective after deployment.

## Part 3 — Lifecycle Governance, Third-Party Model Assurance, Incident Management, and Risk Treatment

### 19. Model Lifecycle Governance and Accountability

Model lifecycle governance establishes the accountability, decision rights, controls, and evidence requirements that govern an AI model from initial development or acquisition through deployment, operation, modification, and retirement.

An AI model may be technically sound but still create unacceptable enterprise risk if it is deployed without an accountable owner, used outside its approved purpose, modified without authorization, or retained in production after its assumptions are no longer valid.

The objective of lifecycle governance is to ensure that every model has a defined governance status, an accountable owner, documented approval conditions, and a controlled process for changes and retirement.

#### 19.1 Model lifecycle stages

A typical enterprise model lifecycle includes the following stages:

| Lifecycle Stage | Primary Activities | Governance Requirements | Expected Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification | Identify a business need or proposed AI use case | Establish an accountable business sponsor and preliminary scope | Use-case proposal |
| 2. Classification | Determine the model's purpose, impact, and risk tier | Apply the organization's model classification criteria | Classification record |
| 3. Acquisition or development | Select a provider, develop a model, or adapt an existing model | Review data, security, privacy, intellectual property, and contractual considerations | Development or acquisition assessment |
| 4. Initial assessment | Assess model risks and limitations | Identify inherent risks, controls, and residual risks | Model risk assessment |
| 5. Validation | Evaluate performance, robustness, security, and suitability | Establish whether the evidence supports the proposed use | Validation report |
| 6. Approval | Determine whether the model may be deployed and under what conditions | Obtain authorization from the appropriate decision-maker | Formal approval record |
| 7. Deployment | Integrate the model into the production environment | Verify approved configuration, permissions, interfaces, and safeguards | Deployment evidence |
| 8. Operation | Monitor behavior, performance, security, and risk indicators | Maintain oversight and execute defined escalation procedures | Monitoring records |
| 9. Change and reassessment | Modify the model, data, configuration, or use case | Reassess material changes and obtain appropriate approval | Change and revalidation records |
| 10. Retirement | Withdraw the model from use | Revoke access, address data retention, preserve required evidence, and update inventories | Retirement record |

Organizations may use different lifecycle terminology, but the governance responsibilities and decision points should remain explicit.

#### 19.2 Model governance roles

Model governance requires coordination across business, technical, security, risk, privacy, legal, and operational functions.

| Role | Primary Accountability | Typical Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| Business Model Owner | Business accountability for the approved model use | Defines intended use, confirms business requirements, and owns business risk |
| Model Developer | Technical development or adaptation | Documents methodology, assumptions, limitations, and testing |
| Model Validator | Independent or appropriately separated validation | Reviews validation methodology, evidence, and conclusions |
| AI Governance Function | Consistency of AI governance processes | Maintains governance requirements, classification criteria, and oversight processes |
| Cybersecurity | Security risk assessment | Evaluates model threats, architecture, access, integrity, and security controls |
| Data Owner | Accountability for relevant data assets | Confirms data authorization, classification, quality, and permitted use |
| Privacy Function | Privacy and personal-data oversight | Evaluates relevant privacy risks and data-protection obligations |
| Legal and Compliance | Legal and regulatory oversight | Assesses applicable obligations, contractual requirements, and restrictions |
| Model Operations or Platform Team | Operational reliability and controlled deployment | Implements monitoring, configuration management, rollback, and operational safeguards |
| Internal Audit | Independent assurance over governance and controls | Evaluates control design and effectiveness within its mandate |
| Risk Acceptance Authority | Formal acceptance of risks within delegated authority | Approves or rejects residual-risk acceptance where authorized |

One person may perform multiple roles in a smaller organization. However, the organization should avoid conflicts of interest, particularly when the same individual develops a model, validates it, and approves its use.

A model should not advance automatically from one lifecycle stage to the next.

9. Are approval conditions tracked to completion?

### 20. Model Versioning, Configuration Control, and Artifact Integrity

Model versioning and configuration control provide traceability between the model that was assessed, the model that was approved, and the model actually operating in production.

Without adequate version control, an organization may be unable to determine whether its current model is the same model that passed validation or whether a change introduced new risks.

For third-party hosted models, exact internal model weights may not be accessible. In such cases, the organization should capture the strongest available provider identifiers and configuration evidence.

#### 20.1 What should be version-controlled?

Version control should cover more than model weights.

| Component | Examples | Governance Importance |
|---|---|---|
| Model artifact | Weights, binary artifacts, or model package | Establishes the assessed technical artifact |
| Model identifier | Provider model ID, release identifier, or deployment ID | Helps identify the model being used |
| Model version | Version number, dated release, or provider revision | Supports change tracking |
| Training configuration | Hyperparameters, training procedure, and selected features | Supports reproducibility and review |
| Training dataset reference | Dataset identifier, version, or approved snapshot | Establishes data lineage |
| Evaluation datasets | Test-set identifiers and versions | Supports interpretation of validation results |
| Prompt templates | System instructions and approved prompt versions | Helps track behavior changes in generative systems |
| Inference configuration | Temperature, sampling parameters, output limits, and other relevant settings | Supports reproducibility and behavior analysis |
| Retrieval configuration | Index version, retrieval settings, and source collection | Helps track changes in retrieved information |
| Safety configuration | Content filters, policy settings, and safety constraints | Supports verification of safeguards |
| Tool configuration | Approved APIs, functions, and permissions | Establishes the model's authorized capabilities |
| Deployment configuration | Environment, endpoints, network controls, and access policies | Connects model approval to production deployment |
| Monitoring configuration | Metrics, thresholds, and alert rules | Supports ongoing assurance |

The required level of detail should be proportionate to model risk and the capabilities of the deployment environment.

#### 20.2 Artifact integrity

For artifacts controlled by the organization, integrity controls may include:

- Cryptographic hashes of approved model artifacts.
- Restricted access to model repositories.
- Signed artifacts or verified provenance where supported.
- Controlled build and deployment pipelines.
- Separation of development and production environments.
- Approval records for artifact promotion.
- Audit logs for artifact modification.
- Verification that the deployed artifact matches the approved artifact.

For a hosted model service, the organization may instead rely on provider release identifiers, documented deployment configuration, contractual change notifications, service records, and available technical evidence.

A hash proves that two files are identical with respect to the hash calculation, assuming the integrity mechanism is sound. It does not prove that the model is safe, accurate, or free from malicious behavior.

#### 20.3 Model change traceability

Each production model should have a traceable relationship between:

1. The registered model identity.
2. The assessed version or provider release.
3. The approved configuration.
4. The associated validation evidence.
5. The approved intended use.
6. The deployment record.
7. The monitoring configuration.
8. The applicable risk assessment.
9. The approval decision.
10. The change history.

This relationship enables the organization to investigate incidents and establish whether a problem originated from model changes, configuration changes, data changes, or the surrounding application.

#### 20.4 Model configuration record

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Model ID | Unique inventory identifier |
| Model Provider | Internal development team or external provider |
| Model Name | Name of the model |
| Model Version | Exact known version or release identifier |
| Deployment ID | Identifier for the deployed instance |
| Artifact Reference | Repository or artifact location |
| Artifact Hash | Hash where applicable |
| Training Data Reference | Dataset version or documented source |
| Evaluation Reference | Validation report and test evidence |
| Prompt Version | Approved prompt or instruction version, if applicable |
| Retrieval Version | Approved index or knowledge-source version, if applicable |
| Inference Settings | Relevant generation or inference parameters |
| Tool Permissions | Authorized tools, APIs, and functions |
| Safety Configuration | Relevant safety settings and enforcement controls |
| Approval Reference | Governance decision identifier |
| Deployment Date | Date the version entered production |
| Change Reference | Related change record |
| Configuration Owner | Responsible technical owner |

#### 20.5 Configuration drift

Configuration drift occurs when the deployed configuration differs from the approved baseline.

Examples include:

- A model receives access to a new API without authorization.
- A prompt is modified outside the approved change process.
- A retrieval index includes unapproved documents.
- A security filter is disabled.
- A deployment changes to a different provider model version.
- An inference setting changes materially.
- Monitoring thresholds are altered without appropriate review.

The organization should define how drift is detected, how deviations are investigated, and when the deployment must be restored or reassessed.

**Core Principle:** The organization must be able to identify what is operating, how it is configured, and which evidence supports its approval. Version control and integrity checks provide traceability, but they do not replace substantive model validation.

### 21. Third-Party Model Risk and Provider Assurance

Organizations increasingly obtain models through external AI providers, cloud platforms, SaaS applications, foundation-model APIs, embedded AI features, and managed AI services.

Using an external model does not eliminate the organization's responsibility to evaluate the risks associated with its intended use.

The provider may control the underlying model while the customer controls the business process, prompts, data sources, permissions, integrations, and decisions made using the output.

The assessment must establish which party controls each relevant component and which risks remain with the organization using the model.

#### 21.1 Third-party model dependency categories

| Dependency | Example | Potential Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation model | External large language model | Limited visibility into training data, internal behavior, or model changes |
| Hosted inference service | Managed model API | Service availability, retention, processing, or provider dependency |
| Embedded AI feature | AI capability included in an enterprise SaaS platform | Limited control over model selection, configuration, or release timing |
| External model artifact | Pretrained model downloaded from a repository | Artifact integrity, provenance, licensing, and vulnerability concerns |
| Fine-tuning provider | Supplier adapts a model using organization-specific data | Data handling, confidentiality, and ownership concerns |
| AI-enabled application | Vendor application built on one or more models | Limited transparency into underlying model dependencies |
| Retrieval provider | External service manages embeddings or vector search | Data exposure, tenant isolation, and retrieval integrity risks |
| AI agent platform | Provider supplies models and tool-execution capabilities | Expanded permissions, execution risks, and dependency complexity |

A single AI solution may involve several of these dependencies. The organization should assess the complete service chain rather than reviewing only the primary model provider.

#### 21.2 Provider assurance domains

The assessment should consider the following areas.

**A. Model transparency and documentation**

- Is the model's intended purpose documented?
- Are known limitations disclosed?
- Are relevant evaluation results available?
- Are supported use cases and prohibited uses documented?
- Does the provider explain material changes to the service?
- Is sufficient information available to assess the organization's intended use?

**B. Data processing and confidentiality**

- What data is transmitted to the provider?
- Is customer data used to train or improve provider models?
- What retention options are available?
- Where is data processed and stored?
- Are subcontractors involved?
- How are data deletion requests handled?
- Are appropriate contractual and privacy arrangements in place?

**C. Security controls**

- How is customer access authenticated and authorized?
- Is tenant isolation addressed?
- How are service credentials protected?
- Are relevant security assurance reports available?
- How are vulnerabilities and security incidents managed?
- Are audit logs and security notifications available?
- Can the organization restrict service access to approved environments?

**D. Availability and resilience**

- What availability commitments apply?
- Are service limitations documented?
- Are quotas and rate limits understood?
- Is there an alternative operating procedure if the service becomes unavailable?
- Can the organization migrate to another provider or model?
- What happens when the provider retires a model version?

**E. Governance and legal requirements**

- Are the provider's contractual terms compatible with the intended use?
- Are intellectual-property and usage restrictions understood?
- Are applicable regulatory requirements assessed?
- Are material changes communicated in time for review?
- Are responsibilities for incidents and investigations documented?
- Is the provider's use of subprocessors or other model providers sufficiently transparent?

Provider assurance should be based on evidence such as contracts, technical documentation, independent assurance reports, security questionnaires, service commitments, and direct clarification from the provider.

#### 21.3 Shared responsibility model

Responsibility varies by service type and contractual arrangement.

| Control Area | Typical Provider Responsibility | Typical Customer Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation model development | Model development and provider-side testing | Determine suitability for the intended business use |
| Model hosting | Infrastructure and service operation | Configure and use the service appropriately |
| Customer data access | Provider-side safeguards and isolation mechanisms | Limit data submitted and configure access correctly |
| Identity and access | Service authentication capabilities | Manage identities, credentials, permissions, and access policies |
| Application integration | API capabilities and documented service behavior | Secure integration, application logic, and downstream actions |
| Model evaluation | Provider documentation and available evaluation evidence | Validate the model in the organization's own context |
| Business decisions | Generally outside the provider's direct control unless explicitly agreed otherwise | Establish decision authority, human oversight, and accountability |
| Monitoring | Provider service and infrastructure monitoring | Monitor the use case, output quality, business outcomes, and local controls |
| Incident handling | Investigate provider-side incidents within its responsibility | Detect local incidents, assess impact, contain exposure, and coordinate response |
| Regulatory compliance | Fulfil applicable provider obligations | Assess and fulfil applicable obligations associated with the organization's use |
| Retirement and migration | Provide documented service lifecycle information where available | Plan migration, update integrations, and retire dependent components |

This table is a starting point, not a universal contractual allocation. Actual responsibilities must be confirmed against the service architecture, contract, and operating model.

#### 21.4 Third-party model risk assessment template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Provider ID | Unique supplier reference |
| Provider Name | External provider |
| Service Name | Model or AI service |
| Model Identifier | Known model name and version |
| Business Owner | Accountable internal owner |
| Intended Use | Approved use case |
| Data Classification | Classification of data submitted or made available |
| Processing Location | Known processing and storage locations |
| Training Use | Provider's documented use of customer data for training or improvement |
| Retention | Applicable service retention arrangements |
| Security Assurance | Available security certifications, reports, and technical evidence |
| Model Documentation | Available model and evaluation documentation |
| Change Notification | Provider notification and version-management arrangements |
| Availability | Relevant service commitments and limitations |

| Residual Risk | Remaining third-party exposure |
| Decision | Approved, conditionally approved, rejected, or pending |

#### 21.5 Provider exit and concentration risk

An enterprise may become dependent on a single model provider for a critical process.

Potential consequences include:

- Disruption when the provider experiences an outage.
- Loss of access when the provider retires a model.
- Increased costs or changed service terms.
- Reduced negotiating leverage.
- Migration difficulty due to proprietary APIs or formats.
- Loss of application behavior consistency after switching models.
- Inability to reproduce prior outputs following a provider update.

A suitable exit strategy may include documented dependencies, alternative providers, abstraction layers, fallback processes, data portability, migration testing, and procedures for revalidating a replacement model.

Using multiple providers does not automatically eliminate concentration risk. Multiple applications may still depend on the same underlying model, cloud platform, or critical infrastructure provider.

**Core Principle:** Third-party models introduce dependencies that must be assessed across technical, contractual, operational, and governance boundaries. Provider assurance supports the organization's decision but does not replace validation of the model in its actual business context.

### 22. Model Incident Management, Investigation, and Recovery

A model incident is an event in which model behavior, a model-related component, or the surrounding operating process causes or may cause a material violation of approved requirements, unacceptable harm, security compromise, or operational disruption.

Not every model error is a security incident. However, a model error may become an incident when its impact, scale, exploitability, or consequences exceed defined thresholds.

The organization should establish a model incident process integrated with cybersecurity incident response, privacy incident management, business continuity, and relevant safety or compliance procedures.

#### 22.1 Model incident categories

| Incident Category | Description | Illustrative Example |
|---|---|---|
| Model integrity compromise | Unauthorized alteration or replacement of a model or associated artifact | A production model artifact is replaced without authorization |
| Unauthorized disclosure | A model or related system exposes protected information | An assistant returns another customer's confidential records |
| Adversarial manipulation | Malicious inputs or techniques influence model behavior | An attacker manipulates model inputs to bypass a security decision |
| Unsafe output | A model produces a recommendation that creates a material risk of harm | A model recommends an unsafe operational action |
| Material performance failure | Model quality deteriorates beyond an approved threshold | A detection model begins missing a significant proportion of relevant events |
| Unauthorized model use | A model is used outside its approved purpose or authority | An employee uses a restricted model for an unapproved consequential decision |
| Configuration compromise | A model's settings, access, or controls are altered improperly | A tool permission is expanded without authorization |
| Provider incident | An external provider experiences a relevant security or availability incident | A provider reports a compromise affecting customer data |
| Governance breach | A mandatory approval or control requirement is bypassed | An unapproved model version is deployed to production |
| Data poisoning or contamination | Training, fine-tuning, or retrieval data is manipulated or corrupted | Untrusted content is inserted into a knowledge source and influences outputs |

The organization should define incident severity using its established incident classification methodology, considering actual and potential impact.

#### 22.3 Immediate containment options

Depending on the incident, containment may involve:

- Suspending the affected model deployment.
- Restricting access to the model or affected feature.
- Disabling tool execution or high-risk integrations.
- Revoking compromised credentials.
- Blocking a compromised data source.
- Disabling retrieval from affected documents or indexes.
- Reverting to a previously approved model version.
- Switching to a controlled manual process.
- Increasing human review requirements.
- Restricting sensitive data processing.
- Applying temporary network or API restrictions.

Containment decisions should consider the consequences of stopping the model. For critical business processes, a documented fallback procedure may be necessary to avoid creating a separate operational risk.

#### 22.4 Evidence preservation

Model incidents can be difficult to investigate if relevant evidence is overwritten, unavailable, or insufficiently linked to the affected version.

Depending on the incident, evidence may include:

- Model identifiers and version information.
- Deployment and configuration records.
- Relevant prompts and outputs, subject to appropriate privacy controls.
- Input-data references.
- Retrieval sources and document identifiers.
- Tool calls and API activity.
- Authentication and authorization logs.
- Security alerts and network records.
- Monitoring metrics.
- Change records.
- Provider incident notifications.
- Human-review decisions.
- Relevant timestamps and correlation identifiers.

Evidence collection should follow the organization's security, privacy, retention, and legal requirements. The organization should avoid indiscriminately retaining sensitive content merely because it may be useful for future investigations.

#### 22.5 Root-cause analysis

The investigation should determine whether the incident originated from:

- A model defect or limitation.
- Inadequate evaluation.
- Data quality or provenance problems.
- Distribution shift.
- Prompt or configuration changes.
- Unauthorized access or artifact modification.
- Application integration defects.
- Inadequate authorization controls.
- Unsafe tool permissions.
- Retrieval contamination.
- Provider-side changes or failures.
- Insufficient monitoring.
- Governance or approval failures.
- Human misuse or inadequate training.
- Multiple interacting causes.

The investigation should distinguish the immediate trigger from underlying control weaknesses.

For example, a model may return a confidential document because a prompt was manipulated. However, the root cause may be that document retrieval did not enforce authorization at the data-access layer.

#### 22.7 Post-incident reassessment

A material model incident should trigger a review of the affected model's risk assessment.

The review should determine whether:

1. The original threat model omitted a relevant attack path.
2. Validation failed to test a material scenario.
3. Existing safeguards were ineffective.
4. The risk rating underestimated impact or likelihood.
5. Monitoring thresholds were insufficient.
6. Approval conditions were not enforced.
7. Similar models or applications are exposed to the same weakness.
8. Provider assurance or contractual arrangements require reconsideration.
9. Additional training or process changes are needed.
10. Revalidation is required before the model resumes operation.

**Core Principle:** Model incident management must connect detection, containment, investigation, recovery, and governance improvement. Restoring service without addressing the underlying risk may allow the same failure to recur.

### 23. Model Retirement, Decommissioning, and Residual Data Risk

Model retirement is the controlled withdrawal of a model from its approved operating environment.

Retirement may occur because the model is obsolete, replaced, no longer needed, no longer supported by its provider, too risky to operate, or no longer appropriate for its intended purpose.

Decommissioning should address the model itself and the surrounding components that may continue to expose data or preserve operational dependencies.

#### 23.1 Retirement triggers

Common triggers include:

- Business requirements change.
- The model fails to meet required performance thresholds.
- Residual risk becomes unacceptable.
- A provider retires or materially changes the model.
- A replacement model is approved.
- Relevant legal or contractual requirements change.
- Required controls cannot be maintained.
- The model becomes unsupported or vulnerable.
- A material incident demonstrates unacceptable risk.
- The organization can no longer establish that the model is suitable for its intended purpose.

Retirement decisions should be documented and authorized according to the organization's governance requirements.

#### 23.2 Retirement activities

| Activity | Purpose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Confirm retirement decision | Establish authorization and scope | Approved retirement record |
| Identify dependencies | Determine which applications and processes use the model | Dependency inventory |
| Notify stakeholders | Coordinate business, technical, and supplier activities | Communication record |
| Disable production access | Prevent further unauthorized or unintended use | Access and deployment records |
| Revoke credentials | Remove access to APIs, service accounts, and associated tools | Credential-revocation evidence |
| Remove integrations | Eliminate obsolete connections and execution paths | Configuration or change records |
| Address model artifacts | Securely archive or dispose of controlled artifacts | Artifact disposition record |
| Address training and inference data | Apply relevant retention, deletion, and legal requirements | Data disposition evidence |
| Address retrieval assets | Review indexes, embeddings, caches, and associated documents | Retrieval-system disposition record |
| Review logs and backups | Apply applicable retention and deletion requirements | Retention and deletion records |
| Update inventory | Change the model's status and record retirement details | Updated model inventory |
| Preserve required evidence | Retain records required for audit, legal, security, or regulatory purposes | Evidence archive reference |
| Verify completion | Confirm that the model is no longer operationally accessible | Decommissioning verification |

Retirement does not necessarily mean deleting all records immediately. The organization must reconcile deletion requirements with applicable retention obligations, legal holds, audit needs, and contractual requirements.

#### 23.3 Model retirement versus data deletion

These are distinct activities.

Retiring a model prevents or ends its authorized use. Data deletion concerns the removal of particular information from specified systems or processing contexts.

For example, retiring a model may leave archived evaluation reports, audit records, or legally required logs. Conversely, deleting a source dataset does not necessarily remove copies, derived artifacts, embeddings, or memorized information associated with an already trained model.

The organization should determine the applicable treatment for each relevant asset rather than assuming that one action automatically resolves the other.

Where deletion from model parameters is required or requested, the organization should assess technical feasibility, applicable obligations, provider capabilities, and the need for retraining or other remediation. Such deletion may be technically complex and cannot be assumed to occur simply because a source record has been removed.

### 24. Model Risk Treatment, Residual Risk, and Acceptance

Model risk treatment determines what the organization will do when an assessment identifies risks that require action.

The objective is not to eliminate every conceivable model risk. It is to identify material risks, select proportionate treatments, verify the effectiveness of controls, and determine whether the remaining risk is acceptable under the organization's risk appetite and approval framework.

Risk treatment should be traceable from the original finding through remediation and the final governance decision.

#### 24.1 Risk treatment options

| Treatment Option | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Do not proceed with the proposed model use | Reject a model that cannot meet mandatory requirements |
| Mitigate | Implement controls that reduce likelihood or impact | Introduce authorization checks and independent review |
| Transfer or share | Allocate defined responsibilities or financial consequences to another party | Use contractual remedies or insurance where appropriate |
| Restrict | Narrow the model's permitted scope or authority | Permit recommendations but prohibit autonomous execution |
| Accept | Formally accept residual risk through an authorized decision | Approve a documented residual risk within delegated authority |
| Retire | Withdraw an existing model from use | Decommission a model whose risks are no longer acceptable |

Transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability for obligations that remain legally or operationally its responsibility. Likewise, acceptance must not be used to override mandatory legal, contractual, or security requirements.

#### 24.2 Inherent risk and residual risk

**Inherent risk** is the risk before considering the relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after the applicable controls have been considered, taking account of their design and demonstrated effectiveness.

An assessment should not reduce residual risk merely because a control is documented. The assessor should consider whether the control is implemented, operates consistently, and has evidence supporting its effectiveness.

For example, a model may have a high inherent risk because its outputs influence consequential customer decisions. Human review may reduce the likelihood of harmful decisions, but only if reviewers have sufficient information, authority, time, and competence to identify and challenge incorrect outputs.

#### 24.5 Risk acceptance requirements

A defensible risk acceptance record should include:

- A clear description of the risk scenario.
- The model, application, and business process affected.
- The inherent and residual risk ratings.
- The controls already implemented.
- The rationale for accepting the residual risk.
- The potential consequences of the risk materializing.
- Any restrictions on model use.
- The person or body authorized to accept the risk.
- The acceptance date and any expiration date.
- Conditions that trigger reassessment.
- Monitoring requirements.
- Any required notification to relevant stakeholders.

Acceptance should be time-bound where appropriate, especially when it relies on temporary compensating controls or incomplete remediation.

#### 24.6 Risk acceptance example

An organization uses an AI model to prioritize customer-service requests.

The model demonstrates acceptable performance for the approved purpose but performs less reliably for a small category of requests.

The organization determines that unrestricted use is not justified.

Potential treatment measures include:

1. Restricting the model's use for the affected category.
2. Routing uncertain predictions to trained personnel.
3. Introducing a minimum confidence or quality requirement, where technically justified.
4. Monitoring outcomes for the affected category.
5. Collecting additional representative evaluation data.
6. Reassessing the model after remediation.

If the remaining risk is accepted, the decision should explicitly identify the restricted operating conditions and the authority responsible for approval.

A general statement that “the model is sufficiently accurate overall” would not adequately address the identified limitation.

**Core Principle:** Risk acceptance is an accountable governance decision, not a substitute for remediation. Residual risk must be supported by evidence, compared with applicable acceptance criteria, and approved by an authorized decision-maker.

### 25. Core Principle — Model Risk Must Remain Traceable and Actionable

An enterprise model risk assessment should create a traceable connection between model identification, risk evaluation, technical evidence, governance decisions, production controls, and corrective actions.

This traceability enables the organization to establish not only that an assessment was performed, but also whether its conclusions remain valid and whether required actions have been completed.

A model risk assessment becomes ineffective when findings are recorded without owners, controls are claimed without verification, or approval decisions are disconnected from the actual production configuration.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Model lifecycle governance vs. model validation | Lifecycle governance controls the model's overall use; validation evaluates whether evidence supports fitness for purpose |
| Model owner vs. model developer | The owner is accountable for the approved business use; the developer is responsible for specified technical development activities |
| Approval vs. deployment | Approval authorizes use under defined conditions; deployment implements the model in the operating environment |
| Model artifact vs. model configuration | The artifact is the model itself or its packaged representation; configuration determines how it is operated and connected |
| Artifact integrity vs. model safety | Integrity checks help establish whether an artifact has changed; safety assessment evaluates potential harmful behavior |
| Provider assurance vs. customer validation | Provider assurance evaluates supplier capabilities and controls; customer validation determines suitability for the customer's intended use |
| Shared responsibility vs. transferred accountability | Responsibilities may be allocated between parties, but the organization retains obligations that remain its responsibility |
| Incident trigger vs. root cause | The trigger initiates an event; root-cause analysis identifies underlying technical or governance weaknesses |
| Containment vs. remediation | Containment limits immediate exposure; remediation addresses the underlying weakness |
| Model retirement vs. data deletion | Retirement ends authorized model use; deletion concerns the treatment of particular data and associated assets |
| Inherent risk vs. residual risk | Inherent risk is assessed before relevant controls; residual risk remains after considering the controls and their effectiveness |
| Documented control vs. effective control | Documentation describes a control; effectiveness requires evidence that it is implemented and operates as intended |
| Risk treatment vs. risk acceptance | Treatment changes the risk or avoids the use; acceptance formally authorizes residual risk within the applicable governance framework |
| Conditional approval vs. unrestricted approval | Conditional approval imposes specific requirements; unrestricted approval does not carry those same outstanding conditions |
| Model retirement vs. service shutdown | Service shutdown may disable an endpoint; retirement also addresses dependencies, artifacts, access, data, and records |

**Completion criterion:** Part 3 establishes the governance and operational mechanisms required to maintain control over model ownership, versioning, external dependencies, incidents, retirement, and residual risk. A mature model risk program must ensure that every material risk leads to an accountable decision, an appropriate treatment, and verifiable evidence.
