# 20.10 — AI Use-Case Assessment Template

## Part 1 — Foundations, Scope Definition, Business Context, and Inherent Risk Classification

### 1. Introduction to AI Use-Case Assessment

An AI use-case assessment is a structured evaluation of a specific business activity that uses artificial intelligence to produce outputs, support decisions, automate workflows, generate content, analyze information, or execute actions.

Its primary purpose is to determine whether a proposed AI application is appropriate for its intended purpose, whether its risks are understood, and whether the organization can implement the necessary governance, security, privacy, operational, and human oversight controls.

The assessment focuses on the **use case**, rather than examining the AI model or supplier in isolation.

A single model can support multiple use cases with materially different risk profiles. For example, a generative AI model used to summarize public documents may present a different risk profile from the same model used to analyze confidential employee records or recommend actions affecting customer eligibility.

The assessment therefore evaluates the relationship between the AI technology, the intended business purpose, the users, the data, the operating environment, and the consequences of failure.

An enterprise-grade assessment should establish:

- The business problem the AI application is intended to solve.
- The intended purpose and permitted use boundaries.
- The AI capabilities and system components involved.
- The users, affected individuals, and relevant stakeholders.
- The data processed and its sensitivity.
- The degree of automation and human involvement.
- The potential benefits, harms, and unintended consequences.
- The applicable security, privacy, regulatory, and contractual requirements.
- The inherent risk before considering proposed controls.
- The approvals, safeguards, and evidence required before deployment.

The assessment should occur before a material commitment to deployment and should be revisited when the use case changes significantly.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Use-Case Assessment Template

The AI Use-Case Assessment Template provides a standardized mechanism for deciding whether an AI initiative may proceed, requires additional analysis, or should be rejected.

It helps the organization distinguish legitimate business opportunities from proposals that are insufficiently defined, disproportionate to the intended benefit, or incompatible with its risk appetite and obligations.

The template should support five primary objectives.

**Objective 1 — Business justification**

Confirm that the use case addresses a defined business need and that the expected benefits justify the proposed AI capability.

**Objective 2 — Risk identification**

Identify foreseeable risks involving cybersecurity, data protection, reliability, fairness, transparency, safety, human oversight, third-party dependency, and operational resilience.

**Objective 3 — Governance and accountability**

Establish accountable business ownership, technical ownership, control responsibilities, approval authority, and escalation paths.

**Objective 4 — Deployment authorization**

Determine the conditions that must be satisfied before the AI application can be tested, piloted, or deployed into production.

**Objective 5 — Lifecycle control**

Establish the reassessment triggers, monitoring requirements, change controls, and retirement conditions applicable to the use case.

The template should not be treated as a substitute for detailed specialist assessments. High-risk use cases may require separate AI impact assessments, security architecture reviews, privacy assessments, model risk assessments, AI vendor assessments, or legal analysis.

The use-case assessment acts as the initial decision and coordination layer that determines which additional reviews are necessary.

### 3. AI Use-Case Assessment as a Governance Control

An AI use-case assessment is a preventive governance control. It creates a formal decision point before the organization introduces AI into a business process.

Without this control, teams may adopt AI tools independently, connect them to sensitive information, automate consequential activities, or create dependencies before the associated risks have been evaluated.

The assessment should be integrated into existing enterprise processes rather than operating as an isolated form.

Relevant integration points include:

- Business case and investment approval.
- Enterprise architecture review.
- Procurement and third-party risk management.
- Cybersecurity risk assessment.
- Privacy and data protection review.
- Legal and regulatory review.
- Software development and change management.
- Model lifecycle management.
- Production release approval.
- Operational risk management.
- Internal audit and compliance assurance.

The assessment should establish an auditable relationship between the original business proposal and the final authorized implementation.

A use case that passes initial screening must not automatically receive production approval. Initial acceptance means that the proposal may proceed through the required assessment process; it does not establish that all deployment conditions have been satisfied.

A useful governance sequence is:

```
Business Idea or AI Request
           |
           v
Use-Case Registration
           |
           v
Initial Screening
           |
           v
Business Purpose and Scope Definition
           |
           v
Inherent Risk Classification
           |
           v
Specialist Assessments Required?
           |
           v
Detailed Risk and Control Evaluation
           |
           v
Authorized Governance Decision
           |
           v
Pilot / Controlled Deployment
           |
           v
Production Approval and Monitoring
```

The organization should maintain records of rejected, deferred, approved, and conditionally approved use cases. These records help identify recurring risks, duplicated initiatives, and opportunities to improve the governance process.

### 4. Distinguishing an AI Use Case from an AI System, Model, and Vendor

Accurate classification requires distinguishing the business use case from the technology and supplier components that support it.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable.

| Concept        | Definition                                                                                          | Example                                                                                            |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI use case    | The business activity or purpose for which AI is applied                                            | Summarizing customer support conversations                                                         |
| AI system      | The integrated technical system that produces AI-enabled outputs or actions                         | A support application integrating an LLM, retrieval service, identity controls, and workflow tools |
| AI model       | The computational model that performs inference or generates predictions or content                 | A large language model used to summarize conversations                                             |
| AI vendor      | The external organization supplying a model, platform, service, or component                        | A provider hosting the language model API                                                          |
| AI application | The software product or application through which the use case is delivered                         | An internal customer-support assistant                                                             |
| AI agent       | An AI-enabled component that can select or invoke tools to pursue a goal within defined permissions | An assistant that retrieves customer records and drafts a response                                 |
| AI workflow    | The sequence of tasks, decisions, integrations, and approvals through which the use case operates   | Retrieve a record, summarize it, draft a response, and request human approval                      |

One use case may depend on several AI systems, models, and vendors. Conversely, one AI system may support several use cases.

The organization should maintain traceability across these relationships so that a change in one component can be evaluated for its effect on every affected business activity.

For example, replacing a model provider may affect several applications. Enabling agentic tool execution in one application may materially change the risk of a specific use case without changing the underlying model.

The assessment record should therefore include identifiers linking the use case to the relevant system inventory, model inventory, vendor inventory, data assets, and risk register.

### 5. Standards, Frameworks, and Regulatory Context

The assessment methodology should align with the organization's governance framework and applicable legal obligations.

Relevant references may include:

- **ISO/IEC 42001** — AI management system requirements and associated governance processes.
- **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** — AI risk management through the Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage functions.
- **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** — Cybersecurity risk governance and management.
- **ISO/IEC 27001** — Information security management system requirements.
- **ISO/IEC 23894** — Guidance on AI risk management.
- **Applicable data protection law** — Requirements governing personal data processing, individual rights, and related safeguards.
- **Applicable AI legislation** — Requirements determined by the AI system's role, intended purpose, jurisdiction, and risk classification.
- **Sector-specific requirements** — Additional obligations for areas such as financial services, healthcare, telecommunications, or critical infrastructure.

These references serve different purposes. They should not be treated as interchangeable or as proof of compliance merely because they are mentioned in the assessment template.

The organization should map applicable requirements to specific assessment questions, control owners, required evidence, and approval conditions.

Where a legal classification or obligation is uncertain, the assessment should document the uncertainty and obtain appropriate legal or compliance review before the organization relies on a particular interpretation.

The use-case assessment should also record the framework versions and regulatory assumptions applied, especially where the decision may later be reviewed by an auditor, regulator, customer, or internal governance body.

### 6. Ownership and Responsibilities

Each AI use case should have clearly assigned accountability. Responsibility must extend beyond the technical team that builds or configures the solution.

The business owner is accountable for the purpose and business consequences of the use case. The system owner is responsible for the technical service and its operational management. Specialist functions evaluate risks within their areas of expertise, while the designated governance authority makes decisions within its delegated powers.

| Role                                | Primary responsibility                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Business owner                      | Defines the business purpose, expected benefits, operational requirements, and business accountability |
| Use-case sponsor                    | Initiates the proposal and secures support and resources                                               |
| AI system owner                     | Maintains system documentation, configuration, operational controls, and technical accountability      |
| AI governance function              | Coordinates governance requirements, classification, review, and decision traceability                 |
| Cybersecurity                       | Evaluates security threats, architecture, access controls, vulnerabilities, and incident readiness     |
| Privacy or data protection function | Evaluates applicable privacy obligations and personal data risks                                       |
| Legal and compliance                | Determines relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, and policy obligations                             |
| Enterprise architecture             | Reviews technical fit, integration patterns, dependencies, and architectural constraints               |
| Data owner or steward               | Confirms data classification, permitted use, access requirements, and data quality responsibilities    |
| Human oversight owner               | Defines review responsibilities, escalation procedures, and intervention authority                     |
| Risk owner                          | Owns eligible residual risks within the assigned authority                                             |
| Internal audit                      | Provides independent assurance over governance and control effectiveness                               |

The organization should identify one accountable business owner for each use case, even when multiple departments contribute to its implementation.

Where the use case affects several business units, a coordinating owner should be designated and the responsibilities of participating units documented.

No role should be assumed to have approval authority simply because it participates in the assessment. Approval rights must be defined in the organization's governance and delegation framework.

### 7. Use-Case Record Identification

Each AI use case should have a unique identifier that remains stable throughout its lifecycle.

The identifier enables the organization to link the assessment to related business cases, technical systems, models, vendors, datasets, risks, incidents, approvals, and changes.

The initial record should capture the following information.

| Field                    | Description                                                          |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Use-case ID              | Unique identifier assigned by the organization                       |
| Use-case name            | Short, descriptive name                                              |
| Business unit            | Department or function sponsoring the use case                       |
| Business owner           | Accountable business role or named owner                             |
| System owner             | Role responsible for the technical implementation                    |
| Use-case description     | Summary of the proposed AI-enabled activity                          |
| Intended purpose         | Explicit statement of the objective the AI is expected to support    |
| AI system references     | Identifiers of the systems supporting the use case                   |
| Model references         | Identifiers of the models used, where known                          |
| Vendor references        | External providers supporting the use case                           |
| Data asset references    | Relevant datasets, repositories, and data classifications            |
| Assessment version       | Version of the assessment record                                     |
| Submission date          | Date the use case entered the assessment process                     |
| Assessment status        | Current stage of review                                              |
| Proposed deployment date | Intended pilot or production date                                    |
| Lifecycle status         | Proposed, under assessment, pilot, production, suspended, or retired |

The record should distinguish the assessment status from the lifecycle status. A use case may be in production while a reassessment is underway, or it may be approved for a pilot but not yet authorized for production.

Changes to key fields should be recorded with the date, responsible person, and reason for the change.

### 8. Use-Case Lifecycle and Assessment Status

AI use cases should be governed throughout their lifecycle, from initial proposal through retirement.

The assessment process should define the conditions under which a use case may move between lifecycle stages.

A typical lifecycle includes:

1. **Proposed:** The business idea has been submitted but has not completed initial screening.
2. **Under assessment:** The purpose, architecture, data, and risks are being evaluated.
3. **Approved for pilot:** A limited and controlled test is authorized within defined boundaries.
4. **Pilot:** The use case is being evaluated in a restricted environment or limited operational context.
5. **Approved for production:** Required assessments, controls, and approvals have been completed.
6. **In production:** The use case operates within its approved scope and monitoring requirements.
7. **Restricted:** Specific users, data, features, or actions have been limited due to risk or operational conditions.
8. **Suspended:** Operation has been temporarily stopped pending investigation, remediation, or a new decision.
9. **Retired:** The use case is no longer authorized for operation and its dependencies are being decommissioned.

The organization may use different status names, but the underlying decision rights and transition conditions should remain explicit.

A pilot should not become a permanent production deployment through inertia. The organization should define a pilot expiry date, evaluation criteria, permitted data, user group, and maximum operational scope.

The assessment should also establish conditions that automatically trigger renewed review, including material changes in purpose, data sensitivity, autonomy, user population, architecture, or regulatory context.

### 9. Business Problem and Opportunity Statement

The assessment must establish the business problem before evaluating the proposed AI solution.

This requirement reduces the risk of adopting AI simply because a model or platform is available, without confirming that it addresses a legitimate operational need.

The business problem statement should identify:

- The existing process or activity.
- The current limitation, inefficiency, risk, or unmet need.
- The people or business functions affected.
- The consequences of the current state.
- The expected benefit from using AI.
- The alternatives considered.
- The reason AI is appropriate for the problem.
- The expected cost and operational implications.

The assessment should distinguish measurable objectives from general aspirations.

For example, "improve customer service" is too broad to support meaningful evaluation. A more useful objective might be to reduce the time required to prepare draft responses while preserving human approval and maintaining existing service-quality requirements.

The organization should also consider whether conventional automation, improved search, process redesign, or a non-AI solution would deliver the required benefit with lower complexity or risk.

AI adoption should be justified by the suitability of the approach, not by an assumption that an AI-enabled solution is inherently superior.

### 10. Intended Purpose and Permitted Use Boundaries

The intended purpose describes what the AI use case is authorized to accomplish. Permitted use boundaries specify what it may and may not do.

These boundaries are central to risk assessment because the same technical capability can create substantially different risks depending on how it is used.

The assessment should define:

- The primary business objective.
- The tasks the AI is expected to perform.
- The decisions it may support.
- The actions it may execute.
- The information it may access.
- The users authorized to interact with it.
- The contexts in which it may be used.
- The circumstances requiring human review.
- The activities that are explicitly prohibited.

For an internal knowledge assistant, permitted use might include searching approved corporate documentation and summarizing results for employees. Prohibited uses might include making employment decisions, disclosing restricted documents, or executing administrative actions without separate authorization.

The intended purpose should be sufficiently precise to support testing and monitoring.

Terms such as "assistant," "copilot," or "autonomous agent" are not adequate substitutes for a description of the actual tasks, permissions, inputs, outputs, and consequences.

If the business owner cannot clearly define the intended purpose or prohibited activities, the use case should remain under assessment until the ambiguity is resolved.

### 11. AI Capability and Function Classification

The assessment should identify the AI capabilities used by the proposed application because different capabilities introduce different risk scenarios and control requirements.

Common capability categories include:

| AI capability                  | Example                                                        | Assessment focus                                                         |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Classification                 | Categorizing support tickets                                   | Accuracy, bias, explainability, and error handling                       |
| Prediction                     | Forecasting equipment failures                                 | Data quality, uncertainty, drift, and operational impact                 |
| Recommendation                 | Suggesting products or business actions                        | Influence on human decisions and potential downstream harm               |
| Generative AI                  | Drafting reports or communications                             | Confidentiality, hallucination, misuse, and content review               |
| Retrieval-augmented generation | Answering questions using enterprise documents                 | Retrieval authorization, data leakage, and source integrity              |
| Computer vision                | Inspecting equipment images                                    | Image privacy, detection errors, and safety implications                 |
| Speech processing              | Transcribing customer calls                                    | Consent where applicable, data protection, accuracy, and retention       |
| AI agents                      | Executing tasks through external tools                         | Delegated authority, action validation, and execution controls           |
| Automated decision support     | Ranking applications or prioritizing cases                     | Fairness, transparency, human oversight, and impact                      |
| Autonomous action              | Executing an approved workflow without per-action human review | Permission boundaries, reversibility, monitoring, and emergency shutdown |

A use case may combine several capabilities. The assessment should record all material capabilities rather than assigning only one broad label.

It should also distinguish AI-generated recommendations from AI-initiated actions. A system that drafts a transaction for human approval may present a different risk profile from a system that can execute the transaction directly.

### 12. AI System Boundary and Dependency Identification

The use-case assessment should establish the boundaries of the complete AI-enabled solution.

The system boundary includes the components required to deliver the business outcome, not merely the model itself.

Potential components include:

- User interfaces and business applications.
- Model APIs or self-hosted models.
- Orchestration and prompt-management layers.
- Enterprise identity and access management.
- Retrieval and vector databases.
- Data ingestion and transformation pipelines.
- External tools and connectors.
- Logging, monitoring, and audit systems.
- Cloud infrastructure and networking.
- Human review and approval interfaces.
- Upstream vendors and subprocessors.

The assessment should identify which components are owned by the organization and which are managed by external providers.

A boundary diagram should show relevant trust zones, external interfaces, sensitive data flows, privileged components, and points at which human approval is required.

The organization should avoid defining the boundary so narrowly that significant risks are excluded. For example, an AI assistant connected to an enterprise document repository must be evaluated together with its identity integration and retrieval permissions.

Dependency identification supports the determination of which additional technical assessments and vendor reviews are required.

### 13. User Groups and Access Context

The assessment should identify who will use the AI system and how access will be controlled.

Different user populations may have different levels of knowledge, authority, trust, and exposure to the system's outputs.

Relevant user groups may include:

- Employees.
- Contractors.
- Administrators.
- Customers.
- Business partners.
- External service providers.
- Members of the public.
- Specialized operators.
- Individuals affected by AI-supported decisions.

The assessment should document the intended user population, authentication requirements, role-based permissions, access channels, training needs, and restrictions on use.

It should also consider whether users may misunderstand the system's limitations, over-rely on generated content, or attempt to use the system outside its approved purpose.

For customer-facing or public-facing use cases, the organization should assess abuse risks, unauthorized access attempts, sensitive-input exposure, and the need for clear user disclosures.

For privileged or operational users, the assessment should determine whether AI-generated recommendations could influence administrative decisions, security configurations, financial transactions, or other high-impact activities.

User access should be based on business need and minimum required privileges, not merely on the availability of a convenient interface.

### 14. Stakeholder and Impacted-Person Identification

An AI use case may affect people who never interact directly with the system.

The assessment should identify both direct users and individuals or groups whose data, opportunities, treatment, or outcomes may be influenced by AI-generated outputs.

Stakeholders may include:

- Employees whose work is monitored or evaluated.
- Customers receiving AI-generated recommendations.
- Applicants subject to ranking or screening.
- Patients or service users affected by decision support.
- Suppliers assessed using AI-generated information.
- Members of the public affected by AI-supported operations.
- Data subjects whose information is used in model inputs or retrieval.
- Operators responsible for supervising AI-enabled processes.

For each relevant group, the assessment should consider the nature of the potential impact, the severity of possible harm, the likelihood of occurrence, and the available safeguards or remedies.

The organization should determine whether affected people need notice, meaningful explanation, human review, correction mechanisms, or an avenue to challenge an outcome, according to the use case and applicable requirements.

The assessment should not assume that impacts are limited to the business unit sponsoring the technology.

Where significant impacts are possible, the organization should consider a dedicated AI impact assessment and appropriate engagement with relevant stakeholders.

### 15. Business Criticality and Operational Dependency

Business criticality measures the importance of the AI use case to the organization's operations and the consequences of its failure, compromise, or unavailability.

A use case may be technically simple but operationally critical if many business processes depend on it. Conversely, a sophisticated model used for an optional internal experiment may have limited operational impact.

The assessment should evaluate:

- The importance of the supported business process.
- The number of users or services dependent on it.
- The consequences of incorrect outputs.
- The impact of unavailability.
- The availability of manual or technical alternatives.
- The time required to restore service.
- The reversibility of AI-generated actions.
- The effect of data corruption or unauthorized modification.
- The consequences of supplier suspension or termination.
- The potential for cascading failures across connected systems.

Business criticality should inform resilience requirements, approval authority, monitoring intensity, and exit planning.

The organization should distinguish the criticality of the overall business service from the criticality of the AI component. If the AI component is unavailable, the business process may continue through a manual fallback, or it may become impossible to operate safely.

A documented dependency analysis should identify the assumptions behind the classification and the conditions that would require it to be revised.

### 16. Data Classification and Processing Context

The assessment should identify the categories of information processed by the AI use case and the purpose for which each category is used.

Data may include user prompts, uploaded files, retrieved documents, structured business records, model outputs, telemetry, feedback, and records generated through tool execution.

The assessment should record:

- Data categories and classification levels.
- Data owners and authorized sources.
- Whether personal or sensitive information is involved.
- Whether confidential or regulated information is processed.
- Whether the system accesses production records.
- Whether data is transferred to external providers.
- Whether data is retained, indexed, logged, or used for training.
- Whether outputs are stored or used in downstream processes.
- Whether data is transferred across jurisdictions.
- Whether deletion, correction, or access restrictions must propagate to derived artifacts.

The organization should apply data minimization and purpose limitation. The system should not receive unrestricted access to enterprise information simply because the AI platform supports broad connectivity.

The assessment should also evaluate whether the data is appropriate for the intended use. Poor-quality, outdated, biased, or unauthorized data may undermine the reliability and legitimacy of AI-generated results.

Data classification should be linked to enforceable technical controls, such as access restrictions, approved connectors, encryption, retention settings, and monitoring.

### 17. AI Autonomy and Human Involvement Classification

The level of autonomy describes how much discretion the AI system has in producing outputs, selecting actions, and executing tasks without human intervention.

The assessment should distinguish between generating information, recommending decisions, preparing actions, and executing actions.

A practical classification is:

| Level                                      | Description                                                                                 | Example                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 0 — Informational                    | AI generates information without initiating a consequential workflow                        | Summarizing a public document                                                 |
| Level 1 — Advisory                         | AI recommends an action for a person to evaluate                                            | Suggesting a response to a customer                                           |
| Level 2 — Human-approved execution         | AI prepares an action that requires approval before execution                               | Drafting a payment instruction for an authorized employee                     |
| Level 3 — Bounded automation               | AI executes defined actions within enforced constraints                                     | Categorizing and routing routine support tickets                              |
| Level 4 — Delegated agent execution        | AI selects and performs multiple actions within a defined permission boundary               | Retrieving information, updating a permitted record, and reporting completion |
| Level 5 — High-impact autonomous operation | AI can initiate or execute consequential actions with limited per-action human intervention | An automated process affecting critical operations or high-impact decisions   |

This scale is an internal assessment aid, not a universal regulatory classification.

The organization should record the actual authority granted to the system, including access to tools, data, transactions, administrative functions, and external communications.

Higher autonomy generally increases the importance of deterministic authorization, action limits, logging, testing, intervention mechanisms, and safe failure behavior.

Human involvement should be evaluated for effectiveness. A nominal approval step is not meaningful if the reviewer cannot understand the proposed action, lacks the authority to reject it, or is pressured to approve without adequate review.

### 18. Decision Impact and Consequence Classification

The assessment should identify the consequences that could arise if the AI system produces an incorrect, biased, manipulated, delayed, or unavailable output.

The impact depends on the context in which the output is used, not merely on the model's technical performance.

Potential consequence categories include:

- Financial loss.
- Operational disruption.
- Cybersecurity compromise.
- Privacy or confidentiality harm.
- Legal or regulatory exposure.
- Reputational damage.
- Unfair or discriminatory treatment.
- Physical or safety-related harm.
- Loss of access to services or opportunities.
- Inaccurate or misleading information.
- Unauthorized transactions or system changes.

The organization should define impact criteria appropriate to its risk framework and record the assumptions used in classification.

For consequential use cases, the assessment should evaluate the worst credible outcomes as well as typical outcomes. A low average error rate may not be sufficient where rare failures could create severe harm.

The assessment should also consider the reversibility of the result. An incorrect draft that can be reviewed before use differs from an irreversible action or decision that affects an individual without an effective remedy.

Impact classification should determine the need for additional testing, stronger safeguards, independent review, and higher-level approval.

### 19. Preliminary AI Risk Classification

The preliminary risk classification identifies the level of governance scrutiny required before the detailed assessment begins.

It should consider the use case's intended purpose, data sensitivity, autonomy, business criticality, affected stakeholders, deployment context, and potential consequences.

A simple four-tier internal classification may be used:

| Tier   | Classification | Typical characteristics                                                                                                                      |
| ------ | -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tier 1 | Low            | Limited impact, low-sensitivity data, narrow functionality, and readily reversible outcomes                                                  |
| Tier 2 | Moderate       | Meaningful business use, some confidential data or operational dependency, and manageable consequences                                       |
| Tier 3 | High           | Sensitive information, significant business impact, consequential recommendations, broad integrations, or elevated autonomy                  |
| Tier 4 | Critical       | Potential for severe harm, major operational disruption, highly consequential actions, or exposure requiring the highest governance scrutiny |

The organization must define precise classification criteria and escalation rules. The table is an illustrative starting point rather than a universal risk taxonomy.

The classification should be conservative when important information is missing. A lack of clarity about data access, model autonomy, or the consequences of failure should trigger further investigation rather than an automatic low-risk rating.

The assessment should distinguish preliminary classification from final residual risk. The preliminary classification determines the required assessment depth; the final risk conclusion incorporates the findings and effectiveness of relevant controls.

Where applicable law establishes a specific risk classification, the internal tiering model must not be used as a substitute for that legal analysis.

### 20. Initial Screening and Prohibited-Use Review

Initial screening determines whether a proposed use case can proceed to detailed assessment.

The screening should identify obvious conflicts with law, organizational policy, contractual commitments, security requirements, or the organization's risk appetite.

Potential screening questions include:

- Is the intended purpose clearly defined?
- Is the business owner identified?
- Is the proposed use consistent with organizational policy?
- Does the use case involve a legally prohibited activity?
- Does it require access to data that the organization is not authorized to disclose?
- Does it introduce an unacceptable risk to individuals or critical operations?
- Does it involve consequential decisions without an appropriate review or challenge mechanism?
- Does it require a capability that cannot be sufficiently constrained or monitored?
- Is the supplier or technology permitted for the intended environment?
- Can the organization obtain the information needed to assess material risks?
- Are there obvious conflicts with existing contracts or regulatory obligations?

The screening decision should result in one of four outcomes:

1. **Proceed:** The proposal may enter detailed assessment.
2. **Proceed with conditions:** Defined preliminary conditions must be met before further progression.
3. **Defer:** The proposal requires clarification, additional evidence, or redesign.
4. **Reject:** The use case conflicts with a binding prohibition or presents an unacceptable risk that cannot reasonably be addressed.

The rejection decision should record the reason and the authority responsible for the determination.

The organization should not treat a screening pass as a finding that the use case is secure, compliant, or ready for production.

### 21. Business Value and Proportionality Assessment

The expected business value should be evaluated alongside the risks and costs associated with the proposed AI use case.

A technically feasible AI application may still be inappropriate if the expected benefit is marginal, the operational burden is excessive, or a simpler solution could achieve the same outcome with fewer risks.

The assessment should consider:

- Expected productivity or service improvement.
- Quality and consistency benefits.
- Reduction in operational errors.
- Time and cost savings.
- Potential improvements in accessibility or decision support.
- Implementation and integration costs.
- Licensing and usage costs.
- Security and compliance overhead.
- Training and change-management requirements.
- Ongoing monitoring and assurance costs.
- Supplier dependency and exit costs.
- Potential harm or remediation costs.

The business case should distinguish measurable benefits from speculative benefits.

Where possible, the organization should define baseline metrics and target outcomes before deployment. These metrics can then be used during the pilot and operational monitoring stages to determine whether the use case delivers the expected value.

Proportionality also applies to controls. The organization should implement safeguards appropriate to the use case's risk, while recognizing that certain legal or policy requirements are mandatory regardless of commercial benefit.

A favorable business case cannot override a binding prohibition or justify an unacceptable residual risk.

### 22. AI Use-Case Feasibility and Readiness

Feasibility assessment determines whether the organization has the technical, operational, data, and governance capabilities required to implement the use case responsibly.

A proposal may have clear business value but still be unsuitable for deployment because data quality is insufficient, integrations are insecure, the supplier cannot provide necessary evidence, or the organization lacks the ability to monitor the resulting system.

Readiness criteria should include:

- Availability and suitability of required data.
- Compatibility with existing architecture.
- Identity and access-control readiness.
- Availability of required security safeguards.
- Model evaluation and testing capability.
- Human oversight and operational ownership.
- Monitoring and incident response readiness.
- Availability of relevant supplier documentation.
- Ability to meet applicable legal and contractual requirements.
- Adequate staffing, expertise, and support.
- Business continuity and fallback arrangements.
- Defined acceptance criteria for a pilot or production deployment.

The assessment should distinguish technical feasibility from operational readiness. A prototype that works in a controlled environment may not be suitable for production if it lacks monitoring, access controls, change management, or reliable failure handling.

Where readiness gaps are remediable, the assessment should identify the required actions, owners, and milestones. Where essential safeguards cannot be established, the use case should be deferred, redesigned, or rejected.

### 23. Intended Environment and Deployment Model

The deployment model influences data exposure, control ownership, operational complexity, and the organization's ability to validate security.

The assessment should identify whether the AI system will operate as a public service, private enterprise service, self-hosted deployment, embedded capability, hybrid architecture, or another supported model.

| Deployment model         | Principal assessment considerations                                                                           |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public AI service        | Data-use terms, external processing, tenant isolation, access controls, and service limitations               |
| Enterprise-managed SaaS  | Tenant configuration, identity integration, administrative controls, retention, and contractual commitments   |
| API-based external model | API security, egress control, logging, version changes, rate limits, and supplier dependencies                |
| Self-hosted model        | Infrastructure hardening, model artifacts, patching, access management, and operational responsibility        |
| Private cloud deployment | Network boundaries, cloud configuration, identity, key management, and shared responsibility                  |
| Hybrid deployment        | Cross-boundary data flows, integration security, consistent policy enforcement, and monitoring                |
| Embedded AI capability   | Data access inherited from the host application, feature controls, update mechanisms, and vendor transparency |
| Agentic platform         | Tool permissions, delegated authority, execution isolation, approval gates, and emergency shutdown            |

The organization should document the actual deployment architecture rather than relying solely on the supplier's marketing description.

For hybrid or integrated systems, the assessment should identify where prompts, files, outputs, embeddings, logs, and tool requests are processed and stored.

The deployment model should also identify which security controls can be configured directly by the organization and which depend on the supplier's implementation.

### 24. Data Sources and Data Provenance

Data provenance describes where data originates, how it is collected or transformed, and whether its use is authorized and appropriate for the AI application.

The assessment should identify the principal data sources used during inference, retrieval, fine-tuning, evaluation, and operational feedback.

Relevant sources may include:

- Public information.
- Internal corporate documents.
- Customer or employee records.
- Transactional systems.
- Third-party datasets.
- Licensed data.
- User-provided content.
- Sensor or telemetry data.
- Synthetic data.
- Model-generated outputs reused in subsequent workflows.

The organization should record data ownership, collection purpose, quality requirements, access restrictions, licensing or usage constraints, and relevant retention requirements.

For retrieval-based applications, the assessment should determine whether source permissions remain enforced when information is indexed and retrieved.

For training or fine-tuning, the assessment should evaluate whether the organization has appropriate rights to use the data and whether the process introduces confidentiality, privacy, intellectual-property, or data-quality concerns.

The organization should also assess whether data provenance can be traced sufficiently to investigate inaccurate outputs, disputed decisions, or unauthorized processing.

Where provenance is incomplete, the assessment should record the limitation and determine whether it can be resolved or whether the affected data source must be excluded.

### 25. Model Output Use and Downstream Dependencies

The assessment should establish how AI-generated outputs will be consumed and whether they can influence other systems, people, or automated processes.

Outputs may be displayed to users, stored as records, used as retrieval context, transformed by another model, submitted to an external API, or used to trigger an action.

The risk depends on what happens after the model generates the output.

The assessment should identify:

- The intended recipients of outputs.
- Whether outputs are recommendations, drafts, classifications, decisions, or executable instructions.
- Whether a person reviews outputs before use.
- Whether downstream systems validate outputs.
- Whether outputs can trigger transactions or configuration changes.
- Whether outputs become part of official business records.
- Whether errors can propagate into other models or workflows.
- Whether output provenance and traceability are retained.
- Whether the organization can correct or reverse an erroneous outcome.

For structured outputs, the system should validate schema, field values, permissions, and business rules before downstream processing.

For natural-language outputs, the organization should evaluate the need for factual verification, source attribution, disclosure of uncertainty, and human review.

A model's ability to produce syntactically valid output does not establish that the output is correct, authorized, or suitable for the intended decision.

### 26. Human Oversight Requirements

Human oversight should be designed according to the consequences of error, the level of autonomy, and the ability of the system to operate safely within defined limits.

The assessment should specify when human review is required, who performs it, what information the reviewer receives, and how the reviewer can intervene.

Relevant questions include:

- Which outputs require mandatory review?
- What level of expertise must the reviewer possess?
- Can the reviewer access relevant source evidence?
- Can the reviewer reject or modify the recommendation?
- Are reviewers given adequate time and authority?
- Is the review decision recorded?
- How are disagreements and escalations handled?
- Can the system be paused or disabled?
- Are override rates and recurring errors monitored?
- What happens when a reviewer is unavailable?

Human oversight should be proportionate. Low-impact information retrieval may require user verification and clear limitations, while consequential decisions may require a structured review process with documented evidence and escalation.

The assessment should consider automation bias, where users place excessive confidence in AI-generated outputs, and alert fatigue, where repeated review requests reduce the effectiveness of supervision.

A human approval step should not be treated as an adequate control unless the reviewer can meaningfully evaluate the output and prevent an inappropriate action.

### 27. Initial Security Threat Identification

The initial security review should identify credible threats associated with the use case's architecture, data, capabilities, and operating context.

This is a preliminary threat-identification exercise. Detailed analysis should be performed through the relevant AI security assessment and architecture review.

Potential threats include:

- Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive prompts or retrieved content.
- Prompt injection through user input or external documents.
- Excessive permissions granted to AI applications or agents.
- Compromise of API keys or service identities.
- Cross-tenant data exposure.
- Abuse of model APIs or denial of service.
- Malicious or manipulated training and retrieval data.
- Insecure output handling.
- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Vulnerabilities in model-serving or orchestration components.
- Inadequate logging and incident investigation.
- Supplier compromise or upstream dependency failure.

The assessment should identify the assets at risk, likely threat actors or sources, exposed interfaces, potential attack paths, and preliminary consequences.

Threat identification should consider both malicious activity and accidental failures, such as incorrect configuration, unintended data sharing, unreliable outputs, and unsafe workflow design.

The assessment should also distinguish threats addressed by supplier controls from those requiring customer-side controls.

The preliminary threat list should be linked to the system's architecture and data flows so that subsequent security reviews can validate and expand it.

### 28. Preliminary Privacy, Legal, and Compliance Screening

The assessment should determine whether the proposed use case requires specialist privacy, legal, compliance, or regulatory review.

The purpose is to identify relevant obligations early, before the organization commits to a deployment architecture or processing arrangement that cannot satisfy them.

Initial screening should establish:

- Whether personal data is processed.
- Whether sensitive or specially protected information is involved.
- Whether the use case affects decisions concerning individuals.
- Whether information is transferred to an external provider.
- Whether international data transfers may occur.
- Whether data is used for training, fine-tuning, or service improvement.
- Whether sector-specific requirements apply.
- Whether the AI use case falls within a regulated or restricted category.
- Whether transparency, documentation, recordkeeping, or human review obligations may apply.
- Whether a formal privacy or AI impact assessment may be required.

The screening result should identify which specialist assessments are required, who owns them, and whether their completion is a prerequisite for pilot or production approval.

The organization should avoid making definitive legal conclusions from a generic questionnaire where the applicable requirements depend on facts that have not yet been established.

Unresolved legal or compliance questions should be documented, assigned, and escalated to the appropriate authority.

### 29. Preliminary Risk Register Entry and Assessment Traceability

The use-case assessment should create or update the enterprise risk register when it identifies a material risk requiring formal tracking.

The record should connect the risk to the specific use case, affected systems, data assets, suppliers, control owners, and relevant approval decisions.

A preliminary risk record should include:

| Field               | Description                                                                            |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID             | Unique risk identifier                                                                 |
| Use-case ID         | Identifier of the assessed AI use case                                                 |
| Risk statement      | Clear description of the potential event and consequence                               |
| Risk category       | Security, privacy, operational, legal, model, human-impact, or other relevant category |
| Affected assets     | Systems, data, services, people, or business processes at risk                         |
| Inherent risk       | Initial risk level before applicable controls                                          |
| Existing controls   | Safeguards already implemented or evidenced                                            |
| Assessment status   | Identified, under analysis, treated, accepted, or closed                               |
| Risk owner          | Person or role accountable for eligible risk decisions                                 |
| Control owner       | Person or role responsible for implementing the relevant safeguard                     |
| Related assessments | Security, privacy, impact, vendor, or architecture reviews                             |
| Decision references | Approval, exception, or risk acceptance identifiers                                    |
| Review trigger      | Date or event requiring reassessment                                                   |

A risk statement should describe a plausible event and its consequences rather than merely naming a generic weakness.

For example, "prompt injection" is a threat category. A more useful risk statement explains that malicious instructions embedded in retrieved documents could influence an AI agent to disclose restricted information or execute an unauthorized action because the application does not enforce sufficient authorization boundaries.

The organization should preserve traceability between the risk, the control intended to address it, the evidence used to evaluate that control, and the decision made by the authorized risk owner.

### 30. Part 1 Core Principle — Define the Use Case Before Evaluating the Solution

An AI use-case assessment establishes the business purpose, permitted boundaries, users, affected stakeholders, data, architecture, autonomy, and potential consequences of an AI-enabled activity.

These foundations determine which risks are relevant, which specialist assessments are necessary, what safeguards must be implemented, and which governance authority can authorize the use case.

A model or vendor cannot be assessed meaningfully without understanding how the organization intends to use it. The same technology may be acceptable in one context and inappropriate in another because the data, permissions, autonomy, or consequences differ.

The assessment must therefore begin with a precise use-case definition and a defensible preliminary risk classification.

**Core distinction:** A use case that is technically feasible is not automatically justified, secure, compliant, or approved for production. Each of these conclusions requires its own evidence and governance decision.

# 20.10 — AI Use-Case Assessment Template

## Part 2 — Detailed Risk Assessment, Control Requirements, Evidence Validation, and Deployment Readiness

### 31. Detailed AI Use-Case Risk Assessment Methodology

The detailed AI use-case risk assessment evaluates the risks identified during initial screening and determines whether the proposed AI application can operate within the organization's approved risk appetite.

Part 1 established the business purpose, system boundaries, users, data, autonomy, and preliminary risk classification. Part 2 builds on those foundations by evaluating specific risk scenarios, control requirements, evidence, and deployment conditions.

The assessment should follow a repeatable methodology:

1. Confirm the approved use-case definition and assessment scope.
2. Identify credible risk scenarios across the AI lifecycle.
3. Determine the inherent risk of each scenario.
4. Identify applicable existing controls.
5. Validate control design and implementation evidence.
6. Evaluate residual risk and remaining uncertainty.
7. Define additional treatments and compensating controls.
8. Determine whether specialist assessments or escalation are required.
9. Record the recommended decision and its conditions.
10. Establish the monitoring and reassessment requirements.

Risk assessment should consider both the probability of an adverse event and the severity of its consequences. However, a simple numerical formula should not conceal high-impact scenarios, mandatory requirements, or significant uncertainty.

The methodology should distinguish between a risk that is reduced by effective controls and a risk that appears low only because relevant information is missing.

### 32. Risk Scenario Construction

A risk scenario describes a plausible event, the conditions that make it possible, and the resulting consequences.

A useful scenario connects a threat or failure condition to an affected asset or stakeholder and explains how the event could create harm.

A standardized format is:

**Because of** a threat, weakness, or operating condition, **an event may occur**, resulting in a defined business, security, privacy, legal, operational, or human impact.

For example:

> Because an AI assistant can retrieve confidential documents without enforcing the requesting user's document permissions, a user may obtain restricted information through a generated response, resulting in unauthorized disclosure of business-sensitive data.

The scenario identifies:

- The threat or failure condition.
- The relevant vulnerability or control gap.
- The event that may occur.
- The affected assets, processes, or individuals.
- The potential consequences.
- The controls that may prevent or mitigate the event.

Risk scenarios should be specific to the intended use case. Generic entries such as "AI may hallucinate" or "AI may be attacked" are insufficient for a defensible assessment unless the relevant failure mode, operating context, and impact are defined.

A single use case may require multiple scenarios covering confidentiality, integrity, availability, reliability, privacy, misuse, and unintended consequences.

### 33. Inherent Risk Assessment

Inherent risk represents the exposure associated with a use case before considering the effectiveness of applicable controls.

The assessment should consider the nature of the business activity, the sensitivity of the data, the level of autonomy, the potential impact of errors, the system's exposure, and the consequences of failure.

A practical assessment may use the following dimensions:

| Dimension              | Assessment considerations                                                                  |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Business impact        | Consequences for critical processes, revenue, service delivery, and operational continuity |
| Data sensitivity       | Confidentiality, personal data, regulated information, and intellectual property           |
| Cybersecurity exposure | External connectivity, privileged integrations, attack surface, and threat opportunities   |
| AI behavior risk       | Hallucination, unpredictable outputs, manipulation, or inappropriate recommendations       |
| Autonomy               | Ability to initiate or execute actions without human approval                              |
| Human impact           | Effects on individuals, access to services, fairness, safety, or opportunities             |
| Regulatory exposure    | Applicable legal duties, restrictions, and accountability requirements                     |
| Dependency risk        | Reliance on external providers, critical infrastructure, or specialized components         |
| Reversibility          | Ability to correct, reverse, or contain an erroneous result                                |
| Scale                  | Number of users, transactions, records, or decisions potentially affected                  |

The organization should define scoring criteria and examples for each dimension.

High inherent risk does not automatically mean that a use case must be rejected. It indicates that stronger safeguards, deeper assessment, and potentially higher-level approval may be necessary.

Conversely, a low inherent risk classification should not exempt a use case from mandatory security, privacy, or legal requirements.

### 34. AI Risk Taxonomy and Scenario Coverage

A structured risk taxonomy helps ensure that the assessment considers more than traditional information security threats.

The taxonomy should cover technical, operational, governance, legal, and human-impact risks.

| Risk category          | Example scenario                                                                           |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Confidentiality        | Sensitive information is disclosed through prompts, retrieval, logs, or generated output   |
| Integrity              | AI-generated content or actions modify business records incorrectly                        |
| Availability           | An AI provider outage interrupts a critical workflow                                       |
| Reliability            | Incorrect or unstable outputs lead to poor operational decisions                           |
| Model behavior         | A model produces misleading or unsafe content in the intended context                      |
| Prompt injection       | Malicious instructions influence model behavior or tool execution                          |
| Data governance        | Data is processed without appropriate authorization or retained beyond the approved period |
| Privacy                | Personal information is exposed, inferred, or processed inappropriately                    |
| Fairness               | Outputs systematically disadvantage a group of affected individuals                        |
| Human oversight        | Users approve consequential outputs without adequate review                                |
| Autonomy               | An agent executes an action beyond its intended authority                                  |
| Supply chain           | A compromised upstream component affects the AI application                                |
| Vendor dependency      | A provider changes service behavior or becomes unavailable                                 |
| Regulatory compliance  | The use case fails to satisfy an applicable legal obligation                               |
| Intellectual property  | Outputs or inputs create licensing, ownership, or confidentiality disputes                 |
| Operational resilience | The organization cannot continue critical operations when AI fails                         |
| Governance             | The use case operates outside its approved purpose or without accountable ownership        |
| Reputational impact    | A harmful or misleading AI-generated result damages stakeholder trust                      |

The taxonomy should be adapted to the organization's sector and risk profile. Not every category applies to every use case, but exclusions should be based on documented reasoning rather than omission.

The assessment should also identify interactions between risks. For example, prompt injection may lead to unauthorized tool execution, which may then cause data disclosure or an operational incident.

### 35. Control Identification and Control Ownership

For each material risk scenario, the assessment should identify the controls intended to prevent the event, detect it, limit its consequences, or support recovery.

Controls may be technical, procedural, contractual, organizational, or human-centered.

Examples include:

- Identity and access management.
- Data classification and access restrictions.
- Input validation and output handling.
- Retrieval authorization.
- Human approval gates.
- Agent permission boundaries.
- Monitoring and alerting.
- Incident response procedures.
- Vendor security commitments.
- Data retention and deletion controls.
- Model evaluation and regression testing.
- Business continuity and fallback procedures.

Each control should have an identifiable owner and a clear implementation requirement.

The assessment should distinguish between:

- **Control design:** Whether the control is appropriately designed to address the risk.
- **Control implementation:** Whether the control has actually been deployed.
- **Operating effectiveness:** Whether the control works consistently under relevant conditions.
- **Evidence confidence:** Whether the available evidence supports the conclusion.

A control described in a policy but not implemented in the application should not be treated as an effective operational safeguard.

Where control ownership is shared between the organization and an external provider, the assessment should explicitly document each party's responsibility.

### 36. Control Effectiveness and Evidence Validation

Control effectiveness should be determined using evidence appropriate to the risk.

The assessment should not rely exclusively on statements from the project team, model provider, or vendor. Claims should be corroborated where necessary through configuration records, test results, independent assurance, technical demonstrations, or other relevant evidence.

A control validation record should include:

| Field                 | Description                                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Control ID            | Unique identifier of the control                                   |
| Risk scenario         | Risk the control is intended to address                            |
| Control owner         | Responsible person or function                                     |
| Design assessment     | Whether the control is suitably designed                           |
| Implementation status | Implemented, partially implemented, planned, or absent             |
| Evidence reference    | Link or identifier for supporting evidence                         |
| Validation method     | Inspection, testing, interview, demonstration, or assurance review |
| Validation date       | Date the control was reviewed                                      |
| Result                | Effective, partially effective, ineffective, or not validated      |
| Limitation            | Scope restrictions, missing evidence, or unresolved uncertainty    |
| Corrective action     | Required remediation and accountable owner                         |

The assessment should distinguish between a control that has not been validated and a control that has been tested and found ineffective.

Both conditions may prevent approval, but they represent different evidence states and may require different remediation approaches.

For high-impact use cases, evidence should be sufficiently specific to the actual deployment, configuration, data, and operating context.

### 37. Residual Risk Determination

Residual risk is the exposure remaining after the effectiveness of relevant controls and proposed mitigations has been considered.

The assessment should evaluate each material scenario separately before determining the overall risk classification for the use case.

A useful process is:

1. Establish the inherent risk.
2. Identify existing controls.
3. Evaluate design and operating effectiveness.
4. Determine the risk reduction supported by evidence.
5. Identify remaining weaknesses and uncertainty.
6. Evaluate additional mitigations.
7. Assign the residual risk rating.
8. Identify the authorized risk owner.
9. Determine whether acceptance, remediation, restriction, or rejection is appropriate.

The organization should not reduce a risk rating merely because a large number of controls are listed. The relevant question is whether those controls effectively address the specific scenario.

Residual risk should also account for dependencies. For example, a customer-side access restriction may reduce exposure only if the application consistently enforces it across all relevant retrieval and tool-execution paths.

Where risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, the organization should consider redesigning the use case, reducing functionality, limiting data access, lowering autonomy, changing providers, or discontinuing the proposal.

### 38. Risk Treatment and Remediation Planning

Risk treatment defines the actions required to bring exposure within acceptable limits.

Treatment options may include risk avoidance, risk reduction, risk transfer where appropriate, or acceptance of eligible residual risk by an authorized owner.

A remediation plan should specify:

- The risk or control deficiency being addressed.
- The required corrective action.
- The accountable owner.
- The target completion date.
- Dependencies and required resources.
- Interim safeguards.
- Validation and closure criteria.
- Escalation conditions.
- The decision authority if remediation cannot be completed.

Examples of risk reduction include restricting model access to approved data, introducing human approval for consequential actions, validating generated outputs, adding application-level authorization, or reducing the system's permitted autonomy.

Risk transfer should not be interpreted as eliminating the organization's accountability. Insurance, supplier contracts, and indemnities may reduce certain financial consequences, but they do not necessarily prevent harm or remove legal obligations.

Remediation plans should prioritize material exposure rather than administrative convenience. Critical deficiencies that undermine safe or lawful operation must be addressed before the affected use case proceeds.

### 39. Compensating Controls and Alternative Architectures

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard used to reduce a risk when the preferred control cannot be implemented or is unavailable.

The assessment should determine whether the alternative addresses the same risk objective and whether its limitations are understood.

For example, if a vendor cannot provide a required network restriction, an organization may be able to reduce exposure through an application gateway, narrowly scoped credentials, restricted egress, and additional monitoring. Whether that combination is sufficient depends on the actual architecture and threat scenario.

The assessment should document:

- The original control requirement.
- The reason the preferred control is unavailable.
- The risk created by the deviation.
- The proposed compensating measure.
- Evidence of implementation and effectiveness.
- Residual limitations.
- The authority approving the alternative.
- The review or expiry date.

Compensating controls must not be treated as automatic equivalents. A monitoring control may detect a violation after it occurs but may not prevent the initial disclosure or unauthorized action.

Where the compensating control cannot provide adequate protection, the organization should redesign the system, reduce the scope of the use case, or reject the proposal.

### 40. AI Security Requirements for the Use Case

The use-case assessment should translate identified security risks into explicit requirements for design, implementation, testing, and operation.

Requirements should be measurable and testable. Statements such as "the AI system must be secure" do not establish an actionable acceptance criterion.

Examples include:

| Security requirement    | Example acceptance criterion                                                                           |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Identity enforcement    | Only authenticated and authorized users can access the approved AI application                         |
| Least privilege         | Each integration receives only the permissions required for its documented purpose                     |
| Data protection         | Sensitive data is transmitted and stored using approved safeguards                                     |
| Retrieval authorization | Retrieved documents are restricted to content the requesting identity is authorized to access          |
| Output validation       | Structured outputs are validated before they influence downstream operations                           |
| Tool execution          | Consequential actions are checked against deterministic authorization rules                            |
| Logging                 | Material access, configuration changes, and tool executions generate reviewable records                |
| Incident readiness      | The application can be restricted or disabled through a documented response procedure                  |
| Change control          | Material model or service changes trigger the required testing and review                              |
| Resilience              | The business process has an approved fallback where service failure would materially affect operations |

The assessment should identify the control owner, implementation evidence, validation method, and acceptance criteria for each requirement.

Requirements should be linked to the AI security assessment, architecture review, development backlog, and production release checklist so that they are not lost between governance approval and technical delivery.

### 41. AI Privacy and Data Protection Requirements

Where personal data is involved, the assessment should determine which privacy requirements apply and whether additional specialist review is necessary.

The assessment should identify the purposes of processing, categories of personal data, data subjects, relevant parties, retention requirements, access arrangements, and possible onward transfers.

Key review areas include:

- Purpose limitation and data minimization.
- Appropriate authority or legal basis for processing.
- Sensitive data and special-category information where applicable.
- Data-subject rights and correction mechanisms.
- Retention, deletion, and backup handling.
- Access to prompts, outputs, and retrieved records.
- Data transfers to external providers.
- Use of personal data for training or service improvement.
- Potential inference of sensitive information.
- Privacy risks arising from generated outputs.
- Relevant transparency and documentation requirements.

The assessment should not assume that removing direct identifiers eliminates privacy risk. Re-identification, linkage, inference, and disclosure may remain possible depending on the dataset and use case.

Where a formal data protection impact assessment or other statutory process is required, the use-case assessment should reference the corresponding record and its conclusions.

Privacy controls should be reflected in the architecture and operational configuration rather than existing only as policy statements.

### 42. AI Reliability, Accuracy, and Performance Requirements

The organization should establish the level of reliability and accuracy required for the intended purpose.

The appropriate criteria depend on the task, the consequences of error, the quality of available data, and the role of human review.

The assessment should define:

- The expected performance baseline.
- Relevant evaluation datasets and test scenarios.
- Acceptable error rates or quality thresholds.
- The severity of different error types.
- The treatment of uncertain or unsupported outputs.
- Human verification requirements.
- Performance monitoring indicators.
- Conditions requiring rollback or suspension.
- Reassessment triggers following model changes.

For a summarization use case, evaluation may focus on factual consistency, omission of material information, source traceability, and protection against disclosure.

For an AI-assisted classification system, evaluation may focus on precision, recall, false-positive and false-negative consequences, and performance across relevant subgroups.

For an AI agent, evaluation should include the correctness and authorization of actions, not merely the quality of its natural-language explanations.

The organization should avoid assuming that a high aggregate benchmark score proves suitability for the specific operational context.

### 43. Fairness, Bias, and Human-Impact Requirements

Use cases that influence people, access to opportunities, services, or treatment may require specific analysis of fairness and potential harm.

The assessment should determine whether the system could create or amplify unequal outcomes through training data, input selection, proxy variables, model behavior, deployment choices, or human over-reliance.

Relevant questions include:

- Which groups may be affected?
- What types of unequal treatment are plausible?
- Are relevant data sources representative and appropriate?
- Could apparently neutral variables act as proxies for protected or sensitive characteristics?
- What performance differences may arise across groups?
- Can affected individuals obtain meaningful review or correction?
- Is the AI output used as the sole basis for a consequential decision?
- Are the evaluation criteria suitable for the intended context?
- Who is responsible for investigating complaints and recurring errors?

Fairness cannot always be reduced to one metric. Different fairness measures may conflict, and the appropriate evaluation depends on the context, applicable law, data limitations, and consequences of error.

The assessment should identify the criteria selected, the rationale for their use, and the limitations of the analysis.

Where substantial human impact is possible, the organization should consider a dedicated impact assessment and ensure that appropriate expertise and governance authority are involved.

### 44. Human Oversight and Intervention Controls

The assessment should define how human oversight will operate in practice and how people can intervene when AI behavior is incorrect, unsafe, or outside the approved scope.

The design should identify the decisions requiring human approval, the expertise required of reviewers, the evidence available to them, and the mechanism for rejecting or reversing an action.

Controls may include:

- Mandatory approval for high-impact decisions.
- Review of AI-generated recommendations against source evidence.
- Restrictions on automated execution.
- Clear escalation paths for uncertain or anomalous outputs.
- Override and correction mechanisms.
- Emergency suspension capabilities.
- Periodic sampling of AI-assisted decisions.
- Training on limitations and automation bias.
- Monitoring of override rates and recurring errors.

The assessment should ensure that oversight is proportionate to the risk and supported by sufficient time, information, and authority.

For low-impact use cases, clear user instructions and verification procedures may be sufficient. For consequential or high-autonomy systems, stronger approval, monitoring, and intervention controls may be required.

The organization should record the evidence needed to demonstrate that the oversight mechanism operates as designed.

### 45. Prompt Injection and Untrusted Input Risk

The assessment should determine whether user inputs, uploaded files, retrieved documents, external content, or tool responses can influence the AI system in unintended ways.

Prompt injection can cause a model to disregard intended instructions, reveal sensitive information, or propose actions that violate the application's purpose.

The assessment should evaluate:

- Sources of untrusted content.
- Separation between trusted configuration and untrusted input.
- Retrieval authorization and content filtering.
- Exposure of system prompts, credentials, or sensitive context.
- Model access to external tools and privileged data.
- Validation of model-generated actions.
- Detection and handling of suspicious behavior.
- Testing against direct and indirect prompt injection.
- Ability to restrict or disable affected functionality.

Prompt-injection defenses should not rely solely on instructions telling the model to ignore malicious content.

The architecture should limit the impact of successful manipulation through least privilege, deterministic authorization, restricted data access, output validation, and controlled tool execution.

Testing should use representative attack scenarios and record whether the system prevents the prohibited outcome, detects it, or fails safely.

### 46. AI Agent Permissions and Action Boundaries

When the use case includes an AI agent, the assessment must define the authority delegated to that agent.

The organization should document the tools available to the agent, the resources it can access, the actions it may perform, and the conditions under which those actions require approval.

The assessment should consider:

- Tool and connector inventory.
- Identity and credential management.
- Read versus write permissions.
- Transaction and resource limits.
- Restrictions on destructive operations.
- Approval requirements for consequential actions.
- Execution isolation and sandboxing where appropriate.
- Protection against recursive or excessive action sequences.
- Logging of proposed and executed actions.
- Emergency shutdown and permission revocation.

The agent should not be able to expand its own permissions through model-generated instructions or by exploiting a tool that provides excessive authority.

The organization should test whether the agent remains within its authorized boundaries under malicious prompts, misleading retrieved content, unexpected tool responses, and repeated execution attempts.

The assessment should distinguish between an agent that recommends an action and one that can execute it. This distinction should influence risk classification, required safeguards, and approval authority.

### 47. AI Vendor and External Dependency Risk

The assessment should identify external providers whose services, models, infrastructure, or data materially influence the use case.

Relevant dependencies may include model providers, hosted AI platforms, cloud services, retrieval providers, orchestration platforms, external APIs, and managed security services.

The assessment should determine:

- Whether each provider has been assessed under the organization's supplier risk process.
- Which data the provider receives or can access.
- Whether the provider uses data for training or service improvement.
- How material model or service changes are communicated.
- What assurance evidence is available.
- Whether security incidents are reported under appropriate commitments.
- Whether required audit and investigation support is available.
- Whether the organization can restrict, replace, or terminate the dependency.
- Whether several critical use cases depend on the same provider.

A supplier's general approval should not automatically authorize every use case involving that supplier. The actual data, autonomy, architecture, and business consequences must remain within the approved scope.

Where vendor evidence is insufficient, the organization should document the limitation and determine whether additional assurance, technical safeguards, contractual changes, or a different provider is necessary.

### 48. AI Incident Response and Failure Management

The use-case assessment should establish how the organization will respond when the AI application produces harmful outputs, exposes information, executes an unauthorized action, becomes unavailable, or behaves outside its approved scope.

The incident process should connect AI-specific events to existing cybersecurity, privacy, operational resilience, and business incident-management procedures.

The assessment should define:

- Events that constitute an AI-related incident or near miss.
- Monitoring and detection responsibilities.
- Escalation and notification requirements.
- Authority to suspend the use case.
- Methods for disabling tools, connectors, or data access.
- Evidence and logging requirements.
- Supplier cooperation and notification arrangements.
- Recovery and fallback procedures.
- Root-cause analysis and corrective actions.
- Conditions for restoring service.

Potential triggers include unauthorized retrieval, sensitive-data disclosure, prompt injection resulting in an unauthorized action, significant unexplained model behavior changes, repeated high-impact errors, and compromise of a critical AI dependency.

The organization should test incident procedures proportionate to the use case's risk. Critical applications may require tabletop exercises or technical tests that validate containment, recovery, and decision authority.

The assessment should also define how near misses are recorded and used to improve controls, even when no material harm occurs.

### 49. Business Continuity and Safe Degradation

The organization should determine how the business process will operate if the AI service becomes unavailable, produces unreliable outputs, or must be suspended for security reasons.

Safe degradation means that the application can reduce or disable risky functionality without creating uncontrolled downstream behavior.

Potential safeguards include:

- Manual operating procedures.
- Conventional software alternatives.
- Secondary service providers.
- Read-only fallback modes.
- Transaction limits during degraded operation.
- Queues or deferred processing.
- Automatic shutdown when critical dependencies fail.
- Clear notification to affected users.
- Procedures for reconciling work after restoration.

The assessment should establish which functions must remain available, which can be temporarily suspended, and which must never continue without the AI component.

For example, an AI assistant used to draft routine internal communications may be safely disabled during an outage. An AI-enabled process supporting a critical operational function may require a tested alternative workflow.

The organization should define recovery objectives where appropriate and test whether fallback procedures are operationally realistic.

A continuity plan should not rely on an alternative that has never been tested or that introduces equivalent risks without adequate assessment.

### 50. AI Change Management and Reassessment

AI use cases may change materially after initial approval because of new models, updated prompts, additional data sources, expanded user groups, new integrations, or increased autonomy.

The organization should establish change criteria that determine whether a modification requires a limited review, full reassessment, or renewed approval.

Material changes may include:

- A new intended purpose.
- New categories of personal or confidential data.
- New external data sources.
- A different model or vendor.
- New retrieval or tool-execution capabilities.
- Expanded user access.
- Increased autonomy.
- New downstream actions.
- A change in the consequences of error.
- Material changes to applicable legal obligations.
- Significant incidents or newly identified vulnerabilities.

The change-management process should identify the affected risks, controls, documentation, tests, approvals, and operational procedures.

The organization should preserve the previous assessment and record the rationale for the new decision. Historical records should not be overwritten in a way that removes traceability.

Where a change materially increases risk, the use case should not proceed merely because the original deployment was approved.

### 51. Testing Strategy and Acceptance Criteria

The use-case assessment should define how the organization will determine whether the AI application is suitable for pilot or production use.

Testing should cover both functional performance and risk controls.

A testing strategy may include:

| Test category                | Objective                                                                           |
| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Functional testing           | Confirm that the application performs the intended tasks                            |
| Accuracy and quality testing | Evaluate output correctness and task-specific performance                           |
| Security testing             | Identify vulnerabilities, unauthorized access, and unsafe execution paths           |
| Privacy testing              | Evaluate data exposure, retention, and access-control behavior                      |
| Robustness testing           | Evaluate behavior under unusual inputs, degraded conditions, and expected variation |
| Adversarial testing          | Evaluate response to malicious inputs and prompt-injection attempts                 |
| Fairness testing             | Evaluate relevant disparities and human-impact concerns                             |
| Integration testing          | Validate interactions with identity, data, APIs, and downstream systems             |
| Human oversight testing      | Confirm that review, rejection, and intervention mechanisms work                    |
| Resilience testing           | Validate fallback, recovery, and controlled suspension                              |
| Regression testing           | Detect material deterioration after changes                                         |

Acceptance criteria should be established before testing begins. Otherwise, teams may interpret results in a way that favors deployment after the fact.

The assessment should identify who approves test results, how failed tests are handled, what evidence is retained, and which findings prevent release.

A successful functional demonstration is not sufficient evidence of security, privacy, reliability, or production readiness.

### 52. Pilot Authorization and Controlled Experimentation

A pilot allows the organization to evaluate an AI use case under restricted conditions before broader deployment.

Pilot authorization should specify the permitted scope, duration, user population, data categories, technical boundaries, monitoring requirements, and success criteria.

The pilot record should include:

- The approved objective and hypothesis.
- The environment and architecture.
- The permitted model and service configuration.
- The users and affected stakeholders.
- The approved data sources.
- Prohibited uses and actions.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Test cases and performance metrics.
- Security and privacy safeguards.
- Incident escalation procedures.
- Pilot end date and exit conditions.
- Criteria for progression to production.

Where possible, pilots should use synthetic, anonymized, or otherwise appropriately restricted data when this reduces risk without undermining the validity of the evaluation.

A pilot should not be used to bypass mandatory production requirements. If the pilot involves real personal data, consequential decisions, or operationally sensitive integrations, applicable obligations and safeguards still apply.

At the end of the pilot, the organization should document the results, unresolved risks, deviations, lessons learned, and recommendation to proceed, redesign, extend under authorization, or stop.

### 53. Production Readiness and Go-Live Approval

Production readiness confirms that the AI use case has satisfied the requirements necessary for its intended operational environment.

The decision should be based on evidence rather than the completion of project milestones alone.

The production readiness review should confirm:

- The intended purpose and scope are approved.
- Required risk and impact assessments are complete.
- Mandatory security and privacy controls are implemented.
- Material findings have been remediated or appropriately resolved.
- Residual risk has been evaluated and accepted by the authorized owner where acceptance is permitted.
- Data access and processing are appropriately controlled.
- Human oversight and intervention mechanisms are operational.
- Logging, monitoring, and incident response are ready.
- Business continuity and fallback arrangements are defined.
- Supplier and contractual requirements are satisfied.
- Users and operators have received appropriate training.
- Change-management and reassessment procedures are established.
- The required approval authority has recorded its decision.

The go-live record should identify any remaining conditions, their owners, and the restrictions imposed on the initial deployment.

If mandatory requirements are not satisfied, production approval should be withheld. Commercial deadlines or project completion targets should not override binding obligations or critical security requirements.

### 54. Post-Deployment Monitoring and Performance Review

Post-deployment monitoring determines whether the use case continues to operate within its approved boundaries and delivers the expected business value.

The monitoring plan should include operational performance, security events, output quality, user behavior, control effectiveness, and changes in the surrounding environment.

Possible indicators include:

- Error rates and quality measures.
- Frequency of human overrides.
- Unauthorized access attempts.
- Prompt-injection detection and response events.
- Data leakage or inappropriate retrieval indicators.
- Unusual tool execution patterns.
- Service availability and latency.
- User complaints and reported harmful outputs.
- Changes in model or provider behavior.
- Incidents and near misses.
- Compliance with approved data-use boundaries.
- Achievement of expected business outcomes.

Monitoring should have defined thresholds, owners, escalation rules, and response actions.

For example, an increase in the frequency of unauthorized tool requests may trigger a security investigation, while a sustained decline in output quality may require regression testing or a model change review.

The organization should avoid collecting excessive monitoring data that introduces unnecessary privacy or confidentiality risk.

Monitoring results should feed into the risk register, management reporting, reassessment decisions, and continuous improvement activities.

### 55. Risk Acceptance and Exception Governance

Risk acceptance records the authorized decision to retain an identified residual risk within the organization's permitted risk appetite.

An exception records a defined deviation from an established requirement or control. These concepts are related but distinct.

The use-case assessment should document:

- The risk or requirement involved.
- The reason the risk remains or the requirement cannot be met.
- The potential consequences.
- Existing controls and proposed compensating measures.
- The residual risk and evidence confidence.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approval authority.
- The duration and expiry conditions.
- Required monitoring and remediation.
- Events that trigger early review or revocation.

Risk acceptance must not be inferred from a project's continuation or the absence of an objection.

The authorized owner must understand the actual exposure and have the authority to accept it. Mandatory legal obligations, prohibited uses, and non-waivable requirements cannot be overridden through ordinary risk acceptance.

Exceptions should be reviewed before expiry, and repeated extensions should trigger escalation to determine whether the underlying design, control, or business justification must change.

### 56. Documentation, Evidence, and Audit Trail

The assessment process should maintain an evidence trail that enables an independent reviewer to reconstruct the decision.

The evidence set should connect the original proposal to the final approved configuration and subsequent operational changes.

Recommended records include:

- Use-case request and business justification.
- Intended-purpose and scope statement.
- Architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Initial screening and risk classification.
- Detailed risk scenarios and control mapping.
- Security, privacy, and impact assessments.
- Vendor assessments and contractual reviews.
- Testing plans, results, and remediation evidence.
- Pilot authorization and evaluation records.
- Residual risk and exception approvals.
- Production readiness and go-live decision.
- Monitoring plans and operational reports.
- Change records and reassessments.
- Incident and corrective action records.
- Suspension, retirement, and exit evidence.

The organization should apply appropriate access controls, retention requirements, versioning, and audit logging to the assessment repository.

Evidence should be relevant to the actual use case and deployment. A general vendor certification or model benchmark should not be treated as conclusive proof that the specific application meets its requirements.

### 57. Assessment Workflow Integration and Automation

The assessment workflow should connect AI use-case governance with procurement, architecture review, security, privacy, legal, development, and production change management.

Workflow automation can improve consistency by assigning reviews, tracking deadlines, enforcing required fields, and preventing progression when mandatory conditions remain incomplete.

Automation may support:

- Use-case registration and unique identifier assignment.
- Risk-based routing to specialist reviewers.
- Evidence collection and validation tracking.
- Risk and control register updates.
- Approval and exception workflows.
- Remediation assignment and deadline monitoring.
- Reassessment scheduling.
- Production release gates.
- Management reporting and audit evidence retrieval.

Automated scoring can support prioritization, but it should not replace judgment where the assessment involves ambiguous evidence, significant human impact, critical security deficiencies, or legal interpretation.

The workflow should distinguish between a completed form and a valid governance decision.

A use case should not automatically progress to production solely because all required fields contain values. The quality and substance of the evidence, the presence of material findings, and the authority of the decision-maker must also be considered.

### 58. Assessment Quality Assurance and Reviewer Consistency

Quality assurance helps ensure that assessments are complete, evidence-based, and consistent across business units.

The organization should periodically review a sample of assessments to determine whether reviewers apply the methodology correctly and whether risk decisions are supported by sufficient evidence.

Quality review criteria should include:

- Clarity of the intended purpose and boundaries.
- Completeness of system, data, and vendor identification.
- Accuracy of inherent risk classification.
- Coverage of material risk scenarios.
- Appropriate evaluation of controls.
- Consistency between findings and residual risk.
- Correct application of mandatory requirements.
- Appropriate approval authority.
- Adequacy of remediation and monitoring plans.
- Traceability of the final decision.

Calibration sessions can help reviewers resolve ambiguous criteria and improve consistency.

Where quality reviews identify recurring weaknesses, the organization should revise the methodology, improve training, clarify assessment questions, or strengthen workflow controls.

Quality assurance should not focus exclusively on documentation completeness. It must also evaluate whether the assessment reached a defensible conclusion.

### 59. Assessment Findings, Decision Outcomes, and Escalation

The assessment should conclude with a clear recommendation supported by documented findings and a decision appropriate to the use case's risk.

Potential outcomes include:

| Outcome                  | Meaning                                                                         |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Approved for next stage  | The use case may proceed to the next defined lifecycle stage                    |
| Approved with conditions | Progression is permitted only within documented restrictions and conditions     |
| Deferred                 | Additional information, redesign, or remediation is required before a decision  |
| Restricted               | Only specified users, data, functions, or operating conditions are permitted    |
| Rejected                 | The use case is prohibited, unjustified, or presents unacceptable risk          |
| Suspended                | An existing use case must temporarily stop pending investigation or remediation |
| Approved for retirement  | The use case may proceed through controlled decommissioning                     |

The assessment record should identify the decision-maker, decision date, applicable scope, supporting evidence, outstanding conditions, and review triggers.

Material findings should be assigned to accountable owners and tracked until closure or an authorized decision is made.

Critical issues should be escalated promptly rather than waiting for the normal review cycle.

If the final decision differs from the assessment team's recommendation, the rationale and approving authority should be documented.

### 60. Part 2 Core Principle — Risk Decisions Must Be Evidence-Based and Enforceable

A detailed AI use-case assessment must connect each material risk scenario to specific controls, evidence, remediation, and an authorized decision.

The organization should not confuse the presence of a control with its effectiveness, the completion of a questionnaire with production readiness, or a business approval with acceptance of all associated risks.

The assessment should produce measurable requirements, validated safeguards, explicit approval conditions, and a monitoring plan that remains relevant throughout the use case's lifecycle.

**Core distinction:** Inherent risk determines the level of scrutiny required; control effectiveness determines how much exposure is reduced; residual risk informs the decision; and governance authority determines whether the use case may proceed within the organization's rules.

## Part 3 — Governance Operating Model, Decision Authority, Accountability, and Lifecycle Oversight

### 61. AI Use-Case Governance Operating Model

An AI use-case governance operating model defines how the organization evaluates, authorizes, supervises, and periodically reassesses AI-enabled business activities.

The operating model translates assessment findings into formal decisions and enforceable responsibilities. It establishes who may propose a use case, who evaluates its risks, who approves its deployment, who accepts eligible residual risk, and who remains accountable for its operational performance.

The model should integrate AI governance with existing enterprise processes rather than create a disconnected approval structure.

Core governance components include:

- **Business accountability:** Ownership of the purpose, expected benefits, and business consequences.
- **AI governance oversight:** Consistent classification, assessment requirements, and decision traceability.
- **Technical assurance:** Validation of architecture, security, integration, and operational controls.
- **Risk and compliance oversight:** Evaluation of residual risk and applicable obligations.
- **Deployment authorization:** Formal approval of pilots, production releases, and material changes.
- **Lifecycle monitoring:** Continuous oversight of performance, incidents, dependencies, and emerging risks.
- **Independent assurance:** Evaluation of whether the governance framework and controls operate effectively.

The governance model should distinguish approval of an AI use case from approval of the technology supporting it. An approved model or vendor does not automatically authorize every business application that uses that component.

Similarly, a business case approved for a controlled pilot should not be interpreted as production authorization.

The organization should document the governance model in a charter or policy, including decision rights, escalation paths, approval thresholds, and mandatory review requirements.

### 62. Three Lines of Accountability for AI Use Cases

A Three Lines model can clarify responsibilities across business operations, risk oversight, and independent assurance.

The model should be adapted to the organization's size, operating structure, and regulatory context.

| Line                                            | Primary responsibility                                                       | Typical activities                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line — business and technology operations | Own the use case and operate the required controls                           | Define purpose, implement safeguards, monitor outputs, manage changes, respond to operational issues    |
| Second line — risk and compliance oversight     | Establish requirements and independently challenge first-line risk decisions | Set assessment methodology, review material risks, challenge exceptions, monitor aggregate exposure     |
| Third line — independent assurance              | Evaluate governance and control effectiveness independently                  | Audit approval records, test control design and operation, assess compliance with internal requirements |

The first line must not assume that completing an assessment transfers accountability to cybersecurity or AI governance.

The second line should not become the operational owner of every use case it reviews. Its role is to establish standards, challenge decisions, and provide oversight within its mandate.

The third line should retain appropriate independence and avoid taking responsibility for management decisions that it may later need to audit.

Where the organization cannot maintain complete separation, compensating measures may include independent review, second-person approval, external assurance, and periodic retrospective testing.

### 63. Executive Accountability for AI Use-Case Risk

Executive accountability is necessary when an AI use case creates material business, operational, financial, legal, cybersecurity, or human-impact exposure.

The organization should identify an executive accountable for the AI governance program and determine which additional executives are responsible for specific high-impact business activities.

Executive responsibilities may include:

- Ensuring the governance framework has sufficient authority and resources.
- Approving the organization's AI risk appetite through the appropriate governance process.
- Reviewing material risks and unresolved control deficiencies.
- Resolving conflicts between business objectives and mandatory controls.
- Ensuring that high-risk use cases receive appropriate independent scrutiny.
- Establishing escalation and suspension authority.
- Reviewing material incidents and systemic governance failures.
- Ensuring that the organization can demonstrate accountability for its AI deployments.

Executive approval must operate within applicable legal requirements and organizational delegations. It cannot legitimize a prohibited use or waive a mandatory obligation.

The organization should define the circumstances requiring executive or board-level review, such as high-impact decisions, critical operational dependencies, significant autonomous actions, or material residual risks outside normal delegated limits.

### 64. AI Governance Committee Charter and Responsibilities

An AI governance committee can coordinate decisions involving multiple business units and specialist functions.

Its charter should specify its mandate, membership, authority, quorum, meeting cadence, escalation procedures, and recordkeeping requirements.

The committee may be responsible for:

1. Reviewing high-risk or strategically significant use cases.
2. Challenging incomplete assessments and unsupported assumptions.
3. Confirming that specialist reviews are completed where required.
4. Resolving cross-functional disagreements.
5. Reviewing proposed exceptions and material residual risks within its authority.
6. Monitoring the use-case portfolio and emerging risk trends.
7. Reviewing significant incidents and lessons learned.
8. Escalating decisions beyond its delegated authority.
9. Monitoring overdue remediation and expired approvals.
10. Recommending suspension, restriction, or retirement where appropriate.

The committee should distinguish between making a recommendation, granting an approval, and accepting a risk. These are separate decisions and should be recorded accordingly.

For each material decision, the record should identify the use-case ID, scope, evidence reviewed, unresolved findings, decision authority, conditions, accountable owners, and review date.

A committee should not become a substitute for accountable business owners or technical control owners. Each use case must retain clearly assigned operational accountability.

### 65. Use-Case Decision Rights and Approval Matrix

Decision rights determine who is authorized to approve a use case at each lifecycle stage.

The approval model should reflect inherent risk, residual risk, data sensitivity, autonomy, business criticality, human impact, and applicable regulatory obligations.

| Decision                              | Typical authority                                        | Required consultation                                        |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Register a proposed use case          | Business sponsor or delegated intake authority           | Business owner                                               |
| Approve initial screening             | AI governance or designated screening authority          | Relevant specialist functions                                |
| Authorize a low-risk pilot            | Delegated business authority within policy               | System owner and applicable control owners                   |
| Authorize a high-risk pilot           | Designated senior governance authority                   | Cybersecurity, privacy, legal, AI governance, business owner |
| Approve production deployment         | Authority specified by the risk-based approval framework | Required technical and risk control owners                   |
| Accept eligible residual risk         | Authorized risk owner within delegated limits            | Relevant risk and control functions                          |
| Approve a material scope expansion    | Authority appropriate to the revised risk                | Affected specialist functions                                |
| Suspend a use case during an incident | Predefined operational or emergency authority            | Incident management and relevant stakeholders                |
| Approve retirement                    | Accountable service or business authority                | Security, data, legal, records, and operations as applicable |

The matrix should identify which reviews are mandatory, which approvals may be delegated, and which decisions require escalation.

Where legal or regulatory requirements specify particular responsibilities, the internal matrix must reflect those requirements.

The organization should also distinguish approval to experiment with a technology from approval to use it with production data or to influence consequential business decisions.

### 66. Business Owner Accountability and Decision Ownership

The business owner remains accountable for the business purpose and consequences of the AI use case throughout its lifecycle.

This responsibility includes determining whether the use case remains justified, whether the outputs are suitable for the intended process, and whether the organization continues to operate within the approved boundaries.

The business owner should:

- Maintain an accurate use-case description.
- Confirm the business objective and expected benefits.
- Ensure the use case does not expand beyond its approved purpose.
- Provide resources for required safeguards and remediation.
- Confirm that users understand the limitations of the system.
- Monitor business performance and relevant user feedback.
- Ensure operational fallback arrangements remain viable.
- Initiate reassessment when material changes occur.
- Escalate incidents, control failures, and unacceptable outputs.
- Participate in renewal, restriction, and retirement decisions.

The business owner may delegate operational activities, but the organization should explicitly document any change in accountability.

A technical team cannot independently determine whether the use case remains commercially justified or whether its consequences are acceptable to affected stakeholders.

Likewise, the business owner should not unilaterally override mandatory security, privacy, or legal requirements.

### 67. Independent Review of High-Risk Use Cases

High-risk use cases require an appropriate level of independent challenge to reduce the possibility that project pressure or optimistic assumptions distort the assessment.

The depth of review should be proportionate to the potential consequences, uncertainty, technical complexity, and level of autonomy.

Independent review may evaluate:

- Whether the business purpose and permitted boundaries are sufficiently precise.
- Whether the risk scenarios cover credible failure and misuse conditions.
- Whether the inherent risk classification is justified.
- Whether evidence supports the claimed control effectiveness.
- Whether testing reflects the intended operating context.
- Whether human oversight is meaningful.
- Whether residual risk is within the organization's risk appetite.
- Whether the proposed monitoring and intervention mechanisms are adequate.
- Whether the approval authority is appropriate.
- Whether unresolved uncertainties prevent a defensible decision.

For high-impact applications, independent review may involve specialist security assessment, privacy review, model evaluation, human-impact analysis, or external assurance.

The reviewer should disclose conflicts of interest and should not be the sole validator of their own work where meaningful independence is required.

If the reviewer disagrees with the project team's conclusion, the disagreement and its resolution should be documented.

### 68. AI Risk Appetite and Use-Case Acceptance Criteria

AI risk appetite defines the types and levels of AI-related exposure the organization is willing to retain while pursuing its objectives.

The organization should translate risk appetite into practical acceptance criteria, escalation thresholds, and mandatory control requirements.

Risk appetite may address:

- Confidential or regulated data exposure.
- Use of AI in consequential decisions.
- Uncontrolled autonomous actions.
- Unreliable outputs in critical processes.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Insufficient model or supplier transparency.
- Dependence on a single provider for critical operations.
- Inability to investigate or reconstruct material AI actions.
- Unacceptable impacts on individuals.
- Inability to meet applicable legal obligations.

Acceptance criteria should distinguish risks that may be accepted within delegated authority from risks that require remediation, redesign, restriction, or rejection.

A use case should not be approved solely because its aggregate risk score falls below a numerical threshold. Mandatory requirements and severe risk scenarios may require separate decision rules.

Risk appetite should also be reviewed when the organization's operating environment, regulatory obligations, AI capabilities, or threat landscape changes materially.

### 69. Risk Thresholds and Escalation Triggers

Risk thresholds establish when a use case requires enhanced review, higher-level approval, additional safeguards, or suspension.

Thresholds should combine quantitative indicators with qualitative triggers.

| Trigger                                                        | Governance response                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Use case processes highly sensitive or regulated information   | Enhanced data protection, security, and legal review                |
| AI output influences consequential decisions about individuals | Human-impact assessment and appropriate legal review                |
| Agent can perform privileged or irreversible actions           | Enhanced authorization design, testing, and approval                |
| Critical control lacks sufficient evidence                     | Additional validation or restriction before progression             |
| Supplier cannot support required incident investigation        | Escalation and determination of whether the use case remains viable |
| Material model behavior changes                                | Reassessment and regression testing                                 |
| Critical security finding remains unresolved                   | Remediation, containment, restriction, or suspension as appropriate |
| AI failure could disrupt a critical service                    | Resilience testing and approved fallback arrangements               |
| Use case expands beyond approved data or user boundaries       | Formal change review before expansion                               |
| Significant uncertainty remains about legal permissibility     | Defer the affected activity pending appropriate legal determination |

Each trigger should have a designated owner, a required response, a target timeframe, and an escalation authority.

Thresholds should be periodically tested for consistency. Similar use cases should receive comparable scrutiny unless documented differences justify different outcomes.

### 70. Residual Risk Acceptance and Authorization

Residual risk acceptance is a formal decision to retain eligible risk after considering applicable controls and mitigations.

The decision must be informed by the assessment evidence and made by an authorized risk owner.

A risk acceptance record should include:

- Use-case ID and approved scope.
- Relevant risk scenarios.
- Inherent and residual risk ratings.
- Supporting evidence and evidence limitations.
- Existing controls and compensating measures.
- Potential consequences and affected stakeholders.
- Reasons the risk remains unresolved.
- Accountable risk owner and approving authority.
- Required monitoring and restrictions.
- Expiry date or review trigger.
- Remediation milestones, where applicable.
- Conditions that trigger suspension or renewed review.

Risk acceptance does not transfer all accountability to the approving executive or remove the responsibilities of the business and technical owners.

It also does not override binding legal requirements, prohibited-use restrictions, or other obligations that cannot lawfully be waived.

Acceptance should be time-bound where the risk is temporary or dependent on remediation. Repeated extensions should trigger review of the underlying architecture, control strategy, and business justification.

### 71. Segregation of Duties and Conflict Management

Segregation of duties reduces the risk that commercial pressure, personal incentives, or inadequate challenge lead to inappropriate AI deployment decisions.

The organization should separate incompatible responsibilities where practicable.

Examples include:

- The business sponsor should not be the sole independent reviewer of the use case.
- The person implementing a critical control should not be the only person validating its effectiveness.
- A project manager should not unilaterally approve a material risk exception.
- The person recording an approval should not be able to alter the decision evidence without traceability.
- Procurement should not override a mandatory security or compliance rejection.
- An assessor should disclose material conflicts that could affect the objectivity of their review.

Where full separation is impractical, compensating measures may include second-person approval, independent technical testing, external review, or periodic retrospective assessment.

The organization should also address conflicts between project delivery targets and governance requirements. A launch deadline is not sufficient justification for bypassing mandatory assessment or control implementation.

### 72. Remediation Prioritization and Corrective Action Governance

Assessment findings must be converted into tracked corrective actions.

The remediation process should prioritize findings based on severity, exploitability, exposure, business criticality, human impact, regulatory significance, and the effectiveness of available compensating controls.

A corrective action record should contain:

- Finding ID and associated use-case ID.
- Description of the control deficiency.
- Relevant risk scenario.
- Required remediation.
- Accountable owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards.
- Dependencies and resource requirements.
- Validation criteria.
- Closure evidence.
- Escalation requirements if overdue.

Critical findings that undermine safe or lawful operation should prevent the affected activity from proceeding until the required conditions are satisfied.

For less severe findings, the organization may permit restricted operation if the residual risk is acceptable and the applicable policy permits it.

Closure should require evidence that the corrective action has been implemented and that the relevant control operates as intended. Administrative completion of a task is not sufficient proof of effective remediation.

### 73. Dependencies, Shared Controls, and Inherited Risk

AI use cases frequently depend on controls operated by other teams or external providers.

Examples include identity services, cloud infrastructure, data platforms, model APIs, retrieval systems, monitoring services, and enterprise applications.

The assessment should distinguish between:

- Controls implemented directly by the use-case team.
- Controls inherited from enterprise platforms.
- Controls operated by external providers.
- Controls shared between the organization and a supplier.
- Controls that remain unimplemented or unverified.

For each material dependency, the assessment should document the responsible party, applicable control, evidence source, limitations, and consequences if the dependency fails.

An inherited control should not be treated as effective merely because another team or supplier claims responsibility for it.

The organization should verify that the control covers the actual deployment and that any complementary customer controls are implemented.

For example, a cloud provider may supply identity integration capabilities, but the use-case owner may remain responsible for configuring permissions, restricting service identities, and reviewing access.

Where a critical dependency cannot provide sufficient assurance, the organization should determine whether alternative controls, architectural changes, or a different provider are required.

### 74. Human Oversight Governance and Accountability

Human oversight must be assigned to specific roles and supported by clear procedures.

The organization should identify who reviews outputs, who may authorize consequential actions, who investigates disputed results, and who can suspend the use case.

Oversight governance should address:

- Required reviewer qualifications and training.
- Access to relevant source evidence.
- Authority to reject or modify AI recommendations.
- Mandatory review points and escalation criteria.
- Documentation of material review decisions.
- Management of conflicts of interest.
- Monitoring for automation bias and review fatigue.
- Procedures for correcting errors and responding to complaints.
- Emergency intervention and suspension authority.

The assessment should distinguish routine user verification from formal human oversight of consequential decisions.

Human oversight is ineffective if reviewers cannot understand the information presented, do not have sufficient time, or lack authority to prevent the system from proceeding.

For agentic systems, the organization should also define which actions may occur automatically and which require a separate authorization decision.

### 75. Stakeholder Engagement and Human-Impact Governance

Use cases that may affect individuals or groups should include an appropriate process for identifying and evaluating stakeholder impacts.

The process should consider foreseeable effects on privacy, fairness, access to services, employment, safety, accessibility, and other relevant interests.

Governance requirements may include:

- Identifying directly and indirectly affected stakeholders.
- Evaluating potential benefits and adverse impacts.
- Determining whether consultation is appropriate.
- Establishing transparency and communication requirements.
- Defining complaint, review, and correction mechanisms.
- Assigning responsibility for monitoring adverse outcomes.
- Determining whether a formal AI impact assessment is required.
- Escalating severe or disproportionate impacts.

Stakeholder engagement should be proportionate to the nature and scale of the potential impact.

For high-impact use cases, the organization should not rely solely on the views of the project sponsor or technical team to determine whether the system's effects are acceptable.

The assessment should record material assumptions, limitations, and the rationale for the governance decision.

### 76. Regulatory and Legal Accountability

The organization should establish which legal and regulatory requirements apply to the use case and who is accountable for ensuring that they are addressed.

Relevant obligations may depend on jurisdiction, intended purpose, sector, data processing, organizational role, and the way the AI system influences decisions.

The assessment should identify:

- Applicable legal or regulatory requirements.
- The relevant organizational role or responsibility.
- Required documentation and records.
- Necessary technical or organizational safeguards.
- Transparency and human oversight obligations, where applicable.
- Data protection and transfer requirements.
- Contractual obligations and third-party dependencies.
- Conditions that prohibit or restrict the use case.
- Evidence required to demonstrate compliance.
- The owner responsible for maintaining compliance over time.

The organization should distinguish legal analysis from technical control assessment. A technically secure system may still be unsuitable if its intended use is impermissible or its processing lacks the required legal basis.

Likewise, a legal review does not establish that technical safeguards are implemented effectively.

Where legal interpretation remains uncertain, the use case should be escalated to the appropriate legal or compliance authority before the affected activity proceeds.

### 77. AI Use-Case Portfolio Governance

Portfolio governance evaluates AI use cases collectively rather than treating every application as an isolated project.

This perspective helps the organization identify duplicated initiatives, shared dependencies, concentration risk, recurring control weaknesses, and aggregate exposure.

Portfolio governance should track:

- Number of proposed, approved, active, restricted, and retired use cases.
- Distribution of use cases by risk tier.
- Critical business processes dependent on AI.
- Use cases involving sensitive or regulated data.
- Applications with autonomous or consequential capabilities.
- Shared models, platforms, and suppliers.
- Outstanding critical findings and exceptions.
- Expired approvals and overdue reassessments.
- Material incidents and recurring failure patterns.
- Use cases without viable fallback arrangements.

The portfolio should be linked to the AI system inventory, model inventory, vendor inventory, enterprise risk register, and relevant business service records.

Portfolio reporting should identify whether several individually acceptable use cases create a combined risk that exceeds the organization's risk appetite.

For example, widespread dependence on one external model provider may create a significant concentration risk even when each application has a satisfactory individual assessment.

### 78. AI Use-Case Change Governance

Changes to an AI use case can invalidate earlier assumptions about risk, authorization, or control effectiveness.

The organization should establish formal change criteria that determine whether a change requires a limited review, a full reassessment, or renewed approval.

Material changes include:

- A new or expanded business purpose.
- New user groups or affected populations.
- Additional sensitive data sources.
- A new model or supplier.
- New connectors, APIs, or tool permissions.
- Increased autonomy.
- Changes to human review requirements.
- Integration with a critical business system.
- Changes in hosting or data-processing locations.
- Significant changes in output quality or behavior.
- New legal or regulatory obligations.

The change record should identify affected controls, risk scenarios, testing requirements, approval authority, and implementation conditions.

The organization should not assume that an approved use case remains acceptable after a material expansion simply because the underlying model or application name has not changed.

Where the change increases exposure, the affected capability should remain restricted until the required review and approval are completed.

### 79. Periodic Reassessment and Approval Renewal

Periodic reassessment confirms that an AI use case remains appropriate as technology, business requirements, threats, and obligations evolve.

The reassessment frequency should be based on risk, system criticality, level of autonomy, data sensitivity, and the pace of change.

The process should review:

- Continued validity of the intended purpose.
- Current architecture and data flows.
- Model and supplier changes.
- Control effectiveness and evidence currency.
- Incident history and near misses.
- Output quality and performance trends.
- Human oversight effectiveness.
- Open findings and risk acceptances.
- Compliance with approved operating boundaries.
- Continued business value and availability of alternatives.
- Changes in applicable requirements.
- Readiness of continuity and exit arrangements.

The organization should define both scheduled reassessment dates and event-driven triggers.

A scheduled review should not delay action when a significant incident or material change occurs.

The reassessment outcome should confirm continued approval, impose new conditions, restrict the use case, require remediation, suspend operation, or initiate retirement.

The organization should preserve the previous decision record and document the reasons for any revised risk classification or approval status.

### 80. Suspension, Restriction, and Emergency Intervention Governance

The organization should define the circumstances under which an AI use case may be restricted or suspended.

Suspension may be necessary when continued operation creates unacceptable exposure, a critical control fails, the system behaves outside its approved boundaries, or the organization cannot establish whether a material incident has been contained.

The governance procedure should specify:

- Events that trigger intervention.
- Who may impose an immediate restriction.
- Who may authorize full suspension.
- How technical access and tool permissions are disabled.
- How affected users and stakeholders are notified.
- How evidence is preserved.
- How business continuity is maintained.
- Who determines whether service can be restored.
- What testing and approvals are required before resumption.

Emergency containment should not depend on a routine governance committee meeting.

For high-risk AI agents, intervention may require revoking credentials, disabling connectors, stopping scheduled tasks, blocking API access, or restricting the system to read-only operation.

The organization should document emergency actions and review them after the immediate threat is controlled.

Restoration should require evidence that the underlying risk has been addressed or that a formally authorized, policy-compliant decision permits restricted operation.

### 81. Retirement and Decommissioning Governance

AI use cases should be retired through a controlled process that removes unnecessary exposure and preserves required records.

Retirement may occur because the business purpose is no longer valid, the system is replaced, the supplier relationship ends, the technology becomes unsupported, or the residual risk is no longer acceptable.

The retirement plan should address:

- Formal authorization to retire the use case.
- Notification to affected users and business owners.
- Disabling interfaces, workflows, and scheduled executions.
- Revoking credentials and service identities.
- Removing integrations and external permissions.
- Returning or deleting data as required.
- Retaining required audit and business records.
- Handling model artifacts, embeddings, logs, and derived data.
- Closing open risk acceptances and exceptions.
- Updating system, model, vendor, and risk inventories.
- Confirming that replacement processes are operational.
- Verifying that the retired capability can no longer execute actions.

The organization should distinguish between retiring the business use case and retiring a shared AI platform that supports other approved applications.

Shared components should not be removed without assessing the effect on dependent services.

Closure should be evidenced through a decommissioning record and, where appropriate, independent validation.

### 82. Management Reporting and Key Risk Indicators

Management reporting should provide actionable information about the health of the AI use-case portfolio and the effectiveness of governance controls.

Potential indicators include:

| Indicator                                                         | Governance purpose                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Percentage of active use cases with current assessments           | Measures assessment coverage                  |
| Number of high-risk use cases without valid production approval   | Identifies unauthorized or outdated operation |
| Number of critical findings past deadline                         | Highlights unresolved material exposure       |
| Number of expired risk acceptances                                | Identifies governance and authorization gaps  |
| Percentage of material changes assessed before implementation     | Measures change-control effectiveness         |
| Percentage of high-risk use cases with tested fallback procedures | Indicates resilience readiness                |
| Number of incidents linked to known control weaknesses            | Supports root-cause analysis                  |
| Percentage of use cases with assigned business owners             | Measures accountability coverage              |
| Number of overdue reassessments                                   | Identifies lifecycle oversight weaknesses     |
| Percentage of high-risk assessments passing quality review        | Measures assessment consistency               |

Each indicator should have a defined calculation, data source, owner, reporting frequency, threshold, and escalation response.

Metrics should be interpreted in context. A low incident count may indicate effective controls, but it may also reflect weak detection or underreporting.

Management reporting should connect the metrics to decisions and corrective actions rather than merely presenting status information.

### 83. Board-Level AI Risk Reporting

Board-level reporting should focus on strategic exposure, material risks, risk appetite, and decisions requiring senior oversight.

It should avoid overwhelming the board with routine assessment details that are better managed operationally.

Relevant reporting topics may include:

- Aggregate AI use-case exposure by risk tier.
- High-impact applications and their approval status.
- Material incidents and emerging risk trends.
- Concentration in critical AI providers or platforms.
- Significant human-impact or regulatory concerns.
- Material residual risks and exceptions.
- Overdue remediation affecting critical operations.
- Dependence on AI for essential business services.
- Effectiveness of continuity and suspension arrangements.
- Progress against the organization's AI governance objectives.

Board reporting should explain the potential business consequences, the safeguards in place, the remaining uncertainty, and the management action required.

A single portfolio risk score should not obscure a critical individual use case or a risk that falls outside the organization's risk appetite.

The board should receive material information needed to exercise its oversight responsibilities, consistent with the organization's governance arrangements and applicable obligations.

### 84. Independent Assurance and Audit Readiness

Audit readiness requires evidence that AI use cases were assessed, authorized, implemented, and monitored in accordance with the organization's governance requirements.

The evidence chain should connect the original proposal to the current operating configuration and the most recent approval decision.

Typical evidence includes:

- Approved governance policies and methodology versions.
- Use-case inventory and risk classifications.
- Business justification and intended-purpose statements.
- Assessment questionnaires and risk scenarios.
- Technical, privacy, legal, and impact assessments.
- Control validation evidence and test results.
- Approval and risk acceptance records.
- Remediation and exception registers.
- Pilot and production authorization records.
- Monitoring reports and change records.
- Incident and reassessment documentation.
- Retirement and decommissioning evidence.

Independent assurance should test both documentation and operational effectiveness.

For example, an auditor may select a sample of production use cases and verify that the approved purpose matches actual functionality, access restrictions are implemented, required human review occurs, and material changes are subject to review.

The organization should be able to explain why a decision was made, who authorized it, what evidence was considered, and which conditions remain in force.

### 85. Governance Effectiveness and Continuous Improvement

The AI use-case governance framework should be evaluated periodically to determine whether it reduces risk and supports responsible adoption.

Effectiveness should be measured through outcomes, not only the number of completed assessments.

Useful measures include:

- Consistency of risk classifications.
- Quality of assessment evidence.
- Timeliness of remediation.
- Frequency of repeat control failures.
- Effectiveness of human oversight.
- Number of unauthorized scope expansions.
- Accuracy of portfolio and approval records.
- Response time to material incidents.
- Percentage of critical use cases with tested fallback procedures.
- Effectiveness of reassessment and retirement processes.

Continuous improvement should use information from incidents, near misses, audits, user feedback, model evaluations, vendor assessments, and changes in applicable requirements.

Where recurring weaknesses are identified, the organization should determine whether the cause lies in unclear policy, insufficient technical controls, weak training, poor workflow design, inadequate resources, or inappropriate use-case selection.

Methodology changes should be controlled, documented, and assessed for their effect on existing approvals.

The governance function should also evaluate whether the process is proportionate. Excessive administrative burden can encourage workarounds, while insufficient scrutiny can allow material risks to go undetected.

The objective is a consistent, risk-based governance capability that enables justified AI adoption while preserving accountability and control.

### 86. Part 3 Core Principle — Governance Must Remain Accountable Throughout the AI Lifecycle

AI use-case governance must establish clear decision rights, independent challenge, accountable ownership, risk acceptance boundaries, and continuous lifecycle oversight.

The organization should ensure that every use case has a responsible business owner, an authorized decision-maker, documented control responsibilities, and a process for responding to material changes or incidents.

Approval is not permanent permission to operate without review. The authorization remains valid only while the use case stays within its approved purpose, controls, data boundaries, and operating conditions.

**Core distinction:** Governance establishes who may decide and under what conditions; risk assessment establishes what exposure exists; technical controls reduce that exposure; and continuous oversight determines whether the original decision remains valid.

# 20.10 — AI Use-Case Assessment Template

## Part 4 — Technical Architecture, Implementation, Validation, Operational Assurance, and Assessment Deliverables

### 87. AI Use-Case Reference Architecture

The AI use-case reference architecture defines the technical components, trust boundaries, data flows, integrations, and control points required to deliver an approved business outcome.

The assessment must evaluate the complete AI-enabled solution rather than treating the model as an isolated component. A business use case may depend on an AI model, orchestration layer, identity provider, enterprise data repositories, external APIs, policy enforcement services, and human approval workflows.

The architecture should demonstrate how the organization enforces the approved use-case boundaries and prevents the AI system from accessing data or performing actions beyond its authorization.

```
Business Users and Applications
              |
              v
     Enterprise Identity Layer
              |
              v
       AI Application Layer
              |
       +------+------+
       |             |
       v             v
  Policy Engine   Input Validation
       |             |
       +------+------+
              |
              v
     AI Orchestration Layer
              |
       +------+------+
       |             |
       v             v
   Model API     Retrieval Layer
       |             |
       |        Enterprise Data
       |             |
       +------+------+
              |
              v
     Output Validation Layer
              |
       +------+------+
       |             |
       v             v
 Human Review    Authorized Tools
       |             |
       +------+------+
              |
              v
    Approved Business Action

Cross-Cutting Controls:
Identity | Logging | Monitoring | Data Protection
Change Management | Incident Response | Governance
```

This is an illustrative architecture. Actual deployments may place orchestration, retrieval, output validation, and policy enforcement within different trust zones.

The assessment should identify:

- Components operated by the organization.
- Components managed by external providers.
- Trust boundaries between components.
- Data and control flows crossing those boundaries.
- Points where authorization is enforced.
- Points where human review is required.
- Dependencies that could introduce correlated failures.
- Controls available to restrict, suspend, or disable the use case.

The architecture record should be linked to the use-case assessment, AI system inventory, model inventory, vendor inventory, data-flow documentation, and relevant security assessment.

### 88. Trust Boundaries and Data-Flow Validation

A trust boundary separates components or environments that operate under different security assumptions or administrative control.

The assessment should identify how prompts, files, retrieved information, model outputs, telemetry, and tool requests move between users, enterprise systems, AI providers, and downstream applications.

| Data flow                                | Principal risk                                              | Required validation                                          |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| User input to AI application             | Sensitive or malicious content enters the system            | Input handling, data classification, and access restrictions |
| Application to external model            | Unauthorized disclosure or unapproved processing            | Approved data-use terms, encryption, and transfer controls   |
| Enterprise repository to retrieval layer | Excessive access to confidential documents                  | Authorization enforcement and retrieval scope                |
| Retrieved content to model               | Sensitive data or malicious instructions influence output   | Context restrictions and prompt-injection safeguards         |
| Model output to downstream application   | Incorrect or malicious content triggers unintended behavior | Output validation and business-rule enforcement              |
| Model output to external tool            | Unauthorized action or privilege escalation                 | Deterministic authorization and action constraints           |
| Application to logging service           | Sensitive prompts or outputs are retained unnecessarily     | Logging policy, minimization, and retention controls         |
| Vendor or subprocessor access            | Uncontrolled access to data or configuration                | Access governance, contractual controls, and auditability    |

The assessment should validate the actual data flow rather than rely exclusively on architecture diagrams supplied during the proposal stage.

Where the system's design is incomplete, the assessment should record the uncertainty and determine whether it prevents a reliable risk decision.

Encryption does not independently establish that a transfer is appropriate. The recipient must be authorized, the processing purpose must be permitted, and the applicable safeguards must be implemented.

### 89. Identity, Authentication, and Authorization Architecture

The use case should integrate with the organization's identity and access-management framework wherever appropriate.

The assessment should establish how users, administrators, applications, service accounts, and AI agents authenticate and receive permissions.

Required evaluation areas include:

- Authentication and identity federation.
- Multifactor authentication for appropriate access paths.
- Role-based or attribute-based authorization.
- Least-privilege permissions.
- Service-account and workload-identity management.
- Credential storage, rotation, and revocation.
- Administrative access restrictions.
- Privileged-access monitoring.
- User lifecycle management.
- Separation of development, testing, and production access.

For retrieval-based applications, authorization must be enforced at the point where data is accessed. A user should not obtain information through the AI application that the user could not legitimately access through the underlying repository.

For agentic applications, the identity used to execute a tool call should have only the permissions required for the approved task. The model must not be able to expand those permissions simply by generating an instruction or requesting a different tool.

Testing should include revoked users, expired credentials, unauthorized requests, privilege escalation attempts, and attempts to access information outside the user's authorized scope.

### 90. API Security and Integration Controls

AI use cases frequently depend on APIs connecting the application to external models, internal data repositories, enterprise applications, and downstream services.

The assessment should determine whether these interfaces enforce appropriate authentication, authorization, input validation, rate limiting, and monitoring.

Relevant controls include:

- Strong API authentication.
- Narrowly scoped API credentials.
- Secure transport and certificate validation.
- Secrets management and credential rotation.
- Request validation and payload-size restrictions.
- Rate limiting and abuse prevention.
- Network egress restrictions where appropriate.
- API version management.
- Secure error handling.
- Monitoring for anomalous request patterns.
- Controlled access to administrative endpoints.
- Documented integration dependencies.

The organization should assess whether model-generated content can influence API parameters, database queries, workflow commands, or external requests.

Generated output must not be treated as inherently trustworthy simply because it originates from an approved model. Where output influences a downstream operation, the application should validate its structure, values, authorization, and business meaning.

API security should be evaluated together with the use case's business logic and permission model, not only at the network perimeter.

### 91. Data Isolation and Retrieval Security

When an AI application retrieves enterprise information, the assessment should verify that access restrictions remain effective throughout ingestion, indexing, retrieval, and response generation.

The organization should determine:

- Which repositories and data sources are connected.
- Whether source permissions are preserved.
- How identities and access policies are propagated.
- How revoked permissions affect indexed content.
- How deleted documents are removed from retrieval indexes.
- Whether tenant and user boundaries are enforced.
- How embeddings and retrieval metadata are protected.
- How retrieved content is logged and retained.
- How unauthorized retrieval is detected.
- How source provenance is preserved.

Embeddings and vector representations should not automatically be considered anonymous or non-sensitive. Their risk depends on the source information, embedding process, available auxiliary data, and potential inference or reconstruction.

The assessment should test unauthorized retrieval, cross-user access, deleted documents, stale permissions, and attempts to retrieve restricted information through indirect queries.

If retrieval security depends on a vendor-managed service, the organization should obtain evidence appropriate to the service's risk and document the division of responsibility between the supplier and customer.

### 92. Prompt Injection and Untrusted Input Architecture

Prompt injection occurs when instructions embedded in user input, retrieved documents, uploaded files, or external content attempt to influence the AI system in ways that violate its intended purpose or security boundaries.

The assessment should determine how the architecture limits the consequences of malicious or misleading content.

Required safeguards may include:

- Clear separation between trusted configuration and untrusted content.
- Restricted access to sensitive context.
- Retrieval authorization.
- Input and content handling controls.
- Deterministic authorization for external actions.
- Validation of model-generated tool requests.
- Restricted execution permissions.
- Monitoring of suspicious behavior.
- Testing against direct and indirect prompt injection.
- Emergency restriction or shutdown capability.

Prompt instructions such as "ignore malicious instructions" are not sufficient by themselves to establish a reliable security boundary.

The architecture should assume that model behavior may be manipulated and prevent that manipulation from automatically granting access to sensitive resources or executing unauthorized actions.

For example, if a retrieved document instructs an agent to export confidential records, the agent should still be unable to perform that action unless the request independently satisfies the application's authorization rules.

### 93. AI Agent Permission Boundaries and Tool Governance

Agentic use cases require a distinct assessment of delegated authority because the system may select tools, execute multiple steps, and interact with external resources.

The assessment should identify each tool, the identity used to invoke it, the resources it can access, the permitted operations, and the conditions under which approval is required.

| Control area        | Assessment requirement                                                     |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tool inventory      | Maintain an approved list of available tools and connectors                |
| Permission scope    | Restrict each tool to the minimum necessary operations                     |
| Identity            | Use managed identities or appropriately protected credentials              |
| Action validation   | Validate parameters and business rules before execution                    |
| High-impact actions | Require additional approval where warranted                                |
| Execution isolation | Restrict code execution and untrusted workloads                            |
| Resource limits     | Prevent excessive transactions, loops, or resource consumption             |
| Audit logging       | Record material tool requests, authorization decisions, and outcomes       |
| Emergency control   | Support rapid revocation, disablement, or suspension                       |
| Delegation          | Prevent unauthorized privilege expansion or uncontrolled downstream agents |

The assessment should distinguish between an agent that recommends an action, one that prepares an action for approval, and one that executes the action autonomously.

Testing should evaluate malicious prompts, misleading retrieval results, invalid tool arguments, unauthorized resources, repeated action attempts, and attempts to bypass approval controls.

### 94. Output Validation and Downstream Action Safety

AI-generated outputs may be inaccurate, incomplete, manipulated, or inconsistent with the intended business purpose.

The assessment should determine how outputs are validated before they influence business decisions, create records, communicate externally, or trigger technical actions.

Controls may include:

- Schema validation for structured outputs.
- Validation of values against business rules.
- Source verification for factual claims.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Restrictions on executable content.
- Safe rendering and output encoding.
- Authorization checks before downstream actions.
- Reconciliation of changes to business records.
- Monitoring of error patterns and anomalous outputs.
- Procedures for correcting or reversing mistakes.

For example, an AI system that generates a database update should not execute the update merely because the generated statement is syntactically valid. The application should independently verify the requesting identity, target record, permitted operation, and applicable business rules.

The assessment should also identify whether generated content becomes part of an official record and whether provenance or review information must be retained.

Output validation must be proportionate to the consequences of error. High-impact outputs require stronger validation and oversight than low-impact drafts.

### 95. Model and Application Evaluation Strategy

The assessment should define how the model and surrounding application will be evaluated for the intended purpose.

Model-level benchmarks may provide useful evidence, but they do not establish that the complete application is safe, reliable, or suitable for its operational context.

The evaluation strategy should include:

- Representative inputs and expected outputs.
- Relevant edge cases and failure conditions.
- Task-specific accuracy and quality criteria.
- Security and adversarial test scenarios.
- Privacy and data-exposure tests.
- Human oversight validation.
- Integration and authorization tests.
- Performance and resilience testing.
- Regression testing following material changes.
- Documented acceptance criteria.

For generative AI, evaluation may include factual consistency, unsupported claims, omission of important information, source attribution, harmful content, and instruction-following behavior.

For AI agents, evaluation should include whether actions remain within permitted boundaries and whether the agent stops or escalates when necessary.

The organization should record the evaluation dataset, test methodology, limitations, results, and approval decision. Testing should be repeated when changes materially affect the model, application, data, or operating context.

### 96. AI Safety and Failure-Mode Analysis

The assessment should identify how the use case can fail and what safeguards prevent those failures from causing unacceptable harm.

Failure analysis should include both technical malfunctions and foreseeable misuse or over-reliance.

Examples include:

- Incorrect or misleading outputs.
- Unexpected model behavior after a service update.
- Missing or stale source information.
- Unauthorized data retrieval.
- Failure to recognize uncertainty.
- Unsafe tool selection.
- Excessive action execution.
- Failure of human review.
- Unavailable external dependencies.
- Corrupted or manipulated input data.
- Incomplete logging.
- Failure to stop after an error.

For each material failure mode, the assessment should identify its potential effect, detection method, containment mechanism, recovery action, and accountable owner.

The organization should define safe failure behavior. If the system cannot verify an authorization decision or critical dependency, it may need to deny the action, request human review, or switch to a restricted mode.

For high-impact applications, failure analysis should consider whether errors can cascade into other systems or affect multiple individuals before detection.

### 97. Data Protection and Retention Architecture

The assessment should determine how the use case protects information throughout collection, processing, storage, retrieval, logging, export, and deletion.

Relevant safeguards include:

- Data minimization.
- Purpose-based access restrictions.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Approved data-processing locations.
- Retention limits.
- Secure deletion and lifecycle management.
- Restrictions on training or secondary use.
- Protection of prompts and generated outputs.
- Access controls for logs and telemetry.
- Appropriate handling of backups and derived data.
- Data-subject rights support where applicable.

The organization should distinguish between data stored in the primary application, data processed by an external model provider, data retained in observability systems, and data embedded in retrieval indexes.

A deletion request or retention-policy change may need to propagate across several systems. The assessment should determine whether the organization can identify and manage those dependencies.

Where personal data is processed, the relevant privacy and legal reviews should confirm the applicable requirements. Technical controls alone do not establish a lawful purpose or appropriate authority for processing.

### 98. Logging, Monitoring, and Auditability

The use case should generate sufficient operational evidence to support security monitoring, incident investigation, performance review, and accountability.

The assessment should determine which events are logged, how long records are retained, who can access them, and how they are protected against unauthorized modification.

Relevant events may include:

- User authentication and access decisions.
- Configuration and permission changes.
- Model and service version changes.
- Data retrieval and export activity.
- Tool requests and executed actions.
- Policy enforcement and blocked operations.
- Human approval and override decisions.
- Security alerts and incident response actions.
- Administrative access.
- Material errors and degraded operation.

Logging should be designed to avoid unnecessary collection of sensitive prompts, outputs, credentials, or personal data.

Where full content logging is inappropriate, metadata and event identifiers may provide sufficient traceability for some investigations.

The assessment should also verify that logs can be correlated with enterprise identity, application, and security monitoring systems.

Monitoring must have defined thresholds, response owners, and escalation paths. Collecting telemetry without an operational process for acting on it does not establish effective monitoring.

### 99. Operational Resilience and Safe Degradation

The assessment should establish how the business process will operate when the AI service becomes unavailable, produces unreliable outputs, or must be suspended.

Resilience requirements should reflect business criticality and the consequences of interruption.

Potential controls include:

- Manual fallback procedures.
- Alternative software or service providers.
- Read-only operating modes.
- Transaction or action limits.
- Queuing and deferred processing.
- Automatic suspension of unsafe functions.
- Recovery and restoration procedures.
- Communication to affected users.
- Reconciliation after service restoration.
- Tested exit or migration arrangements.

The organization should identify which functions can safely continue without AI and which must stop if the AI component becomes unavailable.

For example, an AI assistant that drafts internal summaries may be disabled with limited impact. An AI-dependent workflow supporting a critical operational process may require a tested alternative procedure.

The assessment should record relevant recovery objectives, dependencies, assumptions, and evidence that the fallback can operate as intended.

A continuity plan should not rely on an alternative that has never been tested or whose own risks remain unassessed.

### 100. AI Change Control and Configuration Management

The use case should have a controlled process for managing changes to models, prompts, system instructions, retrieval sources, connectors, permissions, and deployment configuration.

Changes can affect the behavior and risk profile of the application even when the underlying model remains unchanged.

The change-management process should classify changes by risk and determine the required review, testing, and approval.

Material changes may include:

- Replacing the model or provider.
- Expanding the intended purpose.
- Adding data sources.
- Increasing user access.
- Enabling new tools or connectors.
- Increasing autonomy.
- Changing output-validation logic.
- Altering data retention or processing locations.
- Modifying critical security controls.
- Changing human review requirements.

The organization should maintain versioned records of relevant configurations, test results, approval decisions, and rollback procedures.

Emergency changes should be governed by a defined process and reviewed after implementation.

Where a change materially increases risk, the affected capability should remain restricted until the necessary assessment and approval have been completed.

### 101. Secure Development and Deployment Requirements

Where the use case involves custom development or significant integration, the assessment should determine whether the organization applies appropriate secure development practices.

The development process should address application security, AI-specific threats, configuration management, dependency security, testing, and release control.

Relevant requirements include:

- Threat modeling.
- Secure coding and code review.
- Dependency and vulnerability scanning.
- Secrets management.
- Separation of development and production.
- Restricted deployment permissions.
- Infrastructure-as-code validation.
- Security testing before release.
- Controlled rollout and rollback.
- Model and prompt configuration management.
- Logging and monitoring readiness.
- Documented remediation of material findings.

AI-specific development testing should include prompt injection, unsafe output handling, retrieval authorization, agent permission boundaries, and model behavior changes where applicable.

The assessment should confirm that the production deployment uses the configuration that was evaluated and approved.

A successful prototype or demonstration should not be treated as sufficient evidence of production security.

### 102. Technical Acceptance Criteria and Release Gates

The assessment should define measurable criteria that must be satisfied before a use case progresses to pilot or production.

Acceptance criteria should be linked to the risks identified and the controls required to address them.

Examples include:

| Acceptance criterion                               | Verification evidence                         |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Only authorized users can access the application   | Identity and access testing                   |
| Retrieval respects source permissions              | Positive and negative retrieval tests         |
| High-impact actions require appropriate approval   | Workflow testing and approval records         |
| Material outputs meet the agreed quality threshold | Evaluation results against defined criteria   |
| Sensitive data is handled according to policy      | Configuration review and data-flow validation |
| Critical security findings are resolved            | Retest results and remediation evidence       |
| Monitoring and alerting are operational            | Test events and alert-response records        |
| Emergency suspension works                         | Controlled disablement exercise               |
| Fallback arrangements are viable                   | Continuity test or documented exercise        |
| Required governance approvals are complete         | Formal decision record                        |

Acceptance criteria should be defined before testing begins. This prevents the project team from changing the standard after seeing unfavorable results.

The organization should specify which failures block release, which may be addressed through authorized conditions, and who may approve the final decision.

Mandatory requirements must not be bypassed through a general project waiver.

### 103. Pilot Controls and Experimentation Boundaries

A pilot should be treated as a controlled stage of the AI lifecycle, with explicit limits on users, data, functionality, duration, and operational consequences.

The pilot authorization should identify:

- The objective and evaluation hypothesis.
- Approved model and system configuration.
- Permitted user population.
- Data categories and approved sources.
- Prohibited uses and actions.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Testing and monitoring procedures.
- Incident escalation and shutdown arrangements.
- Start and end dates.
- Success and failure criteria.
- Conditions for progression to production.

Where feasible, the organization should use synthetic or appropriately restricted data to reduce exposure during experimentation.

A pilot involving real personal data, sensitive information, or consequential decisions must still satisfy applicable requirements. Pilot status does not automatically create an exemption from security, privacy, or legal obligations.

The organization should prevent pilots from becoming permanent production deployments without a formal review.

At the end of the pilot, the decision record should document results, deviations, unresolved risks, and the recommendation to proceed, redesign, extend under authorization, or terminate.

### 104. Production Readiness and Go-Live Validation

Production readiness confirms that the AI use case has satisfied the required technical, operational, and governance conditions.

The review should verify that:

- The intended purpose and scope are formally approved.
- Required assessments are complete.
- Mandatory controls are implemented.
- Material findings have been resolved.
- Residual risk has been evaluated and authorized where acceptance is permitted.
- Data access and processing are appropriately controlled.
- Human oversight is operational where required.
- Monitoring and incident response are ready.
- Business continuity and fallback procedures are documented.
- Supplier and contractual requirements are satisfied.
- Users and operators have appropriate training.
- Change management and reassessment procedures are established.
- Required approvals are recorded.

The go-live decision should specify any remaining conditions, their owners, and the restrictions imposed on the initial deployment.

If mandatory controls are absent or required approvals are incomplete, production release should be withheld.

The organization should also verify that the actual deployment configuration matches the configuration tested and approved. Significant differences require evaluation before the affected functionality is enabled.

### 105. Post-Deployment Monitoring and Performance Assurance

Post-deployment monitoring determines whether the AI use case remains within its approved boundaries and continues to meet its intended objectives.

The monitoring plan should cover performance, security, privacy, human oversight, operational resilience, and business value.

Potential indicators include:

- Task accuracy and quality measures.
- Error and failure rates.
- Human override frequency.
- Unauthorized access attempts.
- Prompt-injection events.
- Unexpected tool execution.
- Sensitive-data exposure indicators.
- Changes in model behavior.
- Service availability and latency.
- User complaints and reported harmful outputs.
- Material incidents and near misses.
- Compliance with approved data-use restrictions.
- Achievement of expected business outcomes.

Each indicator should have an owner, monitoring frequency, threshold, and required response.

The organization should determine when a trend requires investigation, reassessment, restriction, or suspension.

Monitoring data should be collected proportionately and protected against unnecessary exposure of confidential or personal information.

The results should feed into the risk register, management reporting, change management, and the next scheduled reassessment.

### 106. Assessment Evidence Repository and Traceability

The organization should maintain a controlled repository for the evidence supporting the use-case assessment and its subsequent lifecycle decisions.

The repository should enable a qualified reviewer to reconstruct the reasoning behind the approval and determine whether the current system remains within its authorized scope.

Recommended evidence includes:

- Business case and use-case definition.
- System architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Risk scenarios and risk classifications.
- Security, privacy, and impact assessments.
- Vendor assurance and contractual records.
- Control implementation and validation evidence.
- Test plans and results.
- Pilot authorization and evaluation records.
- Residual risk acceptance and exception records.
- Production readiness and go-live decisions.
- Monitoring and incident records.
- Change and reassessment history.
- Suspension, retirement, and decommissioning evidence.

Each evidence item should identify its source, owner, date, applicable control, version, and scope.

The repository should apply appropriate access controls, retention rules, versioning, and audit logging.

Evidence reuse may improve efficiency, but the organization must verify that the evidence applies to the actual use case, deployment, and configuration.

### 107. AI Use-Case Assessment Scoring and Decision Model

A scoring model can support consistent classification and prioritization, provided that it is transparent and supplemented by mandatory decision rules.

The model should distinguish inherent risk, control effectiveness, residual risk, evidence confidence, and the final decision status.

A sample scoring scale is:

| Score | Meaning            |
| ----- | ------------------ |
| 1     | Very low exposure  |
| 2     | Low exposure       |
| 3     | Moderate exposure  |
| 4     | High exposure      |
| 5     | Very high exposure |

The organization should define scoring anchors for each risk dimension and provide examples that help reviewers apply the criteria consistently.

The scoring model should not allow low scores in several dimensions to conceal a critical failure in one dimension.

For example, a use case with limited financial impact may still be unacceptable if it processes data unlawfully or performs prohibited actions.

Evidence confidence should be recorded separately. A low-confidence assessment indicates that the conclusion is not sufficiently supported; it does not automatically prove that the underlying control is absent.

The final decision should consider the risk profile, mandatory requirements, control evidence, unresolved findings, business justification, and the authority of the decision-maker.

### 108. Assessment Quality Assurance and Independent Validation

Quality assurance helps ensure that use-case assessments are complete, consistent, and defensible.

The review should evaluate both the documentation and the substance of the assessment.

Quality criteria include:

- Clear intended purpose and boundaries.
- Accurate identification of systems, models, vendors, and data.
- Appropriate inherent risk classification.
- Coverage of material risk scenarios.
- Sufficient evidence for control conclusions.
- Consistency between findings and residual risk.
- Correct application of mandatory requirements.
- Appropriate approval authority.
- Complete remediation and monitoring plans.
- Traceability of the final decision.

For high-risk use cases, independent technical testing or specialist review may be necessary.

Quality assurance should also identify recurring weaknesses across the portfolio. Repeatedly incomplete data-flow diagrams, unsupported supplier claims, or inconsistent risk ratings may indicate that the methodology or training requires improvement.

Assessment quality should be measured through outcomes, including the proportion of reviewed assessments requiring material correction and the recurrence of previously identified deficiencies.

### 109. Assessment Workflow Automation and Governance Integration

Workflow automation can improve consistency by assigning reviews, enforcing required fields, tracking deadlines, and preserving approval records.

The workflow should connect the use-case assessment to procurement, architecture review, security assessment, privacy review, legal review, development, and production release.

Automation may support:

- Unique use-case registration.
- Risk-based routing.
- Evidence collection.
- Specialist review assignment.
- Remediation tracking.
- Approval and exception workflows.
- Reassessment scheduling.
- Production release gates.
- Portfolio reporting.
- Audit evidence retrieval.

Automated scoring should not independently approve high-risk use cases where material uncertainty, critical findings, or legal interpretation requires human judgment.

The workflow must distinguish a complete form from a valid assessment decision.

A use case should not progress automatically simply because all mandatory fields are populated. The evidence must be relevant, material findings must be addressed, and the required decision authority must approve the outcome.

### 111. AI Use-Case Key Risk Indicators and Performance Metrics

Metrics should have clear definitions, data sources, owners, reporting frequencies, and escalation thresholds.

A low number of incidents should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of effective control. The organization should also consider detection coverage, reporting quality, and independent testing results.

The assessment should establish how monitoring results influence the risk rating and when adverse trends require formal reassessment.

### 112. AI Use-Case Governance Maturity Model

A maturity model helps the organization determine whether its assessment capability is informal, standardized, consistently implemented, or continuously improved.

| Level | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                                   |
| ----- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Initial    | Use cases are adopted informally, with limited documentation and inconsistent review              |
| 2     | Developing | Basic intake and assessment forms exist, but control coverage and approval practices vary         |
| 3     | Defined    | Standardized methodology, ownership, risk classification, and formal approvals are established    |
| 4     | Managed    | Risk-based workflows, monitoring, quality assurance, and portfolio reporting operate consistently |
| 5     | Optimized  | Governance outcomes are measured, validated, and continuously improved using operational evidence |

Maturity should be assessed across separate dimensions, including governance, risk methodology, technical assessment, evidence quality, lifecycle monitoring, automation, and independent assurance.

An organization may have a mature approval workflow but limited ability to evaluate AI-specific technical threats. A single maturity score can conceal such weaknesses.

The maturity assessment should produce a prioritized improvement plan with accountable owners, milestones, resources, and measurable outcomes.

### 113. Implementation Roadmap for the AI Use-Case Assessment Program

The organization should implement the assessment program in stages, prioritizing material risks and critical business dependencies.

| Phase               | Activities                                                                | Expected deliverables                     |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Phase 1 — Establish | Define policy, scope, roles, risk tiers, and approval authority           | Approved methodology and governance model |
| Phase 2 — Register  | Identify AI use cases and link them to systems, models, vendors, and data | Use-case inventory and ownership records  |
| Phase 3 — Classify  | Apply initial screening and inherent risk classification                  | Risk-based assessment priorities          |
| Phase 4 — Assess    | Complete detailed risk, security, privacy, and impact reviews             | Findings, controls, and remediation plans |
| Phase 5 — Authorize | Apply approval gates and record eligible risk acceptance                  | Formal pilot and production decisions     |
| Phase 6 — Monitor   | Implement operational monitoring, change control, and reassessment        | Monitoring plan and reporting dashboard   |
| Phase 7 — Optimize  | Review outcomes, incidents, audit findings, and methodology quality       | Continuous improvement roadmap            |

Each phase should have defined completion criteria.

The organization should prioritize high-impact and high-autonomy use cases, particularly those involving sensitive information, consequential decisions, critical business processes, or significant external dependencies.

Implementation should also account for available staffing, technical capability, and workflow tooling. A methodology that cannot be applied consistently may create assessment backlogs and encourage uncontrolled workarounds.

### 114. Part 4 Core Principle — Technical Assurance Must Validate the Approved Use Case

An AI use-case assessment becomes operationally effective when the approved purpose is translated into an enforceable architecture, measurable control requirements, validated testing, production gates, and continuous monitoring.

The organization must verify that the actual implementation matches the assessed design and that identity, data access, output handling, tool permissions, and human oversight remain within the approved boundaries.

Technical evidence must support the governance decision, while monitoring and reassessment determine whether that decision remains valid over time.

**Final lesson principle:** A defensible AI use-case assessment connects business purpose, risk, controls, technical evidence, governance authority, and lifecycle assurance into one traceable decision process. A use case is not ready merely because it works; it is ready when its purpose is justified, its risks are controlled to an acceptable level, and its operation is authorized and continuously governed.
