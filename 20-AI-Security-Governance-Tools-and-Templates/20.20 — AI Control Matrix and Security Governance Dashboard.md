# 20.20 — AI Control Matrix and Security Governance Dashboard

## Part 1 — AI Control Matrix Foundations, Control Architecture, and Governance Dashboard Design

### 1. Purpose and Scope of the AI Control Matrix

An AI Control Matrix is a structured governance artifact that connects AI-related risks, governance requirements, security controls, implementation responsibilities, evidence, and assessment results. It provides a consolidated view of how an organization manages AI risks across its systems, business units, suppliers, and operational environments.

Organizations frequently implement AI governance through separate policies, risk registers, security assessments, supplier questionnaires, compliance worksheets, and monitoring reports. Although each artifact serves a specific purpose, disconnected records can create duplicated work, inconsistent control definitions, incomplete risk coverage, and difficulties demonstrating accountability.

The AI Control Matrix addresses this problem by providing a common reference for identifying, assigning, implementing, assessing, and monitoring AI controls.

It should function as a governance mechanism rather than merely a spreadsheet containing control descriptions.

#### 1.1 Primary Objectives

An enterprise AI Control Matrix should enable an organization to:

1. Establish a centralized inventory of AI governance and security controls.
2. Map controls to identified AI risks and business requirements.
3. Connect controls to relevant frameworks, standards, and legal obligations.
4. Assign accountable owners and operational responsibilities.
5. Define control implementation and effectiveness criteria.
6. Identify missing, duplicated, or insufficiently implemented controls.
7. Link control assessments to verifiable evidence.
8. Support AI system approval and deployment decisions.
9. Monitor control deficiencies and corrective actions.
10. Provide management with a consolidated view of AI governance performance.

The matrix should also support consistent reporting across different AI technologies, including traditional machine learning, generative AI, large language models, retrieval-augmented generation, AI agents, and AI-enabled enterprise applications.

However, the control requirements for these technologies will differ. A predictive model used for demand forecasting does not necessarily require the same controls as an AI agent that can access confidential information and execute transactions.

#### 1.2 What the AI Control Matrix Is—and Is Not

| Artifact | Primary purpose | Relationship to the AI Control Matrix |
|---|---|---|
| AI Governance Policy | Establishes governance principles, authority, and mandatory organizational expectations | Defines the policy basis for selected controls |
| AI Risk Register | Records AI risk scenarios, assessments, treatment decisions, and residual risks | Identifies risks that controls must address |
| AI System Inventory | Records AI systems, owners, purposes, dependencies, and lifecycle status | Identifies where controls must apply |
| AI Impact Assessment | Evaluates potential effects on individuals, groups, and other stakeholders | Identifies impacts requiring safeguards |
| AI Security Assessment | Evaluates technical and operational security risks | Provides control requirements and assessment findings |
| AI Vendor Assessment | Evaluates suppliers and external AI dependencies | Identifies supplier-related control requirements |
| Statement of Applicability | Records the applicability and selection of controls within the relevant management-system context | Provides an important source of control applicability information when ISO/IEC 42001 is used |
| AI Control Matrix | Consolidates controls, ownership, applicability, implementation, evidence, and effectiveness | Provides the central control reference |
| AI Governance Dashboard | Presents summarized governance status, risks, gaps, and trends | Converts control and risk information into management reporting |

The matrix should reference authoritative records rather than duplicate every detail from the risk register, system inventory, or assessment reports.

For example, the matrix may record that control `AIC-SEC-014` addresses risk `AIR-023` for system `AIS-CRM-004`. The detailed risk scenario remains in the risk register, while the matrix maintains the control relationship and implementation status.

This approach reduces duplication and helps preserve consistency across governance artifacts.

#### 1.4 Core Control Matrix Design Principles

**Principle 1: Risk-based control selection**

Controls should address identified risks, applicable requirements, and organizational objectives. A large control inventory does not automatically mean that AI governance is effective.

**Principle 2: Explicit applicability**

Each control should have a documented applicability decision. A control may apply to all AI systems, only particular system categories, or a specific deployment context.

**Principle 3: Clear accountability**

Every applicable control should have an accountable owner. Operational execution may be delegated, but accountability must remain explicit.

**Principle 4: Evidence-based assessment**

Implementation status should be supported by evidence. A policy statement alone does not demonstrate that a control operates effectively.

**Principle 5: Lifecycle coverage**

Controls should be considered across AI acquisition, development, testing, deployment, operation, change management, and retirement.

**Principle 6: Framework traceability**

Framework mappings should identify the specific requirements or outcomes supported. Mapping a control to a framework does not automatically establish conformity or legal compliance.

**Principle 7: Continuous improvement**

Control deficiencies, incidents, changes, and assessment findings should inform corrective actions and periodic control reviews.

#### 1.5 AI Control Matrix Governance Checklist

- [ ] The matrix has an approved purpose and scope.
- [ ] An accountable owner is assigned for the matrix.
- [ ] Each control has a unique identifier.
- [ ] Controls are linked to relevant AI systems and risks.
- [ ] Applicable frameworks and legal requirements are recorded.
- [ ] Applicability decisions have documented rationales.
- [ ] Control owners and operational responsibilities are assigned.
- [ ] Implementation and effectiveness are assessed separately.
- [ ] Evidence is linked to the relevant control.
- [ ] Control gaps are connected to corrective actions.
- [ ] Material control changes are version-controlled.
- [ ] Dashboard reporting uses controlled and traceable data.

### 2. AI Control Taxonomy and Control Domains

A control taxonomy organizes AI controls into logical domains so that the organization can identify coverage, assign responsibilities, and report results consistently.

Without a defined taxonomy, control inventories often become inconsistent. Different departments may describe the same control differently, use different identifiers, or maintain overlapping procedures without recognizing the duplication.

A standardized taxonomy provides a common structure for AI governance while allowing business units to implement controls according to their systems and risk profiles.

The taxonomy should be detailed enough to support assessment and reporting without creating unnecessary administrative complexity.

#### 2.1 Recommended Enterprise AI Control Domains

The following domains provide a practical starting point for an enterprise AI Control Matrix.

| Domain ID | Control domain | Purpose | Example control topics |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOV | AI Governance and Accountability | Establish organizational authority, accountability, and oversight | Governance charter, roles, policies, approval gates |
| RSK | AI Risk Management | Identify, assess, treat, and monitor AI risks | Risk assessments, risk acceptance, risk treatment |
| INV | AI Inventory and Lifecycle | Maintain visibility of AI systems and their lifecycle | AI inventory, ownership, lifecycle status |
| DAT | AI Data Governance | Manage data quality, provenance, access, and appropriate use | Data classification, lineage, retention, quality |
| MOD | Model Governance | Govern model selection, development, validation, and changes | Model documentation, validation, version control |
| SEC | AI Security | Protect AI systems against technical and operational threats | Access control, prompt injection testing, secure configuration |
| APP | AI Application and Integration Security | Protect interfaces, plugins, APIs, retrieval services, and connected systems | API authorization, input validation, integration security |
| PRI | Privacy and Personal Data Protection | Manage privacy risks and applicable data-protection requirements | Data minimization, privacy assessment, rights handling |
| ETH | Fairness, Human Rights, and Responsible AI | Address relevant societal impacts and harmful bias | Impact assessments, fairness evaluation, stakeholder review |
| HUM | Human Oversight and Transparency | Establish appropriate human supervision and communication | Review procedures, escalation, user information |
| TPR | Third-Party and Supply-Chain Risk | Govern external models, AI services, and suppliers | Supplier due diligence, contractual safeguards, change notifications |
| TST | AI Testing and Evaluation | Evaluate performance, security, safety, and relevant trustworthiness characteristics | Red teaming, regression testing, evaluation benchmarks |
| OPS | AI Operations and Monitoring | Maintain secure and controlled operation | Monitoring, logging, performance alerts, configuration management |
| INC | AI Incident Management | Detect, investigate, contain, and learn from AI-related incidents | Incident classification, response playbooks, post-incident review |
| CMP | Compliance and Assurance | Assess applicable requirements and maintain assurance evidence | Control mapping, internal audits, legal obligation tracking |
| BCP | Resilience and Business Continuity | Maintain appropriate recovery and continuity capabilities | Recovery planning, fallback processes, provider outage handling |
| CHG | Change and Release Management | Govern changes that may affect AI behavior or risk | Model updates, release approvals, change impact assessment |
| RET | AI Retirement and Data Disposal | Manage decommissioning and associated data, access, and dependency risks | Access revocation, data disposition, archival, supplier termination |

These domain identifiers are illustrative internal conventions. They are not official identifiers defined by ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, or the EU AI Act.

#### 2.2 Control Hierarchy

A mature control library should distinguish between control objectives, individual controls, implementation procedures, and evidence.

| Level | Component | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Control domain | AI Security |
| Level 2 | Control objective | Prevent unauthorized access to AI-connected enterprise data |
| Level 3 | Individual control | Enforce authorization at the underlying data source before retrieval |
| Level 4 | Implementation procedure | Validate user identity and permissions for every retrieval request |
| Level 5 | Evidence | Authorization test results, configuration records, and access logs |

This hierarchy helps prevent vague control statements.

For example, "secure AI" is too broad to assess effectively. A control such as "the retrieval service verifies the authenticated user's permissions before returning each protected CRM record" is more specific and testable.

#### 2.3 Control Classification by Function

In addition to domains, controls may be classified by their primary function.

| Control type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Preventive | Prevent an undesirable event | Reject unauthorized API requests |
| Detective | Identify an undesirable event or condition | Alert on anomalous AI tool usage |
| Corrective | Restore an acceptable condition | Revoke an exposed credential and remediate the cause |
| Deterrent | Discourage prohibited activity | Communicate and enforce approved AI-use restrictions |
| Compensating | Reduce risk where the preferred control is unavailable | Apply a temporary access restriction while a permanent fix is implemented |
| Governance | Establish authority and decision processes | Require approval for high-risk AI deployment |
| Assurance | Verify that controls are designed and operating as intended | Conduct independent control testing |

A control may perform more than one function. The organization should record its primary function and any secondary functions that are useful for analysis.

#### 2.4 Control Lifecycle Classification

AI controls should also be associated with the lifecycle stages in which they operate.

| Lifecycle stage | Typical control considerations |
|---|---|
| Idea and use-case intake | Purpose definition, initial risk screening, ownership |
| Acquisition | Supplier assessment, contractual terms, data-use restrictions |
| Design and development | Threat modeling, secure architecture, data governance |
| Testing and validation | Security testing, model evaluation, impact assessment |
| Approval and deployment | Release authorization, documented risks, readiness decisions |
| Production operation | Access management, monitoring, incident response |
| Change and retraining | Impact analysis, regression testing, reapproval where required |
| Retirement | Access revocation, data disposition, dependency removal |

The same control may operate at multiple lifecycle stages. For example, access authorization should be designed during development, tested before release, monitored during operation, and reviewed when permissions change.

#### 2.5 Taxonomy Design Rules

To maintain a consistent control library:

- Use unique and persistent control identifiers.
- Define each control in clear, testable language.
- Avoid combining unrelated requirements into a single control.
- Identify the risk or objective addressed by each control.
- Record the control's owner and operational executor.
- Define the expected evidence.
- Establish applicability criteria.
- Link controls to the relevant lifecycle stages.
- Identify related controls and dependencies.
- Maintain a controlled taxonomy version.

**Core Principle**

An effective AI control taxonomy provides a common language for governance, security, compliance, and assurance. It should organize controls around identifiable objectives and testable activities, enabling the organization to understand not only which controls exist but also where they apply, who owns them, and how their effectiveness is demonstrated.

### 3. AI Control Definition and Minimum Control Record

Each control in the matrix should have a standardized definition. Inconsistent descriptions make it difficult to compare control coverage across systems, assess implementation quality, or generate reliable dashboard metrics.

A control record should explain what must happen, why it matters, where it applies, who is responsible, how it operates, and what evidence demonstrates its implementation.

The control statement should describe an expected activity or condition rather than a vague aspiration.

For example:

- **Weak statement:** "AI data must be secure."
- **Stronger statement:** "Before an AI application retrieves protected enterprise records, the retrieval service must verify the authenticated user's authorization for the requested records and reject unauthorized requests."

The stronger statement identifies a specific control activity and an observable result.

#### 3.1 Minimum Control Record Fields

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Control ID | Unique identifier |
| Control Title | Concise name |
| Control Statement | Specific activity or condition required |
| Control Objective | Intended security or governance outcome |
| Control Domain | Primary taxonomy domain |
| Control Type | Preventive, detective, corrective, or other defined type |
| Risk Linkage | Related risk IDs |
| AI System Scope | Systems or system categories covered |
| Applicability Criteria | Conditions determining whether the control applies |
| Framework Mapping | Verified framework references |
| Legal Mapping | Applicable legal references, where established |
| Control Owner | Person accountable for the control |
| Control Operator | Team or individual performing the activity |
| Frequency | Continuous, event-driven, periodic, or another defined cadence |
| Implementation Method | How the control is implemented |
| Required Evidence | Records needed to demonstrate implementation |
| Design Criteria | Conditions for an adequate control design |
| Operating Criteria | Conditions for effective operation |
| Testing Method | How the control is assessed |
| Failure Condition | What constitutes a control deficiency |
| Escalation Requirement | Who must be notified and under what conditions |
| Corrective Action Link | Reference to the remediation process |
| Last Assessment Date | Date of the latest assessment |
| Next Review Date | Planned review date |
| Control Version | Version of the control definition |
| Approval Status | Status of control approval |

The exact fields may vary by organization, but the record should support traceability from control objective through implementation and assessment.

#### 3.2 Control Statement Design Pattern

A useful control statement can be constructed using the following pattern:

**Responsible actor + required activity + applicable scope or condition + expected result + evidence or verification requirement.**

For example:

> The AI Platform Security Team must enforce authenticated, least-privilege authorization for AI tool calls that access enterprise systems, reject requests outside the caller's approved permissions, and retain sufficient audit records to support security investigation.

This statement identifies the responsible function, required action, scope, expected outcome, and evidence objective.

It should be supplemented by detailed implementation procedures where necessary. The control statement itself should remain concise enough to be understood and assessed consistently.

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Control ID | AIC-SEC-014 |
| Control Title | Authorization of AI Data Retrieval |
| Control Statement | The retrieval service must validate the authenticated user's authorization before returning protected enterprise records |
| Control Objective | Prevent unauthorized disclosure through AI retrieval workflows |
| Control Domain | SEC — AI Security |

| Review Trigger | Changes to identity, permissions, retrieval architecture, or connected systems |

This is an illustrative internal control. Its identifier and wording are not official requirements from a particular standard.

#### 3.4 Control Quality Criteria

A control should be assessed against the following criteria before inclusion in the production control library.

| Quality criterion | Validation question |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Can the responsible team understand the required activity? |
| Testability | Can an assessor determine whether the control is implemented and effective? |
| Accountability | Is an owner identified? |
| Risk relevance | Does the control address a defined risk or governance objective? |
| Applicability | Are the systems and circumstances covered clearly stated? |
| Evidence | Can implementation and operation be demonstrated? |
| Proportionality | Is the control appropriate to the system's risk and context? |
| Feasibility | Can the control be implemented and maintained? |
| Independence | Where necessary, can effectiveness be assessed independently of the control operator? |
| Maintainability | Can the control be updated when systems, risks, or requirements change? |

#### 3.5 Control Record Validation Checklist

- [ ] The control has a unique identifier.
- [ ] The statement describes an observable activity or condition.
- [ ] The control objective is explicit.
- [ ] Applicable systems and conditions are defined.
- [ ] Related risks and requirements are identified.
- [ ] Control ownership is assigned.
- [ ] Implementation procedures are available where necessary.
- [ ] Evidence requirements are defined.
- [ ] Design and operating criteria are documented.
- [ ] Testing and failure conditions are clear.
- [ ] Escalation and remediation processes are identified.
- [ ] Changes to the control are version-controlled.

**Core Principle**

A control is useful only when its intended outcome can be understood, its application can be determined, and its implementation can be evaluated. Standardized control records make AI governance measurable and auditable while reducing ambiguity across technical, operational, risk, and compliance teams.

### 4. AI Control Applicability and Ownership Assessment

Not every control applies to every AI system. Control applicability depends on factors such as the system's intended purpose, data sensitivity, technical capabilities, external integrations, legal context, risk assessment, and deployment environment.

An organization should avoid two opposite errors:

1. Applying every control identically to every AI system regardless of risk or context.
2. Excluding controls without a documented rationale or appropriate approval.

The control applicability process should establish where a control is required, why it applies, who is responsible, and what evidence is expected.

Applicability must be reassessed when the system changes materially.

#### 4.1 Applicability Assessment Fields

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Applicability ID | Unique decision identifier |
| Control ID | Control being evaluated |
| AI System ID | System under consideration |
| System Category | Generative AI, predictive model, AI agent, or other relevant category |
| Intended Purpose | Purpose of the system |
| Risk Context | Relevant risk assessment findings |
| Data Sensitivity | Relevant data classification |
| System Capability | Relevant access, automation, or decision capabilities |
| External Dependencies | Relevant models, APIs, suppliers, and integrations |
| Legal Context | Applicable legal or contractual considerations |
| Applicability Decision | Applicable, not applicable, partially applicable, or pending assessment |
| Decision Rationale | Evidence-based explanation |
| Compensating Control | Alternative safeguard, where relevant |
| Decision Owner | Person authorized to make the decision |
| Reviewer | Independent or specialist reviewer where required |
| Approval Reference | Decision record |
| Review Trigger | Conditions requiring reassessment |

The applicability status labels are recommended internal values. They are not official classifications defined by ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF.

#### 4.3 Example Applicability Decisions

|---|---|---|---|

The examples illustrate contextual decisions. They do not prescribe universal applicability outcomes.

#### 4.4 Control Ownership Model

Control ownership should distinguish accountability from execution.

- **Control owner:** Accountable for the control's design, suitability, and overall operation.
- **Control operator:** Performs the operational activity.
- **Control assessor:** Evaluates implementation and effectiveness.
- **Risk owner:** Accountable for the associated risk and its treatment.
- **Framework or compliance owner:** Coordinates relevant requirements and evidence.
- **System owner:** Accountable for the AI system within the organization's governance model.

One person may hold more than one role in a smaller organization, but conflicts of interest and independence requirements should be considered.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Implement technical controls | C | A/R | C | I | I |
| Maintain governance evidence | R | R | A | C | I |
| Assess control effectiveness | C | C | C | C | A/R |
| Approve remediation closure | A or delegated authority | R | C | C | R |
| Review applicability after material change | A | R | R | C | I |

**RACI legend:** R = Responsible; A = Accountable; C = Consulted; I = Informed.

The matrix is illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's formal authority structure.

#### 4.6 Applicability and Ownership Checklist

- [ ] Control applicability is assessed for the relevant system or system category.
- [ ] The decision is supported by documented facts.
- [ ] Partial applicability is clearly explained.
- [ ] Exclusions are justified and approved where required.
- [ ] Unresolved decisions remain visible.
- [ ] A control owner is assigned.
- [ ] Operational execution responsibilities are defined.
- [ ] Assessment independence is considered.
- [ ] Related risks and legal requirements are traceable.
- [ ] Material changes trigger reassessment.

**Core Principle**

Control applicability must be justified by the AI system's purpose, risks, architecture, and applicable requirements. Ownership must be explicit, and exclusion decisions must remain reviewable. This prevents control matrices from becoming either indiscriminate checklists or incomplete records that conceal material governance gaps.

### 5. AI Control Implementation Status and Effectiveness Rating Model

A control matrix should distinguish between whether a control has been designed, whether it has been implemented, and whether it operates effectively.

These are separate questions. A control may be documented but not implemented, implemented but poorly designed, or appropriately designed but inconsistently operated.

A reliable status model allows management to understand the difference between documented intent and demonstrated control performance.

#### 5.1 Recommended Control Statuses

| Status | Definition | Evidence expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Not Assessed | The control has not been evaluated | Assessment not yet completed |
| Not Implemented | The control has not been put into operation | Evidence of the gap and its impact |
| Planned | Implementation has been approved but not completed | Approved implementation plan and owner |
| In Progress | Implementation activities are underway | Project or implementation records |
| Implemented — Not Tested | The control has been put in place, but effectiveness has not been assessed | Implementation evidence |
| Partially Effective | The control operates but has identified deficiencies | Test results and documented gaps |
| Effective | Evidence supports that the control meets defined criteria | Design assessment and operating evidence |
| Ineffective | The control fails to meet its defined objective or criteria | Test results, findings, and remediation record |
| Not Applicable | A documented applicability decision excludes the control | Approved rationale and supporting evidence |
| Temporarily Compensated | A temporary safeguard reduces exposure while a deficiency remains | Approved exception, compensating-control evidence, and expiry or review date |

These are recommended internal statuses, not official ratings prescribed by ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF.

The organization should define whether the status represents implementation state, effectiveness conclusion, or both. Combining them into a single field may conceal important distinctions.

#### 5.2 Separate Implementation and Effectiveness Fields

A stronger design maintains separate fields for implementation and effectiveness.

| Implementation status | Effectiveness status | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Not implemented | Not assessed | Control is absent and has not been tested |
| In progress | Not assessed | Implementation is incomplete |
| Implemented | Not assessed | Control exists, but its performance has not been evaluated |
| Implemented | Effective | Available evidence supports the defined effectiveness criteria |
| Implemented | Partially effective | Some criteria are met, but deficiencies remain |
| Implemented | Ineffective | The control does not achieve its defined objective |
| Not applicable | Not applicable | Approved applicability assessment excludes the control |

This structure helps prevent an implemented control from being automatically treated as effective.

#### 5.3 Control Assessment Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Unique assessment identifier |
| Control ID | Control being evaluated |
| AI System ID | System in scope |
| Assessment Scope | Control instances and environments reviewed |
| Design Criteria | Requirements for an adequate control design |
| Implementation Evidence | Records showing the control is in place |
| Operating Criteria | Requirements for effective operation |
| Testing Method | Interview, inspection, configuration review, sampling, or testing |
| Sample Selection | Basis for selecting evidence or instances |
| Test Period | Period covered by the assessment |
| Observed Results | Test observations |
| Exceptions Identified | Deviations from the expected result |
| Implementation Status | Status under the approved model |
| Effectiveness Status | Assessment conclusion |
| Limitations | Constraints affecting confidence in the conclusion |
| Finding ID | Linked deficiency, if applicable |

| Assessor | Person conducting the assessment |

| Next Assessment Date | Planned reassessment |

#### 5.4 Control Effectiveness Rating Guidance

The organization should define assessment criteria before evaluating the control.

For example, an authorization control for an AI retrieval service might be assessed against these criteria:

1. Requests are associated with an authenticated identity.
2. Authorization is checked against the relevant access policy.
3. The check is enforced at the appropriate server-side boundary.
4. Unauthorized requests are rejected.
5. Relevant access events are logged.
6. Changes to permissions are reflected within the required timeframe.
7. Tests cover both authorized and unauthorized access scenarios.
8. Identified failures are remediated and retested.

The organization may rate the control as effective only when the evidence supports the criteria within the assessment scope. If a required criterion is not tested, the conclusion should acknowledge that limitation.

#### 5.5 Evidence Confidence

Assessment conclusions should also reflect the quality of the evidence.

| Evidence condition | Assessment consideration |
|---|---|
| Current configuration and successful tests | May support a strong conclusion within the tested scope |
| Policy and procedure documents only | May demonstrate design intent but not operating effectiveness |
| Evidence from a limited sample | Conclusion should reflect sampling limitations |
| Outdated evidence | May not demonstrate current operation |
| Owner self-attestation without corroboration | May require additional verification according to risk |
| Missing or inconsistent records | May prevent a reliable conclusion |
| Independent testing with documented methodology | May increase confidence, subject to scope and competence |

No single evidence type guarantees effectiveness. The assessor should consider the control objective, system risk, assessment scope, and reliability of the records.

#### 5.6 Assessment and Escalation Checklist

- [ ] Implementation status and effectiveness status are distinct.
- [ ] Assessment criteria are documented before testing.
- [ ] Evidence supports the conclusion.
- [ ] Testing scope and limitations are recorded.
- [ ] Failed tests are linked to findings.
- [ ] Corrective actions have accountable owners.
- [ ] Material deficiencies are escalated.
- [ ] Temporary compensating controls have defined review dates.
- [ ] Closure requires appropriate validation.
- [ ] Reassessment occurs after material changes.

**Core Principle**

Control status must reflect demonstrated conditions, not assumptions. An implemented control is not necessarily effective, and missing evidence is not proof of success. Separating implementation state from effectiveness conclusions gives management a more reliable view of AI security and governance performance.

### 6. AI Control Matrix Data Model and Relationship Mapping

A control matrix becomes significantly more useful when its records can be connected through stable identifiers. These relationships enable the organization to trace a risk to its controls, identify the systems affected by a control deficiency, locate the supporting evidence, and report remediation progress.

Without a consistent data model, organizations may maintain multiple spreadsheets with duplicated descriptions, inconsistent status values, and broken references.

The data model should distinguish between the principal entities and the relationships connecting them.

#### 6.1 Core Data Entities

|---|---|---|
| AI System | Identifies an AI system or application | AIS-CRM-004 |
| AI Risk | Records an AI-related risk scenario | AIR-023 |
| Control | Defines a governance or security control | AIC-SEC-014 |
| Requirement | Identifies a framework, contractual, or legal requirement | REQ-ISO-001 |
| Control Assessment | Records an assessment of a control | CAS-2026-015 |
| Evidence Artifact | Identifies a supporting record | EVD-AI-145 |

### 7. Framework Mapping and Requirement Traceability

The AI Control Matrix can consolidate mappings to multiple frameworks, standards, internal policies, contractual requirements, and applicable laws.

However, framework mapping requires discipline. Two requirements may address similar topics while imposing different scopes, conditions, evidence expectations, or legal consequences.

The organization should therefore record the relationship between a control and a requirement rather than simply marking both as "covered."

For example, an access-control measure may support an ISO/IEC 42001 control objective, contribute to an outcome in NIST AI RMF, and help address a technical safeguard relevant to a legal obligation. These relationships must be evaluated separately.

#### 7.1 Requirement Traceability Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Requirement ID | Unique internal identifier |
| Source Framework or Law | Name and version of the source |
| Official Reference | Verified clause, control, category, subcategory, or legal provision |
| Requirement Summary | Controlled internal summary |
| Applicability | Whether the requirement applies to the assessed scope |
| Related Control ID | Control intended to address the requirement |
| Mapping Relationship | Direct support, partial support, complementary, dependent, or unresolved |
| Mapping Rationale | Explanation of the relationship |
| Additional Measures | Other controls or actions needed |
| Evidence Required | Records needed to assess implementation |
| Legal Review | Whether legal interpretation is required |
| Mapping Owner | Person responsible for maintaining the mapping |
| Last Review Date | Date of the most recent review |

The relationship labels are internal mapping conventions, not official framework classifications.

#### 7.2 Recommended Framework Mapping Categories

| Source | Role in the control matrix | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Supports the AI management system and relevant control selection and implementation | Management-system conformity must be assessed against the standard's applicable requirements |
| NIST AI RMF | Organizes AI risk management outcomes across Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage | The framework is not itself a statutory compliance determination |
| EU AI Act | Identifies legally binding requirements where the Act applies | Compliance depends on the relevant legal provisions, role, system, and facts |
| Internal AI policies | Establishes organizational requirements | Internal policy compliance does not automatically establish external legal compliance |
| Security standards and technical baselines | Provides additional security control expectations | The organization must assess whether the baseline addresses the specific AI risk |
| Supplier contracts | Defines contractual commitments and responsibilities | Contractual allocation does not automatically remove statutory obligations |

The following is illustrative and does not assert that the example control fully satisfies any named framework or legal obligation.

| Internal control | ISO/IEC 42001 relationship | NIST AI RMF relationship | EU AI Act relationship | Required validation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI risk assessment | Supports relevant AIMS risk management processes | Supports Map, Measure, and Manage outcomes | May support applicable risk-related duties | Verify scope, methodology, and applicable legal criteria |
| AI system inventory | Supports AI management planning and operational visibility | Supports contextual understanding and governance | May provide information needed to determine relevant obligations | Verify inventory completeness and legal role information |
| AI security testing | Supports the organization's selected AI controls | Supports Measure and Manage activities | May support applicable cybersecurity or risk-related obligations | Verify test scope against the actual requirement |

| AI incident management | Supports operational control and continual improvement | Supports governance, measurement, and risk management | May support applicable incident-related obligations | Verify whether specific reporting or response duties apply |

#### 7.4 Mapping Review Triggers

Mappings should be reviewed when:

- A standard or framework is updated.
- Applicable law or authoritative guidance changes.
- The organization's legal role changes.
- The AI system's intended purpose changes.
- A control is materially redesigned.
- A supplier changes its model or service.
- An audit identifies an inaccurate mapping.
- Evidence shows that the control does not address the intended requirement.
- A new legal or contractual obligation is identified.

#### 7.5 Traceability Checklist

- [ ] Requirement references are verified against authoritative sources.
- [ ] Applicable versions are recorded.
- [ ] Each control-to-requirement relationship has a rationale.
- [ ] Partial mappings are explicitly identified.
- [ ] Additional measures are documented.
- [ ] Legal obligations are assessed independently where necessary.
- [ ] Evidence is linked to the specific requirement and control.
- [ ] Mapping owners are assigned.
- [ ] Changes trigger review and version control.
- [ ] Unsupported equivalence claims are avoided.

**Core Principle**

Framework mapping improves consistency only when it preserves the meaning and conditions of each source requirement. A control may support multiple frameworks, but each mapping must be justified, evidence-based, and reviewed against the applicable criteria. Similarity of subject matter must never be mistaken for proof of conformity or legal compliance.

### 8. AI Control Evidence and Assurance Requirements

Evidence is the basis for evaluating whether a control has been designed appropriately, implemented, and operated effectively. The AI Control Matrix should therefore define the evidence expected for each control and link it to the assessment that uses it.

Evidence quality is particularly important for AI systems because some controls involve dynamic model behavior, external services, changing datasets, and probabilistic outputs. A static policy document may be necessary, but it will rarely be sufficient to demonstrate the operating effectiveness of a technical AI security control.

#### 8.1 Evidence Categories

| Evidence category | Examples | What it may demonstrate |
|---|---|---|
| Governance evidence | Approved policy, governance charter, meeting minutes | Formal decisions, accountability, and governance processes |
| Design evidence | Architecture diagrams, threat models, control specifications | Intended control design and system boundaries |
| Configuration evidence | Identity settings, API policies, deployment configuration | Implementation of specific technical safeguards |
| Operational evidence | Logs, monitoring records, access reviews | Operation over a defined period |
| Testing evidence | Penetration test results, adversarial evaluations, regression tests | Observed behavior under specified test conditions |
| Assessment evidence | Risk assessments, impact assessments, supplier reviews | Evaluation of risks and requirements |
| Approval evidence | Release approvals, exception decisions, risk acceptance records | Authorized decisions |
| Training evidence | Training records, role-based competency assessments | Completion of defined training activities |
| Remediation evidence | Change records, retest results, closure approvals | Correction and validation of identified deficiencies |
| Supplier evidence | Independent assurance reports, contractual commitments, technical documentation | Specific supplier claims or capabilities, subject to validation |

Evidence should be assessed against the claim being made. For example, a supplier's assurance report may support a particular control assertion but may not cover the customer's deployment configuration or the complete AI application.

#### 8.2 Evidence Sufficiency Worksheet

|---|---|
| Evidence Review ID | Unique review identifier |

The assessment labels are recommended internal conventions. The organization should define them in its assurance methodology.

#### 8.3 Example: Evidence for AI Authorization Controls

A control requiring authorization before retrieval of sensitive enterprise records may need several types of evidence.

| Evidence item | Purpose | Limitation to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture diagram | Shows where authorization is intended to be enforced | Does not prove that the enforcement operates correctly |
| Access-control configuration | Shows configured permissions | May not demonstrate that all relevant paths use the configuration |
| Positive authorization test | Demonstrates permitted access in selected scenarios | Does not establish that unauthorized access is blocked |
| Negative authorization test | Demonstrates rejection of selected unauthorized requests | Coverage may be limited to the tested scenarios |
| Operational logs | Provide evidence of relevant requests and outcomes | Logging may be incomplete or may not identify all bypass paths |
| Independent security test | Provides additional assurance over the assessed scope | Results remain limited to the test scope and period |
| Change history | Shows modifications to the control configuration | Does not by itself demonstrate current effectiveness |

The assessor should evaluate the combined evidence and its limitations rather than rely on a single artifact.

The existence of a document, configuration, or test report is not sufficient by itself; the organization must determine whether the evidence is current, reliable, complete, and relevant to the conclusion being made.

### 9. AI Control Testing Strategy and Assessment Coverage

A control testing strategy defines how the organization evaluates whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively. It should establish testing objectives, methods, scope, frequency, independence requirements, and escalation criteria.

AI control testing should combine documentary review with technical testing and operational validation where appropriate. The required methods depend on the control's objective and the risk associated with failure.

For example, a governance approval control may be tested by reviewing approval records and deployment workflows. An AI retrieval authorization control may require configuration inspection, negative authorization testing, and review of operational logs.

#### 9.1 Testing Method Categories

| Method | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Inquiry | Understand the process and responsibilities | Interview the control owner |
| Inspection | Examine documented records or configurations | Review approved AI deployment criteria |
| Observation | Observe a control being performed | Observe an access review |
| Sampling | Test selected instances from a larger population | Review a sample of AI deployment approvals |
| Configuration review | Inspect technical settings | Examine API authorization policies |
| Technical testing | Evaluate actual system behavior | Attempt unauthorized retrieval |
| Data analysis | Examine patterns across operational records | Analyze failed authorization events |
| Independent assessment | Obtain an assessment from a suitably independent reviewer | Independent review of high-impact control design |
| Reperformance | Repeat a control activity to validate its result | Reperform a risk-rating calculation using the documented method |

The method should be selected according to the control objective and risk. Interviews alone are rarely sufficient to demonstrate that a technical safeguard operates effectively.

#### 9.2 Control Testing Plan

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Test Plan ID | Unique identifier |
| Control ID | Control being tested |
| AI System ID | Relevant system |
| Control Objective | Intended result |
| Test Objective | What the assessment must establish |
| Testing Method | Selected assessment method |
| Scope | Systems, environments, configurations, and period |
| Population | Total set of relevant instances |
| Sampling Method | How samples are selected, where applicable |
| Sample Size | Number of records or scenarios tested |
| Test Procedure | Steps used to evaluate the control |
| Expected Result | Defined pass criteria |
| Failure Criteria | Conditions constituting failure |
| Test Environment | Environment used for technical tests |
| Required Permissions | Authorizations needed to conduct the test |
| Safety Constraints | Boundaries to avoid unintended impact |
| Assessor | Person conducting the test |
| Independence Requirement | Required degree of independence |
| Evidence Reference | Test records and results |
| Conclusion | Result under the approved methodology |
| Finding ID | Linked deficiency |
| Retest Requirement | Conditions requiring further testing |

#### 9.3 Example Test Plan: AI Tool Authorization

**Control objective:** Prevent an AI application or agent from executing actions beyond the permissions authorized for its user and operational context.

**Test scenarios:**

1. Attempt an authorized action using a valid identity and approved permissions.
2. Attempt an action without an authenticated identity.
3. Attempt an action using a valid identity but insufficient permissions.
4. Attempt to access a record outside the user's permitted scope.
5. Attempt to invoke a tool that is not approved for the use case.
6. Attempt to bypass authorization through adversarial instructions.
7. Verify that rejected actions produce the required security records.
8. Verify that relevant failures generate alerts or escalation events where required.

**Expected outcome:** Authorized actions succeed within the defined scope, while unauthorized actions are rejected by the appropriate enforcement layer. Logging and monitoring behavior must meet the control's documented criteria.

Testing must be conducted in an approved environment with appropriate safeguards. Production testing should require explicit authorization and controls to prevent unintended changes, disclosure, or service disruption.

#### 9.4 Test Coverage and Limitations

Control testing cannot prove that every possible AI failure has been eliminated. Testing provides evidence about defined scenarios and assessed conditions.

The organization should record:

- Which system versions were tested.
- Which risks and scenarios were covered.
- Which environments and integrations were included.
- Which scenarios were excluded and why.
- The limitations of the test methodology.
- Whether testing was performed independently.
- Whether material changes occurred after testing.
- Whether additional monitoring or testing is required.

AI security testing should be updated as new capabilities, integrations, attack techniques, or operating conditions introduce additional risks.

#### 9.5 Testing Frequency Model

| Control context | Potential testing approach |
|---|---|
| High-impact control with significant consequences if it fails | More frequent or event-driven testing, based on risk |
| Control affected by model or application releases | Test before release and after material changes |
| Supplier-dependent control | Assess at onboarding, during periodic reviews, and after material supplier changes |
| Governance approval control | Periodic sampling of decisions and workflow operation |
| Continuous technical enforcement | Automated monitoring supplemented by periodic validation |
| Low-risk administrative control | Periodic review proportionate to its significance |

These are planning examples, not prescribed frequencies from ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF. The organization should define and justify its own testing schedule.

#### 9.6 Control Testing Checklist

- [ ] Testing objectives are defined.
- [ ] Methods match the control objective.
- [ ] Test scope and criteria are documented.
- [ ] Samples are selected using a defined method.
- [ ] Technical testing is authorized and safely bounded.
- [ ] Results are recorded and reproducible where practicable.
- [ ] Test limitations are documented.
- [ ] Failed controls produce findings.
- [ ] Corrective actions are validated through appropriate retesting.
- [ ] Material changes trigger reassessment.
- [ ] Testing frequency is risk-based.
- [ ] Independence requirements are addressed.

**Core Principle**

Control testing should evaluate whether the control achieves its intended objective, not merely whether documentation exists. A defensible testing strategy combines appropriate methods, defined criteria, recorded limitations, and risk-based reassessment to provide credible evidence of control performance.

### 10. AI Control Exceptions and Compensating Controls

An AI control exception is a formally documented decision to permit a defined deviation from an internal control requirement for a specified scope and period, where such a deviation is permitted by applicable law and organizational policy.

Exceptions may be necessary when a technical limitation, business dependency, supplier constraint, or implementation delay prevents immediate compliance with an internal control standard. However, an exception must not be used to waive a legal obligation that cannot lawfully be waived.

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard designed to reduce the risk created by the deviation. Its effectiveness must be assessed rather than assumed.

The organization should not simply mark the authorization control as "not applicable." Instead, it should document the deficiency, evaluate the risk, identify temporary safeguards, and obtain approval from the appropriate authority.

- Restricting the AI application to a smaller, explicitly authorized user group.
- Disabling access to highly sensitive records.
- Restricting tool functionality to read-only operations.
- Applying additional access monitoring.
- Requiring human approval for specified actions.
- Setting a fixed expiry date and remediation deadline.

- Repeated requests for the same control deviation.

Exceptions should be controlled, time-bound, transparent, and supported by a documented risk decision. Repeated or long-running exceptions should trigger analysis of the underlying control design and implementation process.

### 11. AI Control Deficiency Management and Corrective Action Integration

Control deficiencies occur when a control is missing, inadequately designed, incorrectly implemented, inconsistently operated, or unsupported by sufficient evidence.

The organization should establish a consistent process for recording, prioritizing, remediating, and validating deficiencies. The process should integrate with the AI risk register, incident management, internal audit, and governance reporting mechanisms.

The objective is not merely to close individual findings but to reduce the likelihood of recurrence and improve the overall effectiveness of AI governance.

#### 11.1 Deficiency Classification

| Deficiency type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Design deficiency | The control design does not adequately address the risk | Authorization logic does not cover all retrieval paths |
| Implementation deficiency | The approved control has not been fully deployed | A production environment lacks the required enforcement configuration |
| Operating deficiency | The control is inconsistently or incorrectly performed | Access reviews are repeatedly missed |
| Evidence deficiency | Available records do not sufficiently support the control conclusion | No reliable test evidence exists for a critical safeguard |
| Ownership deficiency | Accountability or execution responsibility is unclear | No team is assigned to maintain AI model access permissions |
| Monitoring deficiency | Control failures are not detected or escalated as required | Unauthorized tool calls are not monitored |
| Lifecycle deficiency | Controls are not reassessed after relevant changes | A model update occurs without required regression testing |
| Systemic deficiency | A common underlying cause affects multiple systems or controls | AI procurement bypasses risk assessment across business units |

#### 11.2 Corrective Action Record

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | Unique deficiency identifier |
| Control ID | Affected control |
| AI System ID | Relevant system or systems |
| Deficiency Type | Classification of the finding |
| Finding Description | Clear statement of the observed deficiency |
| Expected Condition | Required control state |
| Evidence | Records supporting the finding |
| Root Cause | Underlying cause, where established |
| Impact Assessment | Potential consequence |
| Severity | Rating under the approved methodology |
| Immediate Containment | Actions needed to reduce immediate exposure |
| Corrective Action | Action addressing the deficiency |
| Preventive Improvement | Change intended to reduce recurrence |
| Accountable Owner | Person responsible for resolution |
| Action Owner | Person performing the remediation |
| Target Date | Planned completion date |
| Dependencies | Required approvals or technical changes |
| Validation Criteria | Conditions for successful closure |
| Retest Evidence | Evidence from validation |
| Residual Risk | Risk remaining after remediation |
| Closure Approval | Authorized closure decision |

#### 11.3 Correction versus Corrective Action

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Correction:** Resolves the immediate instance of a detected problem.

**Corrective action:** Addresses the cause of the problem to reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

For example, correcting an incomplete AI system record may resolve the immediate inventory issue. Corrective action may require modifying the procurement workflow so that AI-enabled products are consistently identified before purchase or deployment.

The organization should use root-cause analysis proportionate to the severity and recurrence of the issue.

#### 11.4 Root-Cause Analysis Questions

- What control was expected to operate?
- What was observed instead?
- Under which conditions did the failure occur?
- Was the control design adequate?
- Was the control implemented in every relevant environment?
- Were responsibilities and competencies sufficient?
- Were procedures clear and practical?
- Did a supplier or technical dependency contribute to the failure?
- Was the issue detected promptly?
- Have similar failures occurred elsewhere?
- What evidence demonstrates the root cause?
- Which changes would prevent recurrence?

Root-cause analysis should be evidence-based. The organization should avoid assigning a cause such as "human error" without examining whether the process, training, interface, or governance arrangement contributed to the outcome.

### 12. AI Control Matrix Review, Change Management, and Version Control

An AI Control Matrix must evolve as AI systems, organizational responsibilities, risks, frameworks, and legal requirements change.

Without formal change management, the matrix can become outdated even if it was initially well designed. Control definitions may no longer reflect the architecture, mappings may refer to obsolete requirements, and assessment results may remain associated with superseded configurations.

The organization should therefore establish a controlled process for reviewing and updating the matrix.

#### 12.1 Change Triggers

Review the control matrix when:

- A new AI system is introduced.
- An existing system changes its intended purpose.
- A model or model provider changes.
- New data sources or integrations are introduced.
- AI agents gain additional tools or permissions.
- A material security incident occurs.
- A control assessment identifies a significant deficiency.
- A supplier's assurance or service changes.
- Applicable standards or legal requirements change.

#### 12.3 Version Control Principles

The control library should maintain a clear distinction between:

- The current approved control definition.
- Previous versions of the control.
- The assessment results associated with each version.
- The date a change became effective.
- The reason for the change.
- The approval decision.
- The systems and risks affected.

Historical assessment records should not be silently overwritten when a control definition changes. The organization must preserve sufficient history to explain which control criteria were used for a past assessment.

#### 12.4 Review Frequency and Event-Driven Review

The organization should define a periodic review schedule appropriate to its AI portfolio and governance obligations. It should also establish event-driven reviews for material changes.

Periodic review alone is insufficient where a significant change occurs between scheduled review dates. Conversely, every minor technical change does not necessarily require a complete reassessment of every control.

A proportionate approach identifies the affected controls, evaluates whether previous assumptions remain valid, and records the decision about the required scope of reassessment.

- [ ] Control definitions are version-controlled.
- [ ] Historical assessments remain traceable.
- [ ] Approvals are recorded.
- [ ] Post-change validation is performed where required.
- [ ] Evidence repositories are updated.
- [ ] The dashboard reflects the current approved state.

**Core Principle**

The AI Control Matrix is a controlled governance artifact, not a static document. Version control, change impact assessment, and event-driven review preserve the reliability of control definitions and prevent outdated assessments from being treated as evidence of current effectiveness.

### 13. AI Control Coverage and Gap Analysis

Control coverage analysis evaluates whether the organization has identified and implemented controls for the relevant risks, systems, requirements, and lifecycle stages.

A high control count does not demonstrate adequate coverage. An organization may maintain hundreds of controls while leaving a material risk unaddressed, or it may have a smaller but well-designed set of controls that provides more meaningful risk coverage.

The analysis should therefore examine the relationships between controls, risks, systems, and requirements.

#### 13.1 Coverage Dimensions

| Coverage dimension | Assessment question |
|---|---|
| Risk coverage | Are material AI risks addressed by one or more appropriate controls? |
| System coverage | Are applicable controls assigned to every in-scope AI system? |
| Lifecycle coverage | Are relevant controls implemented across the system lifecycle? |
| Requirement coverage | Are applicable framework, legal, and contractual requirements mapped? |
| Ownership coverage | Does every applicable control have accountable ownership? |
| Evidence coverage | Is sufficient evidence available for material control claims? |
| Testing coverage | Are controls assessed using appropriate methods? |
| Supplier coverage | Are relevant external dependencies and supplier risks addressed? |
| Monitoring coverage | Are material risks monitored where necessary? |
| Remediation coverage | Are identified deficiencies tracked to validated closure? |

#### 13.2 Control Coverage Worksheet

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Coverage Assessment ID | Unique identifier |
| Assessment Scope | Portfolio, business unit, system, or control domain |
| AI System Population | Systems included |
| Risk Population | Risks included |
| Control Population | Controls included |
| Requirement Population | Applicable requirements included |
| Coverage Dimension | Dimension being evaluated |
| Numerator Definition | What counts as covered |
| Denominator Definition | Total eligible population |
| Coverage Result | Calculated result |
| Exclusions | Excluded items and rationale |
| Known Limitations | Data-quality or scope limitations |
| Gap References | Related findings |
| Responsible Owner | Person responsible for the analysis |
| Review Date | Date of assessment |

#### 13.3 Coverage Metric Example

Suppose an organization has identified 80 AI systems that require a documented risk assessment under its internal methodology. Of these, 68 have a current, approved assessment.

The risk assessment coverage metric would be:

\[
\text{Risk Assessment Coverage} =
\frac{\text{Systems with Current Approved Assessments}}
{\text{Systems Requiring Assessment}}
\times 100
\]

\[
= \frac{68}{80} \times 100 = 85\%
\]

The result indicates that 85% of the defined population has a current approved assessment. It does not establish that the assessments are complete, accurate, or effective.

The dashboard should therefore report coverage alongside quality measures, such as the results of assessment sampling or the number of overdue reassessments.

#### 13.4 Risk-to-Control Coverage Matrix

| Risk ID | AI system | Risk description | Required controls identified | Controls implemented | Evidence verified | Gap status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AIR-023 | AIS-CRM-004 | Unauthorized CRM data retrieval | Yes | Yes | Partially | Open |
| AIR-024 | AIS-CRM-004 | Prompt injection through retrieved content | Yes | Partially | No | Open |
| AIR-031 | AIS-HR-002 | Inappropriate reliance on AI-assisted screening | Yes | Yes | Yes | Under review |
| AIR-039 | AIS-AGENT-006 | Unauthorized agent tool execution | Yes | No | No | Open |

The entries are illustrative. Actual statuses should be based on the organization's control records and evidence.

#### 13.5 Coverage Interpretation

A coverage result should be accompanied by its scope and limitations.

For example:

- **High coverage, high effectiveness:** The defined population is broadly covered, and testing supports the effectiveness of material controls.
- **High coverage, low effectiveness:** Controls are mapped across the portfolio, but testing reveals deficiencies.
- **Low coverage, high assessed effectiveness:** Tested controls may be effective, but substantial portions of the relevant population remain unassessed.
- **Unknown coverage:** The organization cannot reliably determine the denominator or identify all in-scope systems.

The last case may indicate an inventory or governance weakness that should be addressed before management relies on the reported metric.

#### 13.6 Coverage Analysis Checklist

- [ ] The assessment population is clearly defined.
- [ ] The numerator and denominator are documented.
- [ ] Exclusions are justified.
- [ ] Risk-to-control relationships are reviewed.
- [ ] Material risks without controls are identified.
- [ ] Controls without supporting evidence are visible.
- [ ] Coverage is distinguished from effectiveness.
- [ ] Data-quality limitations are disclosed.
- [ ] Gaps are assigned for remediation.
- [ ] Results are reported with appropriate context.

**Core Principle**

Control coverage measures the extent to which the defined population is addressed; it does not prove that the controls are effective. Reliable coverage analysis requires a defensible denominator, explicit scope, traceable risk-to-control relationships, and complementary evidence about implementation and performance.

### 6. AI Control Matrix Design and Structure

An AI control matrix is a structured governance instrument that translates AI-related risks, organizational policies, legal obligations, standards, and security requirements into defined controls that can be implemented, assigned, tested, and monitored.

It provides a common reference point for AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, internal audit, engineering, procurement, and business teams.

Without a control matrix, organizations often manage requirements in separate spreadsheets, policies, risk registers, and audit reports. This fragmentation creates duplicated work, inconsistent control definitions, unclear accountability, and gaps in evidence.

A well-designed matrix connects these elements through traceable relationships.

**Core design principle:** Every control should have a defined purpose, an accountable owner, an implementation method, measurable assessment criteria, and evidence demonstrating its operation.

#### 6.1 Recommended Control Matrix Fields

| Field | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Control ID | Unique identifier for the control | AI-GOV-001 |
| Control Domain | Governance or technical category | AI governance |
| Control Name | Short control title | AI System Inventory |
| Control Objective | Intended outcome | Maintain visibility of organizational AI systems |
| Risk Addressed | Risk reduced by the control | Unauthorized or unmanaged AI deployment |
| Control Statement | Expected control requirement | All in-scope AI systems must be recorded in an approved inventory |
| Control Type | Preventive, detective, corrective, or directive | Detective |
| Implementation Method | How the control operates | Central AI inventory with periodic reconciliation |
| Control Owner | Accountable role | Head of AI Governance |
| Control Operator | Role performing operational activities | AI Governance Analyst |
| Applicability | Systems, processes, or business units in scope | All approved enterprise AI systems |
| Implementation Status | Current implementation state | Partially implemented |
| Design Effectiveness | Whether the control is suitably designed | Effective |
| Operating Effectiveness | Whether the control operates consistently | Needs testing |
| Evidence Reference | Location or identifier of supporting evidence | EVD-AI-INV-001 |
| Assessment Frequency | How often the control is assessed | Quarterly |

These fields form a practical baseline rather than a universal mandatory schema. Organizations should tailor the matrix to their risk profile, regulatory obligations, governance model, and reporting requirements.

#### 6.2 Distinguishing Control Design from Control Operation

A control may be documented correctly but fail to operate effectively. Conversely, a process may be operating informally without sufficient documentation or accountability.

Control assessment should therefore distinguish design effectiveness from operating effectiveness.

| Assessment dimension | Key question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Design effectiveness | Would the control, if implemented as designed, address the identified risk? | Does the AI inventory process require registration before deployment? |
| Implementation status | Has the control actually been established? | Is the inventory process operating in production? |
| Operating effectiveness | Does the control operate consistently and produce the intended result? | Do sampled AI deployments appear in the inventory? |
| Evidence sufficiency | Is there reliable evidence supporting the assessment? | Are inventory records, approvals, and reconciliation results available? |
| Outcome effectiveness | Is the targeted risk being reduced to an acceptable level? | Have unauthorized AI deployments declined? |

These dimensions should not be collapsed into a single status field. An implemented control is not automatically effective, and the absence of detected incidents does not by itself prove that a control is effective.

#### 6.4 Control Identity and Version Management

Control definitions change as AI systems evolve, new risks emerge, organizational responsibilities change, or legal and contractual requirements are updated.

Each control should therefore have a stable identifier and a controlled change history.

Recommended metadata includes:

- Control ID and control version.
- Effective date and review date.
- Control owner and approving authority.
- Previous and revised control statements.
- Reason for the change.
- Affected systems, risks, and framework mappings.
- Required changes to procedures, testing, or evidence.
- Approval record and implementation deadline.

A change to a control statement should not silently overwrite previous assessment results. Historical records should preserve the control definition that was in force when the assessment took place.

For example, if an organization introduces a mandatory human review for high-impact AI-generated recommendations, the control matrix should record when the requirement became effective, which systems are subject to it, and how compliance with the revised control will be tested.

#### 7.2 Mapping Controls to Risk Categories

Control domains classify controls by subject. Risk categories describe the potential adverse outcomes those controls address. The two concepts should remain distinct.

For example, a single access-control requirement may mitigate unauthorized model modification, exposure of confidential data, and misuse of an AI agent's tools.

| Risk category | Illustrative risk | Potential control response |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Sensitive information is disclosed through AI outputs | Data-loss prevention, access restrictions, output monitoring |
| Integrity | An attacker manipulates model inputs or supporting data | Input validation, integrity checks, controlled data pipelines |
| Availability | An AI service becomes unavailable | Resilience controls, capacity management, recovery procedures |
| Safety | An AI system produces outputs that may cause physical or operational harm | Safety testing, operational constraints, human intervention |
| Privacy | Personal data is processed or disclosed inappropriately | Privacy assessment, data minimization, access restrictions |
| Fairness | Outputs systematically disadvantage a group | Appropriate testing, impact assessment, remediation |
| Transparency | Users cannot determine when or how AI is being used where disclosure is appropriate or required | Notices, documentation, user-facing explanations |
| Accountability | Decisions cannot be traced to responsible parties | Ownership assignment, decision records, audit trails |
| Legal compliance | Applicable legal obligations are not met | Legal applicability review, obligation mapping, compliance testing |
| Third-party dependency | A provider changes its model or service in a way that increases risk | Contractual controls, change notifications, reassessment |

The control response must be selected according to the actual risk scenario. A generic mapping between a risk category and a control domain is not sufficient evidence that the risk has been mitigated.

#### 7.3 Control Taxonomy Governance

A control taxonomy becomes unreliable when teams create overlapping categories or assign controls inconsistently.

A designated control framework owner should maintain:

1. Approved domain definitions.
2. Naming and identification conventions.
3. Rules for creating new domains or subdomains.
4. Criteria for primary and secondary classification.
5. Mapping conventions for risks and framework requirements.
6. Rules for retiring obsolete controls.
7. Periodic reviews of duplicated or conflicting control definitions.

Where a control belongs to multiple domains, the organization should establish one authoritative control record and use relationships or cross-references to represent its multiple purposes.

This avoids creating duplicate controls that have different owners, inconsistent wording, and conflicting assessment results.

### 8. Framework-to-Control Mapping Methodology

Framework mapping connects an organization's controls with relevant requirements, outcomes, recommendations, or legal obligations.

The objective is to establish traceability without misrepresenting the authority or meaning of the source requirement.

A framework mapping exercise should distinguish between:

- **Requirement:** A binding or formally stated obligation that applies in the relevant context.
- **Control:** An organizational measure implemented to address a risk or satisfy a requirement.
- **Framework outcome:** A desired result described by a framework.
- **Implementation activity:** Work performed to establish or operate a control.
- **Evidence:** Information demonstrating the control's design, implementation, or operation.
- **Assessment result:** A conclusion reached by evaluating evidence against defined criteria.

These objects are related, but they are not interchangeable.

#### 8.1 Recommended Mapping Workflow

```text
STEP 1: IDENTIFY THE SOURCE
        |
        v
STEP 2: CONFIRM APPLICABILITY AND SCOPE
        |
        v
STEP 3: INTERPRET THE REQUIREMENT OR OUTCOME
        |
        v
STEP 4: IDENTIFY THE RELEVANT RISK
        |
        v
STEP 5: SELECT OR DESIGN THE CONTROL
        |
        v
STEP 6: ASSIGN AN OWNER AND IMPLEMENTATION METHOD
        |
        v
STEP 7: DEFINE TESTING CRITERIA AND EVIDENCE
        |
        v
STEP 8: ASSESS DESIGN AND OPERATION
        |
        v
STEP 9: RECORD GAPS AND REMEDIATION
        |
        v
STEP 10: REVIEW THE MAPPING WHEN CONDITIONS CHANGE
```

The workflow should be performed by people with the appropriate subject-matter expertise. Legal applicability decisions may require legal counsel; technical control design may require security engineering; and AI impact analysis may require privacy, safety, ethics, or business specialists.

#### 8.2 Framework Mapping Register

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mapping ID | Unique mapping record |
| Source framework | Name of the standard, framework, law, or contractual source |
| Source reference | Exact clause, provision, outcome, or other reference |
| Source version | Edition or version used for the assessment |
| Applicability rationale | Why the source applies to the organization or system |
| Source intent | Summary of the requirement or intended outcome |
| Related risk IDs | Risks addressed by the mapping |
| Related control IDs | Organizational controls intended to address the source |
| Coverage assessment | Degree to which the control addresses the source intent |
| Evidence references | Supporting documentation or test results |
| Gap description | Missing or insufficient coverage |
| Remediation action | Work needed to close the gap |
| Mapping owner | Person responsible for maintaining the mapping |
| Validation date | Date of last review |
| Validation status | Confirmed, pending validation, partially covered, or not covered |

Exact references should be verified against the applicable source edition. Where a source is a law or regulation, the mapping should identify the relevant legal provision and the basis for determining applicability.

#### 8.3 Coverage Assessment Categories

An organization may use the following internal categories to describe control coverage.

| Category | Definition | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Fully mapped | The source has been mapped to relevant controls and the mapping has been validated | Does not automatically prove that the controls operate effectively |
| Partially mapped | Some aspects of the source are addressed, but material elements remain unresolved | Additional controls, evidence, or interpretation may be needed |
| Not mapped | No relevant control mapping has been established | A mapping gap exists |
| Not applicable | The source does not apply within the documented scope or circumstances | The rationale and approving authority should be recorded |
| Pending validation | A proposed mapping exists but has not been sufficiently reviewed | It should not be reported as confirmed coverage |

**Important distinction:** Mapping completeness is not the same as compliance. A requirement may be mapped to a control that has not been implemented, has failed testing, or does not satisfy the full requirement.

For legal obligations, an internal coverage category should never be presented as a definitive legal-compliance conclusion unless the organization's assessment process and evidence support that conclusion.

#### 8.4 Example of Cross-Framework Mapping

Consider an enterprise AI assistant that retrieves internal documents and generates responses for employees.

The organization may establish a control requiring access restrictions, authorization checks, and logging for document retrieval.

| Mapping element | Illustrative record |
|---|---|
| AI system | Internal knowledge assistant |
| Risk | The assistant retrieves documents that a user is not authorized to access |
| Control ID | AI-SEC-014 |
| Control objective | Ensure document retrieval respects approved user permissions |
| Control statement | Retrieval requests must enforce authorization using the organization's approved access-control model |
| Implementation | Identity-aware retrieval, document-level permission enforcement, security logging |
| Testing | Attempt retrieval using accounts with different access privileges |
| Evidence | Test results, configuration records, access-control logs |
| Owner | AI Platform Security Lead |
| Framework mapping | Relevant ISO/IEC 42001 requirements or controls, applicable NIST AI RMF outcomes, and any relevant legal or contractual obligations |
| Assessment | Pending validation until implementation and testing evidence are reviewed |

The control can support multiple governance objectives. However, each mapping must be evaluated independently against the source's actual intent.

For example, effective authorization controls may reduce the risk of unauthorized disclosure, but they do not automatically demonstrate compliance with every applicable privacy, transparency, or AI regulatory obligation.

#### 8.5 Mapping Quality Assurance

A mapping review should confirm that:

- The source reference is accurate and uses the correct version.
- Applicability has been evaluated rather than assumed.
- The control addresses the substance of the source requirement.
- Control wording is specific enough to be tested.
- Evidence supports the stated coverage.
- Known limitations and exceptions are documented.
- The same control has not been duplicated unnecessarily.
- Legal obligations are not reduced to voluntary framework recommendations.
- Mapping results distinguish implementation from operating effectiveness.
- Changes in source requirements, AI systems, or organizational scope trigger reassessment.

A mapping should be rejected or returned for revision if it relies only on keyword similarity, generic control descriptions, or an unsupported assertion that one framework is equivalent to another.

### 9. Control Ownership, Accountability, and Segregation of Duties

An AI control matrix should identify who is accountable for the control, who performs it, who provides supporting expertise, and who evaluates its effectiveness.

Unclear ownership is a significant governance weakness. A control may exist in documentation while no individual or team is responsible for ensuring it operates.

Ownership must also reflect the distinction between system accountability and control-framework accountability.

For example, an AI Platform Owner may be accountable for implementing technical safeguards, while the AI Governance function maintains the control methodology and monitors overall governance performance.

#### 9.1 Recommended Control Roles

| Role | Typical responsibility |
|---|---|
| Executive Sponsor | Provides senior-level direction and resolves material governance conflicts |
| AI Governance Committee | Reviews significant AI risks, exceptions, and governance performance |
| AI Governance Lead | Maintains the governance framework and oversees control coordination |
| Control Owner | Accountable for the design and effectiveness of a specific control |
| Control Operator | Performs the operational activity required by the control |
| AI System Owner | Accountable for the AI system's business purpose, lifecycle, and operational risks |
| AI Product Owner | Coordinates product requirements and delivery decisions |
| AI Engineering Lead | Implements technical controls and engineering safeguards |
| Security Architect | Reviews security design and control integration |
| Privacy Officer or DPO, where applicable | Advises on privacy obligations and relevant assessments |
| Legal and Compliance | Interprets applicable obligations and advises on compliance requirements |
| Procurement and Vendor Risk | Assesses provider risks and contractual safeguards |
| Internal Audit | Provides independent assurance within its mandate |
| Business Risk Owner | Accepts or escalates risk decisions within delegated authority |

These roles may be combined in smaller organizations, but incompatible responsibilities should be separated where necessary to preserve accountability and credible assurance.

#### 9.2 RACI Model for AI Control Activities

RACI means Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed.

- **Responsible (R):** Performs the work.
- **Accountable (A):** Owns the result and approves the decision.
- **Consulted (C):** Provides input or specialist advice.
- **Informed (I):** Receives relevant updates.

The following matrix is illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's actual structure.

| Activity | AI Governance | AI System Owner | Security | Privacy/Legal | Internal Audit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Define enterprise AI control methodology | A/R | C | C | C | I |
| Identify system-specific AI risks | C | A/R | R | C | I |
| Design technical security controls | C | A | R | C | I |
| Assess privacy and legal implications | C | R | C | A/R | I |
| Implement system controls | C | A | R | C | I |
| Collect operational evidence | C | A | R | C | I |
| Assess control effectiveness | R | C | R, where independent assessment is appropriate | C | I or independent assurance |
| Approve risk acceptance | C | R, within delegated authority | C | C | I |
| Conduct independent audit | C | C | C | C | A/R |
| Track corrective actions | R | A/R for system-specific actions | R for security actions | R for assigned actions | I or follow-up assurance |

The matrix is not a universal allocation of authority. In particular, privacy and legal accountability depends on the organization's governance structure and applicable law. Internal Audit should retain appropriate independence and should not assume management's responsibility for operating the controls it later audits.

#### 9.3 Control Ownership Register

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Control ID | Unique control identifier |
| Control Owner | Accountable role or individual |
| Control Operator | Person or team performing the activity |
| Business Owner | Owner of the affected business process |
| Technical Owner | Owner of the relevant technology or platform |
| Risk Owner | Person authorized to manage the associated risk |
| Evidence Owner | Role responsible for maintaining evidence |
| Reviewer | Role responsible for reviewing assessment results |
| Approver | Role authorized to approve exceptions or decisions |
| Delegation Rules | Conditions under which authority may be delegated |
| Escalation Path | Route for unresolved gaps or overdue actions |
| Backup Owner | Alternate role for continuity |
| Last Ownership Review | Date accountability was last validated |

A named individual should not be the only identifier in the control repository. Roles should be recorded so that accountability survives staff turnover, and current individual assignments can be maintained in an associated ownership directory.

#### 9.4 Escalation and Segregation of Duties

Control failures should be escalated according to their potential impact, urgency, and the organization's delegated authority model.

An example escalation sequence is:

```text

                 |

                 |

                 |
                 v

                 |
                 v

                 |
        +--------+--------+
        |                 |
        v                 v
    APPROVED          REJECTED
        |                 |
        v                 v
```

Risk acceptance should be limited to authorized decision-makers and should include a documented rationale, scope, residual-risk assessment, duration, compensating measures, and review or expiry date where appropriate.

For material AI risks, the control operator should not be the sole authority approving their own exception. Likewise, the team responsible for implementing a control should not automatically be treated as independent assurance of that control's effectiveness.

### 10. Control Implementation Status and Maturity Assessment

A control matrix should distinguish whether a control has been designed, implemented, tested, and shown to operate effectively.

Organizations often use a single status field such as *Complete*, *In Progress*, or *Not Started*. These values are useful for project tracking but insufficient for a mature governance assessment.

A control can be fully deployed while its design is inadequate. A well-designed control can remain unimplemented. An implemented control can also fail an effectiveness test.

#### 10.1 Recommended Implementation Lifecycle

| Status | Definition | Minimum evidence expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Not Assessed | The control has not yet been evaluated | No assessment conclusion |
| Not Started | No meaningful implementation activity has begun | Assigned owner and planned approach, where available |
| Planned | An approved implementation plan exists | Plan, owner, milestones, target date |
| In Design | Requirements and implementation approach are being defined | Design document, requirements, review records |
| In Implementation | Deployment or process rollout is underway | Configuration records, procedures, implementation evidence |
| Implemented | The control has been established | Evidence of deployment or operational procedure |
| Testing | Design or operating effectiveness testing is underway | Test plan, samples, preliminary results |
| Effective | Assessment criteria have been met and sufficient evidence supports the conclusion | Approved assessment and supporting evidence |
| Partially Effective | Some requirements are met, but material weaknesses remain | Documented test results and identified gaps |
| Ineffective | The control fails material assessment criteria | Findings, risk assessment, remediation plan |
| Not Applicable | The control is outside the documented scope or genuinely inapplicable | Rationale and approval according to policy |
| Retired | The control has been formally withdrawn or superseded | Approval, effective date, impact assessment, replacement mapping where relevant |

An organization may simplify this lifecycle, but it should not use *Implemented* and *Effective* as synonyms.

#### 10.2 Internal Control Maturity Scale

A maturity scale can help management understand the consistency and institutionalization of AI governance practices.

The following is an **illustrative internal maturity model**, not an official ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF scoring system.

| Level | Name | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent | No established process or reliable evidence |
| 1 | Ad hoc | Activities are informal, reactive, and dependent on individuals |
| 2 | Developing | Basic procedures and ownership exist but application is inconsistent |
| 3 | Defined | Documented, approved, and consistently assigned processes exist |
| 4 | Managed | Performance is measured, monitored, and reviewed against defined criteria |
| 5 | Optimized | Evidence-driven improvements are institutionalized and processes adapt to changing risks |

Maturity describes the development of a capability or process. It does not necessarily establish legal compliance, technical effectiveness, or acceptable residual risk.

A highly mature process can still be inadequate if its control objectives are incorrect or if it fails to address a material risk.

#### 10.3 Maturity Assessment Worksheet

| Assessment field | Example |
|---|---|
| Control ID | AI-GOV-001 |
| Control Name | AI System Inventory |
| Current Maturity | Level 2 — Developing |
| Target Maturity | Level 4 — Managed |
| Assessment Rationale | Inventory exists but is not reconciled consistently across business units |
| Evidence Reviewed | Inventory export, owner assignments, reconciliation records |
| Identified Gap | Unregistered AI tools may not be detected promptly |
| Risk Implication | Incomplete oversight of AI use and associated risks |
| Required Improvement | Introduce periodic reconciliation and accountable system-owner attestations |
| Remediation Owner | AI Governance Lead |
| Target Date | 31 January |
| Success Measure | All in-scope business units complete reconciliation and unresolved discrepancies are tracked |
| Validation Method | Sample testing, reconciliation review, and confirmation of unresolved items |
| Approver | Authorized governance representative |

The target maturity level should be justified by risk and business need. Not every control requires Level 5 maturity; some may need a simpler, proportionate approach.

#### 10.4 Defining Objective Success Criteria

Maturity and implementation assessments should use measurable criteria rather than subjective statements such as “the control is mostly good.”

For an AI inventory control, measurable criteria might include:

- Percentage of in-scope systems with complete inventory records.
- Percentage of records with a current accountable owner.
- Percentage of systems reviewed within the required period.
- Number of unresolved reconciliation discrepancies.
- Time taken to register newly approved AI systems.
- Percentage of overdue inventory remediation actions.

Thresholds should be set according to organizational risk tolerance, applicable obligations, and the control's intended purpose. A high inventory completion rate, for example, does not prove that every AI system has been identified if the organization lacks a reliable method for discovering unregistered systems.

#### 10.5 Maturity Reporting and Governance Decisions

Maturity results should be interpreted alongside risk severity, control effectiveness, evidence quality, and remediation progress.

A useful management view includes:

1. Current maturity and target maturity.
2. Critical control weaknesses.
3. High-risk systems affected by those weaknesses.
4. Overdue remediation actions.
5. Resource requirements.
6. Residual-risk implications.
7. Decisions requiring management approval.
8. Evidence supporting reported improvements.

A maturity score should never conceal a critical failure. For example, a domain with a high average maturity score may still contain a single ineffective control protecting a highly sensitive AI system. That failure should remain visible in the governance dashboard and be escalated according to risk.

### Core Principle

An effective AI control matrix is more than a catalogue of safeguards. It is a governed, traceable model connecting AI systems, risks, control objectives, implementation responsibilities, framework references, evidence, assessments, and corrective actions.

Control design, implementation status, operating effectiveness, maturity, and legal compliance must remain distinct assessment dimensions. Reliable governance depends on explicit ownership, accurate source mapping, objective testing criteria, controlled change history, and evidence that supports the conclusions reported to management.
