# 20.13 — AI Security Architecture Review Template

## Part 1 — Architecture Review Foundations, Scope, System Context, and Security Requirements

### 1. Introduction to the AI Security Architecture Review Template

An **AI Security Architecture Review** is a structured assessment of an AI system's architecture, trust boundaries, data flows, security controls, dependencies, and operational design to determine whether the system can be deployed and operated within the organization's security and risk requirements.

The review examines how the AI system is constructed, how it interacts with users and enterprise systems, how data moves through the environment, and how security controls protect the system against misuse, unauthorized access, data exposure, manipulation, and operational compromise.

Unlike a conventional application security review, an AI security architecture review must account for risks introduced by model behavior, training or retrieval data, prompts, model-serving infrastructure, external model providers, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), AI tools, and autonomous or semi-autonomous execution.

The review should be tailored to the architecture being assessed. A traditional machine-learning prediction service, a generative AI assistant, a RAG-based enterprise knowledge assistant, and an agentic AI system do not have identical attack surfaces or control requirements.

#### 1.1 Objectives of an AI security architecture review

The review should establish whether:

- The AI system's intended purpose, capabilities, and security boundaries are clearly defined.
- The architecture provides appropriate authentication, authorization, and access control.
- Sensitive data is protected throughout collection, processing, storage, retrieval, transmission, and disposal.
- Model inputs, outputs, and interactions with external systems are subject to appropriate security controls.
- The system's trust boundaries and attack surfaces are identified.
- The architecture addresses relevant AI-specific threats, including prompt injection, insecure output handling, data leakage, model abuse, and unsafe tool execution.
- Third-party models, AI services, datasets, libraries, and infrastructure dependencies are assessed.
- Logging, monitoring, incident response, and operational recovery capabilities are adequate.
- Residual risks are documented and assigned to authorized risk owners.
- The system meets applicable internal security requirements and relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, and industry obligations.

The review is not intended to prove that an AI system is completely secure. Its purpose is to identify architectural weaknesses, evaluate the adequacy of proposed controls, and determine whether the remaining risks are acceptable under the organization's governance framework.

#### 1.2 Architecture review versus other AI assessments

Several assessment activities contribute to AI security governance, but they serve different purposes.

| Assessment Type | Primary Focus | Typical Questions | Main Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI Use-Case Assessment | Business purpose and appropriateness | Is the proposed AI use case justified and permitted? | Use-case assessment |
| AI Risk Assessment | Overall risk exposure | What risks could arise, and how should they be treated? | AI risk assessment and risk register |
| AI Security Architecture Review | Technical design and security controls | Does the architecture adequately protect the AI system and its dependencies? | Architecture review report |
| AI Model Risk Assessment | Model behavior and model-specific risks | Could model limitations, drift, bias, or unreliable outputs create unacceptable exposure? | Model risk assessment |
| LLM Security Assessment | Large language model-specific threats | Can prompts, retrieved content, or outputs compromise the application? | LLM security assessment |
| AI Vendor Assessment | Third-party provider risks | Can the supplier meet the organization's security, privacy, resilience, and contractual requirements? | Vendor assessment |
| AI Impact Assessment | Potential effects on people, groups, and society | Could the system produce significant adverse impacts on affected parties? | AI impact assessment |
| AI Penetration Test | Exploitable weaknesses under defined test conditions | Can an attacker exploit identified attack paths? | Penetration test report |
| AI Compliance Assessment | Conformity with applicable obligations | Are relevant requirements implemented and evidenced? | Compliance assessment |

These assessments should inform one another. For example, a security architecture review may identify excessive agent permissions, while an AI risk assessment evaluates the resulting business impact and the AI risk register tracks treatment.

A successful architecture review does not replace penetration testing, privacy assessment, legal review, model validation, or formal deployment authorization.

### 2. Scope and Applicability of the Architecture Review

The review must establish precisely what is being assessed. AI systems frequently span multiple services, providers, business applications, infrastructure environments, and organizational boundaries.

A narrowly defined review may overlook important dependencies. Conversely, an unnecessarily broad scope can make the assessment difficult to complete and obscure the most material risks.

The reviewer should therefore document the system boundary, connected components, relevant external dependencies, and exclusions before evaluating controls.

#### 2.1 Components that may fall within scope

Depending on the system design, the review may include:

| Architecture Component | Examples | Security Review Focus |
|---|---|---|
| User interface | Web application, mobile application, employee portal | Authentication, session security, input validation |
| Application layer | API gateway, backend services, orchestration service | Authorization, secure processing, API protection |
| AI model | Hosted LLM, self-hosted LLM, classification model | Model access, configuration, abuse resistance |
| Model-serving infrastructure | Inference server, GPU cluster, model endpoint | Isolation, administrative access, network exposure |
| Prompt management | System prompts, templates, prompt configuration | Integrity, access control, change governance |
| Retrieval layer | Vector database, search engine, RAG service | Document authorization, retrieval isolation, injection risks |
| Data sources | CRM, ERP, knowledge repositories, data lakes | Data classification, permissions, provenance |
| Agent framework | Planning engine, tool router, workflow orchestrator | Tool permissions, action authorization, execution limits |
| Enterprise tools | Salesforce APIs, ticketing systems, email, databases | Least privilege, transaction controls, auditability |
| Identity services | Identity provider, IAM, service identities | Authentication, role assignment, credential protection |
| External AI services | Model APIs, hosted embedding services | Data disclosure, supplier controls, endpoint security |
| Logging and monitoring | SIEM, application logs, model telemetry | Detection, evidence integrity, sensitive-data minimization |
| Security infrastructure | WAF, network controls, secrets management, EDR | Threat prevention, containment, credential protection |
| Deployment pipeline | CI/CD, model registry, artifact repository | Artifact integrity, approvals, supply-chain security |
| Human oversight | Review console, escalation workflow | Decision authority, override capability, traceability |

The review should distinguish between components controlled directly by the organization and those operated by a third party.

This distinction affects which controls can be implemented internally, which must be verified through supplier evidence, and which risks must be addressed contractually or through compensating measures.

#### 2.2 In-scope and out-of-scope documentation

The reviewer should record the following before beginning the assessment:

| Scope Element | Required Information |
|---|---|
| System name | Official name of the AI application or service |
| System identifier | Unique inventory or architecture reference |
| Business owner | Accountable business representative |
| Technical owner | Person responsible for technical operation |
| Intended purpose | Business objective and authorized use |
| User population | Employees, customers, suppliers, or other users |
| Deployment environment | Cloud, on-premises, hybrid, or third-party SaaS |
| AI capability | Predictive ML, generative AI, RAG, agentic AI, or another approach |
| Model dependencies | Model provider, model version, endpoint, or deployed artifact |
| Data categories | Personal, confidential, financial, regulated, public, or other data |
| Connected systems | Applications, databases, APIs, identity services, and infrastructure |
| External dependencies | Suppliers, APIs, hosted models, and other third-party services |
| Review boundaries | Components and interfaces explicitly included |
| Exclusions | Components not assessed and the rationale |
| Assumptions | Conditions assumed to be true during the review |
| Known limitations | Missing documentation, unavailable evidence, or untested components |
| Review date | Date of assessment and relevant architecture version |

Exclusions must not be interpreted as evidence that an excluded component is secure. Dependencies outside the formal review boundary may still create material risk and should be documented.

#### 2.3 Architecture review triggers

An AI security architecture review should be considered at appropriate lifecycle decision points, including:

- Introduction of a new AI use case.
- Adoption of a new model provider or materially different model.
- Introduction of RAG or new data retrieval capabilities.
- Addition of AI agents or new tool permissions.
- Connection to a sensitive enterprise system.
- Expansion from internal use to customer-facing deployment.
- Introduction of personal, confidential, regulated, or otherwise sensitive data.
- Significant changes to hosting, network exposure, identity, or trust boundaries.
- Material changes to prompts, orchestration, permissions, or execution logic.
- Significant security incidents or newly identified attack paths.
- Major changes to regulatory, contractual, or organizational requirements.

The organization should define mandatory review triggers in its architecture governance and change management processes.

### 3. AI System Architecture Context and Reference Model

An AI security architecture review begins with an accurate understanding of how the system works.

A component list alone is insufficient. The reviewer must understand how users, applications, models, data stores, tools, and external services interact, including which components can initiate actions and which trust boundaries are crossed.

A reference architecture provides a common vocabulary for discussing these interactions.

#### 3.1 Logical architecture layers

A typical enterprise AI application can be represented using the following logical layers.

```text id="a13arch"
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|                    1. USER EXPERIENCE                     |
| Employees | Customers | Administrators | External Users   |
+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
                              |
                              v
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|                    2. ACCESS AND IDENTITY                 |
| SSO | MFA | IAM | RBAC/ABAC | API Authentication          |
+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
                              |
                              v
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|                    3. APPLICATION LAYER                   |
| Web/API Services | Business Logic | Input Validation      |
+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
                              |
                              v
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|                    4. AI ORCHESTRATION                    |
| Prompt Assembly | Context Management | Routing | Policies  |
+---------------+----------------------+--------------------+
                |                      |
                v                      v
+---------------------------+  +-----------------------------+
| 5. MODEL SERVICES         |  | 6. RETRIEVAL AND DATA       |
| Hosted or Self-Hosted LLM |  | Search | Vector DB | RAG    |
| Inference Endpoints       |  | Documents | Enterprise APIs |
+-------------+-------------+  +---------------+-------------+
              |                                |
              +----------------+---------------+
                               |
                               v
+-----------------------------------------------------------+
|                    7. TOOLS AND ACTIONS                   |
| CRM | ERP | Ticketing | Email | Databases | Workflows     |
+-----------------------------+-----------------------------+
                              |
                              v
+-----------------------------------------------------------+

+-----------------------------------------------------------+

Cross-Cutting Security and Governance:
Identity | Data Protection | Secrets | Network Security
Logging | Monitoring | Testing | Change Control | Incident Response
```

This is a logical model, not a mandatory physical deployment pattern. A particular implementation may combine layers, separate them across services, or omit components that are not applicable.

#### 3.2 Questions the reviewer should answer

**User and access layer**
- Who can use the system?
- How are users authenticated?
- Are administrative and ordinary user privileges separated?
- Can users access AI capabilities beyond their business authorization?

**Application and orchestration layer**
- Where are input validation and policy enforcement performed?
- Can users manipulate system prompts or other privileged configuration?
- Does the orchestration layer enforce authorization independently of model-generated instructions?
- Can an error in one component expose sensitive context to another?

**Model layer**
- Is the model hosted internally or by an external provider?
- What data is transmitted to the model?
- Which model versions and configurations are permitted?
- How are model updates, endpoint changes, and provider changes controlled?

**Retrieval and data layer**
- Are retrieval permissions aligned with the user's authorization?
- Can the system retrieve information across tenants, departments, or customer accounts?
- Are documents and embeddings protected against unauthorized modification?
- Can retrieved content contain instructions designed to manipulate the model?

**Tools and action layer**
- Which actions can the AI system perform?
- Are tool permissions limited to the minimum required?
- Are high-impact actions independently authorized?
- Can the model initiate irreversible transactions without confirmation?

**Operational layer**
- Are material AI interactions logged appropriately?
- Can security teams detect abuse or unauthorized activity?
- Is there a defined process to disable model access or revoke tool permissions?
- Can the system recover safely from a compromise?

The architecture review should produce a coherent account of these interactions before concluding that individual controls are adequate.

### 4. Establishing Trust Boundaries and Security Zones

A **trust boundary** is a point where information or control passes between components with different security assumptions, ownership, privilege levels, or exposure to threats.

AI systems often contain more trust boundaries than conventional applications because model services, retrieved content, user instructions, external tools, and generated outputs may originate from sources with different levels of trust.

The reviewer must identify these boundaries and determine which controls protect each transition.

#### 4.1 Common trust boundaries

| Boundary | Example | Primary Security Concern |
|---|---|---|
| External user to application | Customer submits a question to an AI chatbot | Malicious input, authentication bypass |
| User to orchestration service | Employee submits prompts to an internal assistant | Unauthorized access, prompt manipulation |
| Application to model provider | Backend sends context to an external LLM API | Data disclosure, endpoint security |
| Retrieval service to document repository | RAG service searches enterprise content | Permission leakage, unauthorized retrieval |
| Retrieved content to model context | Documents are inserted into a prompt | Indirect prompt injection, untrusted instructions |
| Model to tool router | Model requests a CRM or database operation | Unauthorized actions, unsafe tool selection |
| Tool router to enterprise API | Agent executes a transaction | Excessive privilege, transaction abuse |
| Tenant to tenant | Shared service processes data for multiple customers | Cross-tenant access and data leakage |
| Development to production | Model configuration or artifacts are promoted | Supply-chain compromise, unauthorized changes |
| Organization to supplier | Hosted AI service processes enterprise data | Supplier risk, contractual and operational dependency |
| Administrator to production environment | Engineer changes model or agent configuration | Privileged misuse, configuration tampering |

Each boundary should have a documented security purpose and a corresponding set of controls.

#### 4.2 Trust-boundary review questions

For each boundary, ask:

1. What information or authority crosses the boundary?
2. Which component initiates the interaction?
3. Which component receives and processes the request?
4. Is the sender authenticated?
5. Is the requested operation authorized independently of the AI model's decision?
6. Is the data validated and classified?
7. Can instructions or data from a lower-trust source influence a higher-trust component?
8. Are credentials or secrets exposed across the boundary?
9. Are interactions logged and monitored?
10. What happens if the receiving component is compromised or behaves unexpectedly?

#### 4.3 Example: Trust-boundary failure in an AI agent

Suppose an enterprise AI agent can retrieve a customer's account information and use a CRM API to update account records.

A user submits a request that causes the model to generate a tool call. If the tool router accepts the model-generated action without checking the user's authorization, the model may become an unintended route around the application's access controls.

The architectural weakness is not simply that the model generated an unsafe response. The system allowed an untrusted model output to influence a privileged operation without an independent authorization decision.

Appropriate controls may include:

- Explicit allowlisting of permitted tools and operations.
- Server-side authorization checks for each requested action.
- Binding tool permissions to the authenticated user and approved workflow.
- Restricting service identities to the minimum necessary permissions.
- Human confirmation for designated high-impact actions.
- Validation of tool arguments and target resources.
- Rate limits and transaction limits.
- Logging of the requesting user, selected tool, parameters, authorization result, and outcome.
- A mechanism to revoke agent permissions or disable affected tools.

The reviewer should verify that these controls are implemented at enforceable system boundaries rather than relying solely on instructions in the system prompt.

### 5. AI Security Threat Modeling and Attack-Surface Identification

Threat modeling is the systematic process of identifying how a system could be attacked or misused, what assets may be affected, which weaknesses create exposure, and what controls can reduce the resulting risk.

For AI architecture reviews, threat modeling must consider conventional application threats alongside threats arising from AI-specific behavior and integrations.

A useful threat model connects each threat to a specific asset, entry point, trust boundary, attack path, business impact, and mitigation.

#### 5.1 AI attack-surface categories

| Category | Example Attack Surface | Example Threat |
|---|---|---|
| User inputs | Prompts, uploaded files, API requests | Prompt injection or malicious content |
| Model interface | Inference endpoint, model API | Unauthorized model access or service abuse |
| System prompts | Prompt templates and privileged instructions | Prompt disclosure or unauthorized modification |
| Retrieval system | Vector database, search index, document store | Cross-user data leakage or indirect prompt injection |
| Training and fine-tuning | Training pipeline, datasets, model artifacts | Data poisoning or compromised artifacts |
| Tool integration | Tool router, plugins, external APIs | Unauthorized action execution |
| Identity and secrets | Service identities, API keys, tokens | Credential theft or privilege escalation |
| Application output | Generated HTML, SQL, commands, or workflow parameters | Unsafe output handling or injection |
| Model supply chain | External models, libraries, containers | Malicious or vulnerable dependencies |
| Infrastructure | Cloud workloads, containers, network services | Runtime compromise or unauthorized exposure |
| Monitoring and logs | Prompts, outputs, traces, telemetry | Sensitive-data leakage or detection gaps |
| Human workflows | Review queues, approval interfaces | Automation bias or approval bypass |
| Availability and cost | Inference endpoints and expensive tool calls | Resource exhaustion or economic abuse |
| Multi-tenant architecture | Shared models, storage, or orchestration | Tenant isolation failure |

Not every threat category applies to every system. The reviewer should document applicability and justify material exclusions.

#### 5.2 Threat-modeling workflow

```text id="a13threat"
1. Define System Purpose and Boundaries
                 |
                 v
2. Identify Assets and Security Objectives
                 |
                 v
3. Map Components, Data Flows, and Trust Boundaries
                 |
                 v
4. Identify Threat Actors and Attack Surfaces
                 |
                 v
5. Develop Plausible Threat Scenarios
                 |
                 v
6. Evaluate Existing and Proposed Controls
                 |
                 v
7. Assess Likelihood, Impact, and Residual Risk
                 |
                 v
8. Define Mitigations and Accountable Owners
                 |
                 v
9. Validate Controls Through Testing and Evidence
                 |
                 v
10. Record Findings and Obtain Required Decisions
```

#### 5.3 Threat scenario template

Each material threat scenario should capture:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Threat ID | Unique reference |
| Threat Name | Short descriptive title |
| Threat Actor | External attacker, malicious insider, compromised supplier, or other actor |
| Asset at Risk | Data, model, credential, service, transaction, or business process |
| Entry Point | Prompt, API, uploaded document, tool, endpoint, or other interface |
| Trust Boundary | Boundary crossed during the attack |
| Preconditions | Required access, configuration, or environmental conditions |
| Attack Path | Sequence of actions that could produce the threat |
| Vulnerability or Weakness | Architectural or control weakness |
| Threat Event | What the attacker or system could cause |
| Potential Impact | Confidentiality, integrity, availability, safety, compliance, or business consequences |
| Existing Controls | Safeguards currently implemented |
| Control Gaps | Missing or insufficient safeguards |
| Recommended Mitigations | Proposed preventive, detective, or corrective measures |
| Residual Risk | Remaining risk after proposed treatment |
| Owner | Person accountable for treatment |
| Validation Method | Inspection, testing, simulation, or other verification |
| Status | Open, in treatment, accepted, closed, or another defined state |

The threat model should remain a living artifact. Material architecture changes, new tool integrations, new data sources, and significant incidents should trigger review when they alter the system's threat profile.

### 6. Security Requirements for AI Architecture

Security requirements define the outcomes the architecture must achieve. They should be specific enough to guide design, implementation, testing, and assurance.

Requirements should be derived from the organization's security policies, data classification rules, threat model, risk assessment, contractual commitments, and applicable legal or regulatory obligations.

The reviewer should avoid treating a general statement such as "the AI system must be secure" as an actionable requirement.

#### 6.1 Core security requirement domains

| Domain | Example Requirement |
|---|---|
| Identity and authentication | Access to the AI service must be authenticated through approved identity mechanisms. |
| Authorization | Every access to protected resources must be authorized independently of model-generated instructions. |
| Least privilege | AI components and service identities must receive only the permissions necessary for their approved functions. |
| Data protection | Sensitive data must be protected in transit and at rest according to organizational requirements. |
| Data minimization | Only data necessary for the approved purpose should be transmitted to model services or retrieval components. |
| Tenant isolation | Data and operations must remain isolated according to tenant boundaries and authorization requirements. |
| Prompt and configuration integrity | Privileged prompts and security-relevant configurations must be protected against unauthorized modification. |
| Input handling | User inputs and retrieved content must be handled according to their trust level and the application's threat model. |
| Output handling | Model-generated content must be validated before it is used in executable contexts or consequential business actions. |
| Tool execution | Tool calls must be restricted, validated, authorized, and logged according to their potential impact. |
| Secrets management | API keys, tokens, and credentials must be stored and accessed through approved secrets-management mechanisms. |
| Network security | Model endpoints, data stores, and tools must have appropriate network exposure restrictions. |
| Logging and monitoring | Security-relevant events must be recorded and monitored with appropriate privacy and retention controls. |
| Resilience | The architecture must address service failure, rate limiting, recovery, and relevant availability risks. |
| Supply-chain security | Model artifacts, dependencies, images, and external services must be subject to appropriate integrity and supplier controls. |
| Change management | Material changes to models, prompts, permissions, and architecture must follow defined approval and validation processes. |
| Incident response | The organization must be able to investigate, contain, and recover from relevant AI security incidents. |
| Human oversight | High-impact decisions or actions must include the human review or authorization required by the system's risk classification. |
| Data retention and deletion | Data, logs, prompts, embeddings, and other artifacts must follow applicable retention and deletion requirements. |
| Secure decommissioning | AI services, credentials, integrations, and retained artifacts must be managed when the system is retired. |

These are example requirement statements. The organization should adapt them to the system's capabilities and define measurable acceptance criteria.

#### 6.2 Turning requirements into verifiable controls

Each requirement should be associated with:

- A unique requirement identifier.
- The security objective.
- The relevant architectural component.
- The control owner.
- The implementation method.
- The evidence required.
- The verification method.
- The acceptance criteria.
- The applicable risk or threat scenario.
- The status and any approved exception.

For example:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Requirement ID | AISAR-REQ-001 |
| Security Objective | Prevent unauthorized access to customer records |
| Requirement | The AI service must enforce server-side authorization for every customer-record retrieval and action. |
| Component | AI orchestration service and CRM integration |
| Threat Addressed | Cross-customer data access through manipulated prompts or tool calls |
| Control Owner | Application Security Lead and CRM Technical Owner |
| Implementation | Identity-bound authorization checks and least-privilege service permissions |
| Evidence | Architecture diagram, access-control configuration, test results |
| Verification | Negative authorization tests using users with different access rights |
| Acceptance Criteria | Unauthorized record retrieval and modification attempts are denied and logged. |
| Status | To be determined during review |

A requirement should not be marked as implemented merely because a design document mentions it. The reviewer should seek appropriate implementation evidence and validation results.

### 7. AI Security Architecture Review Governance and Responsibilities

The architecture review must have clear ownership, defined decision authority, and appropriate independence between design, assessment, and risk acceptance.

The exact roles will depend on the organization's structure. In a smaller organization, one person may perform multiple functions, but the organization should identify conflicts of interest and ensure that material risks are reviewed by an appropriately authorized party.

#### 7.1 Key roles

| Role | Primary Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Business Owner | Defines the business purpose and remains accountable for the business outcome |
| AI System Owner | Coordinates lifecycle ownership, documentation, and operational responsibilities |
| Enterprise Architect | Evaluates architecture alignment, integration, and design principles |
| AI Security Architect | Leads or contributes to AI-specific security architecture analysis |
| Security Engineering | Evaluates security control implementation and technical feasibility |
| CISO or Security Authority | Establishes security requirements and exercises assigned security decision rights |
| Data Owner | Defines permitted data use, access, and classification requirements |
| Data Protection Officer or Privacy Function | Advises on privacy obligations and relevant assessment requirements |
| Legal and Compliance | Evaluates applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations |
| Model Owner | Manages model configuration, versioning, validation, and relevant lifecycle controls |
| AI Engineering Lead | Explains implementation details and addresses technical findings |
| Cloud or Infrastructure Team | Provides evidence about hosting, network, identity, and infrastructure controls |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Assesses relevant external provider and supplier risks |
| Enterprise Risk Management | Supports risk evaluation and governance escalation |
| Internal Audit or Independent Assurance | Evaluates control design or effectiveness within its independent mandate |
| Authorized Risk Owner | Accepts residual risk where permitted and within delegated authority |
| Deployment or Release Authority | Authorizes production release under the organization's formal release process |

The same person may hold multiple roles, but the assignment must not undermine required independent challenge, control ownership, or formal approval authority.

#### 7.2 Responsibilities across the review lifecycle

| Activity | Responsible Party | Accountable Party | Consulted Parties |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiate architecture review | AI System Owner or Project Lead | Designated Review Sponsor | Enterprise Architect, Security |
| Define review scope | Architecture Review Lead | Designated Review Sponsor | System Owner, Security, Privacy |
| Provide architecture documentation | Architecture and Engineering Teams | AI System Owner | Infrastructure, Data Owners |
| Perform threat modeling | Security Architecture | Designated Security Authority for the assigned review outcome | Engineering, Enterprise Architecture |
| Assess data flows | Architecture and Data Teams | Designated System Owner or Data Authority | Privacy, Security |
| Evaluate security controls | Security Architecture and Security Engineering | Designated Security Authority | Relevant Technical Owners |
| Record architecture findings | Review Lead | Designated Review Sponsor | Control Owners |
| Define remediation | Relevant Engineering or Control Owners | Assigned Remediation Owner | Security Architecture |
| Evaluate residual risk | Risk Owner and relevant specialists | Authorized Risk Acceptance Authority | Security, Business, Compliance |
| Approve architecture or recommend approval | Designated Architecture or Security Authority | Authority defined by organizational governance | System Owner, Risk Owner |
| Authorize production deployment | Designated Release Authority | Authority defined by release governance | Business Owner, Security, Operations |
| Validate remediation | Designated independent or appropriately separate reviewer | Assigned Control or Release Authority | Engineering, Security |
| Preserve review evidence | Review Lead or designated record owner | AI Governance Function or designated owner | System Owner, Risk Management |

The table is a starting point, not a universal assignment of authority. The organization's approved RACI and delegated authority matrix take precedence.

#### 7.3 Distinguishing architecture approval from risk acceptance

Architecture approval, security review completion, and residual-risk acceptance are related but distinct decisions.

- **Architecture approval** confirms that the design meets the applicable architecture decision criteria.
- **Security review completion** confirms that the defined review has been conducted and findings have been documented.
- **Risk acceptance** records an authorized decision to tolerate a specific residual risk where acceptance is permitted.
- **Production authorization** confirms that the system satisfies the applicable release conditions.

A positive architecture review does not automatically authorize production deployment. Similarly, a business sponsor cannot automatically accept security risk beyond their delegated authority.

The governance framework should define how these decisions interact and which conditions must be met before the system may proceed.

### 8. Architecture Review Evidence and Documentation Requirements

A reliable architecture review depends on accurate and sufficiently current evidence.

Reviewers should not rely exclusively on verbal assurances, presentation slides, or diagrams that do not reflect the implemented system. Where possible, documentation should be corroborated with configuration evidence, access-control settings, test results, supplier documentation, and relevant operational records.

#### 8.1 Minimum evidence package

| Evidence ID | Evidence Item | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| EVD-01 | System overview and intended-purpose statement | Establish the system's business function and boundaries |
| EVD-02 | Current logical and physical architecture diagrams | Identify components and deployment relationships |
| EVD-03 | Data-flow diagrams | Trace data movement, processing, storage, and external transfers |
| EVD-04 | Trust-boundary diagram | Identify transitions between different trust domains |
| EVD-05 | Asset and dependency inventory | Identify models, services, libraries, APIs, and connected systems |
| EVD-06 | Data classification and handling requirements | Establish permitted use and protection requirements |
| EVD-07 | Identity and access-control design | Evaluate authentication, authorization, and privilege boundaries |
| EVD-08 | Threat model | Identify attack paths and associated mitigations |
| EVD-09 | Security requirements and control mapping | Establish expected security outcomes |
| EVD-10 | Model and provider documentation | Understand relevant model characteristics and provider responsibilities |
| EVD-11 | RAG and retrieval configuration | Assess retrieval authorization, document sources, and indexing controls |
| EVD-12 | Agent tool inventory and permission matrix | Assess permitted actions and execution authority |
| EVD-13 | Secrets and network security design | Review credential protection and network exposure |
| EVD-14 | Logging and monitoring design | Evaluate detection and investigation capabilities |
| EVD-15 | Security testing results | Review evidence of control validation |
| EVD-16 | Vulnerability and remediation records | Identify unresolved weaknesses |
| EVD-17 | Supplier assessment and contractual requirements | Evaluate third-party dependencies |
| EVD-18 | Incident response and recovery procedures | Assess containment and recovery readiness |
| EVD-19 | Change management and release procedures | Evaluate configuration integrity and deployment controls |
| EVD-20 | Risk assessment and risk acceptance records | Establish residual-risk decisions and ownership |

Not all evidence items will apply to every system. The review lead should document applicable evidence requirements and explain significant gaps.

#### 8.2 Evidence quality criteria

Evidence should be evaluated against the following criteria:

- **Relevance:** Does the evidence support the control or requirement being assessed?
- **Currency:** Does it reflect the architecture and configuration currently under review?
- **Completeness:** Does it cover the relevant components and trust boundaries?
- **Authenticity:** Can its origin and integrity be reasonably established?
- **Traceability:** Can the evidence be linked to a requirement, finding, decision, or control owner?
- **Reproducibility:** Can another qualified reviewer verify the result where appropriate?
- **Confidentiality:** Is the evidence stored and shared according to its classification?
- **Independence:** Where required, does the evidence come from a source sufficiently separate from the activity being assessed?

If evidence is missing, outdated, or inconclusive, the reviewer should record the limitation rather than assuming that the relevant control is effective.

### 9. Initial Architecture Review Checklist

The initial checklist establishes whether the review has sufficient information to proceed. It is not a final security approval.

A system with missing architecture diagrams, unclear data ownership, undocumented external dependencies, or unknown tool permissions may require additional discovery before a reliable security conclusion can be reached.

| ID | Review Question | Expected Evidence | Initial Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARC-CHK-01 | Is the system's intended purpose documented? | Approved use-case description | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-02 | Is the system owner identified? | Ownership record | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-03 | Is the technical architecture documented? | Current architecture diagrams | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-04 | Are major components and dependencies identified? | Component and dependency inventory | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-05 | Are data flows documented? | Data-flow diagrams | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-06 | Are trust boundaries identified? | Trust-boundary diagram or equivalent analysis | Not assessed |
| ARC-CHK-07 | Are data classifications known? | Data inventory and classification records | Not assessed |

### 10. Core Principle — Architecture Security Must Be Demonstrated, Not Assumed

An AI security architecture review establishes whether the system's design provides appropriate protection for its data, models, integrations, identities, tools, and business processes.

The review must account for both conventional cybersecurity weaknesses and AI-specific risks arising from model behavior, untrusted inputs, retrieved content, external model services, and model-mediated actions.

Its conclusions should be based on documented architecture, explicit security requirements, credible threat scenarios, implementation evidence, and validation appropriate to the system's risk.

**The central principle is that an AI system should not be considered architecturally secure merely because its model performs as expected or its application passes functional testing. Security depends on the complete system—including its trust boundaries, data flows, access controls, external dependencies, execution permissions, monitoring, and governance decisions.**

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Model security vs. system security | Model safeguards address model-specific risks; system security includes the surrounding application, data, infrastructure, and integrations |
| Logical architecture vs. physical architecture | Logical architecture describes functions and relationships; physical architecture describes deployment and infrastructure |
| Trust boundary vs. network boundary | A trust boundary represents a change in security assumptions; it may exist even when components share a network |
| Prompt instruction vs. security enforcement | Instructions influence model behavior; enforceable controls restrict what the system can actually do |
| Threat identification vs. risk assessment | Threat identification describes plausible attack scenarios; risk assessment evaluates their likelihood, impact, and treatment |
| Architecture review vs. penetration testing | Architecture review evaluates design and controls; penetration testing attempts to identify exploitable weaknesses under defined test conditions |
| Security finding vs. accepted risk | A finding identifies a weakness or gap; risk acceptance is an authorized decision about specified residual risk |
| Architecture approval vs. production authorization | Architecture approval addresses design criteria; production authorization addresses release conditions |
| Documentation vs. implementation evidence | Documentation describes intended controls; implementation evidence supports whether those controls exist and operate |
| AI capability vs. AI authority | A model may generate a proposed action, but the surrounding system must independently enforce whether that action is permitted |

**Completion criterion:** The foundational review package should be considered ready for detailed control evaluation when the review scope, system context, architecture components, data flows, trust boundaries, threat-modeling approach, security requirements, review responsibilities, and minimum evidence requirements have been documented sufficiently to support a structured and traceable assessment.

### 11. AI Data-Flow Analysis and Security Review

Data-flow analysis is a core activity in AI security architecture reviews because sensitive information may pass through multiple components before reaching a model, retrieval system, external service, or downstream application.

Unlike a conventional application that processes predefined inputs through deterministic business logic, an AI application may assemble context dynamically from user prompts, retrieved documents, conversation history, enterprise databases, and tool responses. This creates additional opportunities for unauthorized data disclosure, inappropriate data use, and security boundary failures.

The objective of data-flow analysis is to establish **what data moves through the system, where it originates, how it is transformed, who or what can access it, where it is stored, and where it ultimately goes**.

#### 11.1 Data-flow review objectives

The architecture reviewer should determine whether:

- All significant sources and destinations of data have been identified.
- Sensitive data is classified before being processed by AI components.
- Data transfers to external model providers are documented and authorized.
- Data minimization is applied to prompts, retrieved context, model requests, and logs.
- User permissions are preserved throughout retrieval and downstream processing.
- Conversation history and cached information cannot expose one user's data to another.
- Embeddings and vector indexes receive appropriate protection.
- Model outputs do not disclose information beyond the requester's authorization.
- Retention, deletion, and backup requirements are implemented.
- Data-flow changes are incorporated into architecture and risk reviews.

#### 11.4 Data-flow review example

Suppose an employee asks an AI assistant to summarize a customer complaint. The orchestration service retrieves the complaint from a CRM system, combines it with internal support procedures, and sends the resulting context to an external model provider.

The reviewer should ask:

1. Does the employee have permission to access the specific customer complaint?
2. Does the retrieval service enforce the employee's permissions?
3. Is the internal support content appropriate for the intended model-processing environment?
4. Does the assembled prompt include unnecessary customer identifiers?
5. Is the selected model provider authorized to process this category of information?
6. What retention and data-use terms apply to the provider?
7. Are prompts and responses included in application or provider logs?
8. Can sensitive information be exposed through debugging or telemetry?
9. Is the generated summary checked before being stored in the CRM?
10. Can the system demonstrate which source records contributed to the response?

The review should trace the data from the original source through every relevant transformation and destination.

A system may encrypt data in transit and still be insecure if it sends information to an unauthorized provider or retrieves records the requesting employee is not permitted to access.

#### 11.5 Data-flow control principles

**Minimize data before transmission.** Remove unnecessary fields and sensitive identifiers when they are not required for the approved purpose.

**Preserve authorization across components.** A user who can invoke an AI assistant must not automatically inherit access to every record accessible to the assistant's service identity.

**Treat derived data as potentially sensitive.** Embeddings, summaries, conversation histories, cached responses, and generated reports may retain or reveal information from their source data.

**Control secondary use.** Data submitted for inference should not automatically be assumed suitable for provider training, product improvement, or unrelated processing.

**Maintain traceability.** Material data transfers should be documented sufficiently to support investigation, compliance review, and impact assessment.

### 12. Data Classification, Privacy, and Information Protection

AI architecture reviews must evaluate whether data classification and information-protection requirements remain effective when information is processed by models, retrieval systems, external providers, and AI-generated workflows.

Traditional data controls often focus on databases and files. AI systems introduce additional locations where information can appear, including prompts, model context, embeddings, generated responses, agent memory, traces, and evaluation datasets.

The reviewer should therefore examine the complete information lifecycle rather than only the primary database.

#### 12.1 AI-specific data locations

| Data Location | Potential Exposure | Review Focus |
|---|---|---|
| User prompts | Sensitive information entered directly by users | Input warnings, data minimization, access restrictions |
| Conversation history | Information from previous interactions | Session isolation, retention, access controls |
| System prompts | Privileged instructions or embedded configuration | Confidentiality, integrity, controlled access |
| Retrieved documents | Internal information included in model context | Source authorization and retrieval filtering |
| Embeddings | Derived representations of source information | Access control, isolation, retention, and leakage testing |
| Model request payloads | Prompt, context, parameters, and metadata | Provider authorization and transmission controls |
| Model responses | Generated content containing sensitive information | Output controls and authorized disclosure |
| Agent memory | Persisted facts, summaries, and previous task context | Ownership, access isolation, expiry, and deletion |
| Tool arguments | Parameters sent to enterprise APIs | Data minimization and authorization |
| Application logs | Prompts, outputs, errors, and request traces | Sensitive-data minimization and retention |
| Evaluation datasets | Examples containing prompts, expected responses, or sensitive records | Dataset authorization, access, and handling |
| Fine-tuning datasets | Data incorporated into model adaptation | Provenance, consent or other lawful basis where applicable, and access control |
| Backups and archives | Historical copies of AI-related data | Retention, access, and deletion obligations |

Not every system uses every data location. The inventory should reflect the actual implementation.

#### 12.2 Data classification requirements

The organization should define how its data classification scheme applies to AI processing.

For example:

| Classification | Illustrative AI Processing Expectations |
|---|---|
| Public | May be processed within approved services, subject to integrity and acceptable-use controls |
| Internal | May require approved enterprise services and authenticated access |
| Confidential | May require explicit service approval, access restrictions, encryption, and restricted retention |
| Highly Restricted or Regulated | May require enhanced authorization, specific processing environments, stronger monitoring, and additional approvals |

These categories are illustrative. The organization's approved classification policy determines the actual handling requirements.

The architecture review should verify that classification rules are enforced technically where feasible rather than relying exclusively on users to recognize every sensitive item.

#### 12.3 Privacy and data protection questions

The reviewer should coordinate with the appropriate privacy function to determine whether the system's processing activities require additional assessment.

Questions include:

- Does the system process personal data?
- What is the defined purpose of the processing?
- Is the data necessary and proportionate to that purpose?
- What lawful basis or other applicable legal justification supports the processing?
- Are individuals provided with required information?
- Does the system introduce profiling or decisions that may significantly affect individuals?
- Are special-category or otherwise sensitive data involved?
- Are data transferred to external providers or across geographic jurisdictions?
- Are retention and deletion procedures documented?
- Can individuals exercise applicable data protection rights?
- Are automated decisions subject to applicable safeguards?
- Is a Data Protection Impact Assessment required?
- Are relevant data processing agreements and supplier terms in place?

The answers depend on the system, jurisdiction, processing context, and applicable law. The security architect should not independently substitute a technical assessment for a required legal or privacy determination.

#### 12.4 Embeddings and vector databases

Embeddings convert data into numerical representations used for semantic search and other machine-learning operations.

Although an embedding is not necessarily a direct copy of its source text, it should not automatically be treated as anonymous or non-sensitive.

The architecture review should examine:

- Whether embeddings are derived from confidential or personal information.
- Whether each vector retains an association with its source document and access policy.
- Whether retrieval authorization is enforced at query time.
- Whether deleted or reclassified source records are removed or appropriately handled in associated indexes.
- Whether tenant-specific information is isolated.
- Whether vector database administrators have excessive access.
- Whether embedding generation and indexing are protected against unauthorized changes.
- Whether logs and diagnostic tools expose vector content or associated metadata.
- Whether the organization has tested retrieval behavior for unauthorized disclosure.

**Key architectural principle:** Retrieval must not become a mechanism for bypassing the authorization controls of the original data source.

### 13. Identity and Access Management for AI Systems

Identity and Access Management (IAM) is a foundational control domain for AI security architecture.

AI applications may operate using several distinct identities: the human user, the application service, the orchestration component, the model endpoint, the retrieval service, and one or more tool-specific identities.

If these identities are poorly designed, the AI system may exercise permissions that exceed the requesting user's authorization or allow one compromised component to affect unrelated systems.

The architecture review must determine how identity is established, propagated, restricted, and verified throughout the system.

#### 13.1 AI identity categories

| Identity Type | Example | Primary Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Human user | Employee accessing an AI assistant | Unauthorized access or impersonation |
| Customer identity | Customer using an AI support portal | Account takeover or cross-customer exposure |
| Application identity | Backend service calling a model endpoint | Excessive service permissions |
| Orchestrator identity | AI workflow service accessing multiple components | Broad access across systems |
| Retrieval identity | Service account querying a vector database | Access beyond the intended document scope |
| Agent identity | Identity assigned to an AI agent or workflow | Excessive or persistent action authority |
| Tool-specific identity | CRM integration service account | Unauthorized business transactions |
| Model provider identity | Credential used to access an external inference endpoint | API-key theft or service abuse |
| Administrator identity | Engineer managing model configuration | Privileged misuse or configuration tampering |
| Pipeline identity | CI/CD service deploying models or prompts | Supply-chain compromise or unauthorized deployment |

A design may use fewer identities, but the reviewer should identify every identity that can access data, change configuration, invoke models, or execute actions.

#### 13.3 Human identity propagation

When an AI system retrieves or modifies information on behalf of a user, the architecture should define how that user's authorization is preserved.

Possible approaches include:

- **Delegated user authorization:** The downstream service receives a token or authorization context representing the user.
- **Service identity with explicit authorization checks:** The backend uses its own identity but verifies the requesting user's permissions before every protected operation.
- **Scoped delegated access:** The service receives limited authority for a specific resource, purpose, or duration.
- **Approved workflow authorization:** The application allows only predefined operations under a controlled business process.

The selected pattern should reflect the downstream system's capabilities and the sensitivity of the operation.

A broadly privileged service account combined with weak application-level checks is a significant architectural risk.

#### 13.4 IAM review questions

The reviewer should verify:

1. Are human users authenticated through approved identity services?
2. Are privileged users subject to appropriate additional controls?
3. Are service identities distinct where separation is necessary?
4. Are credentials stored in approved secrets-management systems?
5. Are service permissions limited to the required resources and operations?
6. Are tokens scoped and protected appropriately?
7. Are permissions checked at the resource or operation boundary?
8. Are user permissions preserved during retrieval and tool execution?
9. Are access grants reviewed periodically?
10. Can access be revoked promptly when a user, service, or provider is compromised?
11. Are administrative and operational duties appropriately separated?
12. Are identity and authorization failures logged and monitored?

#### 13.5 Example: Excessive privilege in a CRM-connected agent

A customer-service agent uses a service account with permission to read and update every customer record in the CRM.

The agent is intended to modify only the record associated with the authenticated customer's current interaction. However, the backend does not independently validate the record identifier generated by the model.

A manipulated prompt could cause the agent to request an operation against another customer's record.

The primary architectural weakness is the combination of excessive service permissions and missing resource-level authorization.

Recommended controls include:

- Reducing the service account's privileges.
- Validating the target customer record against the authenticated user or approved case context.
- Allowlisting permitted operations and fields.
- Enforcing authorization server-side.
- Applying human confirmation or additional approval to sensitive operations.
- Logging the requester, target record, operation, authorization result, and outcome.
- Testing attempts to access records outside the authorized scope.

The review should verify that unauthorized operations are rejected by enforceable controls, even if the model produces a malicious or incorrect tool call.

### 14. Authentication, Authorization, and Privileged Access Controls

Authentication, authorization, and privileged access management solve different problems and should be assessed separately.

- **Authentication** establishes the identity of a user, service, or system.
- **Authorization** determines what that identity is permitted to access or perform.
- **Privileged access management** governs elevated access to sensitive systems and administrative capabilities.

A system may authenticate every user correctly and still be insecure if authorization checks are missing or service identities have excessive privileges.

#### 14.1 Authentication controls

The architecture review should evaluate:

- Approved enterprise identity integration.
- Multi-factor authentication for applicable users and administrators.
- Secure session management.
- Protection against credential theft and replay.
- API authentication and token validation.
- Service-to-service authentication.
- Secure credential rotation and revocation.
- Protection against unauthorized use of model provider credentials.

Public-facing AI services may also require abuse controls such as rate limiting, bot detection, and restrictions on unauthenticated access to expensive or sensitive capabilities.

#### 14.2 Authorization controls

Authorization should be evaluated at every significant resource and action boundary.

| Control | Purpose | AI-Specific Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) | Assign permissions based on roles | Roles should limit access to approved AI capabilities and data |
| Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) | Make decisions using attributes and context | Authorization may depend on user, resource, tenant, purpose, or other conditions |
| Resource-level authorization | Restrict access to specific records or objects | Model-generated resource identifiers must not bypass checks |
| Operation allowlisting | Restrict permitted actions | Agents should invoke only approved tools and operations |
| Field-level restrictions | Limit access to sensitive fields | AI responses should not reveal restricted CRM or HR attributes |
| Context-bound authorization | Limit actions to a specific user request or workflow | Prior conversation content should not automatically grant new privileges |
| Time-limited access | Restrict access duration | Temporary administrative or agent permissions should expire |
| Segregation of duties | Prevent incompatible responsibilities from being combined | High-impact actions may require separate review or approval |

The organization should document how authorization is enforced and identify which component is the authoritative decision point.

#### 14.3 Privileged access management

AI systems can create privileged-access risks when an agent can execute administrative actions, change configurations, retrieve secrets, or access sensitive repositories.

The review should determine:

- Which identities have administrative privileges.
- Whether privileged credentials are stored and managed securely.
- Whether administrative actions require additional authentication or approval.
- Whether privilege elevation is limited in scope and duration.
- Whether model and prompt changes require controlled authorization.
- Whether privileged operations are logged and reviewed.
- Whether production administration is separated from development.
- Whether emergency access is controlled and monitored.
- Whether AI components can retrieve credentials they do not require.
- Whether access can be revoked without disabling unrelated business services.

#### 14.4 Authorization enforcement pattern for tool execution

```text id="a13auth"
AI Model Proposes Tool Call
            |
            v
Validate Tool Name and Arguments
            |
            v
Identify Requesting User and Context
            |
            v
Check Resource-Level Authorization
            |
            v
Evaluate Business and Security Policy
            |
            +---- Denied ----> Reject + Log + Monitor
            |
            v
Check Additional Approval Requirements
            |
            +---- Required --> Obtain Authorized Approval
            |

Execute With Least-Privilege Identity
            |
            v

```

This pattern should be adapted to the risk and complexity of the system. Not every tool call requires human approval, but every consequential action requires appropriate authorization.

#### 14.5 Acceptance criteria

The architecture should not pass its IAM review solely because SSO or MFA is enabled.

Acceptance should be based on evidence that:

- Identity is established appropriately.
- Permissions match the approved business purpose.
- Service identities cannot access unrelated resources.
- AI-generated instructions cannot override authorization controls.
- Sensitive operations receive the required authorization.
- Privileged access is restricted and monitored.
- Revocation and recovery procedures are operational.
- Negative tests demonstrate that unauthorized actions are denied.

### 15. Model Endpoint Security and Inference Service Protection

Model endpoints expose the capability to submit inputs to an AI model and receive outputs. Depending on their configuration, they may be accessible through a public API, private network, managed cloud service, or internally hosted inference platform.

An exposed or inadequately protected model endpoint can be abused for unauthorized inference, data extraction attempts, service disruption, excessive cost generation, or attacks against the surrounding application.

The architecture review should examine endpoint exposure, authentication, authorization, network restrictions, request handling, resource controls, and operational monitoring.

#### 15.1 Model endpoint deployment patterns

| Pattern | Description | Principal Security Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Public managed API | Application accesses a provider-hosted model over an external API | API credentials, provider controls, outbound restrictions, data handling |
| Private managed endpoint | Model service is accessed through private connectivity or equivalent restricted access | Network configuration, identity, endpoint authorization |
| Self-hosted inference service | Organization operates the model and inference infrastructure | Infrastructure hardening, model artifacts, patching, access controls |
| Containerized inference | Model runs in containers or orchestrated workloads | Image integrity, runtime isolation, secrets, workload identity |
| Dedicated model environment | Model service operates in an isolated or dedicated environment | Isolation validation, administration, operational resilience |
| Hybrid model architecture | Some components are hosted externally and others internally | Cross-boundary data flows, identity, policy consistency, dependency management |

The deployment pattern alone does not establish security. A private endpoint can still be misconfigured, and a public API can be appropriately protected when its exposure is controlled and its use is authorized.

#### 15.2 Endpoint security requirements

**Endpoint access**
- Require approved authentication for protected model endpoints.
- Restrict endpoint access to authorized applications and identities.
- Avoid exposing internal inference services directly to untrusted networks.
- Apply network restrictions where appropriate.
- Separate development, testing, and production endpoints.

**Credential protection**
- Store API keys and tokens in approved secrets-management systems.
- Avoid hardcoding credentials in source code, prompts, configuration files, or logs.
- Rotate credentials according to risk and policy.
- Revoke credentials promptly after suspected compromise.

**Request protection**
- Validate request structure and parameters.
- Restrict request size and processing limits.
- Apply rate limits and concurrency controls.
- Prevent callers from selecting unauthorized models or configurations.
- Enforce quotas or budgets where inference costs create material exposure.

**Output and error handling**
- Handle errors without exposing secrets, internal prompts, or sensitive infrastructure details.
- Validate model outputs before downstream use.
- Prevent model responses from being interpreted as trusted executable instructions.
- Avoid exposing internal diagnostic traces to unauthorized users.

**Operational security**
- Monitor unusual request patterns and resource consumption.
- Record relevant access and security events.
- Maintain procedures to disable or restrict a compromised endpoint.
- Validate endpoint configuration after material changes.

#### 15.3 Model endpoint abuse scenarios

| Scenario | Potential Impact | Architectural Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Stolen API key | Unauthorized inference and cost exposure | Secrets management, scoped credentials, rotation, anomaly detection |
| Publicly exposed internal endpoint | Unauthorized access to model capabilities | Network restrictions, authentication, endpoint inventory |
| Unrestricted request volume | Resource exhaustion or excessive charges | Rate limits, quotas, concurrency controls |
| Unauthorized model selection | Use of unapproved or more expensive models | Server-side model allowlisting |
| Verbose error responses | Disclosure of internal configuration or secrets | Secure error handling and response filtering |
| Cross-environment credential reuse | Development access affecting production | Environment separation and distinct identities |
| Excessive response logging | Sensitive prompt or response disclosure | Logging minimization and controlled access |
| Uncontrolled model version changes | Unexpected behavior or control regressions | Version control, change approval, regression testing |

#### 15.4 Model endpoint review checklist

| ID | Review Question | Expected Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| END-01 | Is the endpoint's exposure documented? | Network and deployment architecture |
| END-02 | Is access restricted to approved identities? | Authentication and access-control configuration |
| END-03 | Are credentials protected and rotatable? | Secrets-management configuration and procedures |
| END-04 | Are requests validated and constrained? | API configuration and implementation evidence |
| END-05 | Are rate limits and resource controls configured? | Gateway, platform, or application configuration |
| END-06 | Are approved models and versions enforced? | Model registry or allowlist |
| END-07 | Are development and production environments separated? | Environment and identity configuration |
| END-08 | Are security events logged appropriately? | Logging specification and sample events |

### 16. Model Integrity, Versioning, and Configuration Governance

Model integrity refers to confidence that the model artifact or hosted model being used is the intended, approved version and has not been altered or replaced through unauthorized means.

Model configuration governance covers the controls used to manage model versions, system prompts, inference parameters, safety settings, tool definitions, and related components that influence system behavior.

An AI application may become insecure even when its underlying infrastructure remains unchanged. For example, changing a model version, modifying a system prompt, expanding an agent's tool permissions, or changing a retrieval configuration may introduce new attack paths.

The architecture review should therefore examine model and configuration changes as security-relevant lifecycle events.

#### 16.1 Model and configuration assets

The inventory should identify, where applicable:

- Model name and version.
- Model provider or source.
- Model artifact identifier or digest.
- Deployment environment and endpoint.
- Model configuration and inference parameters.
- System prompt and privileged instruction versions.
- Tool definitions and permitted operations.
- Safety and content-control settings.
- Retrieval configuration and source repositories.
- Fine-tuning artifacts and training datasets.
- Dependency versions and container images.
- Approval and release records.
- Rollback procedures and previous approved versions.

The required level of detail depends on the deployment model. A managed external model may not expose a downloadable artifact or cryptographic digest. In such cases, the organization should document the available provider-side version identifiers, contractual commitments, configuration controls, and limitations in its ability to verify the underlying artifact.

#### 16.3 Model change review questions

- Is the proposed model or configuration change authorized?
- Is the target model version approved for the intended use case?
- Could the change alter data handling or provider processing?
- Could it change the model's tool-selection behavior or output characteristics?
- Are existing threat assumptions still valid?
- Are regression tests appropriate for the identified risks?
- Have relevant security and privacy assessments been updated?
- Is the deployment performed through a controlled process?
- Can the organization identify the version currently running in production?
- Is rollback feasible and adequately tested?
- Are material changes recorded in the system inventory and decision records?

#### 16.4 Prompt and policy integrity

System prompts and related configuration may contain privileged behavioral instructions, tool descriptions, or business rules.

They should therefore be managed as security-relevant configuration rather than treated as informal text.

Appropriate controls may include:

- Restricted write access.
- Version control and change history.
- Peer review for material changes.
- Separation of development and production configuration.
- Automated testing against security-relevant behaviors.
- Approval requirements for changes to tool permissions or security rules.
- Monitoring for unauthorized modifications.
- Controlled rollback to a previously approved configuration.

A prompt should not be the sole enforcement mechanism for critical security requirements. Authorization, access restrictions, transaction controls, and other high-impact safeguards should be enforced through appropriate application and infrastructure controls.

### 17. AI Model and Service Supply-Chain Security

AI systems may depend on external model providers, open-source models, model repositories, datasets, embedding services, orchestration frameworks, libraries, container images, and other third-party components.

Each dependency may introduce security, operational, licensing, provenance, integrity, or supplier-concentration risks.

The architecture review should establish which components originate externally, how they are obtained and updated, and what controls reduce the risk of compromised or unsuitable dependencies.

#### 17.1 AI supply-chain components

| Component | Potential Risk | Review Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Hosted model provider | Provider incident, service change, data-handling concerns | Supplier assessment, contractual controls, provider assurance |
| Open-source model artifact | Tampered or unsuitable artifact | Provenance, integrity verification where available, controlled acquisition |
| Model repository | Malicious or compromised downloads | Approved sources, access restrictions, artifact validation |
| Training or fine-tuning dataset | Poisoned, unauthorized, or low-quality data | Provenance, integrity, permitted use, validation |
| Embedding model | Unexpected data processing or retrieval behavior | Version control, testing, provider assessment |
| AI framework or library | Vulnerabilities or compromised dependencies | Dependency scanning, patching, approved versions |
| Container image | Vulnerable packages or unauthorized changes | Image provenance, scanning, signing where appropriate |
| Orchestration framework | Unsafe tool execution or access-control weaknesses | Secure configuration, code review, patch management |
| External plugin or connector | Excessive permissions or compromised integration | Permission review, supplier assessment, monitoring |
| Model update service | Uncontrolled or unexpected behavior changes | Update controls, version monitoring, change review |

#### 17.2 Supply-chain control requirements

The reviewer should assess whether the organization:

1. Maintains an inventory of material AI dependencies.
2. Uses approved sources for models and associated artifacts.
3. Validates artifact integrity where technically possible.
4. Assesses relevant vulnerabilities in frameworks, libraries, and deployment images.
5. Controls access to model and dataset repositories.
6. Evaluates supplier security and operational resilience.
7. Documents relevant contractual and data-handling obligations.
8. Controls model, framework, and dependency updates.
9. Maintains a process for responding to newly discovered vulnerabilities.
10. Has contingency arrangements for critical provider outages or unacceptable supplier changes.

#### 17.3 Supplier assurance limitations

Supplier certifications and assurance reports can provide useful evidence, but they do not establish that every customer-specific configuration is secure.

The organization should distinguish between:

- Controls operated by the provider.
- Controls operated by the customer.
- Controls shared between provider and customer.
- Controls that cannot be independently verified with available evidence.

For example, a hosted model provider may secure its underlying inference infrastructure, while the customer remains responsible for preventing unauthorized users from submitting sensitive prompts or granting an agent excessive access to internal systems.

The architecture review should document these responsibility boundaries and verify that the organization's own controls address the risks it retains.

### 18. Core Principle — Security Must Follow Data, Identity, and Control Across Every Boundary

AI security architecture cannot be assessed reliably by reviewing the model endpoint in isolation. Data flows, user identities, service permissions, model configurations, retrieval mechanisms, and external dependencies collectively determine the system's exposure.

A secure architecture must preserve authorization as data moves between components, restrict privileged actions independently of model-generated instructions, protect sensitive information throughout its lifecycle, and maintain control over model and dependency changes.

**The central principle is that every material data flow, identity transition, model interaction, and privileged action must have a defined security purpose, an enforceable control, an accountable owner, and evidence sufficient to demonstrate that the control works.**

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Data movement vs. data authorization | Knowing where data moves does not establish that the requester is permitted to access it |
| Encryption vs. authorized processing | Encryption protects data in transit or at rest; it does not determine whether a destination is permitted to process it |
| Embedding vs. anonymization | An embedding is a derived representation and should not automatically be treated as anonymous |
| Authentication vs. authorization | Authentication establishes identity; authorization determines permitted access and actions |
| User identity vs. service identity | The requesting user and the backend service may have different permissions and must be governed accordingly |
| Model output vs. authorized action | A generated tool call is a proposed operation, not authorization to execute it |
| Private endpoint vs. secure endpoint | Private connectivity reduces certain exposure paths but does not eliminate identity, configuration, or application risks |
| Model versioning vs. model integrity | Versioning identifies a model or configuration; integrity controls help establish that the intended artifact or configuration is being used |
| Supplier assurance vs. customer control effectiveness | Provider evidence does not prove that customer-side configuration and integration controls are effective |
| Prompt guidance vs. enforceable security control | Prompt instructions influence behavior; application and infrastructure controls enforce access restrictions |
| Data-flow documentation vs. data-flow verification | Diagrams describe expected flows; implementation evidence verifies actual behavior |
| Architecture review vs. risk acceptance | The review identifies and evaluates design concerns; risk acceptance requires a separate authorized decision where applicable |

**Completion criterion:** The architecture review should have sufficient evidence to evaluate data flows, classification and privacy requirements, identity and authorization, model endpoint protection, configuration integrity, and supply-chain dependencies before the reviewer determines whether the system's technical controls adequately address its identified threats.

### 19. Prompt Injection and Instruction-Hierarchy Security

Prompt injection is an AI-specific security threat in which an attacker supplies or manipulates content that influences a model to behave contrary to the application's intended instructions, security assumptions, or authorized purpose.

Prompt injection can originate directly from a user or indirectly from content that an AI system retrieves, processes, or receives from an external source.

The architectural concern is not merely whether a model follows an undesirable instruction. It is whether that instruction can influence privileged context, expose protected information, bypass application controls, or trigger unauthorized operations.

Prompt injection should therefore be evaluated as part of the system's complete security architecture.

#### 19.1 Types of prompt injection

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct prompt injection | A user supplies instructions intended to override the application's intended behavior | A user asks an assistant to ignore its normal restrictions and reveal internal configuration |
| Indirect prompt injection | Instructions are embedded in content that the model retrieves or processes | A document contains malicious instructions intended to influence a RAG assistant |
| Persistent prompt injection | Malicious instructions enter a data source or memory store and affect later interactions | An agent stores attacker-controlled content that influences subsequent tasks |
| Multimodal prompt injection | Instructions are embedded in images, audio, video, or other supported input formats | A document image contains instructions that are interpreted during visual analysis |
| Tool-oriented prompt injection | Manipulated instructions attempt to influence an agent to invoke tools or perform unauthorized actions | Retrieved content instructs an agent to send confidential information to an external endpoint |
| Cross-context injection | Content from one user, tenant, task, or session influences another context | Shared memory introduces one user's instructions into another user's session |

These categories may overlap. A single attack may begin with indirect prompt injection, influence persistent memory, and ultimately attempt to trigger a tool call.

#### 19.2 Why prompt injection is an architectural problem

A model may receive instructions from several sources:

- System-level instructions established by the application.
- Developer-defined instructions and tool descriptions.
- User prompts.
- Retrieved documents and search results.
- Tool responses.
- Conversation history and persistent memory.
- External content such as websites, files, emails, or support tickets.

These sources do not all have the same trust level. However, a language model processes them as part of its context and may not reliably maintain the security boundaries the application intends.

A system prompt that says "never disclose confidential information" is not equivalent to an enforceable data-access control.

The architecture must therefore ensure that a model's interpretation of instructions cannot independently authorize access to protected information or privileged actions.

#### 19.3 Recommended architectural controls

| Control | Purpose | Implementation Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit trust labeling | Distinguishes trusted configuration from untrusted content | Identify retrieved documents, user inputs, and external content as untrusted |
| Server-side authorization | Prevents model instructions from granting access | Check permissions at the data or operation boundary |
| Context minimization | Reduces information available to a manipulated model | Supply only the context needed for the current task |
| Tool allowlisting | Restricts available capabilities | Expose only approved tools and operations |
| Argument validation | Prevents unsafe tool parameters | Validate resource identifiers, destinations, formats, and operation types |
| Output validation | Prevents unsafe downstream interpretation | Validate generated content before executing commands or processing structured outputs |
| Human authorization | Adds oversight for designated consequential actions | Require approval for high-impact or irreversible operations |
| Memory isolation | Prevents cross-user or cross-task contamination | Bind memory to an authorized identity, tenant, and purpose |
| Monitoring | Detects suspicious interaction patterns | Record and investigate policy violations and unusual tool activity |
| Adversarial testing | Evaluates resistance to manipulation | Test direct, indirect, multimodal, and persistent attack scenarios |

No single control guarantees that prompt injection will be prevented. Security should rely on layered controls that restrict the impact of a successful manipulation.

#### 19.4 Architecture review questions

The reviewer should establish:

1. Which input sources can influence model behavior?
2. Can the model access confidential information that the current user is not authorized to retrieve?
3. Are retrieved documents and external content treated as untrusted?
4. Can model-generated content modify system prompts, policies, or tool permissions?
5. Can a manipulated model invoke tools with elevated privileges?
6. Are tool calls authorized independently of the model's reasoning?
7. Can malicious content persist in conversation history or agent memory?
8. Can one user's context influence another user's session?
9. Are high-impact actions subject to independent authorization?
10. Have prompt-injection scenarios been tested against the actual architecture?

#### 19.5 Example: Prompt injection through an internal document

An enterprise assistant retrieves a document from a knowledge repository. The document contains legitimate business information alongside instructions designed to make the model reveal confidential system information.

The assistant incorporates the document into its context. The model then generates a response containing information that should not be disclosed.

The architecture review should determine whether the disclosure was possible because:

- Sensitive information was included unnecessarily in the model context.
- Retrieval authorization was insufficient.
- The model was permitted to access privileged configuration.
- Output controls failed to restrict disclosure.
- The application relied on prompt instructions rather than enforceable access controls.

The appropriate remediation depends on the actual failure path.

Potential controls include reducing context exposure, enforcing source-level authorization, separating privileged configuration from user-visible context, applying appropriate output controls, and testing retrieval and disclosure boundaries.

**Important distinction:** Detecting suspicious instructions may help, but the architecture should remain safe even when detection fails.

#### 20.2 RAG-specific threats

| Threat | Description | Potential Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized retrieval | The system retrieves content the user cannot access directly | Confidentiality breach |
| Cross-tenant retrieval | Search returns content belonging to another customer or tenant | Data leakage and contractual exposure |

| Source attribution failure | Responses cannot be traced to source material | Reduced auditability and unreliable decisions |
| Inadequate deletion | Removed documents remain retrievable from associated indexes or caches | Retention and privacy violations |
| Overly broad context | Excessive retrieved content is supplied to the model | Increased disclosure and prompt-injection exposure |

#### 20.3 RAG security requirements

A RAG implementation should address the following requirements.

**Source authorization**

The system should enforce the appropriate access policy before or during retrieval. Filtering results after unauthorized content has already been exposed to an external model may be insufficient.

**Permission synchronization**

Changes to source permissions must propagate to the retrieval system within an acceptable and documented period. The architecture should define how permission revocation is handled.

**Ingestion security**

Documents should be validated before indexing. Where relevant, controls should address malicious files, unsupported formats, malware, unauthorized sources, and unexpected content.

**Metadata integrity**

Security-relevant metadata, including tenant identifiers, access labels, source identifiers, and classification, should be protected against unauthorized modification.

**Context minimization**

Only relevant and authorized content should be included in the model context. Retrieval should not expose entire repositories merely because a user asks a broad question.

**Untrusted-content handling**

Retrieved documents should be treated as data rather than trusted security instructions. The system should not allow retrieved text to change authorization policy or independently grant tool access.

**Source traceability**

Where required by the use case, responses should retain references to supporting sources so users and reviewers can assess the origin of the information.

**Deletion and lifecycle management**

Deletion and retention procedures should account for source documents, derived embeddings, indexes, caches, replicas, and backups according to applicable requirements.

#### 20.4 Example: Cross-department data exposure

An organization deploys a RAG assistant for employees. The assistant searches HR, Finance, Legal, and IT repositories.

The vector database is accessible through a service identity that can retrieve documents from every repository. The application relies on the model to answer only questions that are appropriate for the employee's department.

This is an inadequate security boundary.

The model's behavior cannot replace access enforcement. An employee may manipulate the query, exploit retrieval behavior, or obtain sensitive information through an unexpected interaction.

A safer architecture should:

1. Authenticate the employee through the enterprise identity provider.
2. Determine the employee's authorized data scope.
3. Enforce access restrictions during retrieval.
4. Validate the authorization of each returned source.
5. Minimize the context supplied to the model.
6. Treat retrieved content as untrusted input.
7. Test unauthorized retrieval and disclosure scenarios.
8. Log relevant access decisions and investigate failures.

The reviewer should verify that authorization is enforced in the actual retrieval path and not merely described in application instructions.

- Execution monitoring.
- Emergency revocation.
- Post-execution verification.

#### 21.5 Example: AI agent with CRM and email access

- Whether the agent's CRM access is restricted to authorized records.
- Whether email recipients are validated against approved destinations.
- Whether sensitive information is filtered according to policy.
- Whether sending external messages requires approval under defined conditions.
- Whether CRM modifications are limited to approved fields and operations.
- Whether the agent can retrieve or expose credentials.
- Whether actions can be stopped and investigated.
- Whether every consequential tool invocation is traceable to an authenticated request.
- Whether prompt injection can cause the agent to change its intended task.
- Whether repeated or recursive tool execution is bounded.

The reviewer should assess the full execution path, including how the model proposes actions, how the authorization layer evaluates them, and how the target system enforces its own permissions.

### 22. AI Agent Memory, State, and Context Isolation

AI agents may retain context between actions or across sessions to support task continuity, personalization, planning, or long-running workflows.

Memory can be implemented through conversation history, databases, summaries, vector stores, task state, or external workflow systems.

Persistent memory introduces security risks when information from one user, task, tenant, or trust domain is reused in another context without appropriate authorization.

#### 22.1 Memory categories

| Memory Type | Description | Security Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| In-context conversation | Messages retained within the active model context | Session isolation and context minimization |
| Short-term task state | Temporary information used during a workflow | Task boundaries, expiration, integrity |
| Summarized memory | Compressed representation of previous interactions | Sensitive-data retention and accuracy |
| Persistent user memory | Information retained across sessions for an authorized user | User access, correction, deletion, and scope |
| Shared organizational memory | Information accessible to multiple authorized users | Source authorization and tenant or departmental boundaries |
| Tool execution state | Records of previous tool calls and results | Integrity, replay protection, and auditability |
| Long-running workflow state | Information retained while an agent performs a multi-stage task | Ownership, timeout, cancellation, and recovery |

#### 22.2 Memory security requirements

The architecture review should determine:

1. Who can create or modify memory?
2. Who can read each memory item?
3. Is memory associated with a specific user, task, tenant, or authorized purpose?
4. Can external content cause malicious instructions to persist?
5. Are sensitive details retained only when necessary?
6. Are expiration and deletion rules enforced?
7. Can a user or administrator inspect or correct relevant retained information where required?
8. Are memory updates protected against unauthorized modification?
9. Can a compromised task contaminate another task's context?
10. Are memory access and modification events logged where appropriate?

#### 22.3 Example: Cross-user memory contamination

A shared assistant stores summaries of previous interactions in a common vector database. The retrieval logic does not consistently filter by user or tenant.

A new user asks a question, and the system retrieves a previous user's confidential project details because they appear semantically relevant.

This is an access-control failure involving persistent context.

Appropriate controls include:

- Explicit memory ownership and scope.
- Authorization-aware retrieval.
- Tenant and user isolation.
- Defined retention and deletion rules.
- Restrictions on what information may be persisted.
- Validation of memory writes.
- Testing for cross-session and cross-tenant leakage.

Memory should not be treated as inherently trustworthy merely because it was stored by the application previously.

### 23. AI Output Security and Downstream Consumption

AI-generated outputs may be displayed to users, stored in business systems, passed to other models, used as tool parameters, or interpreted by software.

An output that appears to be ordinary text may become a security risk if a downstream component treats it as executable code, a trusted decision, a database query, or an authorized business instruction.

The architecture review should therefore examine both the generation of outputs and their subsequent use.

#### 23.1 Output-related threats

| Threat | Example | Potential Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitive information disclosure | Response includes confidential context not authorized for the user | Confidentiality breach |
| HTML or script injection | Generated content is rendered as executable markup | Client-side compromise |
| SQL injection | Generated query fragments are inserted into a database query without safe handling | Unauthorized data access or modification |
| Command injection | Generated text is passed to a shell or command interpreter | System compromise |
| Unsafe structured output | Malformed or manipulated JSON triggers unintended application behavior | Workflow or data integrity failure |
| Unauthorized business action | Generated recommendation is executed without independent authorization | Financial or operational impact |
| Inaccurate high-impact output | Incorrect information is used in a consequential workflow | Business, safety, or compliance impact |
| External destination manipulation | Generated content directs sensitive information to an unapproved destination | Data exfiltration |
| Tool-result trust failure | Untrusted tool output is treated as authoritative instructions | Further compromise or unauthorized action |

#### 23.2 Output security controls

The appropriate controls depend on how outputs are consumed.

**For displayed text**
- Apply context-appropriate output encoding.
- Avoid rendering generated content as trusted HTML or executable code.
- Enforce access controls on any underlying data references.
- Provide source references where required by the use case.

**For structured outputs**
- Validate against a defined schema.
- Reject unexpected fields, types, or values.
- Apply business-rule validation independently of the model.
- Avoid assuming that syntactically valid JSON is semantically safe.

**For database operations**
- Use parameterized queries or approved data-access abstractions.
- Enforce resource-level authorization.
- Restrict available operations.
- Avoid executing arbitrary model-generated SQL in privileged contexts.

**For commands and code**
- Avoid passing model-generated content directly to shell interpreters.
- Use constrained execution environments where code execution is an approved capability.
- Apply sandboxing, resource limits, and network restrictions.
- Require additional review for high-impact operations.

**For external actions**
- Validate destinations and recipients.
- Apply disclosure controls.
- Require authorization for sensitive or irreversible actions.
- Record the action, requesting identity, and execution outcome.

#### 23.3 Architecture review example

An AI assistant generates a JSON object containing a proposed CRM update:

```json
{
  "customer_id": "C-10482",
  "action": "update_account",
  "credit_limit": 50000
}
```

Valid JSON does not prove that the proposed update is authorized.

The application must determine whether:

- The authenticated user can modify the specified customer account.
- The requested field is permitted for this workflow.
- The requested value is within applicable business limits.
- The action requires additional approval.
- The target account is within the authorized resource scope.
- The operation should be recorded and independently verified.

The model's output should be treated as an untrusted proposal until the application validates it and authorizes the operation.

### 24. AI Security Testing and Validation Strategy

Architecture reviews should identify how the proposed security controls will be validated.

Documentation alone cannot establish that a control works. Where the risk warrants it, the reviewer should require testing that demonstrates the system rejects unauthorized access, withstands relevant attack scenarios, and behaves correctly when components fail.

Testing should be risk-based and should cover both conventional application security and AI-specific behavior.

#### 24.1 Security testing categories

| Testing Category | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Architecture inspection | Evaluate design and control placement | Review trust boundaries and authorization paths |
| Configuration review | Verify implemented settings | Inspect endpoint exposure and IAM permissions |
| Static application testing | Identify weaknesses in source code | Analyze unsafe output handling or secrets exposure |
| Dependency scanning | Identify known vulnerable components | Scan AI frameworks and container dependencies |
| API security testing | Evaluate authentication and authorization | Test access to unauthorized resources |
| Prompt-injection testing | Assess susceptibility to manipulated instructions | Test direct and indirect injection scenarios |
| RAG security testing | Evaluate retrieval authorization and data isolation | Attempt unauthorized document retrieval |
| Agent tool testing | Verify operation restrictions and execution boundaries | Attempt prohibited CRM modifications |
| Privacy and leakage testing | Evaluate unintended disclosure | Test cross-user leakage and sensitive output exposure |
| Model behavior evaluation | Assess relevant model characteristics | Evaluate reliability or policy adherence for the defined use case |
| Penetration testing | Attempt to exploit weaknesses under an authorized test plan | Test application, API, identity, and integration attack paths |
| Red-team exercise | Simulate realistic adversarial activity within defined rules | Evaluate coordinated attack paths across model, retrieval, and tools |
| Resilience testing | Evaluate recovery and service failure behavior | Test model endpoint failure and safe shutdown |
| Regression testing | Detect security degradation after changes | Re-run approved security tests after model or prompt updates |

Testing methods should be selected according to the architecture, threat model, and risk classification. No single test type is sufficient for every AI system.

#### 24.2 Security test scenario template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Test ID | Unique identifier |
| Test Objective | Security property being evaluated |
| Threat Reference | Related threat-model scenario |
| Component | Target application, model, retrieval service, or tool |
| Preconditions | Required identity, configuration, and environment |
| Test Input or Action | Controlled test stimulus |
| Expected Secure Behavior | Required system response |
| Observed Behavior | Actual result |
| Evidence | Logs, screenshots, request traces, or test output |
| Result | Pass, fail, blocked, inconclusive, or not applicable |
| Severity | Risk-based classification of any finding |
| Remediation Owner | Person responsible for corrective action |
| Retest Requirement | Validation needed after remediation |

Testing should be performed only within authorized environments and under an approved test plan.

#### 24.3 Example security acceptance criteria

For a CRM-connected AI agent, the architecture review might require evidence that:

- A user cannot retrieve another customer's restricted records.
- A prompt cannot grant additional privileges.
- A model-generated tool call cannot bypass server-side authorization.
- A tool cannot modify fields outside its approved scope.
- External data cannot be sent to an unapproved destination.
- High-impact actions trigger the required approval workflow.
- Unauthorized tool calls are denied and logged.
- Revoked permissions prevent further protected actions.
- Material model or configuration changes trigger required validation.
- Incident responders can identify and disable the affected integration.

The exact criteria must be derived from the approved system requirements and threat model.

#### 24.4 Handling inconclusive test results

A test that could not be completed is not automatically a pass.

The reviewer should distinguish between:

- **Pass:** Evidence demonstrates that the defined acceptance criterion is satisfied.
- **Fail:** The system does not satisfy the criterion.
- **Inconclusive:** The evidence does not support a reliable conclusion.
- **Not tested:** The test was not performed.
- **Not applicable:** The scenario does not apply, with documented justification.

Critical or high-risk controls that remain unverified should be escalated according to the organization's governance and release requirements.

### 25. Architecture Security Findings and Remediation Management

The architecture review should produce actionable findings rather than a list of general observations.

Each finding should identify the affected component, the weakness or missing control, the plausible attack path, the potential impact, the required corrective action, and the authority responsible for resolving or accepting the associated risk.

Findings should be linked to the organization's risk register and change-management process where appropriate.

#### 25.1 Finding classification

An organization may use a severity model such as the following:

| Severity | Illustrative Characteristics | Typical Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | A credible attack path could cause severe compromise, major unauthorized actions, or extensive sensitive-data exposure | Immediate escalation; deployment restriction or containment as appropriate |
| High | A material weakness creates significant security or business exposure | Prioritized remediation and formal review before affected release or operation, as required by policy |
| Medium | A meaningful weakness exists, but the expected impact or exploitability is more constrained | Planned remediation with assigned ownership and deadline |
| Low | Limited exposure or a localized improvement opportunity | Remediation according to established prioritization |
| Informational | Observation or recommendation without a confirmed material control failure | Track as an improvement item where appropriate |

Severity should reflect the organization's approved methodology and the context of the specific system. A numeric score should not replace consideration of data sensitivity, exploitability, business impact, exposure, and compensating controls.

### 26. Architecture Review Decision and Approval Framework

The architecture review should culminate in a documented decision that accurately reflects the review scope, findings, evidence, unresolved issues, and applicable approval authority.

The decision must distinguish the reviewer's technical recommendation from the formal authority to approve the architecture, accept residual risk, or authorize production deployment.

#### 26.1 Recommended decision outcomes

| Outcome | Meaning | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Approved | The architecture satisfies applicable review criteria | Required evidence and approvals are complete |
| Approved with Conditions | The architecture may proceed subject to explicit and enforceable conditions | Conditions, owners, deadlines, and verification requirements are documented |
| Rework Required | Material design changes or additional evidence are needed | A new review or validation is required before approval |
| Rejected | The architecture does not meet essential requirements or the identified risk is not acceptable under the applicable authority | The design must be changed or the proposal discontinued |
| Unable to Conclude | Evidence or scope limitations prevent a reliable determination | Missing information or testing must be addressed |

These outcomes are illustrative and should align with the organization's formal architecture governance process.

#### 26.2 Approval conditions

Where conditional approval is permitted, the decision should specify:

- The exact conditions attached to approval.
- The person accountable for each condition.
- The completion deadline.
- The evidence required to demonstrate completion.
- Whether deployment is permitted before completion.
- Any operational restrictions.
- The authority responsible for verifying compliance.
- The escalation route if conditions are not satisfied.

A condition that is vague, unowned, or unenforceable should not be treated as an effective security safeguard.

For example, "improve access controls as soon as possible" is not a sufficient condition for a high-risk deployment.

A more actionable condition would identify the required authorization control, the affected integration, the responsible owner, the validation test, and the requirement that the control be verified before production release.

#### 26.4 Core Principle — Technical Recommendation and Formal Authority Must Remain Distinct

The architecture reviewer evaluates the design and provides an evidence-based conclusion. The designated authority makes the decision assigned to that role under the organization's governance framework.

This separation helps prevent commercial pressure, technical assumptions, or informal agreement from overriding documented security requirements.

### 27. Core Principle — AI Security Must Be Enforced at System Boundaries

AI-specific threats frequently exploit weaknesses in the relationships between components rather than vulnerabilities in a model alone.

Prompt injection can influence model behavior. Insecure retrieval can expose unauthorized information. Excessive agent permissions can convert a manipulated response into a real business action. Unsafe output handling can allow generated content to compromise a downstream application.

These risks require architecture-level safeguards that remain effective even when the model behaves unexpectedly.

**The central principle is that untrusted inputs, retrieved content, model-generated outputs, and proposed tool calls must never be allowed to independently override enforceable identity, authorization, data-protection, and business-control requirements.**

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Prompt injection vs. authorization failure | Prompt injection manipulates model behavior; an authorization failure allows an operation beyond permitted access |
| RAG relevance vs. RAG authorization | Relevant content is not necessarily content the requesting user is permitted to access |
| Agent capability vs. agent authority | The ability to invoke a tool does not establish permission to use it in a particular context |
| Memory persistence vs. memory trustworthiness | Retained information may persist across tasks but must still be evaluated for authorization, integrity, and relevance |
| Valid output vs. safe output | Syntactically valid content may still cause an unsafe action |
| Model evaluation vs. system security testing | Model evaluation assesses selected model behaviors; system testing examines the complete application and its control boundaries |
| Finding remediation vs. finding closure | Implementing a fix is an activity; closure requires appropriate verification |
| Conditional approval vs. unrestricted deployment | Conditional approval applies defined requirements and restrictions; it does not automatically authorize all production activity |
| Technical recommendation vs. formal approval | The reviewer provides an assessment; the designated authority makes the decision assigned to that role |

**Completion criterion:** The AI security architecture review should have a documented threat model, RAG and agent security analysis where applicable, output-handling requirements, a risk-based testing strategy, a finding-management process, and an explicit decision framework before concluding that the system is ready for formal architecture approval.

| Boundary ID | Source Zone | Destination Zone | Information or Action | Principal Risk | Required Controls |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

1. Confirm that the component exists in the authoritative architecture.
2. Identify the responsible owner.

5. Identify preventive, detective, and recovery controls.
6. Collect evidence that the controls are implemented.
7. Record deficiencies and associated risks.
8. Link deficiencies to remediation actions and validation tests.

A diagram without corresponding ownership, control requirements, and evidence is insufficient for a complete architecture assurance process.

### 30. AI Security Architecture Control Validation Matrix

The control validation matrix connects architecture requirements to evidence and testing.

It helps reviewers distinguish between a control that has been designed, a control that has been implemented, and a control whose effectiveness has been demonstrated.

#### 30.1 Control status model

| Status | Meaning | Minimum Evidence Expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Designed | The control is specified in the architecture or security requirements | Approved design or requirement |
| Implemented | The control has been configured or deployed | Configuration, implementation record, or equivalent |
| Tested | The control has been tested against defined criteria | Test procedure and result |
| Effective | Available evidence supports the conclusion that the control meets its intended objective within the assessed scope | Appropriate test results and supporting operational evidence |
| Partially Effective | The control exists but has material limitations | Documented limitations and associated risk |
| Ineffective | The control does not meet its required objective | Failed test, demonstrated weakness, or other evidence |
| Not Verified | Evidence is insufficient to determine implementation or effectiveness | Outstanding evidence request |
| Not Applicable | The control does not apply to the system | Documented rationale and reviewer confirmation |

A control marked "implemented" should not automatically be marked "effective." Effectiveness requires evidence appropriate to the control's purpose and risk.

#### 30.2 Sample validation matrix

| Control ID | Control Objective | Architecture Area | Validation Method | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ARC-001 | Only authorized users can access the AI application | IAM | Test authenticated and unauthorized access | Authentication and authorization test results | To be assessed |
| ARC-002 | Retrieval respects source permissions | RAG | Test access to restricted documents | Retrieval authorization test results | To be assessed |
| ARC-003 | Model-generated instructions cannot grant additional privileges | Agent security | Attempt prohibited actions under controlled conditions | Tool authorization test results | To be assessed |
| ARC-004 | Tool parameters are validated independently of model output | Application security | Submit invalid and unauthorized parameters | Validation test results | To be assessed |
| ARC-005 | Sensitive information is not sent to unapproved destinations | Data protection | Test destination restrictions and disclosure controls | Policy configuration and test evidence | To be assessed |
| ARC-006 | Model and security configuration changes are controlled | Change management | Review permissions, approvals, and version history | Change records and configuration evidence | To be assessed |
| ARC-007 | Security-relevant events are recorded | Logging and monitoring | Verify event generation, collection, and access | Log samples and monitoring configuration | To be assessed |
| ARC-008 | High-impact actions follow approval requirements | Agent governance | Test approval gates and denied execution | Workflow evidence and test records | To be assessed |
| ARC-009 | Security incidents can be contained | Incident response | Review or exercise revocation and isolation procedures | Exercise report and recovery evidence | To be assessed |
| ARC-010 | Material changes trigger appropriate reassessment | Lifecycle governance | Inspect change triggers and reassessment records | Change-management and review records | To be assessed |

The status values above are placeholders, not claims that the controls have been implemented or tested.

#### 30.3 Control traceability

The architecture review should support traceability in both directions:

**Requirement to evidence**

A reviewer selects a security requirement and identifies its implementation, owner, test result, and supporting evidence.

**Threat to mitigation**

A reviewer selects a threat scenario and identifies the relevant controls, test cases, findings, and residual risk.

**Finding to closure**

A reviewer selects an open finding and identifies the corrective action, responsible owner, validation criteria, and closure evidence.

This traceability reduces the likelihood that a security requirement is documented but never implemented, or that a finding is closed without adequate validation.

### 33. Architecture Review Acceptance Criteria and Release Gates

An architecture review should define explicit acceptance criteria before the final decision.

This reduces ambiguity between the project team's interpretation of readiness and the security team's requirements for acceptable risk.

#### 33.1 Baseline acceptance criteria

The following criteria provide a starting point. They must be tailored to the system's risk, intended use, and applicable requirements.

| Acceptance Area | Example Criterion | Required Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | System boundaries and material dependencies are documented | Approved architecture diagrams |
| Data protection | Data classifications and relevant processing requirements are identified | Data-flow register and applicable assessments |
| Access control | Required user, service, and tool permissions are defined and enforced | IAM configuration and test results |
| Trust boundaries | External and privileged interactions are identified and protected | Boundary register and control evidence |
| Model security | Model identity, endpoint configuration, and relevant changes are governed | Configuration and change records |
| RAG security | Retrieval respects authorization and data-isolation requirements | Retrieval test evidence |
| Agent security | Tool execution is constrained and authorized independently of model reasoning | Tool policy and authorization tests |
| Output security | Downstream consumers validate and safely handle model-generated content | Application design and test evidence |
| Logging | Relevant security events can be detected and investigated | Logging configuration and sample events |
| Incident response | Appropriate containment and recovery mechanisms exist | Runbooks and exercise results |
| Findings | Required remediation is complete or handled through an authorized decision | Finding register and validation evidence |
| Residual risk | Material remaining risks have been evaluated by the appropriate authority | Risk register and decision records |
| Ownership | Operational and security responsibilities are assigned | Responsibility matrix and support arrangements |
| Reassessment | Triggers for future security review are defined | Change-management and monitoring procedures |

#### 33.2 Release gate model

An organization may establish release gates such as:

**Gate 1 — Design readiness**

The architecture is sufficiently defined to assess trust boundaries, data flows, security requirements, and material risks.

**Gate 2 — Implementation readiness**

Required security controls have been implemented, and the evidence needed to validate them is available.

**Gate 3 — Security validation**

Risk-based testing has been completed, material findings have been addressed, and outstanding uncertainties are documented.

**Gate 4 — Formal authorization**

The appropriate authority has approved the architecture and any permitted residual-risk acceptance.

**Gate 5 — Production readiness**

Applicable conditions have been satisfied, operational monitoring is in place, and the release process confirms that deployment requirements have been met.

These gates may be implemented within an existing Secure Software Development Lifecycle (SSDLC), change-management framework, or technology risk process.

#### 33.3 When deployment should be blocked or escalated

Deployment should be blocked or escalated when required by the organization's policies, applicable law, contractual obligations, or formal risk criteria.

Examples include:

- A critical security requirement is demonstrably unmet.
- A material authorization weakness enables access beyond permitted scope.
- Sensitive information can be exposed through an identified and credible attack path.
- A required legal or regulatory condition has not been satisfied.
- A mandatory security test has failed.
- A required approval is missing.
- A material risk has no authorized disposition.
- A conditional approval requirement has not been met.

The architecture reviewer should document the basis for escalation and identify the authority responsible for resolving the decision.

### 34. Post-Approval Monitoring and Architecture Reassessment

Architecture approval reflects the system and evidence assessed at a particular point in time. It does not establish that the system will remain secure indefinitely.

AI systems may change through model updates, prompt modifications, new retrieval sources, altered tool permissions, supplier changes, additional integrations, and shifts in business purpose.

The organization should therefore define reassessment triggers and operational controls that preserve the validity of the original review.

#### 34.1 Reassessment triggers

| Trigger | Why Reassessment May Be Required |
|---|---|
| Model replacement or major version change | May alter behavior, capabilities, data handling, or security assumptions |
| Material system-prompt change | May affect instructions, task boundaries, or model behavior |
| New tool or API integration | Introduces additional privileges and attack surfaces |
| Permission-model change | May alter who can access information or perform actions |
| New data source | Introduces different classification, integrity, privacy, or injection risks |
| RAG pipeline modification | May change retrieval behavior, authorization, or context assembly |
| Agent autonomy increase | May expand the scope or impact of automated actions |
| New geographic deployment | May affect data residency, jurisdiction, or processing requirements |
| Supplier or subprocessor change | May change dependencies, contractual arrangements, or assurance assumptions |
| Security incident | May demonstrate that previous threat assumptions or controls were insufficient |
| Material vulnerability | May undermine a previously accepted security design |
| Business-purpose change | May introduce new stakeholders, data uses, or impact categories |
| Periodic review date | Supports continued assurance according to risk and policy |

The organization should classify changes by materiality so that minor changes do not automatically require a complete review while significant changes cannot bypass appropriate reassessment.

#### 34.2 Operational monitoring requirements

The architecture should identify how the organization will monitor:

- Authentication failures and suspicious access patterns.
- Unauthorized retrieval attempts.
- Unusual tool invocation or action sequences.
- Denied operations and policy violations.
- Unexpected data transfers.
- Changes to privileged configuration.
- Model endpoint availability and abnormal consumption.
- Relevant vulnerability and supplier notifications.
- Security incidents and recurring control failures.
- Completion of conditional approval requirements.

Monitoring must account for privacy, confidentiality, retention, and access restrictions. Logging should capture sufficient evidence for investigation without unnecessarily retaining prompts, documents, credentials, or sensitive model context.

#### 34.3 Change impact assessment

A change impact assessment should answer:

1. What has changed?
2. Which architecture components and trust boundaries are affected?
3. Which data flows or identities have changed?
4. Does the change introduce new threats or alter existing risks?
5. Which security controls require revalidation?
6. Does the change invalidate prior test evidence?
7. Is a new privacy, supplier, or regulatory assessment required?
8. Does the change require renewed architecture approval?
9. Who authorizes deployment?
10. What evidence must be retained?

This process makes architecture assurance a lifecycle activity rather than a one-time approval exercise.

### 37. Core Principle — Architecture Assurance Must Continue Throughout the AI Lifecycle

AI security architecture review is not simply a technical inspection performed before deployment. It is an assurance process that connects system design, threat analysis, control implementation, testing, risk decisions, operational monitoring, and reassessment.

A mature review process demonstrates that:

1. The system's purpose and boundaries are understood.
2. Relevant threats and security requirements are identified.
3. Controls are placed at the appropriate architectural boundaries.
4. Implemented controls are validated through proportionate evidence.
5. Findings are managed through accountable remediation.
6. Residual risks are handled by authorized decision-makers.
7. Approval conditions are enforced.
8. Material changes and incidents trigger reassessment.

**The central principle is that architecture approval must be supported by traceable evidence, explicit risk decisions, and enforceable controls—not merely by design documentation or confidence in the AI model.**

#### Final Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Architecture description vs. architecture assurance | A description explains the design; assurance evaluates whether the design and its controls meet defined requirements |
| Designed vs. implemented control | A designed control is specified; an implemented control exists in the actual system |
| Implemented vs. effective control | Implementation establishes presence; effectiveness requires appropriate evidence |
| Evidence received vs. evidence accepted | Receiving an artifact does not establish that it is relevant, current, or sufficient |
| Security finding vs. residual risk | A finding identifies a deficiency; residual risk represents the risk remaining after applicable controls and treatment decisions |
| Remediation vs. risk acceptance | Remediation changes the underlying weakness; acceptance is an authorized decision to retain specified risk where permitted |
| Architecture approval vs. production authorization | Architecture approval addresses the defined design-review criteria; production authorization follows the organization's broader release requirements |
| Conditional approval vs. unconditional approval | Conditional approval depends on defined requirements that must be tracked and enforced |
| Periodic review vs. change-triggered review | Periodic review follows a schedule; change-triggered review responds to events that may invalidate previous assumptions |
| Model security vs. AI system security | Model security concerns the model and its associated protections; AI system security covers the complete application, data, identity, infrastructure, integrations, and operational lifecycle |

### Lesson 20.13 Completion Criteria

The **AI Security Architecture Review Template** is complete when the organization can use it to document the architecture, identify relevant threats, evaluate security controls, validate evidence, manage findings, record approval decisions, enforce release conditions, and maintain assurance as the AI system evolves.

The template should be tailored to the organization's risk classification, security architecture standards, AI governance framework, contractual commitments, and applicable legal and regulatory requirements. It is a reusable assessment instrument, not a certification that a particular AI system is secure.
