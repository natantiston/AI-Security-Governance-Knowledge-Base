# 20.06 — AI Risk Register Template

## Part 1 — AI Risk Register Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Risk Register

An AI Risk Register is a structured governance artifact used to identify, document, assess, prioritize, treat, monitor, and report risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

It provides a centralized view of risks arising from AI models, data, infrastructure, integrations, suppliers, operational processes, human interactions, and the contexts in which AI outputs are used.

Unlike a general enterprise risk register, an AI Risk Register must account for risks arising from probabilistic outputs, model limitations, training and inference data, autonomous behavior, changing model capabilities, human overreliance, and interactions between AI components.

The register should connect identified risks to their causes, potential consequences, affected stakeholders, existing controls, inherent and residual risk ratings, treatment plans, accountable owners, and acceptance decisions.

It must function as an operational risk management instrument rather than a static list created solely for an audit or compliance exercise.

An effective AI Risk Register enables the organization to answer five fundamental questions:

1. What could go wrong with this AI system or use case?
2. Why could the risk occur, and who or what could be affected?
3. What controls currently reduce the likelihood or impact?
4. What residual risk remains, and who is accountable for it?
5. What actions are required to bring the risk within the organization's risk appetite?

The register should be integrated with the AI System Inventory, AI Impact Assessment, AI Security Assessment, privacy review, vendor risk management, incident management, and enterprise risk management processes.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Risk Register

The primary purpose of the AI Risk Register is to create a consistent and auditable record of AI-related risk decisions.

The register supports the organization in identifying material exposures, determining appropriate risk treatment, assigning accountability, and tracking whether corrective actions reduce risk to an acceptable level.

Its objectives include:

- Establishing a consolidated view of AI-related risks.
- Supporting risk-based prioritization of AI systems and use cases.
- Recording the causes and consequences of identified risks.
- Distinguishing inherent risk from residual risk.
- Connecting risks to preventive, detective, and corrective controls.
- Tracking risk treatment plans and remediation deadlines.
- Recording risk acceptance decisions and their conditions.
- Supporting management oversight and escalation.
- Providing evidence for internal audit and external assurance.
- Supporting reassessment when AI systems or their operating environments change.

The register should enable decision-makers to understand not only the number of risks identified but also their materiality, concentration, treatment status, and potential effect on business objectives and affected stakeholders.

A register containing hundreds of risks without clear ownership, treatment decisions, or evidence of control effectiveness does not demonstrate effective risk management.

### 3. AI Risk Register as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI Risk Register is a foundational component of an organization's AI governance operating model.

It provides the connection between risk identification, control implementation, management decisions, and ongoing oversight.

The register should operate as part of a broader governance process:

```
AI SYSTEM INVENTORY
        |
        v
AI RISK IDENTIFICATION
        |
        v
RISK ANALYSIS AND EVALUATION
        |
        v
CONTROL AND TREATMENT DECISIONS
        |
        v
RESIDUAL RISK ASSESSMENT
        |
        v
APPROVAL OR ESCALATION
        |
        v
ONGOING MONITORING
        |
        v
REASSESSMENT AND IMPROVEMENT
```

The register must support traceability across these stages.

For example, a risk involving unauthorized disclosure of confidential information through an AI assistant should be linked to the relevant system, identified data flows, existing access controls, proposed mitigations, residual risk rating, and accountable decision-maker.

The register should also support enterprise-level aggregation so that management can identify recurring risk patterns across multiple systems, business units, models, or providers.

However, aggregation must not remove the detail needed to understand the circumstances of individual risks.

### 4. Scope of the AI Risk Register

The register should cover AI-related risks within the organization's defined governance scope.

The scope may include risks associated with internally developed AI, third-party AI services, embedded AI capabilities, generative AI applications, machine learning models, AI agents, and AI-enabled business processes.

Risk categories may include:

- AI security and cybersecurity.
- Data protection and privacy.
- Model reliability and performance.
- Bias, discrimination, and unfair outcomes.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Human oversight and decision authority.
- Operational resilience and service continuity.
- Third-party and supply chain dependencies.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Intellectual property and confidentiality.
- Financial and commercial exposure.
- Reputational damage and stakeholder trust.
- Environmental and resource considerations where material.
- Agentic behavior and unauthorized actions.
- Model drift, degradation, and change-related exposure.

The organization should define whether the register covers individual risks, risk scenarios, system-level risks, enterprise-level AI risks, or a combination of these.

A practical implementation commonly uses individual risk records linked to AI systems and use cases, with aggregation mechanisms for portfolio-level reporting.

The register should not attempt to replace specialized risk records required by other enterprise processes. Instead, it should reference and integrate with those processes where appropriate.

### 5. Definition of an AI Risk

An AI risk is the possibility that uncertainty associated with an AI system, its components, its use, or its operating environment will affect objectives, cause harm, or result in undesirable consequences.

AI risk can arise from technical failures, malicious activity, inappropriate use, inadequate governance, external dependencies, human behavior, or interactions among these factors.

A well-formed risk statement identifies the circumstances that could lead to an adverse outcome and the consequences that may follow.

A practical risk statement structure is:

**Because of a defined cause or vulnerability, a particular event may occur, resulting in a specified consequence for the organization or affected stakeholders.**

For example:

"Because the AI assistant can access confidential documents without sufficiently restrictive authorization checks, a user may obtain information outside their approved access scope, resulting in unauthorized disclosure of sensitive business information."

This statement identifies the underlying condition, potential event, and business consequence.

The risk statement should be specific enough to support control selection and risk evaluation. Statements such as "AI security risk," "hallucination risk," or "regulatory risk" are too broad to guide meaningful treatment without further analysis.

### 6. Risk, Threat, Vulnerability, Event, and Impact Distinction

The register should distinguish related concepts that are frequently confused during AI risk assessments.

| Concept        | Meaning                                                 | AI example                                                        |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk           | Potential effect of uncertainty on objectives           | Confidential information may be disclosed through an AI assistant |
| Threat         | Potential source of harm                                | A malicious user attempts to extract restricted information       |
| Vulnerability  | Weakness that may be exploited or contribute to failure | Missing authorization enforcement in retrieval logic              |
| Risk event     | The occurrence that produces an adverse outcome         | The assistant returns restricted content to an unauthorized user  |
| Impact         | The resulting consequence                               | Data exposure, contractual liability, and reputational damage     |
| Control        | A measure intended to modify risk                       | Document-level authorization checks and output filtering          |
| Risk treatment | A decision or action to modify risk                     | Restrict retrieval access and implement security testing          |

These concepts should remain distinct in the register because they support different management activities.

Threat intelligence helps identify plausible attack sources. Vulnerability management identifies weaknesses. Incident management records events that have occurred. Risk management evaluates the possibility and consequences of adverse outcomes and determines how they should be treated.

A single risk scenario may involve several threats, vulnerabilities, events, and impacts. The register should capture these relationships without making the record unnecessarily complex.

### 7. AI Risk Register Ownership and Accountability

The organization must establish accountability for maintaining the register and managing individual risk records.

The AI Risk Register owner is responsible for the integrity of the risk management process, including consistent methodology, record quality, reporting, and escalation.

Individual risk owners are accountable for understanding and managing the risks assigned to them. Their responsibilities may include evaluating exposure, maintaining treatment plans, confirming control operation, and escalating material changes.

Typical responsibilities include:

| Role                       | Responsibility                                                    |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI risk register owner     | Maintains the register framework, structure, and reporting        |
| Business risk owner        | Accountable for the business consequences of the risk             |
| Technical risk contributor | Provides technical analysis and implementation details            |
| AI governance function     | Establishes methodology and challenges risk decisions             |
| Cybersecurity              | Assesses security threats, vulnerabilities, and controls          |
| Privacy function           | Evaluates relevant personal data and privacy risks                |
| Legal and compliance       | Advises on applicable obligations and legal exposure              |
| Control owner              | Implements and operates assigned risk controls                    |
| Risk acceptance authority  | Approves residual risk within delegated authority                 |
| Internal audit             | Independently evaluates risk management and control effectiveness |

One person may perform several roles in a smaller organization, but accountability and approval authority must remain explicit.

The person responsible for implementing a control should not automatically be authorized to accept the residual risk created by that control's failure.

### 8. AI Risk Register Record Identifier

Every risk record should have a unique, persistent identifier.

The identifier enables the organization to reference the same risk across assessment reports, control matrices, treatment plans, audit findings, incidents, and management dashboards.

An illustrative format is:

`AIR-2026-0001`

Where:

- `AIR` identifies an AI risk record.
- `2026` identifies the creation year.
- `0001` represents a sequential record number.

The organization may use another identifier convention provided that uniqueness, persistence, and traceability are maintained.

Risk identifiers should not encode information that may change, such as the current risk rating, business owner, or system status.

If a risk is transferred to another owner or reassessed, its identifier should normally remain unchanged. If two risks are consolidated or one risk is split into several records, the organization should preserve cross-references to maintain historical traceability.

### 9. AI Risk Register Data Model

The data model should capture the minimum information required to understand, evaluate, treat, monitor, and report each risk.

A risk record should generally include the following information groups:

| Data group     | Representative fields                                      |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identification | Risk ID, title, description, creation date                 |
| Context        | AI system ID, use case, business unit, deployment          |
| Risk scenario  | Cause, threat, vulnerability, event, consequence           |
| Classification | Risk category, subcategory, affected objectives            |
| Assessment     | Likelihood, impact, inherent risk, residual risk           |
| Controls       | Existing controls, control owners, evidence references     |
| Treatment      | Treatment decision, actions, owners, deadlines             |
| Accountability | Risk owner, control owner, acceptance authority            |
| Governance     | Review status, escalation status, approval references      |
| Monitoring     | Indicators, thresholds, review dates, incident links       |
| Evidence       | Assessment records, test results, supporting documentation |
| Closure        | Closure rationale, verification evidence, approval         |

The data model should support structured reporting while preserving enough narrative detail to explain each risk scenario.

Mandatory fields should be defined by lifecycle stage. For example, a newly identified risk may initially have an incomplete assessment, while a risk awaiting formal acceptance must have the required residual risk and approval information.

### 10. AI Risk Title and Description

The risk title should be concise, specific, and meaningful to business and technical stakeholders.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized Disclosure Through AI Retrieval.
- Unapproved Autonomous Execution by an AI Agent.
- Model Drift Affecting Credit Decision Reliability.
- Third-Party AI Service Availability Failure.
- Sensitive Data Retention by an External AI Provider.
- Inadequate Human Oversight of Automated Decisions.

The description should explain the scenario, relevant system context, potential event, and consequences.

The organization should avoid titles that merely name a broad category, such as "Privacy," "Security," or "Hallucination."

A useful description should make it possible for a reviewer to understand why the risk matters and what type of control or treatment may be appropriate.

The title and description should remain understandable when viewed independently in management reports or audit findings.

### 11. AI Risk Category and Subcategory

The organization should define a standardized taxonomy for classifying AI risks.

A taxonomy allows risk records to be grouped, compared, prioritized, and reported consistently across systems and business units.

An illustrative taxonomy is:

| Primary category      | Example subcategories                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Security              | Prompt injection, data exfiltration, unauthorized tool use   |
| Privacy               | Excessive collection, unlawful processing, re-identification |
| Model reliability     | Hallucination, drift, poor generalization                    |
| Fairness              | Disparate outcomes, biased training data                     |
| Transparency          | Insufficient explainability, unclear AI involvement          |
| Human oversight       | Automation bias, ineffective intervention                    |
| Operational           | Service disruption, capacity constraints                     |
| Third-party           | Provider compromise, model dependency                        |
| Compliance            | Regulatory non-compliance, inadequate documentation          |
| Intellectual property | Unauthorized use of protected material                       |
| Governance            | Missing ownership, unauthorized deployment                   |
| Agentic systems       | Excessive permissions, uncontrolled execution                |

The taxonomy should be extensible as new risks emerge.

A risk may involve multiple categories, but the organization should define a primary category for consistent reporting and allow secondary tags where useful.

Classification should reflect the actual risk scenario rather than forcing a complex scenario into a single category that obscures its causes or consequences.

### 12. AI System and Use-Case Linkage

Every risk should be linked to the AI system, component, deployment, or use case to which it applies.

The primary relationship should normally reference the AI System Inventory rather than duplicating the system's authoritative information in the risk register.

Relevant links may include:

- AI system identifier.
- Use-case identifier.
- Model or model-component identifier.
- Deployment identifier.
- Provider or supplier record.
- Business process.
- Data asset or data flow.
- Related assessment.
- Related incident.
- Related control record.

A risk may affect multiple systems, while one system may have multiple risks.

Where a risk is common to several systems, the organization should decide whether to maintain one shared risk record with explicit system relationships or separate records for materially different scenarios.

The decision should preserve clear accountability and enable risk aggregation without concealing differences in exposure.

### 13. AI Risk Source and Cause Fields

The register should record the causes and conditions that make each risk plausible.

Risk sources may include technical design weaknesses, incomplete requirements, poor data quality, inadequate permissions, insufficient human oversight, third-party dependencies, ineffective governance, or malicious activity.

Examples include:

- Untrusted content entering a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline.
- Broad permissions granted to an AI agent.
- Training data that does not represent the deployment population.
- Lack of monitoring for model degradation.
- Provider changes without adequate notification.
- Missing validation before AI outputs influence decisions.
- Inadequate access restrictions for confidential information.
- Unclear accountability for AI-generated recommendations.

Recording the cause supports targeted treatment.

For example, if unauthorized disclosure arises from excessive retrieval permissions, improving the user interface alone may not address the underlying risk.

The cause field should distinguish confirmed weaknesses from assumptions that require further validation.

### 14. AI Risk Event and Consequence Fields

The register should describe the adverse event that could occur and the consequences that may follow.

The event should be specific enough to support scenario analysis and control design.

For example:

- Event: An AI agent executes a transaction without the required approval.
- Consequence: The organization incurs financial loss and violates internal authorization requirements.

Potential consequences may include financial loss, service disruption, privacy harm, security compromise, legal exposure, discrimination, physical harm, reputational damage, or loss of stakeholder trust.

The organization should identify affected parties where relevant, including customers, employees, suppliers, members of the public, and other stakeholders.

The consequence analysis should consider direct and indirect effects, including downstream decisions made using AI outputs.

A model output that appears harmless in isolation may create significant risk when incorporated into an automated workflow or relied upon by a human decision-maker.

### 15. AI Risk Inherent Assessment

Inherent risk represents the level of risk before accounting for the effect of the controls being evaluated.

It provides a baseline for understanding the exposure associated with an AI system or use case.

The assessment should consider the nature of the scenario, the likelihood of occurrence, the potential severity of consequences, and the context in which the system operates.

Relevant factors may include:

- Sensitivity of processed data.
- Number and characteristics of affected stakeholders.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Access to external tools or business systems.
- Potential scale and reversibility of harm.
- Criticality of the business process.
- Exposure to untrusted inputs.
- Dependence on third-party services.
- Difficulty of detecting failures.
- Potential for cascading or systemic effects.

The organization should document its scoring assumptions and avoid treating inherent risk as a purely mathematical exercise.

Inherent risk does not imply that controls are absent in the actual environment. It is an assessment baseline used to distinguish the underlying exposure from the risk remaining after relevant controls are considered.

### 16. AI Risk Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood represents the assessed probability or frequency of a defined risk scenario occurring within a specified context and period.

The organization should establish a consistent likelihood scale and define the evidence expected to support each rating.

An illustrative five-level scale is:

| Rating | Level          | Illustrative interpretation                                              |
| ------ | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1      | Rare           | Highly unusual under the defined operating conditions                    |
| 2      | Unlikely       | Possible but not expected in normal circumstances                        |
| 3      | Possible       | Plausible and may occur under relevant conditions                        |
| 4      | Likely         | Expected to occur in a meaningful proportion of comparable circumstances |
| 5      | Almost certain | Expected to occur frequently or under routine conditions                 |

These descriptions are illustrative and should be calibrated to the organization's risk methodology.

AI likelihood assessment should account for adversarial behavior, model variability, environmental change, operational exposure, and the availability of relevant evidence.

Where quantitative data is unavailable, expert judgment may be necessary. The register should record the assumptions, confidence level, and evidence limitations rather than presenting an uncertain estimate as a precise probability.

Likelihood should be reassessed when the threat environment, system design, deployment conditions, or relevant controls change materially.

### 17. AI Risk Impact Assessment

Impact represents the severity of the consequences if the risk scenario occurs.

The organization should evaluate impact across relevant dimensions rather than relying solely on financial loss.

Possible dimensions include:

- Financial.
- Operational.
- Legal and regulatory.
- Security and confidentiality.
- Privacy.
- Human rights and fairness.
- Health and physical safety.
- Reputational.
- Strategic.
- Environmental, where material.

An illustrative five-level impact scale is:

| Rating | Level         | Illustrative interpretation                                                            |
| ------ | ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1      | Insignificant | Negligible disruption or harm with limited remediation                                 |
| 2      | Minor         | Limited and manageable consequences                                                    |
| 3      | Moderate      | Material localized consequences requiring coordinated remediation                      |
| 4      | Major         | Significant business, legal, security, or stakeholder consequences                     |
| 5      | Severe        | Potentially catastrophic consequences or serious harm requiring executive intervention |

The organization should define impact thresholds appropriate to its business, sector, and regulatory context.

Where a risk could cause serious harm to an individual or a vulnerable group, the assessment should not allow a low aggregate financial impact to conceal the severity of that harm.

The impact assessment should identify the relevant dimensions and explain the basis for the selected rating.

### 18. AI Risk Scoring Methodology

The organization should define a consistent method for deriving risk ratings from likelihood and impact.

A simple illustrative model multiplies the two ratings:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

Using five-point scales, the score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Score | Illustrative rating |
| ----- | ------------------- |
| 1–4   | Low                 |
| 5–9   | Moderate            |
| 10–16 | High                |
| 17–25 | Critical            |

These thresholds are examples, not universal standards. The organization should validate the scoring model against its risk appetite, sector requirements, and materiality thresholds.

A multiplication model can create false precision and may not reflect every type of harm. Some risks may require mandatory escalation regardless of the numerical score, particularly where legal requirements, serious safety implications, or potentially unacceptable impacts apply.

The organization should document how qualitative judgments, mandatory escalation rules, and multi-dimensional impacts affect the final rating.

The scoring method must be applied consistently and should not allow risk owners to lower a rating merely to obtain approval.

### 19. AI Risk Rating Matrix

A risk matrix provides a visual mechanism for comparing likelihood and impact.

An illustrative five-by-five matrix is shown below.

| Likelihood / Impact | 1 — Insignificant | 2 — Minor | 3 — Moderate | 4 — Major | 5 — Severe |
| ------------------- | ----------------- | --------- | ------------ | --------- | ---------- |
| 5 — Almost certain  | Moderate          | High      | High         | Critical  | Critical   |
| 4 — Likely          | Moderate          | Moderate  | High         | High      | Critical   |
| 3 — Possible        | Low               | Moderate  | Moderate     | High      | High       |
| 2 — Unlikely        | Low               | Low       | Moderate     | Moderate  | High       |
| 1 — Rare            | Low               | Low       | Low          | Moderate  | Moderate   |

The matrix is an illustrative governance aid and is not mathematically identical to every possible score-band methodology. The organization should ensure that the matrix, score thresholds, and escalation rules are internally consistent.

Risk matrices should support judgment rather than replace it. Reviewers should consider the underlying scenario, evidence quality, affected stakeholders, and any mandatory treatment requirements.

Where uncertainty is high or consequences could be severe, the organization may need conservative assumptions or additional assessment before authorizing the relevant use.

### 20. AI Risk Control Environment

The register should document the controls that currently mitigate each risk.

Controls may be preventive, detective, corrective, or compensating.

Examples include:

| Control type | AI-related example                              |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Preventive   | Least-privilege permissions for an AI agent     |
| Preventive   | Input validation and restricted tool access     |
| Detective    | Monitoring for unusual model or API activity    |
| Detective    | Evaluation for model drift and output anomalies |
| Corrective   | Incident response and credential revocation     |
| Corrective   | Model rollback or deployment suspension         |
| Compensating | Human approval before consequential actions     |

The register should distinguish between a control that is planned, a control that has been implemented, and a control that has been tested and shown to operate effectively.

A policy statement or technical feature does not automatically demonstrate control effectiveness.

Where a control depends on another system, supplier, or manual process, the risk record should identify the dependency and any associated limitations.

### 21. AI Risk Residual Assessment

Residual risk is the risk remaining after the effect of relevant existing controls and other risk treatments has been considered.

The residual assessment should evaluate whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

A residual risk rating should not be calculated by mechanically subtracting a fixed number of points from the inherent score unless the organization's methodology explicitly supports and validates that approach.

Instead, the assessor should reconsider the likelihood and impact of the scenario in light of the evidence available about the control environment.

The assessment should record:

- Existing controls considered.
- Control effectiveness evidence.
- Material control deficiencies.
- Remaining vulnerabilities and uncertainties.
- Revised likelihood and impact.
- Residual risk rating.
- Confidence in the assessment.
- Required further treatment.

A risk may remain high even when several controls are present, particularly when the potential impact is severe or the controls are difficult to validate.

Residual risk should reflect the actual operating environment, not the controls the organization intends to implement in the future.

### 22. AI Risk Treatment Options

The organization should define acceptable treatment options for identified AI risks.

Common options include:

| Treatment         | Meaning                                                         | AI example                                                    |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid             | Stop or do not initiate the activity creating the risk          | Prohibit an AI use case that cannot be operated safely        |
| Mitigate          | Reduce likelihood or impact through controls                    | Restrict data access and add independent output validation    |
| Transfer or share | Allocate or share certain financial or contractual consequences | Use contractual indemnities or appropriate insurance          |
| Accept            | Retain the residual risk through an authorized decision         | Approve a documented residual risk within delegated authority |

Risk transfer does not eliminate the underlying operational, ethical, legal, or stakeholder consequences of an AI failure.

Similarly, risk acceptance does not override mandatory legal obligations, prohibited practices, or non-negotiable internal requirements.

The selected treatment should address the actual causes and consequences of the scenario and be supported by an accountable owner, implementation plan, and verification criteria.

### 23. AI Risk Treatment Plan

Every risk requiring additional mitigation should have a treatment plan that defines the actions needed to reduce exposure.

The plan should include:

- Treatment action identifier.
- Linked risk identifier.
- Description of the proposed control or change.
- Accountable action owner.
- Supporting implementation team.
- Priority.
- Target completion date.
- Required resources.
- Dependencies.
- Expected risk reduction.
- Verification method.
- Evidence requirements.
- Current status.

Treatment plans should be specific and measurable.

For example, "improve AI security" is not an adequate action. A more useful action would specify the implementation of authorization checks for retrieval requests, testing against unauthorized document access, and verification that the control prevents disclosure across defined test scenarios.

The organization should track delays and unresolved dependencies, particularly where they affect high or critical residual risks.

Completion of a treatment action should trigger validation of the control and reassessment of the linked risk where appropriate.

### 24. AI Risk Acceptance and Approval Authority

The organization must define who may accept residual AI risks and under what conditions.

Acceptance authority should be proportionate to risk severity, business impact, regulatory constraints, and the organization's risk appetite.

An illustrative delegation model is:

| Risk level | Illustrative approval authority                                                                                |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low        | Designated operational risk owner                                                                              |
| Moderate   | Business owner with relevant risk oversight                                                                    |
| High       | Senior management or delegated risk committee                                                                  |
| Critical   | Executive-level authority or formal governance body, where acceptance is legally and operationally permissible |

The organization must define its own approval thresholds and ensure they align with its enterprise risk management framework.

A risk acceptance record should document the residual risk, supporting rationale, evidence reviewed, approval authority, conditions, expiry or review date, and circumstances requiring reconsideration.

Acceptance should not be used to bypass mandatory controls or authorize conduct prohibited by law or policy.

If a risk exceeds the organization's appetite or cannot lawfully be accepted, the organization must pursue further treatment, restrict the activity, or discontinue the use case as appropriate.

### 25. AI Risk Monitoring and Review

The organization should define how identified risks are monitored and when they must be reassessed.

Review frequency should reflect the risk level, system volatility, model update frequency, operational criticality, and exposure to external threats.

Monitoring may use:

- Security alerts.
- Model performance metrics.
- Drift indicators.
- Data quality measures.
- Incident and near-miss reports.
- Control testing results.
- Vendor change notifications.
- Audit findings.
- Stakeholder complaints.
- Regulatory developments.
- Changes in intended purpose or deployment.

The register should identify monitoring owners, review dates, relevant indicators, and escalation thresholds.

A scheduled review should not be the only trigger for reassessment. Material changes or incidents may require immediate evaluation.

The organization should retain evidence that reviews occurred and document decisions, revised ratings, and resulting treatment actions.

### 26. AI Risk Register Change Triggers

The organization should define events that trigger the creation, reassessment, or closure of risk records.

Typical triggers include:

- Introduction of a new AI system or use case.
- Model or provider changes.
- Changes to training or inference data.
- Expansion of system permissions.
- Introduction of new tools or integrations.
- Changes in autonomy or human oversight.
- Material performance degradation.
- Security incidents or near misses.
- New vulnerabilities or threat intelligence.
- Changes in regulatory applicability.
- Material audit findings.
- Changes in business purpose.
- Changes to affected stakeholder groups.
- Retirement or decommissioning.

The change management process should identify which events require updates to the risk register and which require a formal reassessment.

Risk records should preserve the history of material changes so that reviewers can understand why ratings and treatment decisions evolved over time.

### 27. AI Risk Register Integration with Enterprise Risk Management

The AI Risk Register should connect with the organization's enterprise risk management (ERM) framework.

AI-related risks may affect broader enterprise risk categories, including strategic, operational, financial, legal, compliance, technology, and reputational risk.

The integration should establish:

- Common risk terminology where practical.
- Consistent risk scoring or documented translation rules.
- Clear escalation thresholds.
- Shared reporting requirements.
- Ownership and accountability relationships.
- Links to enterprise-level risk statements.
- Aggregation rules for systemic AI exposure.
- Governance for overlapping risk records.

For example, multiple AI systems may depend on the same external model provider. A provider outage or compromise could create correlated exposure across several business units.

The organization should avoid double-counting the same underlying risk while ensuring that local system-level consequences remain visible.

AI risk reporting should therefore support both detailed system-level management and consolidated enterprise oversight.

### 28. AI Risk Register Evidence and Auditability

Every material risk assessment and decision should be supported by appropriate evidence.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, threat models, security test results, model evaluations, impact assessments, vendor assurance documentation, monitoring reports, incident records, and formal approvals.

The register should reference evidence rather than requiring every artifact to be stored directly in the risk record.

Evidence management should ensure that records are accessible to authorized reviewers, linked to the correct risk, version-controlled where necessary, and retained according to applicable requirements.

The organization should be able to reconstruct:

- How the risk was identified.
- Which assumptions informed the assessment.
- How likelihood and impact were determined.
- Which controls were considered.
- What treatment was selected.
- Who approved residual risk.
- Whether actions were completed and verified.
- How the risk changed over time.

Auditability is essential because a risk rating without supporting rationale or evidence cannot reliably demonstrate the quality of the organization's risk management decisions.

### 29. AI Risk Register Quality Assurance

The organization should implement quality checks to ensure that risk records are complete, consistent, and useful for decision-making.

Quality assurance should evaluate:

- Whether the risk statement describes a plausible scenario.
- Whether causes, events, and consequences are distinguishable.
- Whether the linked AI system and use case are correct.
- Whether likelihood and impact ratings are supported.
- Whether existing controls are implemented and evidenced.
- Whether residual risk reflects the actual control environment.
- Whether treatment actions are specific and measurable.
- Whether ownership and approval authority are appropriate.
- Whether review dates are current.
- Whether related risks are duplicated or inconsistently rated.

Quality checks may be automated for mandatory fields and classification rules, while substantive risk judgments require qualified review.

The organization should track recurring quality deficiencies and use them to improve the methodology, training, templates, and governance process.

A register should not be considered mature merely because all required fields are populated. The quality and decision usefulness of the content are equally important.

### 30. AI Risk Register Core Implementation Principle

The AI Risk Register provides the structured mechanism through which AI risks become visible, attributable, assessable, treatable, and subject to continuing oversight.

Its value depends on the quality of risk scenarios, the consistency of assessment methodology, the reliability of control evidence, the accountability of risk owners, and the effectiveness of treatment and acceptance decisions.

The organization must distinguish between **risk identification and risk assessment**, **inherent and residual risk**, **planned and effective controls**, **risk treatment and risk acceptance**, and **documented risk decisions and verified risk reduction**.

**Core Principle:** An AI Risk Register is effective only when each material risk is described in a way that supports action, evaluated using a consistent and evidence-based methodology, assigned to an accountable owner, linked to appropriate controls and treatment decisions, and monitored throughout the AI system lifecycle.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Register Controls

### 31. AI Risk Register Control Framework

The AI Risk Register Control Framework establishes the operational controls required to ensure that AI risks are identified, recorded, assessed, treated, monitored, and escalated consistently across the organization.

The framework translates the risk register methodology into enforceable procedures, validation rules, accountability requirements, and assurance activities.

It should define controls across the full risk management lifecycle:

- Risk identification and registration.
- Risk statement quality and classification.
- Inherent risk assessment.
- Existing control validation.
- Residual risk assessment.
- Treatment planning and implementation.
- Risk acceptance and escalation.
- Periodic review and reassessment.
- Evidence retention and auditability.
- Risk closure and archival.

Each control should specify its objective, accountable owner, operating procedure, frequency or trigger, evidence requirements, exception handling, and effectiveness testing method.

The framework must distinguish administrative completeness from substantive risk management. A record containing all mandatory fields may still be ineffective if the risk scenario is poorly defined, control effectiveness is unverified, or the residual risk rating is unsupported.

### 32. Mandatory AI Risk Registration Control

The organization shall require material AI risks to be recorded in the approved AI Risk Register or an authorized integrated risk management platform.

The control shall establish registration requirements for risks identified during AI system development, procurement, assessment, deployment, operation, monitoring, incident investigation, and retirement.

The registration process should capture the minimum information required to identify the risk, establish its context, assign accountability, and determine the next assessment or treatment action.

The control should include:

- Defined registration triggers.
- Required risk record identifiers.
- Minimum mandatory fields.
- Assignment of an accountable risk owner.
- Linkage to the affected AI system or use case.
- Initial risk classification.
- Registration timestamps.
- Validation of duplicate records.
- Escalation of unregistered material risks.

A risk identified during an assessment or incident should not remain only in a report or email when the organization's process requires formal registration.

**Control evidence:** Risk records, registration workflow logs, linked assessment findings, and evidence of registration timeliness.

**Control objective:** Ensure that material AI risks are captured in a controlled repository and cannot be systematically excluded from governance oversight.

### 33. AI Risk Identification Coverage Control

The organization shall maintain a defined process for identifying AI risks across the AI lifecycle.

Risk identification should not be limited to the initial approval stage. New risks may arise from model updates, new data sources, changes in autonomy, evolving threats, supplier changes, incidents, or changes in intended purpose.

The control should require risk identification activities to be performed at defined lifecycle milestones and when material change triggers occur.

Potential identification inputs include:

- AI use-case assessments.
- Security and architecture reviews.
- Privacy and impact assessments.
- Threat modeling.
- Model evaluation and testing.
- Vendor assessments.
- Incident and near-miss investigations.
- Operational monitoring.
- Audit findings.
- Stakeholder complaints.
- Regulatory and contractual reviews.

The organization should maintain evidence that relevant sources have been considered and that identified findings have been evaluated for registration.

**Control evidence:** Assessment records, risk workshops, threat models, monitoring findings, incident reviews, and traceability between findings and risk records.

**Control objective:** Reduce the likelihood that material AI risks remain unidentified because risk identification is performed inconsistently or only at a single point in the lifecycle.

### 34. AI Risk Statement Quality Control

The organization shall validate that risk statements describe specific and actionable risk scenarios.

Each material risk statement should identify a plausible cause or contributing condition, an event that may occur, and the potential consequences for the organization or affected stakeholders.

The control should identify vague or incomplete statements, including descriptions that merely name a category without explaining the scenario.

For example, "AI hallucination risk" should be refined into a scenario that explains how inaccurate output could enter a consequential business process and produce a specific adverse outcome.

Validation should assess whether:

- The cause or contributing condition is identifiable.
- The potential event is clearly described.
- The consequences are sufficiently specific.
- The affected system or use case is identified.
- The statement supports meaningful control selection.
- The scenario is distinguishable from related risks.

Where a risk cannot yet be described precisely, the record should identify the information gap and assign an action to resolve it.

**Control evidence:** Risk quality review results, reviewer comments, corrected risk statements, and quality assurance reports.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risk records support meaningful analysis, prioritization, treatment, and management decisions.

### 35. AI Risk Taxonomy and Classification Control

The organization shall apply a standardized risk taxonomy to AI risk records.

The taxonomy should provide consistent classification across security, privacy, reliability, fairness, transparency, human oversight, operational resilience, third-party exposure, compliance, and other relevant risk domains.

The control should establish:

- Approved primary risk categories.
- Permitted subcategories.
- Classification definitions.
- Assignment responsibilities.
- Rules for multiple applicable categories.
- Review and approval of taxonomy changes.
- Validation of classification consistency.

Automated validation may enforce permitted values, but substantive classification should reflect the actual risk scenario.

Where multiple categories apply, the organization should identify a primary category for reporting and retain secondary classifications where needed.

**Control evidence:** Risk taxonomy, data dictionary, classification validation results, and records of taxonomy changes.

**Control objective:** Enable consistent analysis and aggregation of AI risks across systems, business units, and the enterprise.

### 36. AI Risk Record Uniqueness and Duplicate Management Control

The organization shall implement controls to identify duplicate or substantially overlapping risk records.

Duplicate records can result in fragmented accountability, inconsistent ratings, duplicated treatment work, and misleading reporting.

The control should compare risk identifiers, linked systems, scenarios, causes, consequences, and related assessment findings.

When potential duplicates are identified, the organization should determine whether the records represent:

- The same underlying risk.
- Separate scenarios sharing a common cause.
- A systemic risk affecting multiple systems.
- Distinct risks that require separate owners or treatment plans.

Merging records should preserve historical decisions, relationships, evidence, and references to previous identifiers.

The organization should avoid consolidating materially different risks merely because they share a broad category.

**Control evidence:** Duplicate detection results, consolidation decisions, cross-reference records, and risk register quality reviews.

**Control objective:** Maintain a coherent risk population without obscuring material differences in exposure or accountability.

### 37. AI Risk Ownership Assignment Control

The organization shall assign an accountable risk owner to every material AI risk.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority, business understanding, and access to relevant technical or governance expertise to oversee assessment and treatment.

The control should validate that:

- The owner is identifiable.
- The owner has accepted the assigned responsibility.
- The owner is appropriate for the risk's business context.
- Responsibilities are not confused with control implementation duties.
- Ownership changes are documented.
- Unassigned or disputed risks are escalated.

Where a risk affects multiple business units, the organization should establish a primary accountable owner and document supporting responsibilities.

A technical specialist may provide essential risk analysis without being the person accountable for the business decision to accept or retain the risk.

**Control evidence:** Risk register ownership fields, assignment acknowledgments, responsibility matrices, and escalation records.

**Control objective:** Prevent material AI risks from remaining unmanaged because accountability is unclear or assigned to an inappropriate role.

### 38. AI Risk Assessment Methodology Control

The organization shall maintain an approved methodology for assessing AI risks.

The methodology should define the assessment process, rating scales, evidence expectations, scoring rules, uncertainty treatment, escalation thresholds, and review requirements.

The control should ensure consistent application of the methodology across relevant business units and AI system categories.

It should also specify when specialized assessment is necessary, such as for high-impact decisions, sensitive personal data, autonomous actions, or critical operational dependencies.

Methodology changes should be reviewed and approved through a controlled process.

The organization should test whether assessors apply the methodology consistently and whether risk ratings remain meaningful across different AI use cases.

**Control evidence:** Approved methodology, assessment procedures, assessor guidance, calibration exercises, and quality review results.

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI risk assessments are consistent, explainable, and sufficiently evidence-based to support governance decisions.

### 39. AI Inherent Risk Assessment Control

The organization shall document inherent risk before considering the mitigating effect of the controls being evaluated.

The control should require the assessor to evaluate the underlying exposure associated with the risk scenario, including its likelihood, impact, system context, and affected stakeholders.

Relevant considerations may include:

- Data sensitivity.
- System autonomy.
- Potential scale of harm.
- Business process criticality.
- External exposure.
- Access to tools and connected systems.
- Reversibility of outcomes.
- Vulnerability to malicious inputs.
- Potential for cascading consequences.

The assessment should record the assumptions and evidence supporting the rating.

The organization should not allow inherent risk to be reduced simply because controls are planned, because the system has not yet experienced an incident, or because the risk owner considers the scenario unlikely without supporting analysis.

**Control evidence:** Inherent risk assessments, scoring rationale, supporting evidence, and independent review records where required.

**Control objective:** Establish a credible baseline against which control effectiveness and residual risk can be evaluated.

### 40. AI Likelihood Scoring Consistency Control

The organization shall apply approved likelihood criteria consistently when evaluating AI risks.

The control should require assessors to use the defined rating scale and document the evidence or reasoning supporting the selected level.

Likelihood assessments should consider the relevant operating environment, threat conditions, exposure, system behavior, and available historical or testing evidence.

Where reliable quantitative data is unavailable, qualitative judgment may be used, but material assumptions and uncertainty should be recorded.

The control should identify inconsistent ratings for comparable scenarios and require review when the difference cannot be justified.

Likelihood should be reassessed following material changes in the system, threat environment, or relevant controls.

**Control evidence:** Likelihood ratings, assessment rationale, review comments, calibration results, and reassessment records.

**Control objective:** Reduce arbitrary or inconsistent likelihood scoring and improve the comparability of AI risk assessments.

### 41. AI Impact Scoring Consistency Control

The organization shall assess AI risk impact using defined criteria appropriate to its business and stakeholder environment.

The control should require consideration of material impact dimensions, which may include financial, operational, legal, privacy, security, fairness, safety, reputational, and strategic consequences.

The selected impact rating should reflect the plausible consequences of the scenario rather than only the most convenient or easily quantified outcome.

Where a risk affects several dimensions, the organization should document the material dimensions and apply its approved aggregation rules.

Potentially severe harm to individuals or affected groups should not be obscured by low expected financial cost.

The control should require escalation where mandatory thresholds or non-negotiable risk criteria apply, regardless of the overall numerical score.

**Control evidence:** Impact assessment rationale, affected-stakeholder analysis, scoring records, and review or escalation decisions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI risk impact ratings reflect the material consequences of adverse outcomes.

### 42. AI Risk Matrix and Threshold Validation Control

The organization shall maintain a controlled risk matrix and associated rating thresholds.

The control should ensure that the matrix, score bands, risk labels, and escalation rules are internally consistent.

It should also define how the methodology addresses scenarios where numerical scoring does not adequately represent the severity or nature of potential harm.

The matrix should be reviewed when the organization's risk appetite, operating environment, or relevant obligations change.

Testing should identify inconsistencies between calculated scores and assigned risk levels, as well as inappropriate overrides of the approved methodology.

Where risk ratings are changed through professional judgment or mandatory escalation rules, the rationale should be recorded.

**Control evidence:** Approved matrix, methodology version, scoring validation tests, and documented overrides.

**Control objective:** Maintain consistent risk classification and prevent inappropriate rating manipulation.

### 43. AI Risk Uncertainty and Confidence Control

The organization shall document material uncertainty affecting AI risk assessments.

Uncertainty may arise from limited provider transparency, incomplete training data information, unavailable technical evidence, unknown attack paths, limited operational history, or insufficient information about affected stakeholders.

The control should require assessors to distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions and estimates.

Where uncertainty materially affects the assessment, the organization should consider:

- Additional evidence collection.
- Independent technical review.
- Conservative assessment assumptions.
- Restricted deployment or functionality.
- Additional monitoring.
- Defined reassessment triggers.
- Escalation to an authorized decision-maker.

A low-confidence assessment should not automatically be interpreted as a low-risk result.

**Control evidence:** Assessment assumptions, confidence ratings where used, documented information gaps, and decisions addressing uncertainty.

**Control objective:** Ensure that material unknowns are visible and appropriately managed rather than concealed by apparently precise risk scores.

### 44. AI Control Mapping and Risk Linkage Control

The organization shall link material risks to the controls intended to prevent, detect, or mitigate their occurrence or consequences.

Control mapping should identify the relevant control, its owner, implementation status, operating frequency, evidence source, and relationship to the risk scenario.

The control should distinguish between controls that are planned, implemented, tested, and verified as effective.

Where a control addresses only part of a risk scenario, the record should identify the remaining exposure.

For example, content filtering may reduce certain harmful outputs but may not address unauthorized retrieval access, excessive agent permissions, or inappropriate downstream use.

The organization should also identify common controls that support multiple risk records and shared dependencies that could create correlated control failure.

**Control evidence:** Risk-to-control mappings, control inventory references, implementation records, and effectiveness testing results.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risk treatment decisions are based on identifiable and relevant controls rather than unsupported claims that a risk is mitigated.

### 45. AI Control Effectiveness Evidence Control

The organization shall require evidence to support material claims about control effectiveness.

Evidence may include security testing, access reviews, model evaluations, monitoring results, configuration records, supplier assurance documentation, incident testing, and operational performance data.

The control should distinguish between design effectiveness and operating effectiveness.

Design effectiveness evaluates whether the control, if implemented as intended, is capable of addressing the risk. Operating effectiveness evaluates whether the control has been implemented and functions consistently in practice.

A control described in a policy or vendor datasheet should not automatically be treated as effective.

Evidence should be relevant to the specific system, deployment, model, and operating context being assessed.

Where evidence is unavailable or outdated, the organization should record the limitation and determine whether additional testing or a more conservative residual risk assessment is necessary.

**Control evidence:** Test reports, monitoring records, control attestations, configuration evidence, and independent validation results.

**Control objective:** Prevent unsupported control claims from artificially reducing assessed residual risk.

### 46. AI Residual Risk Reassessment Control

The organization shall reassess residual risk after considering the actual effectiveness of relevant existing controls.

The control should require the assessor to evaluate whether the controls materially reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk scenario.

Residual ratings should be supported by the control evidence available at the time of assessment.

The process should identify:

- Existing controls considered.
- Control deficiencies.
- Remaining vulnerabilities.
- Unresolved dependencies.
- Residual likelihood and impact.
- Overall residual risk rating.
- Material assessment uncertainty.
- Required further treatment.

Planned controls should not be credited as implemented controls unless the organization's methodology explicitly distinguishes forecast risk from current residual risk.

Where control effectiveness cannot be established, the organization should not assume that the intended mitigation has already reduced exposure.

**Control evidence:** Residual risk assessments, control test results, assessment approvals, and documented treatment decisions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that residual risk reflects the actual operating control environment rather than intended future improvements.

### 47. AI Risk Appetite and Tolerance Alignment Control

The organization shall evaluate AI risks against approved risk appetite and tolerance criteria.

Risk appetite defines the nature and level of risk the organization is willing to pursue or retain in support of its objectives. Risk tolerance establishes more specific boundaries for acceptable variation or exposure.

The control should identify risks that exceed applicable thresholds and trigger the required escalation or treatment process.

Risk appetite considerations may include:

- Maximum acceptable operational disruption.
- Restrictions on sensitive data exposure.
- Limits on autonomous execution.
- Acceptable error rates for defined use cases.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Mandatory legal and regulatory constraints.
- Restrictions on consequential decisions.
- Minimum control effectiveness requirements.

Risk appetite cannot override legal prohibitions or mandatory obligations.

The organization should document the basis for determining whether a risk is within appetite and require reassessment when the system or operating context changes.

**Control evidence:** Approved risk appetite statements, tolerance criteria, assessment records, and escalation decisions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI risk decisions remain aligned with formally approved enterprise boundaries.

### 48. AI Risk Treatment Decision Control

The organization shall document and approve the selected treatment for each material risk requiring a management decision.

The treatment decision should explain why the selected option is appropriate in light of the risk scenario, residual rating, available controls, and applicable obligations.

Possible treatments include avoiding the activity, mitigating the risk, transferring or sharing certain consequences, or accepting the residual risk through an authorized process.

The control should require:

- A documented treatment decision.
- An accountable decision owner.
- A rationale linked to the assessed risk.
- Identification of required actions.
- Defined completion criteria.
- Appropriate approval.
- Tracking of unresolved dependencies.
- Reassessment following implementation.

Transfer arrangements and insurance should not be treated as eliminating the underlying operational or stakeholder harm that may result from an AI failure.

**Control evidence:** Treatment decisions, approval records, assigned actions, and implementation plans.

**Control objective:** Ensure that material AI risks receive explicit and accountable treatment decisions.

### 49. AI Risk Treatment Plan Quality Control

The organization shall validate that risk treatment plans contain specific, measurable, and achievable actions.

The control should reject vague actions that do not identify the required change or demonstrate how risk reduction will be verified.

Each material action should define:

- The control or change to be implemented.
- The linked risk.
- An accountable owner.
- Required supporting resources.
- Dependencies.
- A target date.
- Expected risk reduction.
- Completion criteria.
- Verification evidence.
- Current implementation status.

The organization should review whether the planned action addresses the underlying cause or merely reduces a visible symptom.

For example, adding an alert may improve detection but may not prevent unauthorized AI agent execution. The treatment plan should identify whether additional preventive controls or authorization gates are necessary.

**Control evidence:** Treatment plans, action quality reviews, milestone records, and documented verification criteria.

**Control objective:** Ensure that treatment plans produce meaningful and verifiable reductions in AI risk.

### 50. AI Risk Remediation Deadline Control

The organization shall assign target completion dates to material risk treatment actions.

Deadlines should reflect the severity of the residual risk, the complexity of the required mitigation, applicable obligations, and the consequences of continued exposure.

The control should establish escalation thresholds for overdue actions and require documented decisions when deadlines cannot be met.

High or critical risks should receive enhanced oversight, particularly when the delay leaves a system exposed to significant security, safety, privacy, or operational consequences.

The organization should distinguish between a legitimate dependency-driven delay and an action that has stalled because of insufficient ownership or resources.

Repeated deadline extensions should trigger review of the treatment strategy and the acceptability of the remaining exposure.

**Control evidence:** Treatment tracking reports, overdue action alerts, extension approvals, and remediation escalation records.

**Control objective:** Prevent material AI risk remediation from remaining indefinitely incomplete without accountable management decisions.

### 51. AI Risk Acceptance Authority Control

The organization shall maintain a documented delegation of authority for accepting residual AI risks.

The delegation should define approval levels, prohibited acceptance conditions, required evidence, and escalation requirements.

The control should verify that the approving person has the authority and competence required for the relevant risk level.

Acceptance decisions should document:

- The risk and affected system.
- Residual risk rating.
- Supporting assessment and control evidence.
- Rationale for acceptance.
- Applicable restrictions and conditions.
- Approval authority.
- Decision date.
- Review or expiry date.
- Circumstances requiring reconsideration.

Acceptance must not be used to bypass mandatory legal requirements, prohibited practices, or non-negotiable organizational controls.

Where a risk exceeds the authorized threshold, the decision should be escalated or the activity restricted rather than accepted by an unauthorized owner.

**Control evidence:** Delegation matrix, signed or recorded approval decisions, acceptance conditions, and periodic review records.

**Control objective:** Ensure that residual AI risks are accepted only through an authorized, documented, and appropriately governed process.

### 52. AI Risk Acceptance Expiry and Renewal Control

The organization shall define how accepted AI risks are reviewed, renewed, or closed.

Risk acceptance should not be treated as permanent when the underlying system, threat environment, controls, or business purpose may change.

The control should establish review dates or expiry conditions proportionate to the risk.

Before renewal, the responsible owner should confirm that:

- The risk remains accurately described.
- The residual rating remains valid.
- Existing controls remain effective.
- Treatment actions have been evaluated.
- Relevant system changes have been considered.
- Applicable obligations remain satisfied.
- The acceptance authority remains appropriate.

Renewal should require a documented decision rather than an automatic extension.

Material changes or incidents should trigger reassessment before the scheduled review date.

**Control evidence:** Acceptance records, renewal decisions, review reminders, and reassessment results.

**Control objective:** Prevent stale risk acceptance decisions from authorizing exposure that has materially changed or is no longer acceptable.

### 53. AI Risk Exception Management Control

The organization shall maintain a formal process for exceptions to risk management requirements or established controls.

An exception may be necessary when a specific requirement cannot be met immediately, but it must identify the resulting exposure and the conditions under which the deviation is permitted.

The exception record should include:

- The affected risk and system.
- The requirement or control being waived or modified.
- The reason for the exception.
- The residual exposure.
- Compensating measures.
- The authorized approver.
- Effective and expiry dates.
- Remediation owner.
- Required monitoring.
- Conditions for early revocation.

The organization should distinguish between an exception to an internal procedure and a risk acceptance decision. They may be related, but they are not interchangeable.

An exception cannot lawfully authorize conduct that is prohibited by applicable law or override mandatory requirements.

**Control evidence:** Exception register, approval records, compensating control evidence, expiry monitoring, and closure verification.

**Control objective:** Ensure that deviations from established requirements remain visible, time-bound, accountable, and subject to review.

### 54. AI Risk Review Frequency Control

The organization shall establish review frequencies for AI risk records based on risk severity, system volatility, and operating context.

High-risk and critical scenarios may require more frequent review than stable, low-risk use cases.

The control should also establish event-driven review triggers, including:

- Model or provider changes.
- Material configuration changes.
- New data sources.
- Expanded permissions.
- Security incidents.
- Control failures.
- Material performance degradation.
- Changes in intended purpose.
- New legal or regulatory obligations.
- Significant stakeholder complaints.

The review should verify the risk statement, rating, control status, treatment actions, ownership, and acceptance conditions.

A review should result in a recorded conclusion, even when the risk rating remains unchanged.

**Control evidence:** Review schedules, completed review records, reassessment history, and overdue review reports.

**Control objective:** Keep risk assessments current as AI systems, operating conditions, and external threats evolve.

### 55. AI Risk Register Change History Control

The organization shall preserve an auditable history of material changes to risk records.

The history should identify what changed, when it changed, who made the change, and the reason for the change.

Material changes include:

- Risk statement revisions.
- Ownership changes.
- Changes in likelihood or impact.
- Inherent or residual rating changes.
- Control mapping changes.
- Treatment plan updates.
- Acceptance decisions.
- Review status changes.
- Closure or reopening.
- Links to incidents or new evidence.

The organization should protect change history against unauthorized modification or deletion.

Historical records should remain interpretable even when the taxonomy, scoring methodology, or inventory schema changes.

**Control evidence:** Version history, audit logs, approval records, and historical risk snapshots where required.

**Control objective:** Enable reconstruction of material risk decisions and provide reliable evidence for audit, investigation, and governance oversight.

### 56. AI Risk Register Access Control

The organization shall restrict access to risk records according to business need and least-privilege principles.

Risk records may contain sensitive information about vulnerabilities, attack paths, supplier weaknesses, business exposures, and unresolved control deficiencies.

The access model should distinguish permissions to view, create, edit, validate, approve, accept, export, and administer records.

The control should address:

- Role-based permissions.
- Privileged access management.
- Segregation of duties.
- Access reviews.
- Secure exports.
- Protection of sensitive attachments.
- Audit logging.
- Access revocation when responsibilities change.

Users should not be able to approve their own risk acceptance where segregation of duties is required by the governance framework.

**Control evidence:** Access control configuration, access review results, privileged activity logs, and segregation-of-duties testing.

**Control objective:** Protect risk information and prevent unauthorized alteration or approval of material risk decisions.

### 57. AI Risk Register Data Quality Control

The organization shall implement data quality checks to ensure that risk records remain complete, consistent, accurate, and usable.

The control should validate mandatory fields, approved classifications, identifier uniqueness, valid relationships, current ownership, review dates, and required approval references.

Data quality review should also assess substantive issues, including whether the risk scenario remains plausible and whether the assigned rating is supported by evidence.

The organization should identify:

- Missing mandatory information.
- Invalid or inconsistent ratings.
- Unassigned owners.
- Stale assessment records.
- Broken system or control links.
- Duplicate risks.
- Contradictory treatment statuses.
- Expired acceptance records.
- Unsupported closure decisions.

Data quality deficiencies should be assigned to accountable owners and tracked through remediation.

**Control evidence:** Automated validation results, data quality dashboards, sampling results, and remediation records.

**Control objective:** Maintain a reliable risk register that supports decisions and reporting without concealing material deficiencies.

### 58. AI Risk Register Integration Control

The organization shall integrate the AI Risk Register with relevant governance and operational systems where appropriate.

Integration should reduce inconsistent data, improve traceability, and enable risk information to flow into relevant decision processes.

Potential integrations include:

- AI System Inventory.
- GRC platform.
- Security assessment tooling.
- Privacy and impact assessment repositories.
- Vendor risk management.
- Incident management.
- Change management.
- Enterprise risk management.
- Control libraries.
- Audit and remediation tracking.
- Model monitoring platforms.

The control should define authoritative sources, synchronization rules, failure handling, access restrictions, and reconciliation procedures.

Where an integration fails, the organization should identify the affected records and prevent stale or incomplete information from being represented as current and verified.

**Control evidence:** Integration specifications, synchronization logs, reconciliation reports, and integration failure records.

**Control objective:** Maintain consistent and traceable risk information across the systems that support AI governance.

### 59. AI Risk Register Incident Linkage Control

The organization shall link relevant incidents and near misses to the affected risk records.

An incident may reveal a previously unidentified risk, demonstrate that a control is ineffective, or invalidate assumptions used in a previous assessment.

The control should require incident review to determine whether:

- A new risk record is necessary.
- An existing risk statement requires revision.
- Likelihood or impact ratings must change.
- Control effectiveness must be reassessed.
- Treatment actions are required.
- Risk acceptance must be reconsidered.
- Similar systems may be exposed.
- Enterprise-level escalation is necessary.

Incident closure should not automatically close the linked risk.

The organization should verify that the underlying risk has been treated or that a documented, authorized decision supports the remaining exposure.

**Control evidence:** Incident-to-risk mappings, post-incident reviews, updated assessments, and resulting treatment actions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that operational experience informs risk management and prevents recurring AI incidents from being treated as isolated events.

### 60. AI Risk Register Audit and Assurance Control

The organization shall establish assurance activities to evaluate the design and operating effectiveness of AI risk register controls.

Assurance procedures should assess both the completeness of risk records and the quality of the decisions they support.

Testing may include:

- Sampling risk statements for quality.
- Verifying scoring methodology compliance.
- Testing evidence supporting control effectiveness.
- Reviewing risk owner assignments.
- Checking treatment action completion.
- Verifying risk acceptance authority.
- Reviewing expired exceptions.
- Testing reassessment triggers.
- Confirming incident linkage.
- Evaluating register data quality.
- Reconciling risks against independent assessment sources.

Assurance findings should be classified according to their severity and potential consequences.

The organization should track findings to closure and verify remediation where appropriate.

**Control evidence:** Control testing plans, sample results, audit reports, findings registers, and remediation verification.

**Control objective:** Provide independent evidence that the AI risk management process is operating as intended.

### 61. AI Risk Register Reporting Control

The organization shall produce risk reporting appropriate to operational, governance, and executive decision-making.

Reporting should identify material risk exposure, treatment progress, overdue actions, acceptance decisions, recurring weaknesses, and changes in the overall risk profile.

Reports may include:

- Risk distribution by rating.
- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks exceeding risk appetite.
- Overdue treatment actions.
- Expired or approaching-expiry acceptances.
- Risks without accountable owners.
- Control effectiveness deficiencies.
- Risk trends by category and business unit.
- Common-provider and shared-dependency exposure.
- New risks identified through incidents or monitoring.

The control should define reporting frequency, accountable report owners, escalation thresholds, and data validation requirements.

Reports should distinguish between inherent and residual risk and identify material uncertainties or coverage limitations.

**Control evidence:** Published reports, validation records, committee materials, escalation logs, and management decisions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that material AI risk information reaches the appropriate decision-makers in a timely and reliable manner.

### 62. AI Risk Escalation Control

The organization shall establish escalation criteria for risks that require management intervention.

Escalation may be necessary when a risk exceeds appetite, has potentially severe consequences, lacks an accountable owner, remains untreated beyond its deadline, or cannot be adequately assessed because of material uncertainty.

The escalation process should define:

- Trigger conditions.
- Responsible escalation owner.
- Required decision authority.
- Notification timelines.
- Interim risk measures.
- Required evidence.
- Decision recording.
- Follow-up monitoring.

Where an AI system presents a material and immediate threat, escalation may require restricting functionality, suspending deployment, revoking permissions, or discontinuing the use case, depending on the circumstances and authorized procedures.

The organization should not wait for the next scheduled committee meeting when urgent action is required.

**Control evidence:** Escalation records, management decisions, interim safeguards, and follow-up actions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risks requiring higher-level decisions receive timely attention and are not retained through organizational inaction.

### 63. AI Risk Closure and Reopening Control

The organization shall define the conditions under which risk records may be closed or reopened.

Closure may be appropriate when the risk scenario no longer applies, the system has been retired and relevant exposure has been resolved, or the organization's approved methodology supports closure based on verified treatment.

Closure should not occur solely because an action has been marked complete or a system has been removed from active use.

The control should require:

- Documented closure rationale.
- Verification of relevant treatment actions.
- Confirmation that residual exposure has been addressed or appropriately retained.
- Review of dependencies and related systems.
- Required approval.
- Preservation of historical evidence.

A closed risk should be reopened when new evidence, system changes, incidents, or control failures show that the scenario remains relevant.

**Control evidence:** Closure records, verification results, approval decisions, and reopening history.

**Control objective:** Prevent premature closure of material AI risks and preserve the ability to respond when previously resolved scenarios reappear.

### 64. AI Risk Register Retention Control

The organization shall maintain risk records and supporting evidence according to applicable retention, legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements.

The control should define retention periods for active and closed risks, acceptance decisions, treatment evidence, review history, and related assessment records.

It should address:

- Record classification.
- Retention schedules.
- Legal holds.
- Secure archival.
- Authorized deletion.
- Access to historical records.
- Preservation of linked evidence.
- Consistency between the risk register and source repositories.

The organization should ensure that changes to the risk taxonomy or assessment methodology do not make historical records unintelligible.

Retention should be proportionate to governance and evidentiary requirements and should not result in unnecessary indefinite storage of sensitive information.

**Control evidence:** Retention standards, archival records, deletion approvals, and retention compliance reviews.

**Control objective:** Preserve the records required to demonstrate risk management decisions while managing information lifecycle obligations appropriately.

### 65. AI Risk Register Automation Control

The organization may automate risk register activities to improve consistency, timeliness, and traceability.

Automation opportunities include mandatory-field validation, duplicate detection, review reminders, treatment deadline tracking, risk score calculation, approval routing, and integration with incident or change management workflows.

The control should require automated workflows to be tested and monitored for reliability.

Automation must not replace required professional judgment or authorized decisions about material risk acceptance.

Where AI itself is used to classify, summarize, or recommend risk ratings, the organization should evaluate the reliability, limitations, security, and potential bias of that capability.

The organization should preserve a record of material automated recommendations and human decisions where needed for accountability.

**Control evidence:** Workflow configuration, test results, exception logs, automation monitoring, and approval histories.

**Control objective:** Use automation to strengthen risk management controls without introducing opaque decisions or uncontrolled changes.

### 66. AI Risk Register Control Exception Monitoring

The organization shall monitor exceptions and deviations affecting the operation of risk register controls.

Examples include missing risk owners, overdue reassessments, incomplete control evidence, unauthorized rating overrides, expired acceptances, and unresolved treatment actions.

The control should define thresholds for escalation and distinguish isolated administrative errors from recurring or systemic failures.

Repeated exceptions may indicate that the methodology is poorly designed, resources are insufficient, or risk ownership is not functioning as intended.

The organization should track:

- Exception type.
- Affected records.
- Duration.
- Risk severity.
- Responsible owner.
- Compensating measures.
- Remediation status.
- Recurrence.
- Closure evidence.

**Control evidence:** Exception monitoring reports, trend analysis, remediation records, and escalation decisions.

**Control objective:** Detect and address control failures before they undermine the integrity of AI risk management.

### 67. AI Risk Register Control Performance Metrics

The organization shall define metrics to assess the performance and effectiveness of risk register controls.

Potential metrics include:

| Metric                  | Purpose                                                                      |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ownership coverage | Measures the proportion of material risks with accountable owners            |
| Assessment completeness | Measures the proportion of records meeting assessment requirements           |
| Evidence coverage       | Measures whether material ratings and control claims are supported           |
| Treatment timeliness    | Measures completion of actions within approved deadlines                     |
| Reassessment timeliness | Measures whether reviews occur when required                                 |
| Acceptance validity     | Measures the proportion of accepted risks with current, authorized decisions |
| Closure quality         | Measures whether closures contain required verification evidence             |
| Duplicate risk rate     | Indicates weaknesses in risk identification or record management             |
| Escalation timeliness   | Measures how promptly threshold breaches receive attention                   |
| Recurring risk rate     | Indicates whether similar scenarios continue to emerge or remain unresolved  |

Each metric should have a documented definition, calculation method, data source, reporting frequency, owner, and target where appropriate.

Targets should reflect the organization's risk profile and operating model. A high completion rate does not necessarily indicate effective risk reduction if the underlying assessments are weak.

**Control evidence:** Metric definitions, dashboards, source validation, and management review records.

**Control objective:** Provide measurable evidence of risk register performance and highlight areas requiring improvement.

### 68. AI Risk Register Control Deficiency Management

The organization shall establish a process for identifying, prioritizing, assigning, and remediating deficiencies in the risk register control framework.

Deficiencies may include inconsistent assessment methods, unsupported ratings, ineffective approval workflows, missing evidence, unreliable reporting, and failures to capture material risks.

The process should assess each deficiency according to its impact on risk visibility, decision quality, compliance, and control effectiveness.

Required activities include:

- Recording the deficiency.
- Identifying affected controls and risk records.
- Determining the root cause.
- Assessing the resulting exposure.
- Assigning an accountable remediation owner.
- Defining corrective actions and deadlines.
- Escalating material deficiencies.
- Verifying remediation.
- Updating procedures or training where necessary.

A deficiency in the risk register process may affect multiple AI systems even when no individual system has experienced an incident.

**Control evidence:** Deficiency register, root-cause analysis, corrective action plans, verification results, and management reporting.

**Control objective:** Ensure that weaknesses in the risk management process are addressed systematically and do not persist across the AI portfolio.

### 69. AI Risk Register Control Testing and Sampling

The organization shall perform risk-based testing of the AI Risk Register and its associated controls.

Testing should cover both record-level requirements and end-to-end risk management workflows.

Sampling should consider risk severity, system criticality, business unit, risk category, recent changes, and prior control deficiencies.

Testing procedures may include:

- Tracing a risk from identification through closure.
- Verifying inherent and residual ratings.
- Confirming control evidence.
- Testing treatment action completion.
- Reviewing acceptance authorization.
- Verifying reassessment triggers.
- Checking record change history.
- Testing escalation procedures.
- Comparing register records with independent evidence.
- Confirming that closed risks meet closure criteria.

High-risk records may require more extensive testing than low-risk records.

The organization should document the sample selection method, test procedures, results, exceptions, and remediation requirements.

**Control evidence:** Test plans, sampling rationale, workpapers, exception logs, and completed remediation records.

**Control objective:** Demonstrate that risk register controls operate consistently and provide reliable information for governance decisions.

### 70. AI Risk Register Integration with the AI System Inventory

The organization shall maintain a traceable relationship between the AI Risk Register and the AI System Inventory.

Each system-level risk should reference the relevant system or use case, allowing reviewers to understand the technical and business context in which the risk arises.

The integration should support:

- Identification of risks associated with each AI system.
- Visibility of unassessed systems.
- Identification of systems with high residual risk.
- Triggering reassessment after material changes.
- Reporting by system, provider, deployment, and business unit.
- Verification that retired systems have appropriate risk closure or archival treatment.

The inventory should not duplicate the full risk assessment. Instead, it should provide links or summaries that direct authorized users to the authoritative risk record.

Changes to system purpose, provider, model, data flows, or autonomy should trigger evaluation of related risk records where relevant.

**Control evidence:** System-to-risk mappings, integration logs, reconciliation results, and change-trigger records.

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI risk information remains connected to the systems and use cases it governs.

### 71. AI Risk Register Integration with Incident and Change Management

The organization shall establish controlled links between risk management, incident management, and change management.

Incidents may reveal new risks or demonstrate control failure. Changes may introduce new risk scenarios or invalidate previous assessments.

The integration should define when incidents and changes trigger risk record creation, reassessment, escalation, or treatment updates.

For example, introducing a new tool to an AI agent may increase its authority to act on external systems and require reassessment of risks related to unauthorized actions.

The organization should avoid treating incident closure or change approval as automatic confirmation that all related risks have been resolved.

**Control evidence:** Incident-to-risk and change-to-risk references, workflow logs, reassessment results, and resulting decisions.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risk management remains responsive to operational events and material system changes.

### 72. AI Risk Register Governance and Assurance Review

The organization shall periodically review the effectiveness of the AI Risk Register governance framework.

The review should evaluate whether the methodology, ownership model, control framework, reporting, and assurance activities remain appropriate for the organization's AI risk exposure.

Review considerations should include:

- Completeness of risk identification.
- Quality and consistency of assessments.
- Effectiveness of control mapping.
- Timeliness of treatment actions.
- Validity of acceptance decisions.
- Reliability of reporting.
- Repeated control deficiencies.
- Integration with enterprise risk management.
- Adequacy of resources and competencies.
- Changes in AI technology and applicable obligations.

The review should produce documented findings, assigned improvement actions, and follow-up arrangements.

Where the organization identifies systemic weaknesses, it should consider whether the risk register methodology or governance model requires redesign rather than isolated record corrections.

**Control evidence:** Governance review reports, committee minutes, assurance findings, improvement plans, and closure verification.

**Control objective:** Ensure that the AI Risk Register remains a reliable, accountable, and continuously improving component of enterprise AI governance.

### 73. Core AI Risk Register Control Principle

AI Risk Register controls are effective only when the organization can demonstrate that material risks are captured, consistently assessed, linked to relevant controls, assigned to accountable owners, and managed through documented treatment and acceptance decisions.

The control framework must distinguish between **a completed risk record and a valid risk assessment**, **implemented controls and effective controls**, **planned remediation and verified risk reduction**, and **documented acceptance and authorized acceptance**.

**Core Principle:** AI Risk Register controls are effective when they produce reliable risk information, enforce accountability, preserve evidence, trigger timely action, and ensure that material residual risks remain within approved risk appetite or receive the required escalation and treatment.

## Part 3 — AI Risk Register Governance

### 74. AI Risk Register Governance Operating Model

The AI Risk Register must operate within a formal governance model that establishes accountability for risk identification, assessment, treatment, acceptance, escalation, and ongoing monitoring.

The operating model should define how business owners, AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, technology, and enterprise risk management collaborate to maintain a consistent view of AI-related exposure.

The governance model should establish:

- An accountable owner for the risk register framework.
- Risk owners responsible for individual risk scenarios.
- Control owners responsible for implementing and operating mitigating controls.
- Independent oversight of material risk assessments.
- Delegated authority for risk treatment and acceptance decisions.
- Escalation pathways for risks outside approved appetite.
- Standardized reporting and review requirements.
- Evidence and assurance responsibilities.
- Continuous improvement mechanisms.

The register owner is responsible for the integrity of the process, but should not automatically become the accountable owner for every risk recorded.

Individual business and system owners remain accountable for risks arising from their activities, while governance and risk functions provide methodology, challenge, coordination, and oversight.

### 75. AI Risk Register Three Lines Model

The organization should establish clear responsibilities for operational risk management, independent oversight, and assurance.

The Three Lines Model can help prevent conflicts between implementing controls, evaluating their effectiveness, and independently assessing the governance process.

| Governance line | Responsibilities                            | Examples                                                                       |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| First line      | Own and manage AI risks in operations       | Maintain risk records, implement controls, execute treatment plans             |
| Second line     | Establish methodology and provide oversight | Challenge ratings, monitor risk appetite, review material acceptances          |
| Third line      | Provide independent assurance               | Audit risk management processes, test evidence, evaluate control effectiveness |

The first line should provide evidence of how risks are managed. The second line should challenge whether risk assessments and treatment decisions are consistent with policy and appetite. The third line should independently assess whether the overall framework is appropriately designed and operating effectively.

The model should be adapted to the organization's size and operating structure. Smaller organizations may combine some responsibilities, but material conflicts of interest must still be managed.

Internal audit should not become the operational owner of the risk register if it is expected to provide independent assurance over that register.

### 76. Executive Accountability for AI Risk Exposure

Executive management must ensure that AI-related risks receive appropriate oversight, resources, and decision-making authority.

Executives should understand the material exposures associated with the organization's AI portfolio, including risks that may affect customers, employees, business continuity, regulatory compliance, security, and reputation.

Executive responsibilities should include:

- Establishing or sponsoring AI risk governance requirements.
- Approving the relevant risk appetite and escalation thresholds.
- Assigning accountability for material AI risks.
- Reviewing high and critical residual risks.
- Resolving cross-functional disputes that prevent risk treatment.
- Ensuring adequate resources for remediation.
- Reviewing recurring control deficiencies.
- Challenging overdue treatment actions.
- Ensuring that prohibited or legally unacceptable activities are not authorized through risk acceptance.
- Monitoring the effectiveness of the overall AI risk management framework.

Executive oversight should focus on the significance of the exposure, the effectiveness of controls, and the decisions required to address it.

A large number of documented risks does not necessarily indicate poor governance. Equally, a small register does not establish that the organization has a low AI risk profile.

### 77. AI Risk Governance Committee Charter

Where justified by the organization's size, complexity, or risk exposure, an AI governance committee or existing enterprise risk committee should oversee material AI risks.

The committee charter should define its mandate, membership, authority, responsibilities, meeting frequency, decision rights, and reporting arrangements.

Responsibilities may include:

- Reviewing high and critical residual risks.
- Monitoring risks outside approved appetite.
- Reviewing significant risk acceptance requests.
- Resolving disagreements over risk classification.
- Reviewing recurring AI control failures.
- Monitoring treatment plans and overdue actions.
- Evaluating shared-provider and systemic AI risks.
- Reviewing material incidents and emerging risk scenarios.
- Overseeing changes to risk assessment methodology.
- Escalating material concerns to executive management or the board.

Membership should include appropriate business, technology, cybersecurity, privacy, compliance, legal, and enterprise risk representation.

Committee decisions should be recorded with the relevant risk identifiers, decision rationale, assigned owners, deadlines, conditions, and escalation requirements.

The committee should not become a bottleneck for routine decisions that can be handled under approved delegated authority.

### 78. AI Risk Decision Rights Matrix

The organization must establish clear decision rights for risk assessment, treatment, acceptance, and closure.

Decision rights should distinguish between contributing technical expertise, owning a business risk, implementing a control, and approving residual exposure.

An illustrative matrix is provided below.

| Decision or activity            | Primary responsibility                        | Required oversight                         |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Identify and register a risk    | Relevant business or technical owner          | Risk methodology validation                |
| Assess technical likelihood     | Qualified technical or security specialist    | Risk owner review                          |
| Assess business impact          | Business risk owner with relevant specialists | Appropriate governance challenge           |
| Define treatment actions        | Risk owner and control owners                 | Risk management oversight                  |
| Verify control effectiveness    | Control assurance or designated reviewer      | Independent review where warranted         |
| Accept low or moderate risk     | Authorized risk owner                         | Defined approval criteria                  |
| Accept high residual risk       | Delegated senior authority                    | Second-line challenge                      |
| Consider critical residual risk | Executive or designated governance authority  | Mandatory escalation and legal constraints |
| Approve a risk exception        | Authorized exception approver                 | Documented justification and expiry        |
| Close a material risk           | Accountable risk owner                        | Verification and required approval         |

The matrix must be adapted to the organization's risk appetite, regulatory obligations, and delegation framework.

A person authorized to update a risk record should not automatically be authorized to reduce its rating or accept its residual exposure.

### 79. AI Risk Ownership and Accountability Governance

The organization should maintain explicit accountability for each material risk throughout its lifecycle.

Risk ownership should be based on the authority and responsibility required to manage the consequences of the risk, not merely on the technical team that identified it.

The governance model should establish how risk ownership is assigned, accepted, transferred, reviewed, and escalated.

It should address:

- Ownership for risks spanning several business units.
- Risks involving shared AI platforms or providers.
- Ownership disputes.
- Temporary ownership during organizational transitions.
- Ownership changes following system transfers or restructuring.
- Risks without a clear business sponsor.
- Risks identified in enterprise-wide assessments.

Where a risk affects multiple systems, a common underlying risk may be managed centrally while system-specific consequences remain assigned to the relevant owners.

Ownership transfers should preserve historical decisions, open treatment actions, acceptance conditions, and relevant evidence.

An unassigned material risk should be treated as a governance deficiency requiring resolution rather than remaining indefinitely in an administrative queue.

### 80. AI Risk Assessment Independence and Challenge

The organization should establish appropriate independent challenge for material AI risk assessments.

Independent challenge helps identify optimistic assumptions, inconsistent scoring, incomplete impact analysis, and unsupported claims about control effectiveness.

The level of challenge should be proportionate to the risk and complexity of the system.

It may include:

- Review of high and critical assessments by second-line risk functions.
- Independent verification of control evidence.
- Peer review of complex technical scenarios.
- Challenge of likelihood and impact assumptions.
- Review of uncertainty and evidence gaps.
- Comparison with similar risk scenarios.
- Examination of risk acceptance rationale.

The reviewer should have sufficient competence and access to relevant evidence to assess the quality of the decision.

Independent challenge does not necessarily require a separate organizational department for every assessment. However, the organization should document how conflicts of interest are managed and when stronger independence is required.

### 81. AI Risk Appetite Governance

The organization's AI risk appetite should translate enterprise objectives and obligations into decision criteria for AI development, procurement, deployment, and operation.

Risk appetite should establish the types and levels of AI exposure that the organization is prepared to retain, subject to applicable legal and regulatory constraints.

The governance model may define specific boundaries for:

- Sensitive data processing.
- Autonomous execution.
- Consequential decision-making.
- Model reliability and error rates.
- Human oversight.
- Security control effectiveness.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Operational availability.
- Fairness and stakeholder harm.
- Uncertainty and insufficient evidence.

Risk appetite statements should be supported by practical thresholds, escalation criteria, and decision rules.

For example, an organization may permit limited model errors in a low-impact internal summarization use case while requiring substantially stronger validation and human oversight for a system supporting consequential decisions.

The organization should not use a single enterprise-wide numerical threshold as a substitute for context-specific evaluation.

### 82. AI Risk Tolerance and Threshold Governance

Risk tolerance translates risk appetite into operational limits and escalation conditions.

The organization should define measurable or otherwise assessable thresholds for material risk scenarios where practical.

Examples include maximum acceptable downtime, error-rate thresholds, incident severity thresholds, overdue remediation limits, and restrictions on autonomous actions.

Thresholds should identify:

- The metric or condition being monitored.
- The applicable AI system or risk category.
- The acceptable range.
- The escalation threshold.
- The responsible monitoring owner.
- The required response.
- The authority to approve any permitted deviation.

The organization should define how thresholds are recalibrated when the system, business process, or operating environment changes.

A threshold breach should trigger a documented response rather than being treated as an ordinary performance fluctuation.

Where a risk cannot be represented reliably through a single metric, qualitative criteria and mandatory escalation rules should supplement numerical thresholds.

### 83. AI Risk Acceptance Governance

The organization must govern residual risk acceptance through a formal and traceable decision process.

Risk acceptance is appropriate only when the risk is within the accepting authority's delegation and is not prohibited by applicable obligations or organizational requirements.

The acceptance process should verify:

- The risk scenario is clearly defined.
- The residual assessment is current.
- Relevant control evidence has been reviewed.
- Remaining uncertainty is understood.
- Treatment alternatives have been considered.
- The accepting authority has appropriate competence and delegation.
- Conditions and restrictions are documented.
- Review or expiry requirements are defined.

The decision record should reference the relevant risk, system, use case, assessment evidence, rationale, approval authority, and any required follow-up actions.

Acceptance should not be inferred from silence, a missed deadline, or the fact that a system has already entered production.

Where risk cannot lawfully or appropriately be accepted, the organization must pursue additional treatment, restrict operation, or discontinue the activity.

### 84. AI Risk Acceptance Segregation of Duties

The organization should implement segregation of duties for material risk acceptance decisions.

The objective is to prevent an individual or team from unilaterally identifying a risk, determining that controls are effective, lowering the residual rating, and approving acceptance without appropriate challenge.

The governance model should distinguish:

- Risk identification.
- Assessment preparation.
- Control implementation.
- Control effectiveness verification.
- Residual risk determination.
- Acceptance approval.
- Independent assurance.

The degree of separation should reflect the severity of the risk and the organization's operating model.

For example, a technical owner may implement an authorization control, but the control's effectiveness may require independent testing before the associated residual risk is accepted.

Where complete separation is impractical, compensating measures should be documented, such as senior review, independent evidence validation, or periodic assurance testing.

### 85. AI Risk Escalation Governance

The organization must establish escalation procedures for risks that require action beyond the authority of the assigned risk owner.

Escalation triggers may include:

- Residual risk exceeding appetite.
- Critical or potentially severe consequences.
- Material uncertainty affecting the assessment.
- Unresolved control failures.
- Overdue high-risk treatment actions.
- Repeated incidents or near misses.
- Disagreement over risk ownership.
- Unavailable or inadequate treatment resources.
- Expired risk acceptance.
- Potential breach of a mandatory obligation.

The escalation procedure should define the receiving authority, required evidence, response expectations, interim safeguards, and decision-recording requirements.

Escalation should be timely and proportionate. High-impact issues should not wait for routine reporting cycles.

Where immediate exposure is material, the organization should consider appropriate interim restrictions, such as disabling a feature, reducing permissions, requiring human approval, or suspending a deployment under established procedures.

### 86. AI Risk Treatment Governance and Prioritization

The organization should establish a consistent method for prioritizing risk treatment across the AI portfolio.

Treatment prioritization should consider more than the numerical risk score.

Relevant factors include:

- Potential severity of harm.
- Residual risk and risk appetite.
- Legal and regulatory requirements.
- Exposure to active threats.
- Business criticality.
- Number and characteristics of affected stakeholders.
- Shared dependencies.
- Availability of effective mitigations.
- Remediation complexity.
- Cost and resource constraints.
- Consequences of delay.

Where several systems share the same underlying weakness, a common control improvement may reduce exposure more effectively than separate system-level actions.

However, a portfolio-level treatment should not obscure the need for immediate safeguards on an individual critical system.

The organization should document treatment priorities and the rationale for material decisions to defer remediation.

### 87. AI Risk Treatment Dependency Governance

Risk treatment plans often depend on actions across several teams, suppliers, or technology platforms.

The organization should document dependencies that may affect the delivery or effectiveness of planned mitigations.

Dependencies may include:

- Procurement approvals.
- Supplier commitments.
- Platform upgrades.
- Identity and access changes.
- Data governance improvements.
- Model evaluations.
- Architecture redesign.
- Staff training.
- Regulatory interpretation.
- Funding and resource allocation.

Each material dependency should have an owner, expected completion date, and escalation path.

The risk owner should understand whether a delayed dependency leaves the risk above appetite and whether interim safeguards are necessary.

Treatment plans should not be considered adequate merely because tasks have been assigned. The organization must assess whether the actions are likely to reduce the relevant exposure and whether the required dependencies can be delivered.

### 88. AI Risk Governance for Shared Platforms and Common Dependencies

The organization should identify risks that affect multiple AI systems through shared platforms, models, providers, data services, or security controls.

Shared dependencies can create correlated exposure: one provider outage, compromised credential, defective model release, or ineffective common control may affect many business processes simultaneously.

Governance should distinguish between:

- Enterprise or platform-level risks.
- System-specific risk scenarios.
- Common control deficiencies.
- Provider concentration risk.
- Cascading operational dependencies.

The register should link related risks and identify whether treatment is managed centrally, locally, or through a coordinated model.

Management reporting should aggregate the exposure without double-counting the same underlying event as though each linked system represented an independent risk.

The organization should also consider whether a shared failure could exceed the impact assumed during individual system assessments.

### 89. AI Risk Governance for Third-Party Dependencies

The organization should govern risks associated with external AI providers, model suppliers, cloud platforms, data providers, and outsourced AI services.

Third-party risks may include service disruption, security compromise, changes to model behavior, inadequate transparency, contractual limitations, data handling concerns, and concentration of critical capabilities.

The governance process should define how supplier risks are identified, assessed, linked to system-level risks, treated, and monitored.

It should also establish how material supplier changes trigger reassessment.

Relevant considerations include:

- Provider assurance evidence.
- Contractual commitments.
- Data processing and retention.
- Security incident notification.
- Model and service change notifications.
- Business continuity.
- Exit and portability arrangements.
- Dependency concentration.
- Availability of alternative providers.

Contractual indemnity or insurance may allocate certain financial consequences but does not eliminate the organization's responsibility to evaluate operational and stakeholder harm.

### 90. AI Risk Governance for Model and Provider Changes

Material changes to a model or provider should trigger an assessment of whether existing risk evaluations and treatment decisions remain valid.

Changes may include model replacement, new versions, altered capabilities, different data handling, new hosting arrangements, or changes to the provider's service terms.

The organization should define how changes are identified and how their materiality is evaluated.

The reassessment should consider whether changes affect:

- Risk likelihood or impact.
- Existing control effectiveness.
- Data protection and confidentiality.
- Model reliability.
- Security threat exposure.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Regulatory applicability.
- Operational dependencies.
- Risk acceptance conditions.

Where provider-managed changes are not fully visible, the organization should document the limitation and establish appropriate assurance, monitoring, or contractual safeguards.

A change should not be treated as immaterial solely because the provider retains the same product name or API endpoint.

### 91. AI Risk Governance for Agentic Systems

AI agents introduce governance challenges because they may plan, select tools, retain state, invoke external services, and execute multi-step actions.

Risk governance should explicitly address the relationship between the agent's permissions, degree of autonomy, business purpose, and potential consequences.

Relevant risk scenarios may include:

- Unauthorized transactions.
- Excessive tool permissions.
- Prompt injection through external content.
- Inappropriate disclosure through connected systems.
- Uncontrolled action loops.
- Persistence of unsafe instructions or state.
- Failure to stop when an approval is required.
- Cascading errors across connected applications.
- Inadequate logging of actions and decisions.

The organization should define which agent capabilities require enhanced assessment and approval.

Where agents can take consequential actions, governance should evaluate action boundaries, human approval requirements, execution identities, monitoring, rollback, and emergency suspension.

A conversational interface should not be treated as evidence that an agent's authority or impact is limited.

### 92. AI Risk Governance for Human Oversight

The organization should define when human oversight is required and how its effectiveness is evaluated.

Human involvement may include reviewing recommendations, approving consequential actions, investigating exceptions, or intervening when model behavior deviates from expectations.

Governance should consider whether reviewers have the competence, information, time, and authority required to perform meaningful oversight.

The process should examine:

- Whether outputs are presented with appropriate context.
- Whether uncertainty and limitations are communicated.
- Whether reviewers can challenge the output.
- Whether intervention is operationally feasible.
- Whether approval requirements can be bypassed.
- Whether automation bias may influence decisions.
- Whether oversight activity is recorded.
- Whether escalation and reversal mechanisms exist.

A nominal approval step does not establish effective human oversight if reviewers routinely accept outputs without meaningful examination.

The risk register should capture material weaknesses in oversight and link them to the relevant controls and treatment plans.

### 93. AI Risk Governance for Fairness and Stakeholder Harm

The organization should establish governance processes for identifying and managing risks of unfair, discriminatory, or otherwise harmful AI outcomes.

The assessment should consider the system's purpose, affected populations, data characteristics, decision context, and potential consequences.

Governance activities may include:

- Evaluating the suitability and representativeness of data.
- Testing relevant performance differences.
- Assessing potential disparate impacts.
- Reviewing affected-stakeholder characteristics.
- Establishing appropriate human review.
- Monitoring complaints and adverse outcomes.
- Documenting mitigation decisions.
- Reassessing when the population or use case changes.

The organization should avoid assuming that a model is fair merely because a general-purpose benchmark or average performance metric appears acceptable.

Where fairness cannot be adequately evaluated because relevant evidence is unavailable, the limitation should be recorded and addressed through proportionate safeguards and decision-making.

### 94. AI Risk Governance for Privacy and Data Protection

The organization should integrate privacy and data protection risk governance with the AI Risk Register.

Relevant risks may include unauthorized disclosure, excessive data collection, unlawful processing, inappropriate retention, re-identification, and use of personal data beyond the approved purpose.

Governance should establish how the register links to privacy assessments, data processing records, data classification, and applicable legal review.

The organization should determine when privacy specialists must participate and which issues require escalation.

Risk treatment may include data minimization, access restrictions, retention limits, de-identification, encryption, processing restrictions, or redesign of the use case.

The register should distinguish technical privacy controls from the legal determination that a particular processing activity is permitted.

Risk acceptance must not be used as a substitute for satisfying applicable data protection obligations.

### 95. AI Risk Governance for Security Threats

The organization should integrate AI risk governance with cybersecurity risk management.

AI-specific security risks may involve prompt injection, model manipulation, data poisoning, sensitive information disclosure, unauthorized tool use, model extraction, supply chain compromise, and insecure integrations.

The governance process should connect identified risk scenarios to threat models, security assessments, vulnerability management, incident response, and relevant control testing.

Security risk decisions should consider:

- Attack feasibility.
- Exposure and access paths.
- Potential impact.
- Existing preventive and detective controls.
- Exploitability of identified weaknesses.
- Dependencies on external providers.
- Incident evidence.
- Remediation status.

The organization should ensure that technical findings are translated into business-relevant risk statements and accountable treatment decisions.

A technical vulnerability should not remain isolated in a security tool if it materially affects the organization's AI risk profile.

### 96. AI Risk Governance for Regulatory Obligations

The organization should establish a process for identifying regulatory obligations that affect AI risk assessment and treatment.

The process should involve appropriate legal and compliance expertise and should consider the system, intended purpose, organizational role, jurisdiction, and applicable implementation dates.

The EU AI Act, data protection requirements, sector-specific rules, and contractual obligations may impose different requirements depending on the circumstances.

Governance should ensure that regulatory applicability decisions are documented and linked to relevant risks, controls, assessments, and evidence.

Where an obligation is mandatory, the organization must distinguish between legal compliance requirements and discretionary risk appetite decisions.

A high risk rating may require additional mitigation, but a low rating does not remove a legal obligation. Similarly, an accepted business risk cannot override a prohibited practice or mandatory compliance requirement.

### 97. AI Risk Governance for Risk Concentration

The organization should identify concentrations of AI risk arising from common providers, models, infrastructure, business processes, or control dependencies.

Concentration risk may remain hidden when risk records are assessed independently at the system level.

For example, several critical business applications may depend on the same external model provider or a common retrieval service. A single failure could therefore have a much larger enterprise impact than any individual assessment indicates.

Governance should examine:

- Provider concentration.
- Common model dependencies.
- Shared infrastructure.
- Common identity and access controls.
- Shared data pipelines.
- Common operational teams.
- Geographic concentration.
- Single points of failure.
- Correlated control deficiencies.

The organization should consider resilience measures, alternative arrangements, isolation, recovery plans, and coordinated treatment.

Concentration analysis should be reflected in enterprise reporting and should not double-count shared underlying risks.

### 98. AI Risk Governance for Emerging Risks

The organization should establish a process for identifying and evaluating emerging AI risks before they become material operational failures.

Emerging risk sources may include new model capabilities, changes in attack techniques, novel agent architectures, evolving social engineering methods, new provider dependencies, and changes in stakeholder expectations.

The process may use threat intelligence, research monitoring, supplier notifications, incident analysis, technical testing, and expert review.

Emerging risks should be evaluated for relevance to the organization's actual systems and intended uses.

The organization should distinguish speculative possibilities from plausible scenarios supported by evidence, while avoiding the assumption that an unfamiliar risk is negligible simply because no incident has occurred.

Where uncertainty is significant, the organization may require additional testing, restricted deployment, enhanced monitoring, or a defined reassessment trigger.

### 99. AI Risk Governance for Incidents and Lessons Learned

The organization should ensure that incidents and near misses inform ongoing risk governance.

A post-incident review should assess whether the event represents:

- A previously unidentified risk.
- A known risk with an inaccurate rating.
- A control design deficiency.
- A failure in control operation.
- An ineffective treatment plan.
- An inappropriate acceptance decision.
- A weakness in monitoring or escalation.

The review should identify whether other AI systems share the same exposure.

Governance actions should be assigned to accountable owners, with deadlines and verification criteria.

Where an incident demonstrates that assumptions underlying a risk acceptance are no longer valid, the acceptance should be reconsidered promptly.

The organization should retain traceability between the incident, affected risk records, corrective actions, and subsequent assessment decisions.

### 100. AI Risk Governance for Organizational Change

Organizational restructuring, outsourcing, acquisitions, divestitures, and changes in operating responsibilities can affect risk ownership and treatment effectiveness.

The organization should assess whether these changes create gaps in accountability, expertise, control operation, or evidence availability.

The governance process should consider:

- Changes in business ownership.
- Transfer of AI systems.
- Changes to support arrangements.
- Supplier transitions.
- Loss of specialist personnel.
- Changes in risk acceptance authority.
- Migration of risk records.
- Changes to control ownership.
- New or discontinued business activities.

Risk records should remain traceable during transitions.

A risk should not become unmanaged merely because the original owner leaves the organization or because responsibility for a system transfers to another business unit.

### 101. AI Risk Governance for Risk Methodology Changes

The organization should control changes to its risk assessment methodology, taxonomy, scoring model, and acceptance thresholds.

Methodology changes can alter the apparent risk profile even when the underlying AI systems have not changed.

The governance process should evaluate:

- The reason for the change.
- The affected risk categories.
- Implications for historical comparability.
- Reassessment requirements.
- Reporting and dashboard impacts.
- Training requirements.
- System configuration changes.
- Approval authority.
- Implementation and validation.

Where the scoring model changes materially, the organization should determine whether high-risk or accepted records require reassessment.

Historical records should preserve the methodology version used at the time of the original decision so that past ratings can be interpreted accurately.

### 102. AI Risk Governance for Risk Closure and Retirement

The organization should establish governance criteria for closing risks and retiring related risk records.

Closure should be based on a defensible conclusion that the scenario no longer applies, the relevant exposure has been resolved, or the organization's approved methodology supports another documented disposition.

Retirement of an AI system may eliminate some risks while leaving others unresolved, such as residual data exposure, contractual obligations, retained credentials, or dependencies in other systems.

The closure process should evaluate:

- Completion of relevant treatment actions.
- Verification of control changes.
- Removal of affected access and integrations.
- Remaining data and retention obligations.
- Dependencies on other systems.
- Open incidents or claims.
- Historical evidence requirements.
- Required approval.

Closed risks should remain available for historical review according to retention requirements.

### 103. AI Risk Governance Reporting to the Board

Board-level reporting should focus on material AI exposure, governance effectiveness, and decisions requiring executive or board attention.

The board should receive a clear view of the organization's most significant residual risks and the adequacy of the response.

Reporting may include:

- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks outside approved appetite.
- Material incidents and near misses.
- Systemic control weaknesses.
- Significant provider concentration.
- Overdue high-risk remediation.
- Material risk acceptance decisions.
- Emerging risks with strategic implications.
- Trends in risk exposure.
- Major assurance findings.
- Resource or policy decisions required.

Reports should distinguish current exposure from future exposure expected after planned treatment.

The organization should disclose material uncertainty, incomplete assessment coverage, and important assumptions.

Board reporting should enable informed challenge and decision-making rather than merely present a summary of risk scores.

### 104. AI Risk Governance Management Reporting

Management reporting should provide operational detail that enables accountable owners to address risk and control deficiencies.

Reports may include risk aging, treatment progress, overdue reassessments, acceptance expiry, control failures, risk ownership gaps, and recurring scenarios.

Management should review both individual high-risk records and patterns across the portfolio.

For example, repeated risks involving excessive permissions may indicate a common architectural weakness requiring an enterprise control improvement rather than isolated remediation.

Each material issue should have a defined owner, action, target date, and escalation path.

Reporting should be sufficiently timely for the risk involved. A critical incident-related risk may require immediate escalation, while stable low-risk records may be handled through periodic review.

### 105. AI Risk Governance Assurance and Continuous Improvement

The organization should periodically evaluate whether the AI Risk Register governance model remains appropriate and effective.

The review should examine the quality of risk identification, consistency of assessments, control effectiveness evidence, acceptance decisions, treatment performance, and the reliability of reporting.

Assurance findings should be used to improve the methodology, responsibilities, tools, and operating procedures.

Improvement priorities may include:

- Strengthening risk statement quality.
- Improving assessor calibration.
- Enhancing risk-to-control traceability.
- Reducing overdue treatment actions.
- Improving risk acceptance governance.
- Addressing recurring systemic exposures.
- Enhancing portfolio-level concentration analysis.
- Improving evidence quality.
- Strengthening integration with incidents and change management.
- Improving management and board reporting.

The organization should measure whether changes produce meaningful improvements in risk visibility and treatment outcomes.

**Core Principle:** AI Risk Register governance is effective when risk ownership, decision rights, independent challenge, risk appetite, treatment priorities, and executive oversight operate as a coherent framework that ensures material AI risks receive timely, evidence-based, and accountable decisions.
