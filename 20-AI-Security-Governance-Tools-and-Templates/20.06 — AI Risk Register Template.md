# 20.06 — AI Risk Register Template

## Part 1 — AI Risk Register Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Risk Register

An AI Risk Register is a structured governance artifact used to identify, document, assess, prioritize, treat, monitor, and report risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

It provides a centralized view of risks arising from AI models, data, infrastructure, integrations, suppliers, operational processes, human interactions, and the contexts in which AI outputs are used.

Unlike a general enterprise risk register, an AI Risk Register must account for risks arising from probabilistic outputs, model limitations, training and inference data, autonomous behavior, changing model capabilities, human overreliance, and interactions between AI components.

The register should connect identified risks to their causes, potential consequences, affected stakeholders, existing controls, inherent and residual risk ratings, treatment plans, accountable owners, and acceptance decisions.

It must function as an operational risk management instrument rather than a static list created solely for an audit or compliance exercise.

An effective AI Risk Register enables the organization to answer five fundamental questions:

1. What could go wrong with this AI system or use case?
2. Why could the risk occur, and who or what could be affected?
3. What controls currently reduce the likelihood or impact?
4. What residual risk remains, and who is accountable for it?
5. What actions are required to bring the risk within the organization's risk appetite?

The register should be integrated with the AI System Inventory, AI Impact Assessment, AI Security Assessment, privacy review, vendor risk management, incident management, and enterprise risk management processes.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Risk Register

The primary purpose of the AI Risk Register is to create a consistent and auditable record of AI-related risk decisions.

The register supports the organization in identifying material exposures, determining appropriate risk treatment, assigning accountability, and tracking whether corrective actions reduce risk to an acceptable level.

Its objectives include:

- Establishing a consolidated view of AI-related risks.
- Supporting risk-based prioritization of AI systems and use cases.
- Recording the causes and consequences of identified risks.
- Distinguishing inherent risk from residual risk.
- Connecting risks to preventive, detective, and corrective controls.
- Tracking risk treatment plans and remediation deadlines.
- Recording risk acceptance decisions and their conditions.
- Supporting management oversight and escalation.
- Providing evidence for internal audit and external assurance.
- Supporting reassessment when AI systems or their operating environments change.

The register should enable decision-makers to understand not only the number of risks identified but also their materiality, concentration, treatment status, and potential effect on business objectives and affected stakeholders.

A register containing hundreds of risks without clear ownership, treatment decisions, or evidence of control effectiveness does not demonstrate effective risk management.

### 3. AI Risk Register as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI Risk Register is a foundational component of an organization's AI governance operating model.

It provides the connection between risk identification, control implementation, management decisions, and ongoing oversight.

The register should operate as part of a broader governance process:

```
AI SYSTEM INVENTORY
        |
        v
AI RISK IDENTIFICATION
        |
        v
RISK ANALYSIS AND EVALUATION
        |
        v
CONTROL AND TREATMENT DECISIONS
        |
        v
RESIDUAL RISK ASSESSMENT
        |
        v
APPROVAL OR ESCALATION
        |
        v
ONGOING MONITORING
        |
        v
REASSESSMENT AND IMPROVEMENT
```

The register must support traceability across these stages.

For example, a risk involving unauthorized disclosure of confidential information through an AI assistant should be linked to the relevant system, identified data flows, existing access controls, proposed mitigations, residual risk rating, and accountable decision-maker.

The register should also support enterprise-level aggregation so that management can identify recurring risk patterns across multiple systems, business units, models, or providers.

However, aggregation must not remove the detail needed to understand the circumstances of individual risks.

### 4. Scope of the AI Risk Register

The register should cover AI-related risks within the organization's defined governance scope.

The scope may include risks associated with internally developed AI, third-party AI services, embedded AI capabilities, generative AI applications, machine learning models, AI agents, and AI-enabled business processes.

Risk categories may include:

- AI security and cybersecurity.
- Data protection and privacy.
- Model reliability and performance.
- Bias, discrimination, and unfair outcomes.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Human oversight and decision authority.
- Operational resilience and service continuity.
- Third-party and supply chain dependencies.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Intellectual property and confidentiality.
- Financial and commercial exposure.
- Reputational damage and stakeholder trust.
- Environmental and resource considerations where material.
- Agentic behavior and unauthorized actions.
- Model drift, degradation, and change-related exposure.

The organization should define whether the register covers individual risks, risk scenarios, system-level risks, enterprise-level AI risks, or a combination of these.

A practical implementation commonly uses individual risk records linked to AI systems and use cases, with aggregation mechanisms for portfolio-level reporting.

The register should not attempt to replace specialized risk records required by other enterprise processes. Instead, it should reference and integrate with those processes where appropriate.

### 5. Definition of an AI Risk

An AI risk is the possibility that uncertainty associated with an AI system, its components, its use, or its operating environment will affect objectives, cause harm, or result in undesirable consequences.

AI risk can arise from technical failures, malicious activity, inappropriate use, inadequate governance, external dependencies, human behavior, or interactions among these factors.

A well-formed risk statement identifies the circumstances that could lead to an adverse outcome and the consequences that may follow.

A practical risk statement structure is:

**Because of a defined cause or vulnerability, a particular event may occur, resulting in a specified consequence for the organization or affected stakeholders.**

For example:

"Because the AI assistant can access confidential documents without sufficiently restrictive authorization checks, a user may obtain information outside their approved access scope, resulting in unauthorized disclosure of sensitive business information."

This statement identifies the underlying condition, potential event, and business consequence.

The risk statement should be specific enough to support control selection and risk evaluation. Statements such as "AI security risk," "hallucination risk," or "regulatory risk" are too broad to guide meaningful treatment without further analysis.

### 6. Risk, Threat, Vulnerability, Event, and Impact Distinction

The register should distinguish related concepts that are frequently confused during AI risk assessments.

| Concept        | Meaning                                                 | AI example                                                        |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk           | Potential effect of uncertainty on objectives           | Confidential information may be disclosed through an AI assistant |
| Threat         | Potential source of harm                                | A malicious user attempts to extract restricted information       |
| Vulnerability  | Weakness that may be exploited or contribute to failure | Missing authorization enforcement in retrieval logic              |
| Risk event     | The occurrence that produces an adverse outcome         | The assistant returns restricted content to an unauthorized user  |
| Impact         | The resulting consequence                               | Data exposure, contractual liability, and reputational damage     |
| Control        | A measure intended to modify risk                       | Document-level authorization checks and output filtering          |
| Risk treatment | A decision or action to modify risk                     | Restrict retrieval access and implement security testing          |

These concepts should remain distinct in the register because they support different management activities.

Threat intelligence helps identify plausible attack sources. Vulnerability management identifies weaknesses. Incident management records events that have occurred. Risk management evaluates the possibility and consequences of adverse outcomes and determines how they should be treated.

A single risk scenario may involve several threats, vulnerabilities, events, and impacts. The register should capture these relationships without making the record unnecessarily complex.

### 7. AI Risk Register Ownership and Accountability

The organization must establish accountability for maintaining the register and managing individual risk records.

The AI Risk Register owner is responsible for the integrity of the risk management process, including consistent methodology, record quality, reporting, and escalation.

Individual risk owners are accountable for understanding and managing the risks assigned to them. Their responsibilities may include evaluating exposure, maintaining treatment plans, confirming control operation, and escalating material changes.

Typical responsibilities include:

| Role                       | Responsibility                                                    |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI risk register owner     | Maintains the register framework, structure, and reporting        |
| Business risk owner        | Accountable for the business consequences of the risk             |
| Technical risk contributor | Provides technical analysis and implementation details            |
| AI governance function     | Establishes methodology and challenges risk decisions             |
| Cybersecurity              | Assesses security threats, vulnerabilities, and controls          |
| Privacy function           | Evaluates relevant personal data and privacy risks                |
| Legal and compliance       | Advises on applicable obligations and legal exposure              |
| Control owner              | Implements and operates assigned risk controls                    |
| Risk acceptance authority  | Approves residual risk within delegated authority                 |
| Internal audit             | Independently evaluates risk management and control effectiveness |

One person may perform several roles in a smaller organization, but accountability and approval authority must remain explicit.

The person responsible for implementing a control should not automatically be authorized to accept the residual risk created by that control's failure.

### 8. AI Risk Register Record Identifier

Every risk record should have a unique, persistent identifier.

The identifier enables the organization to reference the same risk across assessment reports, control matrices, treatment plans, audit findings, incidents, and management dashboards.

An illustrative format is:

`AIR-2026-0001`

Where:

- `AIR` identifies an AI risk record.
- `2026` identifies the creation year.
- `0001` represents a sequential record number.

The organization may use another identifier convention provided that uniqueness, persistence, and traceability are maintained.

Risk identifiers should not encode information that may change, such as the current risk rating, business owner, or system status.

If a risk is transferred to another owner or reassessed, its identifier should normally remain unchanged. If two risks are consolidated or one risk is split into several records, the organization should preserve cross-references to maintain historical traceability.

### 9. AI Risk Register Data Model

The data model should capture the minimum information required to understand, evaluate, treat, monitor, and report each risk.

A risk record should generally include the following information groups:

| Data group     | Representative fields                                      |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identification | Risk ID, title, description, creation date                 |
| Context        | AI system ID, use case, business unit, deployment          |
| Risk scenario  | Cause, threat, vulnerability, event, consequence           |
| Classification | Risk category, subcategory, affected objectives            |
| Assessment     | Likelihood, impact, inherent risk, residual risk           |
| Controls       | Existing controls, control owners, evidence references     |
| Treatment      | Treatment decision, actions, owners, deadlines             |
| Accountability | Risk owner, control owner, acceptance authority            |
| Governance     | Review status, escalation status, approval references      |
| Monitoring     | Indicators, thresholds, review dates, incident links       |
| Evidence       | Assessment records, test results, supporting documentation |
| Closure        | Closure rationale, verification evidence, approval         |

The data model should support structured reporting while preserving enough narrative detail to explain each risk scenario.

Mandatory fields should be defined by lifecycle stage. For example, a newly identified risk may initially have an incomplete assessment, while a risk awaiting formal acceptance must have the required residual risk and approval information.

### 10. AI Risk Title and Description

The risk title should be concise, specific, and meaningful to business and technical stakeholders.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized Disclosure Through AI Retrieval.
- Unapproved Autonomous Execution by an AI Agent.
- Model Drift Affecting Credit Decision Reliability.
- Third-Party AI Service Availability Failure.
- Sensitive Data Retention by an External AI Provider.
- Inadequate Human Oversight of Automated Decisions.

The description should explain the scenario, relevant system context, potential event, and consequences.

The organization should avoid titles that merely name a broad category, such as "Privacy," "Security," or "Hallucination."

A useful description should make it possible for a reviewer to understand why the risk matters and what type of control or treatment may be appropriate.

The title and description should remain understandable when viewed independently in management reports or audit findings.

### 11. AI Risk Category and Subcategory

The organization should define a standardized taxonomy for classifying AI risks.

A taxonomy allows risk records to be grouped, compared, prioritized, and reported consistently across systems and business units.

An illustrative taxonomy is:

| Primary category      | Example subcategories                                        |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Security              | Prompt injection, data exfiltration, unauthorized tool use   |
| Privacy               | Excessive collection, unlawful processing, re-identification |
| Model reliability     | Hallucination, drift, poor generalization                    |
| Fairness              | Disparate outcomes, biased training data                     |
| Transparency          | Insufficient explainability, unclear AI involvement          |
| Human oversight       | Automation bias, ineffective intervention                    |
| Operational           | Service disruption, capacity constraints                     |
| Third-party           | Provider compromise, model dependency                        |
| Compliance            | Regulatory non-compliance, inadequate documentation          |
| Intellectual property | Unauthorized use of protected material                       |
| Governance            | Missing ownership, unauthorized deployment                   |
| Agentic systems       | Excessive permissions, uncontrolled execution                |

The taxonomy should be extensible as new risks emerge.

A risk may involve multiple categories, but the organization should define a primary category for consistent reporting and allow secondary tags where useful.

Classification should reflect the actual risk scenario rather than forcing a complex scenario into a single category that obscures its causes or consequences.

### 12. AI System and Use-Case Linkage

Every risk should be linked to the AI system, component, deployment, or use case to which it applies.

The primary relationship should normally reference the AI System Inventory rather than duplicating the system's authoritative information in the risk register.

Relevant links may include:

- AI system identifier.
- Use-case identifier.
- Model or model-component identifier.
- Deployment identifier.
- Provider or supplier record.
- Business process.
- Data asset or data flow.
- Related assessment.
- Related incident.
- Related control record.

A risk may affect multiple systems, while one system may have multiple risks.

Where a risk is common to several systems, the organization should decide whether to maintain one shared risk record with explicit system relationships or separate records for materially different scenarios.

The decision should preserve clear accountability and enable risk aggregation without concealing differences in exposure.

### 13. AI Risk Source and Cause Fields

The register should record the causes and conditions that make each risk plausible.

Risk sources may include technical design weaknesses, incomplete requirements, poor data quality, inadequate permissions, insufficient human oversight, third-party dependencies, ineffective governance, or malicious activity.

Examples include:

- Untrusted content entering a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline.
- Broad permissions granted to an AI agent.
- Training data that does not represent the deployment population.
- Lack of monitoring for model degradation.
- Provider changes without adequate notification.
- Missing validation before AI outputs influence decisions.
- Inadequate access restrictions for confidential information.
- Unclear accountability for AI-generated recommendations.

Recording the cause supports targeted treatment.

For example, if unauthorized disclosure arises from excessive retrieval permissions, improving the user interface alone may not address the underlying risk.

The cause field should distinguish confirmed weaknesses from assumptions that require further validation.

### 14. AI Risk Event and Consequence Fields

The register should describe the adverse event that could occur and the consequences that may follow.

The event should be specific enough to support scenario analysis and control design.

For example:

- Event: An AI agent executes a transaction without the required approval.
- Consequence: The organization incurs financial loss and violates internal authorization requirements.

Potential consequences may include financial loss, service disruption, privacy harm, security compromise, legal exposure, discrimination, physical harm, reputational damage, or loss of stakeholder trust.

The organization should identify affected parties where relevant, including customers, employees, suppliers, members of the public, and other stakeholders.

The consequence analysis should consider direct and indirect effects, including downstream decisions made using AI outputs.

A model output that appears harmless in isolation may create significant risk when incorporated into an automated workflow or relied upon by a human decision-maker.

### 15. AI Risk Inherent Assessment

Inherent risk represents the level of risk before accounting for the effect of the controls being evaluated.

It provides a baseline for understanding the exposure associated with an AI system or use case.

The assessment should consider the nature of the scenario, the likelihood of occurrence, the potential severity of consequences, and the context in which the system operates.

Relevant factors may include:

- Sensitivity of processed data.
- Number and characteristics of affected stakeholders.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Access to external tools or business systems.
- Potential scale and reversibility of harm.
- Criticality of the business process.
- Exposure to untrusted inputs.
- Dependence on third-party services.
- Difficulty of detecting failures.
- Potential for cascading or systemic effects.

The organization should document its scoring assumptions and avoid treating inherent risk as a purely mathematical exercise.

Inherent risk does not imply that controls are absent in the actual environment. It is an assessment baseline used to distinguish the underlying exposure from the risk remaining after relevant controls are considered.

### 16. AI Risk Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood represents the assessed probability or frequency of a defined risk scenario occurring within a specified context and period.

The organization should establish a consistent likelihood scale and define the evidence expected to support each rating.

An illustrative five-level scale is:

| Rating | Level          | Illustrative interpretation                                              |
| ------ | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1      | Rare           | Highly unusual under the defined operating conditions                    |
| 2      | Unlikely       | Possible but not expected in normal circumstances                        |
| 3      | Possible       | Plausible and may occur under relevant conditions                        |
| 4      | Likely         | Expected to occur in a meaningful proportion of comparable circumstances |
| 5      | Almost certain | Expected to occur frequently or under routine conditions                 |

These descriptions are illustrative and should be calibrated to the organization's risk methodology.

AI likelihood assessment should account for adversarial behavior, model variability, environmental change, operational exposure, and the availability of relevant evidence.

Where quantitative data is unavailable, expert judgment may be necessary. The register should record the assumptions, confidence level, and evidence limitations rather than presenting an uncertain estimate as a precise probability.

Likelihood should be reassessed when the threat environment, system design, deployment conditions, or relevant controls change materially.

### 17. AI Risk Impact Assessment

Impact represents the severity of the consequences if the risk scenario occurs.

The organization should evaluate impact across relevant dimensions rather than relying solely on financial loss.

Possible dimensions include:

- Financial.
- Operational.
- Legal and regulatory.
- Security and confidentiality.
- Privacy.
- Human rights and fairness.
- Health and physical safety.
- Reputational.
- Strategic.
- Environmental, where material.

An illustrative five-level impact scale is:

| Rating | Level         | Illustrative interpretation                                                            |
| ------ | ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1      | Insignificant | Negligible disruption or harm with limited remediation                                 |
| 2      | Minor         | Limited and manageable consequences                                                    |
| 3      | Moderate      | Material localized consequences requiring coordinated remediation                      |
| 4      | Major         | Significant business, legal, security, or stakeholder consequences                     |
| 5      | Severe        | Potentially catastrophic consequences or serious harm requiring executive intervention |

The organization should define impact thresholds appropriate to its business, sector, and regulatory context.

Where a risk could cause serious harm to an individual or a vulnerable group, the assessment should not allow a low aggregate financial impact to conceal the severity of that harm.

The impact assessment should identify the relevant dimensions and explain the basis for the selected rating.

### 18. AI Risk Scoring Methodology

The organization should define a consistent method for deriving risk ratings from likelihood and impact.

A simple illustrative model multiplies the two ratings:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

Using five-point scales, the score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Score | Illustrative rating |
| ----- | ------------------- |
| 1–4   | Low                 |
| 5–9   | Moderate            |
| 10–16 | High                |
| 17–25 | Critical            |

These thresholds are examples, not universal standards. The organization should validate the scoring model against its risk appetite, sector requirements, and materiality thresholds.

A multiplication model can create false precision and may not reflect every type of harm. Some risks may require mandatory escalation regardless of the numerical score, particularly where legal requirements, serious safety implications, or potentially unacceptable impacts apply.

The organization should document how qualitative judgments, mandatory escalation rules, and multi-dimensional impacts affect the final rating.

The scoring method must be applied consistently and should not allow risk owners to lower a rating merely to obtain approval.

### 19. AI Risk Rating Matrix

A risk matrix provides a visual mechanism for comparing likelihood and impact.

An illustrative five-by-five matrix is shown below.

| Likelihood / Impact | 1 — Insignificant | 2 — Minor | 3 — Moderate | 4 — Major | 5 — Severe |
| ------------------- | ----------------- | --------- | ------------ | --------- | ---------- |
| 5 — Almost certain  | Moderate          | High      | High         | Critical  | Critical   |
| 4 — Likely          | Moderate          | Moderate  | High         | High      | Critical   |
| 3 — Possible        | Low               | Moderate  | Moderate     | High      | High       |
| 2 — Unlikely        | Low               | Low       | Moderate     | Moderate  | High       |
| 1 — Rare            | Low               | Low       | Low          | Moderate  | Moderate   |

The matrix is an illustrative governance aid and is not mathematically identical to every possible score-band methodology. The organization should ensure that the matrix, score thresholds, and escalation rules are internally consistent.

Risk matrices should support judgment rather than replace it. Reviewers should consider the underlying scenario, evidence quality, affected stakeholders, and any mandatory treatment requirements.

Where uncertainty is high or consequences could be severe, the organization may need conservative assumptions or additional assessment before authorizing the relevant use.

### 20. AI Risk Control Environment

The register should document the controls that currently mitigate each risk.

Controls may be preventive, detective, corrective, or compensating.

Examples include:

| Control type | AI-related example                              |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Preventive   | Least-privilege permissions for an AI agent     |
| Preventive   | Input validation and restricted tool access     |
| Detective    | Monitoring for unusual model or API activity    |
| Detective    | Evaluation for model drift and output anomalies |
| Corrective   | Incident response and credential revocation     |
| Corrective   | Model rollback or deployment suspension         |
| Compensating | Human approval before consequential actions     |

The register should distinguish between a control that is planned, a control that has been implemented, and a control that has been tested and shown to operate effectively.

A policy statement or technical feature does not automatically demonstrate control effectiveness.

Where a control depends on another system, supplier, or manual process, the risk record should identify the dependency and any associated limitations.

### 21. AI Risk Residual Assessment

Residual risk is the risk remaining after the effect of relevant existing controls and other risk treatments has been considered.

The residual assessment should evaluate whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

A residual risk rating should not be calculated by mechanically subtracting a fixed number of points from the inherent score unless the organization's methodology explicitly supports and validates that approach.

Instead, the assessor should reconsider the likelihood and impact of the scenario in light of the evidence available about the control environment.

The assessment should record:

- Existing controls considered.
- Control effectiveness evidence.
- Material control deficiencies.
- Remaining vulnerabilities and uncertainties.
- Revised likelihood and impact.
- Residual risk rating.
- Confidence in the assessment.
- Required further treatment.

A risk may remain high even when several controls are present, particularly when the potential impact is severe or the controls are difficult to validate.

Residual risk should reflect the actual operating environment, not the controls the organization intends to implement in the future.

### 22. AI Risk Treatment Options

The organization should define acceptable treatment options for identified AI risks.

Common options include:

| Treatment         | Meaning                                                         | AI example                                                    |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid             | Stop or do not initiate the activity creating the risk          | Prohibit an AI use case that cannot be operated safely        |
| Mitigate          | Reduce likelihood or impact through controls                    | Restrict data access and add independent output validation    |
| Transfer or share | Allocate or share certain financial or contractual consequences | Use contractual indemnities or appropriate insurance          |
| Accept            | Retain the residual risk through an authorized decision         | Approve a documented residual risk within delegated authority |

Risk transfer does not eliminate the underlying operational, ethical, legal, or stakeholder consequences of an AI failure.

Similarly, risk acceptance does not override mandatory legal obligations, prohibited practices, or non-negotiable internal requirements.

The selected treatment should address the actual causes and consequences of the scenario and be supported by an accountable owner, implementation plan, and verification criteria.

### 23. AI Risk Treatment Plan

Every risk requiring additional mitigation should have a treatment plan that defines the actions needed to reduce exposure.

The plan should include:

- Treatment action identifier.
- Linked risk identifier.
- Description of the proposed control or change.
- Accountable action owner.
- Supporting implementation team.
- Priority.
- Target completion date.
- Required resources.
- Dependencies.
- Expected risk reduction.
- Verification method.
- Evidence requirements.
- Current status.

Treatment plans should be specific and measurable.

For example, "improve AI security" is not an adequate action. A more useful action would specify the implementation of authorization checks for retrieval requests, testing against unauthorized document access, and verification that the control prevents disclosure across defined test scenarios.

The organization should track delays and unresolved dependencies, particularly where they affect high or critical residual risks.

Completion of a treatment action should trigger validation of the control and reassessment of the linked risk where appropriate.

### 24. AI Risk Acceptance and Approval Authority

The organization must define who may accept residual AI risks and under what conditions.

Acceptance authority should be proportionate to risk severity, business impact, regulatory constraints, and the organization's risk appetite.

An illustrative delegation model is:

| Risk level | Illustrative approval authority                                                                                |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low        | Designated operational risk owner                                                                              |
| Moderate   | Business owner with relevant risk oversight                                                                    |
| High       | Senior management or delegated risk committee                                                                  |
| Critical   | Executive-level authority or formal governance body, where acceptance is legally and operationally permissible |

The organization must define its own approval thresholds and ensure they align with its enterprise risk management framework.

A risk acceptance record should document the residual risk, supporting rationale, evidence reviewed, approval authority, conditions, expiry or review date, and circumstances requiring reconsideration.

Acceptance should not be used to bypass mandatory controls or authorize conduct prohibited by law or policy.

If a risk exceeds the organization's appetite or cannot lawfully be accepted, the organization must pursue further treatment, restrict the activity, or discontinue the use case as appropriate.

### 25. AI Risk Monitoring and Review

The organization should define how identified risks are monitored and when they must be reassessed.

Review frequency should reflect the risk level, system volatility, model update frequency, operational criticality, and exposure to external threats.

Monitoring may use:

- Security alerts.
- Model performance metrics.
- Drift indicators.
- Data quality measures.
- Incident and near-miss reports.
- Control testing results.
- Vendor change notifications.
- Audit findings.
- Stakeholder complaints.
- Regulatory developments.
- Changes in intended purpose or deployment.

The register should identify monitoring owners, review dates, relevant indicators, and escalation thresholds.

A scheduled review should not be the only trigger for reassessment. Material changes or incidents may require immediate evaluation.

The organization should retain evidence that reviews occurred and document decisions, revised ratings, and resulting treatment actions.

### 26. AI Risk Register Change Triggers

The organization should define events that trigger the creation, reassessment, or closure of risk records.

Typical triggers include:

- Introduction of a new AI system or use case.
- Model or provider changes.
- Changes to training or inference data.
- Expansion of system permissions.
- Introduction of new tools or integrations.
- Changes in autonomy or human oversight.
- Material performance degradation.
- Security incidents or near misses.
- New vulnerabilities or threat intelligence.
- Changes in regulatory applicability.
- Material audit findings.
- Changes in business purpose.
- Changes to affected stakeholder groups.
- Retirement or decommissioning.

The change management process should identify which events require updates to the risk register and which require a formal reassessment.

Risk records should preserve the history of material changes so that reviewers can understand why ratings and treatment decisions evolved over time.

### 27. AI Risk Register Integration with Enterprise Risk Management

The AI Risk Register should connect with the organization's enterprise risk management (ERM) framework.

AI-related risks may affect broader enterprise risk categories, including strategic, operational, financial, legal, compliance, technology, and reputational risk.

The integration should establish:

- Common risk terminology where practical.
- Consistent risk scoring or documented translation rules.
- Clear escalation thresholds.
- Shared reporting requirements.
- Ownership and accountability relationships.
- Links to enterprise-level risk statements.
- Aggregation rules for systemic AI exposure.
- Governance for overlapping risk records.

For example, multiple AI systems may depend on the same external model provider. A provider outage or compromise could create correlated exposure across several business units.

The organization should avoid double-counting the same underlying risk while ensuring that local system-level consequences remain visible.

AI risk reporting should therefore support both detailed system-level management and consolidated enterprise oversight.

### 28. AI Risk Register Evidence and Auditability

Every material risk assessment and decision should be supported by appropriate evidence.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, threat models, security test results, model evaluations, impact assessments, vendor assurance documentation, monitoring reports, incident records, and formal approvals.

The register should reference evidence rather than requiring every artifact to be stored directly in the risk record.

Evidence management should ensure that records are accessible to authorized reviewers, linked to the correct risk, version-controlled where necessary, and retained according to applicable requirements.

The organization should be able to reconstruct:

- How the risk was identified.
- Which assumptions informed the assessment.
- How likelihood and impact were determined.
- Which controls were considered.
- What treatment was selected.
- Who approved residual risk.
- Whether actions were completed and verified.
- How the risk changed over time.

Auditability is essential because a risk rating without supporting rationale or evidence cannot reliably demonstrate the quality of the organization's risk management decisions.

### 29. AI Risk Register Quality Assurance

The organization should implement quality checks to ensure that risk records are complete, consistent, and useful for decision-making.

Quality assurance should evaluate:

- Whether the risk statement describes a plausible scenario.
- Whether causes, events, and consequences are distinguishable.
- Whether the linked AI system and use case are correct.
- Whether likelihood and impact ratings are supported.
- Whether existing controls are implemented and evidenced.
- Whether residual risk reflects the actual control environment.
- Whether treatment actions are specific and measurable.
- Whether ownership and approval authority are appropriate.
- Whether review dates are current.
- Whether related risks are duplicated or inconsistently rated.

Quality checks may be automated for mandatory fields and classification rules, while substantive risk judgments require qualified review.

The organization should track recurring quality deficiencies and use them to improve the methodology, training, templates, and governance process.

A register should not be considered mature merely because all required fields are populated. The quality and decision usefulness of the content are equally important.

### 30. AI Risk Register Core Implementation Principle

The AI Risk Register provides the structured mechanism through which AI risks become visible, attributable, assessable, treatable, and subject to continuing oversight.

Its value depends on the quality of risk scenarios, the consistency of assessment methodology, the reliability of control evidence, the accountability of risk owners, and the effectiveness of treatment and acceptance decisions.

The organization must distinguish between **risk identification and risk assessment**, **inherent and residual risk**, **planned and effective controls**, **risk treatment and risk acceptance**, and **documented risk decisions and verified risk reduction**.

**Core Principle:** An AI Risk Register is effective only when each material risk is described in a way that supports action, evaluated using a consistent and evidence-based methodology, assigned to an accountable owner, linked to appropriate controls and treatment decisions, and monitored throughout the AI system lifecycle.
