# 20.03 — AI Acceptable Use Policy

## Part 1 — AI Acceptable Use Policy Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Acceptable Use Policy

An AI Acceptable Use Policy (AI AUP) is a formal organizational policy that defines how employees, contractors, temporary personnel, and other authorized users may use artificial intelligence systems for business activities.

The policy establishes the boundaries between permitted, restricted, and prohibited AI usage.

Its primary purpose is to enable productive AI adoption while protecting organizational information, intellectual property, personal data, cybersecurity, regulatory compliance, and business integrity.

The policy should address the use of:

- Enterprise-approved AI applications.
- Public generative AI platforms.
- AI-enabled productivity tools.
- Large language models (LLMs).
- AI coding assistants.
- AI-enabled search and research tools.
- AI-powered analytics and decision-support systems.
- AI agents and automated tools.
- Third-party AI services embedded in business applications.
- Locally deployed or internally developed AI systems.

The policy should establish clear behavioral requirements for users rather than relying exclusively on technical security controls.

For example, an organization may implement data-loss prevention (DLP), access controls, and monitoring to reduce unauthorized disclosure. However, users must also understand which information they may submit to an AI system, which tools they may use, and which outputs require verification.

The fundamental concept is:

> **The AI Acceptable Use Policy defines the authorized boundaries of human interaction with AI systems, establishing what users may do, what they must do, and what they must never do when using AI for organizational purposes.**

### 2. Purpose of the AI Acceptable Use Policy

The purpose of the AI AUP is to establish consistent, enforceable rules governing the responsible and secure use of AI across the organization.

The policy should address six primary objectives.

**Security protection**

Prevent unauthorized disclosure, misuse, manipulation, or loss of organizational information through AI systems.

**Data protection**

Ensure that personal data, confidential information, customer records, credentials, and other sensitive information are handled according to approved requirements.

**Responsible AI usage**

Promote appropriate human judgment, transparency, accountability, and verification when using AI-generated outputs.

**Regulatory and contractual compliance**

Ensure that AI usage respects applicable legal requirements, contractual obligations, intellectual-property restrictions, and organizational policies.

**Operational integrity**

Prevent AI-generated errors, fabricated information, unsafe recommendations, and unauthorized automated actions from causing material business harm.

**Controlled innovation**

Enable employees to benefit from AI capabilities without introducing unmanaged security, privacy, legal, or operational risks.

A well-designed policy should not simply prohibit AI usage. It should provide a clear framework for distinguishing acceptable experimentation from activities that require formal approval or are prohibited entirely.

### 3. AI Acceptable Use Policy as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI AUP should operate as an enterprise governance control within the broader AI governance and cybersecurity framework.

It translates high-level governance principles into behavioral requirements that users can understand and follow.

```
Enterprise AI Governance Policy
              │
              ▼
      AI Security Policy
              │
              ▼
    AI Acceptable Use Policy
              │
       ┌──────┼──────┐
       ▼      ▼      ▼
    Permitted Restricted Prohibited
       Use       Use       Use

   Activities  Required   Escalation
       │         │          │
       └─────────┼──────────┘
                 ▼
       Monitoring and Evidence
                 │

```

The policy should align with other enterprise requirements, including:

- Information security policy.
- Data classification policy.
- Data protection and privacy policy.
- Information handling requirements.
- Acceptable technology use policy.
- Intellectual-property policy.
- Records management policy.
- Third-party risk management policy.
- Secure software development policy.
- AI governance policy.

The AI AUP should supplement these policies rather than replace them.

For example, permission to use an approved AI tool does not automatically authorize the user to upload confidential customer information. The data classification policy, contractual restrictions, and AI-specific data-handling requirements continue to apply.

### 4. Scope of the AI Acceptable Use Policy

The policy should apply to AI usage performed for, on behalf of, or in connection with the organization's business activities.

Its scope should cover AI systems regardless of whether they are developed internally, purchased, embedded in existing software, or accessed through external providers.

The policy should address the following categories.

**AI applications**

Enterprise applications that generate, summarize, classify, analyze, or transform information using AI.

**Generative AI systems**

Systems that generate text, images, audio, video, code, summaries, or other content.

**AI development and experimentation tools**

Tools used for prototyping, testing, fine-tuning, evaluating, or developing AI capabilities.

**AI-enabled enterprise platforms**

Business applications incorporating AI features, including collaboration, customer relationship management, productivity, analytics, and service-management platforms.

**AI agents**

Systems capable of invoking tools, accessing information, executing workflows, or taking actions on behalf of users.

**Personal AI accounts used for business**

External AI accounts or services used to process organizational information, even when accessed through personal devices or personal subscriptions.

The policy should also address AI usage through browser extensions, plugins, application integrations, APIs, and other mechanisms that may not be immediately visible to centralized IT teams.

Scope should be determined by the organizational relationship and information being processed, not merely by who owns the device or account.

### 5. Organizational Applicability

The AI AUP should apply to all individuals who are authorized to access organizational information or use AI systems for organizational purposes.

Applicable groups may include:

- Permanent employees.
- Temporary employees.
- Contractors.
- Consultants.
- Interns.
- Agency personnel.
- Third-party service providers.
- Outsourced personnel.
- Authorized business partners.

The organization should determine which requirements apply to each category.

For example, a contractor may be permitted to use an approved enterprise AI application but prohibited from transferring customer information into an external AI service without authorization.

Third-party users should be subject to appropriate contractual obligations and access conditions.

The organization should ensure that policy requirements are communicated before access is granted, with additional training where the user's role or access creates elevated AI-related risk.

### 6. AI Acceptable Use Policy Ownership

The AI AUP should have a formally designated policy owner responsible for maintaining its relevance, consistency, and effectiveness.

Depending on the organization's governance model, ownership may reside with:

- The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO).
- The AI governance function.
- The information security policy owner.
- The technology risk management function.
- A designated cross-functional governance authority.

Policy ownership should be distinguished from approval authority and operational enforcement.

| Role                      | Primary responsibility                                      |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Policy owner              | Maintains and updates the policy                            |
| Policy approver           | Authorizes the policy and material revisions                |
| Information security      | Defines and oversees security requirements                  |
| Privacy function          | Advises on personal-data processing and privacy obligations |
| Legal and compliance      | Evaluates legal and contractual requirements                |
| Business owner            | Ensures appropriate usage within the business function      |
| IT and AI platform owners | Implement technical restrictions and approved access        |
| End user                  | Complies with applicable requirements                       |

The policy owner should coordinate revisions when new AI capabilities, threats, business requirements, or regulatory obligations materially change the acceptable-use environment.

### 7. AI Acceptable Use Policy Approval Authority

The policy should be approved by an authority with sufficient organizational responsibility to establish mandatory AI usage requirements.

Approval may involve the CISO, CIO, AI governance committee, risk committee, or another formally authorized executive authority.

The approval process should establish:

- Policy owner.
- Approving authority.
- Version number.
- Approval date.
- Effective date.
- Review date.
- Applicable organizational scope.
- Superseded version, where applicable.

Material policy changes should undergo appropriate review before becoming effective.

The organization should also define who may authorize deviations from standard usage requirements.

End users and local managers should not independently override enterprise AI restrictions merely because a particular use case appears beneficial.

### 8. AI Acceptable Use Policy Principles

The AI AUP should be based on explicit principles that guide user behavior and organizational decisions.

**Authorized use**

Users may use AI systems only within the boundaries of approved access, business purposes, and applicable policy requirements.

**Data protection**

Users must protect organizational, customer, personal, and confidential information when interacting with AI systems.

**Human accountability**

Users remain accountable for their decisions and actions, even when AI provides recommendations or generates content.

**Verification**

AI-generated information must be verified to a degree appropriate to its intended use and potential impact.

**Transparency**

AI use should be disclosed when required by organizational policy, contractual obligations, applicable law, or the nature of the business process.

**Least privilege**

AI systems and agents should receive only the information and permissions necessary for their authorized functions.

**Security by default**

Users should use approved AI tools and secure configurations rather than introducing unapproved services or bypassing protective controls.

**Prohibited misuse**

AI must not be used to facilitate unauthorized access, fraud, deception, harassment, data theft, security-control evasion, or other prohibited activities.

**Risk proportionality**

The level of oversight and approval should reflect the sensitivity of the data, the autonomy of the AI system, and the potential consequences of its output or actions.

These principles should be converted into specific behavioral requirements in the subsequent policy sections.

### 9. Definition of Approved AI Tools

The organization should establish a process for identifying AI tools that have been approved for business use.

Approval should consider:

- Security controls.
- Data-processing practices.
- Privacy requirements.
- Provider terms.
- Data retention.
- Model training and data reuse.
- Identity and access management.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Integration capabilities.
- Regulatory and contractual obligations.
- Business suitability.

Approved tools should be recorded in an accessible catalogue or equivalent system.

The catalogue should identify relevant usage restrictions rather than merely listing product names.

For example:

| AI tool classification                | Acceptable-use boundary                                                        |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Approved enterprise AI                | Permitted within documented data and usage restrictions                        |
| Approved AI with restricted functions | Permitted only for specified activities                                        |
| AI requiring assessment               | Use requires security, privacy, or business approval                           |
| Unapproved external AI                | Must not be used for organizational information unless specifically authorized |
| Prohibited AI capability              | Must not be used for the prohibited purpose                                    |

Approval should be specific enough to distinguish between different products, configurations, subscriptions, and integration models offered by the same provider.

An enterprise-approved application may offer optional features that have not been assessed or authorized.

### 10. Definition of Permitted AI Use

Permitted AI use refers to activities that the organization explicitly allows when users comply with applicable security, privacy, confidentiality, and business requirements.

Examples may include:

- Drafting non-sensitive internal communications.
- Summarizing approved non-confidential documents.
- Brainstorming ideas for business improvement.
- Generating initial outlines for reports.
- Improving grammar and readability.
- Producing generic research summaries.
- Supporting approved productivity workflows.
- Assisting with authorized analysis of appropriately classified data.
- Generating test data that does not expose real sensitive information.
- Supporting approved development activities within secure environments.

Permission should not be interpreted as unconditional authorization.

For example, an approved AI assistant may be suitable for drafting an internal presentation but unsuitable for processing confidential acquisition information unless the relevant environment and use case have been authorized for that information.

The policy should distinguish between permission to access a tool and permission to perform a particular activity using that tool.

### 11. Definition of Restricted AI Use

Restricted AI use refers to activities that may be permitted only after specified conditions, safeguards, or approvals have been satisfied.

Restricted activities may include:

- Processing confidential information.
- Processing personal data.
- Uploading customer records.
- Using AI for employment-related decisions.
- Using AI in financial or legal decision support.
- Connecting AI systems to production environments.
- Enabling an AI agent to execute business transactions.
- Allowing AI tools to access sensitive repositories.
- Using external AI providers for regulated business processes.
- Deploying AI-generated code into production.
- Integrating AI with privileged enterprise systems.

The applicable approval should depend on the use case, data classification, system capability, and potential impact.

For example, an AI assistant may be approved to summarize a document in a controlled enterprise environment, while an agent that can modify production infrastructure may require security architecture review, restricted permissions, transaction limits, and explicit human approval.

Restricted use should have a defined approval pathway. Otherwise, users may interpret restrictions inconsistently or bypass them when under operational pressure.

### 12. Definition of Prohibited AI Use

Prohibited AI use refers to activities that the organization does not permit, regardless of their potential convenience or productivity benefits, unless an applicable legal requirement or formally authorized exception process establishes otherwise.

The policy should explicitly prohibit activities such as:

- Uploading organizational secrets to unauthorized AI services.
- Submitting passwords, API keys, access tokens, or private cryptographic keys to AI systems.
- Using AI to conduct unauthorized access or cyberattacks.
- Using AI to bypass enterprise security controls.
- Using AI to facilitate fraud, impersonation, or deceptive activity.
- Using AI to expose confidential information without authorization.
- Using unauthorized AI agents to execute privileged actions.
- Circumventing access restrictions through prompts, plugins, or integrations.
- Using AI-generated code or commands to perform unauthorized destructive actions.
- Using AI to fabricate business evidence, audit records, approvals, or compliance attestations.
- Using AI to conceal security incidents or falsify records.
- Using AI in ways that violate applicable law or binding organizational requirements.

The organization should define prohibited behavior precisely enough to support consistent enforcement.

A prohibition should also distinguish malicious activity from legitimate security testing performed under documented authorization.

For example, AI-assisted penetration testing may be permitted within an approved testing engagement, while using AI to attack an external system without authorization remains prohibited.

### 13. Shadow AI Governance

Shadow AI refers to AI tools, services, integrations, or agents used for organizational activities without the required organizational authorization or oversight.

Shadow AI may arise when employees use:

- Personal AI accounts for business tasks.
- Unapproved browser extensions.
- External AI websites.
- Unreviewed plugins.
- Personal API keys.
- Unauthorized AI coding assistants.
- AI features embedded in unassessed applications.
- Self-created agents connected to business systems.

Shadow AI creates governance blind spots because the organization may not know what information is being processed, where it is sent, how it is retained, or whether it is used for provider model improvement.

```
Employee Business Task
         │
         ▼
Unapproved AI Tool
         │
         ├── Confidential Data
         ├── Personal Data
         ├── Business Documents
         └── Credentials
                  │
                  ▼
          External Processing
                  │
                  ▼
       Unmanaged Security Exposure
```

The AI AUP should require users to use approved services and follow the established process for requesting new tools.

The organization should also provide a practical route for evaluating legitimate business needs. A policy that prohibits unapproved tools without offering a workable approval process may encourage users to conceal their usage.

Detection mechanisms may include software discovery, cloud application monitoring, endpoint controls, procurement reviews, and appropriate security telemetry.

### 14. AI Usage Authorization

AI usage should be authorized according to the user's role, business purpose, data access, and requested capabilities.

Authorization should distinguish among:

- Access to an AI application.
- Permission to process a particular data classification.
- Permission to use a particular AI capability.
- Permission to connect external integrations.
- Permission to execute automated actions.
- Permission to deploy or administer AI systems.

Access to an AI application should not automatically grant permission to use every available function.

For example, a user authorized to use a general-purpose AI assistant may not be authorized to connect that assistant to a customer database or configure an agent to send external communications.

Authorization should be reviewed when roles change, access is no longer required, or the AI system gains materially different capabilities.

### 15. AI Usage Registration

The organization should define when AI use cases require registration in an AI inventory or other approved governance system.

Registration requirements may apply to:

- New enterprise AI applications.
- AI tools processing sensitive information.
- AI integrations with enterprise systems.
- AI agents with tool access.
- AI-supported business processes.
- AI systems used for consequential decisions.
- Internally developed or substantially customized AI solutions.

A simple, low-risk use of an approved productivity tool may not require a separate formal assessment for every individual task.

However, the introduction of a new AI-enabled business process, integration, or agent should be assessed according to the organization's established registration criteria.

Registration should help the organization maintain visibility over AI usage without imposing disproportionate administrative burdens.

### 16. Business Purpose and Authorized Use

AI should be used for legitimate business purposes consistent with the organization's objectives and applicable policies.

Users should not introduce AI into a business process merely because the tool is available.

The intended purpose should be clear enough to determine:

- Whether the use is authorized.
- What information may be processed.
- What output is expected.
- Whether human verification is necessary.
- Whether the activity requires additional approval.
- Whether the use creates legal, security, or operational risk.

AI should not be used to make decisions outside the user's delegated authority.

For example, an employee may use AI to prepare a draft procurement comparison but should not use an unapproved agent to commit the organization to a purchase or alter contractual terms without the required authorization.

### 17. Personal AI Accounts and Organizational Information

The AI AUP should establish explicit restrictions on using personal AI accounts to process organizational information.

Users should not assume that a personal subscription provides enterprise-grade security, contractual protection, data isolation, or approved data-processing conditions.

Before organizational information is processed, the relevant service must be authorized for the intended use and data classification.

The policy should address:

- Personal subscriptions.
- Personal API keys.
- Consumer AI applications.
- Personal cloud storage integrations.
- Browser-based AI tools.
- AI applications installed on personal devices.

Where personal devices are permitted for business purposes, the organization should establish appropriate controls without assuming that device ownership determines whether the AI activity is acceptable.

### 18. AI Usage on Personal Devices

AI use from personal devices should comply with organizational access, data protection, and security requirements.

Where applicable, controls may include:

- Approved application access.
- Managed browser configurations.
- Device security requirements.
- Multi-factor authentication.
- Conditional access.
- Restrictions on local downloads.
- Session controls.
- Prevention of unauthorized data transfer.

Users should not download confidential organizational information to personal devices merely to process it through a more convenient AI service.

The policy should clarify which AI activities are permitted on personal devices and which must occur within managed enterprise environments.

### 19. AI Use and Confidentiality Obligations

AI usage does not remove existing confidentiality obligations.

Users must protect confidential information regardless of whether it is entered manually, uploaded as a file, included in a prompt, embedded in a retrieval workflow, or transmitted through an integration.

Confidentiality requirements should apply to:

- Customer information.
- Employee information.
- Financial records.
- Strategic plans.
- Acquisition discussions.
- Trade secrets.
- Proprietary source code.
- Security architecture.
- Vulnerability information.
- Contractual and legal documents.

Before submitting information to an AI system, users should confirm that the tool is authorized to process that information for the intended purpose.

A system's ability to accept a file or prompt does not demonstrate that the user is authorized to disclose its contents.

### 20. AI Use and Data Classification

AI usage should follow the organization's data classification and handling requirements.

The AI AUP should explain how classification determines whether information may be entered into a particular AI environment.

| Data classification | Acceptable-use principle                                                           |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Public              | May be used in approved AI tools, subject to applicable restrictions               |
| Internal            | May be processed only in tools authorized for internal information                 |
| Confidential        | Requires an environment and use case approved for confidential information         |
| Highly restricted   | Requires explicit authorization and enhanced controls; some uses may be prohibited |

The organization should define the actual classifications and permitted processing conditions within its data governance framework.

Users should not downgrade a data classification simply because information has been summarized, paraphrased, or converted into another format.

Derived content may still disclose the underlying sensitive information.

### 21. AI Use and Personal Data Protection

AI processing of personal data should comply with applicable privacy requirements and organizational data protection procedures.

Users should not enter personal data into AI systems unless the processing is authorized and the selected environment is suitable for the intended purpose.

The policy should address:

- Customer records.
- Employee information.
- Applicant information.
- Health-related information.
- Financial information.
- Identification information.
- Sensitive personal data.
- Personal data contained in documents or conversations.

Users should avoid unnecessary disclosure and use approved anonymization or synthetic-data techniques where appropriate.

Removing a person's name does not necessarily anonymize the information if the individual can still be identified through other attributes.

Where a use case involves material privacy risk, the user should follow the applicable privacy assessment and approval process.

### 22. AI Use and Intellectual Property

AI use should comply with the organization's intellectual-property requirements and applicable third-party rights.

Users should not submit proprietary materials to unauthorized services or assume that generated outputs are automatically free of intellectual-property restrictions.

The policy should address:

- Proprietary source code.
- Trade secrets.
- Licensed datasets.
- Copyrighted documents.
- Confidential research.
- Customer-owned materials.
- Third-party software.
- AI-generated content intended for publication or commercial use.

Users should follow applicable licensing and attribution requirements.

AI-generated content should be reviewed before being represented as original work, incorporated into commercial deliverables, or distributed externally where intellectual-property concerns may arise.

### 23. AI Use and Human Accountability

Users remain accountable for decisions, approvals, and actions performed under their authority, even when AI contributes to the outcome.

AI assistance does not automatically transfer responsibility to the tool provider or model developer.

Users should understand the limits of AI-generated recommendations and apply appropriate judgment before relying on them.

Human accountability is particularly important when AI supports:

- Financial decisions.
- Legal analysis.
- Employment decisions.
- Customer eligibility decisions.
- Security operations.
- Regulatory reporting.
- Procurement.
- Production system changes.
- Other consequential business activities.

The required degree of review should depend on the impact and risk of the activity.

A user should not approve a consequential decision merely because an AI system presents its recommendation confidently.

### 24. AI Output Verification

AI-generated information should be verified before it is relied upon for business decisions or distributed as authoritative organizational information.

Verification should be proportionate to the consequences of an error.

Users should assess:

- Factual accuracy.
- Source reliability.
- Completeness.
- Relevance.
- Currency.
- Logical consistency.
- Unsupported claims.
- Fabricated citations.
- Potential bias.
- Confidential information disclosure.
- Security implications.

For high-impact decisions, verification should rely on authoritative evidence, qualified review, or independent validation rather than solely on another AI-generated response.

```
AI-Generated Output
         │
         ▼
     User Review
         │
         ▼
 Verify Facts and Sources
         │
         ▼
 Assess Risk and Relevance
         │
         ▼
 Independent Validation
   Where Required
         │
         ▼
Authorized Business Use
```

If the output cannot be adequately verified, the user should not present it as established fact or use it as the sole basis for a consequential decision.

### 25. AI Use and Organizational Records

AI-generated content that becomes part of an official organizational record should comply with applicable records management and documentation requirements.

Examples may include:

- Audit evidence.
- Risk assessments.
- Security incident reports.
- Compliance submissions.
- Management approvals.
- Contractual documentation.
- Financial analysis.
- Official policies and procedures.

Users should not fabricate, alter, or misrepresent evidence through AI-generated content.

Where AI materially contributes to a regulated, consequential, or formally approved business activity, the organization should define what information must be retained to support traceability and accountability.

The policy should distinguish ordinary productivity interactions from AI activities that require formal records.

### 26. AI Use and Security-Control Evasion

Users must not use AI systems to bypass or undermine organizational security controls.

Prohibited behavior should include attempts to use AI to:

- Circumvent access restrictions.
- Evade monitoring.
- Disable security protections.
- Obtain unauthorized credentials.
- Exfiltrate organizational information.
- Generate unauthorized privileged commands.
- Bypass approved deployment processes.
- Conceal policy violations.
- Exploit systems outside authorized testing.
- Defeat restrictions imposed on AI agents or integrations.

Authorized security testing may use AI tools where the activity is within an approved scope and follows applicable rules of engagement.

The distinction is authorization: AI-assisted security work must remain within defined permission boundaries.

### 27. AI Use and External Disclosure

Users should not disclose AI-generated content or AI-processed organizational information externally unless the disclosure is authorized.

External disclosure may include:

- Sending outputs to customers.
- Publishing content.
- Sharing files with external collaborators.
- Posting content on public platforms.
- Sending information to external AI providers.
- Transferring data through third-party plugins.
- Allowing an agent to communicate with external parties.

The user should verify that the information is suitable for disclosure and that the relevant business, contractual, privacy, and approval requirements have been satisfied.

Where AI-generated material contains confidential information, personal data, security-sensitive details, or unverified claims, additional review may be required before release.

### 28. AI Use and External Integrations

AI integrations should be subject to appropriate authorization and security assessment.

Integrations may connect AI systems to:

- Email.
- Collaboration platforms.
- Document repositories.
- Customer databases.
- Enterprise resource planning systems.
- Source-code repositories.
- Cloud environments.
- Ticketing systems.
- Financial systems.
- External APIs.

Users should not connect an AI tool to an enterprise system simply because the application offers an integration option.

The organization should assess the integration's permissions, data flows, provider access, logging, and potential impact.

Integrations should use approved authentication mechanisms and narrowly scoped permissions.

### 29. AI Use and Automated Actions

AI-generated recommendations and AI-executed actions should be governed differently.

An AI system that suggests a change presents a different risk from an agent that executes the change directly.

The policy should require additional authorization and safeguards where AI can:

- Modify production systems.
- Change access permissions.
- Send external communications.
- Execute financial transactions.
- Delete or modify records.
- Deploy code.
- Change security configurations.
- Initiate privileged workflows.

High-impact actions should use appropriate validation, transaction limits, logging, and human approval where required.

The organization should define when an action must be blocked, escalated, or independently approved.

### 30. AI Acceptable Use Foundations Principle

The AI Acceptable Use Policy should establish clear behavioral boundaries for every authorized user of organizational AI capabilities.

Its foundation should connect approved tools, authorized purposes, data-handling requirements, human accountability, output verification, and prohibited behavior.

```
Approved AI Tool
       │
       ▼
Authorized Business Purpose
       │
       ▼
Approved Data and Permissions
       │
       ▼
Controlled AI Interaction
       │
       ▼
Output Verification
       │
       ▼
Authorized Decision or Action
       │
       ▼
Evidence / Accountability
       │
       ▼
Monitoring and Enforcement
```

The fundamental distinctions remain:

- **Tool approval ≠ approval of every use case**
- **Access to AI ≠ authorization to process every data type**
- **Public information ≠ automatically unrestricted information**
- **Summarized confidential data ≠ automatically non-confidential data**
- **AI-generated content ≠ verified information**
- **AI recommendation ≠ authorized decision**
- **Human involvement ≠ meaningful human oversight**
- **Personal subscription ≠ enterprise authorization**
- **Technical availability ≠ permission to use**
- **AI-generated evidence ≠ reliable audit evidence**
- **Automation capability ≠ authorization to execute**
- **Contractual protection ≠ elimination of confidentiality obligations**
- **AI assistance ≠ transfer of user accountability**
- **Security testing ≠ authorization to attack arbitrary systems**
- **Policy publication ≠ effective user compliance**

**Core Principle:**

> **An effective AI Acceptable Use Policy enables productive AI adoption within clearly defined behavioral and technical boundaries, ensuring that every user understands which tools, data, activities, and actions are permitted, restricted, or prohibited—and remains accountable for using AI in accordance with organizational requirements.**

## Part 2 — AI Acceptable Use Policy Controls

### 31. AI Acceptable Use Control Framework

The AI Acceptable Use Policy must be supported by a defined control framework that translates behavioral requirements into enforceable organizational controls.

The framework should establish a traceable relationship between policy requirements, user activities, technical restrictions, approval workflows, monitoring mechanisms, and compliance evidence.

A policy that only communicates expectations without supporting controls may depend excessively on individual judgment and voluntary compliance. Conversely, technical restrictions without clear policy requirements may prevent legitimate business activities without providing users with an understandable basis for the restriction.

The control framework should combine:

- **Preventive controls:** Restrict unauthorized AI tools, data transfers, and actions before they occur.
- **Detective controls:** Identify policy violations, anomalous usage, and unauthorized AI integrations.
- **Corrective controls:** Contain violations, remediate weaknesses, and restore compliant operations.
- **Governance controls:** Establish ownership, approvals, accountability, and exception authority.
- **Assurance controls:** Verify that policy requirements are implemented and operating effectively.

Controls should be proportionate to the organization's AI exposure, data sensitivity, regulatory obligations, and operational risk.

### 32. AI Acceptable Use Policy Approval Control

The organization must formally approve the AI AUP before establishing it as a mandatory organizational requirement.

The approval control should identify the authorized approving authority, policy owner, approval date, effective date, and version.

The control should ensure that relevant stakeholders have reviewed the policy according to their responsibilities.

Required review may include:

- Information security.
- AI governance.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Legal and compliance.
- Human resources.
- IT operations.
- Relevant business functions.
- Procurement and third-party risk management.

The approval process should verify that the policy is consistent with existing organizational requirements and that its restrictions can be implemented operationally.

**Control evidence** may include an approval record, governance committee minutes, an electronic workflow record, or an authorized policy sign-off.

### 33. AI Acceptable Use Policy Version-Control Control

The organization must maintain controlled versions of the AI AUP to ensure that users and control owners can identify the applicable requirements.

Version control should record:

- Policy version.
- Revision date.
- Policy owner.
- Approving authority.
- Summary of changes.
- Effective date.
- Review status.
- Superseded versions.

Changes should be traceable to their underlying business, security, regulatory, or operational drivers.

For example, introducing AI agents with access to enterprise applications may require new restrictions covering tool permissions, external communications, transaction limits, and human approvals.

The organization should prevent uncontrolled copies from becoming authoritative policy sources.

**Control evidence** may include document-management history, approved change records, version comparisons, and policy publication records.

### 34. AI Acceptable Use Policy Review Control

The AI AUP must be reviewed periodically and when material changes affect the organization's acceptable-use requirements.

Review triggers may include:

- Introduction of new AI capabilities.
- Deployment of autonomous or agentic AI.
- Significant AI-related security incidents.
- Changes to data classification requirements.
- New legal or regulatory obligations.
- Material changes in provider terms.
- Changes to enterprise AI architecture.
- Repeated policy violations.
- Findings from internal or external audits.

The review should determine whether the policy remains relevant, understandable, enforceable, and aligned with actual AI usage.

A review should not be considered complete merely because the document was opened or its review date was updated.

**Control evidence** should demonstrate that the policy was evaluated, findings were documented, necessary revisions were assessed, and resulting actions were assigned.

### 35. AI Acceptable Use Policy Communication Control

The organization must communicate the AI AUP to users who are subject to its requirements.

Communication should explain the practical implications of the policy rather than merely distributing a document.

Users should be able to determine:

- Which AI tools are approved.
- Which information may be processed.
- Which activities require approval.
- Which uses are prohibited.
- How to request a new AI tool.
- How to report a suspected violation.
- Where to obtain guidance.

Communication channels may include onboarding materials, employee portals, security awareness platforms, internal communications, and role-specific training.

Policy changes should be communicated when they materially alter permitted activities or user responsibilities.

**Control evidence** may include publication records, communication campaigns, distribution logs, and acknowledgment records.

### 36. AI Acceptable Use Acknowledgment Control

The organization should require applicable users to acknowledge their understanding of the AI AUP.

Acknowledgment may occur during onboarding, before access to designated AI systems is granted, or following material policy changes.

The acknowledgment process should identify:

- The user or applicable user population.
- The policy version.
- The acknowledgment date.
- The applicable access or employment context.
- Any required follow-up training.

Acknowledgment confirms that the policy was presented and accepted through the organization's process. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the user understands the requirements or follows them in practice.

For this reason, acknowledgment should be complemented by training, technical enforcement, and appropriate monitoring.

**Control evidence** may include learning-management system records, electronic attestations, or access-workflow records.

### 37. AI Acceptable Use Training Control

Users must receive training appropriate to their AI usage responsibilities.

General awareness training should cover the organization's principal acceptable-use requirements, while specialized training should address higher-risk activities.

Training topics may include:

- Approved and prohibited AI tools.
- Confidentiality and data handling.
- Personal-data protection.
- Prompt injection and malicious content.
- AI-generated misinformation.
- Verification of outputs.
- Intellectual-property considerations.
- AI agent permissions.
- Human oversight.
- Incident reporting.
- Policy exceptions.

Training should use realistic scenarios.

For example, a user should understand that removing a customer's name from a document does not necessarily make the remaining information safe to submit to an external AI provider.

The organization should evaluate whether training improves user understanding and reduces recurring mistakes.

**Control evidence** may include completion records, assessment results, scenario-based exercises, and training effectiveness metrics.

### 38. AI Acceptable Use Role-Definition Control

The organization must define acceptable-use responsibilities for roles with different levels of AI access and authority.

A general employee using an approved drafting assistant has a different risk profile from a developer configuring an AI agent with production access.

Role definitions should address:

- Permitted AI activities.
- Authorized data classifications.
- Required approvals.
- Verification responsibilities.
- Escalation obligations.
- Restrictions on external integrations.
- Responsibilities for AI-generated outputs.
- Additional controls for privileged AI capabilities.

The organization should ensure that role definitions are consistent with job responsibilities, access privileges, and delegated authority.

**Control evidence** may include role descriptions, access profiles, responsibility matrices, and approved usage standards.

### 39. AI Acceptable Use Segregation-of-Duties Control

The organization should prevent inappropriate concentration of AI-related permissions, approvals, and oversight responsibilities.

Segregation of duties is particularly important when AI systems support consequential decisions, financial transactions, production changes, or privileged administrative operations.

Examples include separating:

- AI use-case request and final approval.
- AI agent development and production authorization.
- Data access approval and independent compliance review.
- AI-generated financial analysis and final transaction approval.
- Policy exception requests and exception authorization.
- Control operation and independent assurance.

The organization should assess whether the combination of AI capabilities and user permissions could allow one individual to initiate, execute, and approve a high-impact activity without appropriate oversight.

Where full separation is impractical, compensating controls should be documented and approved.

**Control evidence** may include role-conflict assessments, approval workflows, access matrices, and records of compensating controls.

### 40. AI Acceptable Use Tool-Inventory Control

The organization must maintain visibility into the AI tools authorized for organizational use.

The inventory should distinguish approved tools from restricted tools, tools awaiting assessment, and prohibited services.

For each relevant tool, the inventory should identify:

- Tool and provider.
- Business owner.
- Approved business purposes.
- Authorized user groups.
- Permitted data classifications.
- Available integrations.
- Relevant contractual conditions.
- Security and privacy assessment status.
- Approval status.
- Review date.
- Applicable usage restrictions.

The inventory should account for AI capabilities embedded within existing applications, not only standalone AI products.

Where different configurations provide materially different capabilities or data-processing conditions, those configurations should be distinguished.

**Control evidence** may include the approved AI catalogue, procurement records, architecture inventories, and documented approval decisions.

### 41. AI Acceptable Use Access-Authorization Control

Access to AI systems must be granted according to business need, role, and approved usage conditions.

Access authorization should distinguish ordinary application access from permissions to use sensitive datasets, administrative functions, external integrations, or autonomous capabilities.

The organization should implement appropriate identity and access management controls, including:

- Unique user identities.
- Strong authentication.
- Role-based or attribute-based authorization.
- Least-privilege permissions.
- Access approval.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Timely access revocation.

Where AI tools support enterprise single sign-on or centralized identity management, these capabilities should be used where appropriate.

Access should be removed when a user leaves the organization, changes roles, or no longer requires the relevant AI capability.

**Control evidence** may include access requests, approval records, identity-provider logs, entitlement reviews, and revocation records.

### 42. AI Acceptable Use Business-Purpose Control

The organization must establish that AI use is associated with a legitimate and authorized business purpose.

The required level of documentation should be proportionate to the use case's risk.

Low-risk productivity activities may operate under general policy authorization, while material business processes or high-impact AI applications may require a documented use case and formal approval.

The control should prevent users from expanding an approved activity into a materially different purpose without reassessment.

For example, authorization to summarize internal documents should not automatically permit using the same AI tool to determine employee performance ratings.

**Control evidence** may include use-case registrations, business-owner approvals, process documentation, and records of changes in intended purpose.

### 43. AI Acceptable Use Data-Classification Control

Users must apply the organization's data classification rules when selecting AI tools and deciding what information to submit.

The control should establish how data classification affects the permitted AI environment and processing conditions.

Implementation may include:

- Classification labels.
- User-facing handling guidance.
- Data-loss prevention rules.
- Upload restrictions.
- Repository-level permissions.
- Sensitive-data detection.
- Approved processing environments.
- Escalation procedures for uncertain classifications.

Users should not assume that information is safe to disclose simply because it was copied from an internal system or summarized by another tool.

Where classification is unclear, the user should follow the organization's established guidance or seek authorization before processing the information.

**Control evidence** may include classification policies, DLP configurations, access rules, and records of exception decisions.

### 44. AI Acceptable Use Confidential-Information Control

The organization must prevent confidential information from being disclosed to AI systems that are not authorized to process it.

The control should cover information entered through prompts, uploaded documents, connected repositories, APIs, plugins, and automated workflows.

Confidentiality restrictions should be based on the information's sensitivity and the approved processing environment.

Technical measures may include:

- Restrictions on external AI services.
- Upload blocking for designated data categories.
- Approved enterprise AI environments.
- DLP inspection.
- Repository access restrictions.
- Data-flow controls.
- Provider configuration requirements.

Users must not attempt to bypass restrictions by splitting confidential information across multiple prompts or paraphrasing it to evade detection.

**Control evidence** may include data-handling rules, approved environment configurations, DLP events, and documented investigations.

### 45. AI Acceptable Use Personal-Data Processing Control

AI processing of personal data must comply with the organization's privacy requirements and applicable legal obligations.

The control should ensure that personal data is processed only for authorized purposes and through approved environments.

Depending on the use case, safeguards may include:

- Data minimization.
- Appropriate access restrictions.
- Approved retention periods.
- Privacy review.
- Anonymization or pseudonymization where appropriate.
- Restrictions on provider reuse.
- Appropriate transparency and disclosure.
- Additional safeguards for sensitive personal data.

The organization should not assume that all AI processing of personal data is prohibited. Instead, it should establish conditions under which processing is permitted and identify uses requiring additional assessment or prohibition.

**Control evidence** may include privacy assessments, approved processing records, data-flow documentation, and relevant configuration settings.

### 46. AI Acceptable Use Credentials-and-Secrets Control

Users must not submit passwords, authentication tokens, API keys, private keys, recovery codes, or other secrets to unauthorized AI systems.

Secrets may be disclosed through prompts, uploaded files, debugging sessions, source-code examples, screenshots, or connected development tools.

The control should establish secure alternatives for AI-assisted troubleshooting and development.

These may include:

- Synthetic or redacted examples.
- Approved secret-management systems.
- Automated secret scanning.
- Secure development environments.
- Restricted logging.
- Credential rotation after suspected exposure.

If a secret is submitted to an unauthorized system, the organization should assess the exposure and initiate the applicable incident-response process.

Simply deleting the prompt does not establish that the secret has been removed from provider logs, backups, or other retained data.

**Control evidence** may include secret-scanning results, approved development procedures, incident records, and credential-rotation evidence.

### 47. AI Acceptable Use Intellectual-Property Control

The organization must establish acceptable-use requirements for proprietary materials, copyrighted works, licensed datasets, and AI-generated content.

Users should follow the applicable rules governing the disclosure, transformation, reuse, and external distribution of protected materials.

Controls may include:

- Restrictions on uploading proprietary source code.
- Approved AI coding environments.
- License and attribution checks.
- Review of generated content intended for publication.
- Controls over third-party datasets.
- Restrictions on unauthorized reproduction.
- Legal review for material intellectual-property concerns.

The policy should distinguish between permission to use an AI tool and permission to disclose or reproduce a particular work.

**Control evidence** may include licensing records, content-review procedures, tool approvals, and documented intellectual-property assessments.

### 48. AI Acceptable Use External-Provider Control

The organization must assess whether an external AI provider is authorized to process the information and perform the function required by the use case.

The assessment should consider relevant contractual, security, privacy, operational, and data-governance requirements.

Depending on the service, the organization should evaluate:

- Data retention and deletion.
- Use of customer inputs for model training.
- Provider access to submitted information.
- Data location and transfer.
- Subprocessors.
- Authentication and access controls.
- Security incident notification.
- Audit and assurance information.
- Service continuity.
- Exit and data-portability arrangements.

Users must not independently accept material provider terms or enable processing options that conflict with organizational requirements.

**Control evidence** may include supplier assessments, contract reviews, data-processing agreements, approved provider records, and configuration reviews.

### 49. AI Acceptable Use Prompt-Security Control

Users should construct prompts and interact with AI systems in ways that protect organizational information and preserve approved usage boundaries.

Prompt-security requirements should address both user behavior and the technical limitations of the AI system.

Users should not:

- Enter unauthorized secrets or confidential data.
- Attempt to bypass established access restrictions.
- Instruct an AI system to perform unauthorized activities.
- Follow instructions embedded in untrusted content without appropriate validation.
- Treat retrieved documents as inherently trustworthy instructions.
- Use prompt manipulation to evade policy restrictions.

For AI systems that process external documents, web content, or retrieved information, the organization should consider prompt-injection risks and ensure that untrusted content cannot override security policies or authorization boundaries.

Users should understand that prompt wording alone is not a substitute for technical access controls.

**Control evidence** may include user guidance, prompt-security training, AI application configurations, and relevant security testing results.

### 50. AI Acceptable Use Output-Verification Control

The organization must establish verification requirements for AI-generated outputs according to their intended use and potential impact.

Verification requirements should be explicit for consequential or externally distributed content.

Examples include:

- Verifying facts against authoritative sources.
- Checking calculations independently.
- Validating citations and quotations.
- Reviewing generated code.
- Confirming legal and regulatory references.
- Checking the completeness of security analysis.
- Assessing potential bias or discriminatory outcomes.
- Confirming that confidential information has not been included inadvertently.

The control should identify when qualified human review, independent validation, or formal approval is required.

The organization should not rely on a generic instruction to “check the output” where the consequences of an error are significant. The required review should be appropriate to the activity.

**Control evidence** may include review records, testing results, approval records, and documented verification procedures.

### 51. AI Acceptable Use Bias-and-Fairness Control

AI-assisted activities must not bypass applicable organizational requirements for fair treatment, nondiscrimination, and responsible decision-making.

Users should not assume that an AI-generated recommendation is neutral merely because it is produced by a computational system.

Where AI supports decisions involving individuals or groups, the organization should determine whether specialized assessment and safeguards are required.

These may include:

- Review of decision criteria.
- Validation of relevant data.
- Assessment of potential discriminatory effects.
- Qualified human oversight.
- Appropriate explanation and documentation.
- Escalation of suspected unfair outcomes.

Users should not use AI outputs as the sole basis for consequential decisions when organizational rules require additional review.

**Control evidence** may include impact assessments, testing records, decision-review procedures, and documented remediation.

### 52. AI Acceptable Use Transparency-and-Disclosure Control

The organization must define when users are required to disclose AI involvement in creating content, performing analysis, or supporting a business process.

Disclosure requirements should reflect applicable law, contractual obligations, organizational commitments, and the nature of the activity.

Potential disclosure contexts include:

- Customer-facing content.
- Published research or reports.
- AI-generated media.
- Material business recommendations.
- Formal submissions.
- Decisions where AI involvement must be documented.
- Content subject to specific transparency requirements.

The policy should avoid both indiscriminate disclosure requirements and unjustified concealment of material AI involvement.

Users should not falsely represent AI-generated content as independently verified or claim that a person performed a review that did not occur.

**Control evidence** may include disclosure procedures, content-review records, customer communications, and process documentation.

### 53. AI Acceptable Use Automated-Decision Control

The organization must establish additional requirements for AI use in consequential decisions.

The policy should distinguish between AI used for preliminary analysis and AI used to determine or materially influence an outcome.

Potentially consequential contexts include:

- Employment.
- Credit or financial eligibility.
- Access to essential services.
- Customer eligibility.
- Security enforcement.
- Fraud determinations.
- Regulatory decisions.
- Other activities with material effects on individuals or the organization.

Controls should determine whether the use case requires formal assessment, human review, explanation, documentation, or additional authorization.

Users must not assume that an AI recommendation may be adopted automatically simply because it is available through an approved application.

**Control evidence** may include decision-authority matrices, impact assessments, approval records, and human-review documentation.

### 54. AI Acceptable Use AI-Generated-Code Control

AI-generated code must be treated as unverified code until it has passed the organization's applicable development and security requirements.

Users should not copy AI-generated code directly into production merely because it appears syntactically correct or resembles a familiar implementation.

The control should address:

- Secure coding requirements.
- Dependency validation.
- License compliance.
- Secret detection.
- Static analysis.
- Software composition analysis.
- Testing.
- Peer review.
- Change approval.
- Production deployment authorization.

AI-generated scripts and commands should receive particular scrutiny when they can modify infrastructure, access sensitive information, or execute privileged operations.

The organization should also prohibit submitting proprietary source code to unauthorized AI coding services.

**Control evidence** may include code-review records, CI/CD results, security scan reports, dependency checks, and deployment approvals.

### 55. AI Acceptable Use Third-Party-Integration Control

Users must not create or enable AI integrations with enterprise systems without the required authorization.

Integrations may introduce additional data flows, access privileges, and opportunities for unintended actions.

The control should require appropriate review of:

- Integration purpose.
- Data access.
- Permission scope.
- Authentication.
- External data transfers.
- Logging.
- Revocation.
- Provider responsibilities.
- Potential impact of misuse.

For example, connecting an AI assistant to an enterprise document repository may expose substantially more information than submitting a single approved document manually.

Integration permissions should be limited to the specific business need, and users should not grant broad repository or administrative access merely for convenience.

**Control evidence** may include integration inventories, approval records, OAuth permission reviews, API access records, and architecture assessments.

### 56. AI Acceptable Use Agent-Authorization Control

AI agents must operate within explicitly authorized boundaries.

An agent that can call tools, access enterprise resources, or execute transactions requires more stringent controls than a system that only generates suggestions.

The control should define:

- Authorized agent identity.
- Permitted tools.
- Approved data sources.
- Allowed operations.
- Restricted actions.
- Execution environment.
- Human-approval conditions.
- Transaction limits.
- Logging requirements.
- Emergency suspension mechanisms.

Users must not grant agents permissions that exceed their own legitimate authority or the approved business purpose.

Agent permissions should be limited by technical controls rather than relying exclusively on natural-language instructions to behave safely.

**Control evidence** may include agent configuration records, tool allowlists, permission reviews, approval workflows, and execution logs.

### 57. AI Acceptable Use Human-Approval Control

The organization must define when AI-supported actions require human approval before execution.

Human approval should be based on the action's potential impact, reversibility, uncertainty, and delegated authority.

Approval may be required for:

- Financial transactions.
- Production infrastructure changes.
- Privileged access modifications.
- External communications with material consequences.
- Deletion of important records.
- Changes to security configurations.
- High-impact decisions.
- Irreversible or difficult-to-reverse actions.

The approval mechanism should provide sufficient context for the reviewer to understand the proposed action, its consequences, and any relevant supporting evidence.

A nominal approval button is insufficient if the reviewer cannot meaningfully evaluate what the AI intends to do.

**Control evidence** may include approval logs, transaction records, reviewer identity, execution results, and documented approval criteria.

### 58. AI Acceptable Use Logging-and-Traceability Control

The organization should maintain appropriate records of AI usage where necessary for security, accountability, compliance, and operational investigation.

Logging requirements should reflect the AI system's risk and the sensitivity of the information involved.

Relevant records may include:

- User or service identity.
- AI application or model identifier.
- Timestamp.
- Use case or transaction identifier.
- Tool invocation.
- Authorization decision.
- Approval record.
- Relevant system changes.
- Error or security event.
- Outcome of the operation.

The organization should avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive content merely because the AI platform can log it.

Logs must be protected against unauthorized access, alteration, and inappropriate disclosure.

**Control evidence** may include logging configurations, retention settings, access reviews, and samples of traceable AI operations.

### 59. AI Acceptable Use Incident-Reporting Control

Users must promptly report suspected AI-related security, privacy, confidentiality, or policy incidents through established organizational channels.

Reportable events may include:

- Accidental submission of confidential information.
- Exposure of credentials.
- Unauthorized use of an external AI service.
- Suspicious AI-generated commands.
- Prompt-injection exploitation.
- Unauthorized agent execution.
- Unexpected disclosure of information.
- Misuse of AI-generated content.
- Suspected data poisoning or model manipulation.
- Materially harmful AI output.
- Unauthorized integration or access.

The reporting process should provide clear instructions and avoid requiring users to determine the complete technical cause before escalating.

Users should preserve relevant evidence and avoid actions that could worsen the incident, such as continuing to share exposed information or deleting records needed for investigation.

**Control evidence** may include incident tickets, response records, escalation timestamps, investigation findings, and corrective-action tracking.

### 60. AI Acceptable Use Exception-Control

Exceptions to the AI AUP must follow a formal authorization process.

Users should not treat managerial convenience, urgency, or business benefit as sufficient authority to disregard policy requirements.

An exception request should identify:

- The requirement being waived or modified.
- The business justification.
- The proposed duration.
- The affected systems and data.
- The associated risks.
- Existing and proposed safeguards.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approving authority.
- The expiration and review conditions.

Some restrictions may be non-waivable because of law, contractual obligations, or fundamental security requirements.

Exceptions should be time-bound where appropriate and subject to reassessment when circumstances change.

**Control evidence** may include exception requests, risk assessments, approvals, expiration records, and closure evidence.

### 61. AI Acceptable Use Monitoring Control

The organization should monitor AI usage to identify violations, emerging risks, and deviations from approved operating conditions.

Monitoring should be based on risk and should comply with applicable privacy, employment, and monitoring requirements.

Potential monitoring mechanisms include:

- Enterprise AI platform telemetry.
- Identity and access logs.
- Cloud application discovery.
- DLP alerts.
- Endpoint security telemetry.
- API gateway logs.
- Agent execution records.
- Software inventory data.
- Procurement records.
- Security information and event management (SIEM) integrations.

Monitoring should focus on actionable indicators rather than collecting unrestricted user content without a defined purpose.

Examples of useful indicators include attempts to access prohibited services, unusual data transfers, unauthorized integrations, excessive agent permissions, and repeated policy violations.

**Control evidence** may include monitoring rules, alert records, investigation outcomes, and documented tuning decisions.

### 62. AI Acceptable Use Enforcement Control

The organization must define proportionate consequences and corrective actions for violations of the AI AUP.

Enforcement should distinguish between accidental mistakes, repeated negligence, deliberate circumvention, malicious misuse, and violations involving significant harm.

Possible responses include:

- User education.
- Mandatory retraining.
- Removal of specific AI privileges.
- Temporary suspension of AI access.
- Restriction of an integration.
- Incident investigation.
- Corrective or disciplinary action under applicable organizational procedures.
- Contractual remedies for third parties.
- Referral to appropriate legal or regulatory processes where required.

Enforcement decisions should follow established organizational procedures and be applied consistently.

The organization should also examine whether repeated violations indicate unclear policy language, inadequate training, insufficient technical controls, or an impractical approval process.

**Control evidence** may include investigation records, remediation plans, access-change records, and enforcement decisions.

### 63. AI Acceptable Use Compliance-Assessment Control

The organization should periodically assess whether AI usage complies with the AUP.

Assessment methods may include:

- Sample-based reviews.
- Access and entitlement reviews.
- AI inventory reconciliation.
- Configuration assessments.
- DLP trend analysis.
- Incident trend analysis.
- User attestations.
- Internal audits.
- Control testing.
- Targeted reviews of high-risk use cases.

Assessment criteria should be defined in advance so that findings can be evaluated consistently.

The assessment should identify control failures, policy ambiguities, unauthorized uses, and areas where the organization lacks sufficient visibility.

**Control evidence** may include assessment reports, test results, findings, assigned actions, and documented remediation.

### 64. AI Acceptable Use Remediation Control

Policy violations and control deficiencies must be assigned to accountable owners and remediated according to their severity.

Remediation may require changes to:

- User permissions.
- AI application configurations.
- Data-handling procedures.
- Training materials.
- Approval workflows.
- Monitoring rules.
- Integration permissions.
- Policy language.
- Technical enforcement mechanisms.

Each finding should have a defined remediation action, accountable owner, target date, and closure criterion.

High-risk findings should be escalated when they exceed established remediation timelines or require formal risk acceptance.

Closure should be supported by evidence that the corrective action was implemented and, where appropriate, tested.

**Control evidence** may include issue-tracking records, configuration changes, validation results, and closure approvals.

### 65. AI Acceptable Use Retention-and-Deletion Control

The organization should establish requirements for retaining and deleting information associated with AI use.

Requirements should reflect applicable records management, privacy, contractual, security, and operational obligations.

Relevant information may include:

- AI interaction records.
- Uploaded documents.
- Generated outputs.
- Agent execution logs.
- Approval records.
- Incident evidence.
- Business records derived from AI outputs.
- Provider-retained information.

Users should not assume that deleting an interaction from their own interface deletes all provider-side copies, logs, backups, or records.

The organization should use approved retention and deletion mechanisms and verify that provider capabilities meet applicable requirements.

**Control evidence** may include retention schedules, provider configurations, deletion procedures, and verification records.

### 66. AI Acceptable Use External-Publication Control

AI-generated material intended for external distribution must undergo the review required for the intended publication or communication.

External distribution may include:

- Customer deliverables.
- Public reports.
- Website content.
- Marketing materials.
- Regulatory submissions.
- Investor communications.
- Technical publications.
- Software releases.

Review should consider accuracy, confidentiality, intellectual-property restrictions, privacy, misleading claims, and applicable disclosure obligations.

High-impact or regulated content may require approval from legal, compliance, communications, or another designated authority.

The organization should not treat AI-generated content as approved for publication merely because it was produced using an authorized tool.

**Control evidence** may include editorial reviews, approval workflows, publication records, and content validation results.

### 67. AI Acceptable Use Third-Party-Data Control

Users must respect restrictions governing information received from customers, suppliers, business partners, and other third parties.

The organization may have contractual obligations that restrict where and how such information can be processed.

The control should require users to determine whether the relevant agreement permits the intended AI processing and whether the selected provider and environment satisfy applicable conditions.

Examples include:

- Customer source code.
- Supplier pricing.
- Partner research.
- Confidential proposals.
- Licensed datasets.
- Outsourced service records.
- Third-party security reports.

Users should not infer that information may be submitted to an external AI service merely because the organization possesses a copy.

**Control evidence** may include contract requirements, data-processing assessments, approved use-case records, and documented authorization.

### 68. AI Acceptable Use Third-Party-Output Control

AI outputs derived from external or third-party sources must be evaluated before being relied upon or redistributed.

Potential risks include inaccurate attribution, fabricated citations, disclosure of protected information, malicious instructions, and licensing restrictions.

The control should require appropriate verification of source reliability and suitability for the intended use.

Where AI summarizes external documents or retrieves information from external sources, users should distinguish the system's generated interpretation from the source material itself.

For security-sensitive workflows, untrusted retrieved content should not be allowed to override system permissions or trigger unauthorized actions.

**Control evidence** may include source-validation records, content-review procedures, retrieval-security assessments, and approval records.

### 69. AI Acceptable Use Change-Control Requirement

Material changes to an AI use case should trigger reassessment when they alter the activity's risk or authorization boundaries.

Relevant changes may include:

- New data sources.
- Increased data sensitivity.
- Additional integrations.
- Expanded user populations.
- New model capabilities.
- Increased autonomy.
- New external communication functions.
- Changes in provider terms.
- New business purposes.
- Access to production or privileged systems.

The organization should not assume that approval of the original use case covers every future modification.

Changes should be evaluated against the organization's established risk, security, privacy, and approval requirements.

**Control evidence** may include change requests, reassessment records, revised approvals, and updated inventory entries.

### 70. AI Acceptable Use Control-Testing Requirement

AI AUP controls should be tested to determine whether they are implemented correctly and operate as intended.

Testing should examine both control design and operating effectiveness.

Examples include:

- Attempting to access prohibited AI services in a controlled test.
- Verifying restrictions on sensitive data uploads.
- Reviewing whether unauthorized integrations are detected.
- Confirming that access is revoked when required.
- Testing approval gates for agent actions.
- Checking whether policy exceptions expire as intended.
- Validating the completeness of relevant audit logs.
- Reviewing whether users receive required training.

Tests must be authorized and conducted without unnecessarily exposing sensitive information or disrupting production systems.

**Control evidence** may include test plans, test results, identified deficiencies, remediation records, and retest results.

### 71. AI Acceptable Use Metrics-and-Reporting Control

The organization should define measurable indicators to evaluate AUP implementation and identify emerging risks.

Potential indicators include:

- Percentage of applicable users completing training.
- Percentage of required policy acknowledgments completed.
- Number of approved AI tools.
- Number of unauthorized AI tools identified.
- Number of AI-related policy incidents.
- Number of overdue remediation actions.
- Number of active policy exceptions.
- Percentage of exceptions reviewed before expiration.
- Percentage of high-risk AI use cases with documented authorization.
- Percentage of applicable AI integrations reviewed.
- Repeat-violation rate.
- Median time to contain AI-related policy incidents.

Metrics should be interpreted in context. For example, a rise in reported incidents may reflect improved detection and reporting rather than a deterioration in actual behavior.

**Control evidence** may include dashboards, metric definitions, management reports, and documented actions taken in response to trends.

### 72. Core AI Acceptable Use Control Principle

The AI AUP control framework must translate policy expectations into measurable and enforceable organizational requirements.

Each material requirement should have an accountable owner, an implementation mechanism, appropriate monitoring, evidence of operation, and a defined response when the requirement fails.

```
AI AUP Requirement
        │
        ▼
Control Objective
        │
        ▼
Preventive / Detective /
Corrective Mechanism
        │
        ▼
Assigned Control Owner
        │
        ▼
Monitoring and Testing
        │
        ▼
Evidence and Reporting
        │
        ▼
Finding and Remediation
        │
        ▼
Validated Control Effectiveness
```

The control framework should preserve the following distinctions:

- **Policy requirement ≠ technical implementation**
- **Control implementation ≠ operating effectiveness**
- **User acknowledgment ≠ demonstrated understanding**
- **Approved tool ≠ unrestricted use**
- **Access authorization ≠ data-processing authorization**
- **Monitoring ≠ indiscriminate surveillance**
- **Incident reporting ≠ incident remediation**
- **Exception approval ≠ permanent exemption**
- **Control testing ≠ independent assurance**
- **Metric availability ≠ meaningful risk measurement**
- **Corrective action ≠ verified closure**
- **AI-generated output ≠ validated business evidence**
- **Human approval ≠ effective oversight without adequate information**
- **Control existence ≠ proof that the control operates consistently**

**Core Principle:**

> **An effective AI Acceptable Use Policy control framework converts user-facing rules into enforceable, testable, and auditable safeguards. Its effectiveness depends on the alignment of authorization, data protection, human accountability, technical restrictions, monitoring, incident response, and evidence-based remediation.**
