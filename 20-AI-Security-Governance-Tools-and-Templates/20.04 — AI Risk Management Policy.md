# 20.04 — AI Risk Management Policy

## Part 1 — AI Risk Management Policy Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Risk Management Policy

An AI Risk Management Policy establishes the organizational principles, responsibilities, decision-making requirements, and mandatory processes for identifying, assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting risks associated with artificial intelligence systems throughout their lifecycle.

The policy defines how the organization determines whether an AI system is suitable for a proposed purpose, which risks require additional safeguards, who may authorize residual risk, and under what circumstances an AI activity must be restricted, suspended, or discontinued.

Unlike a general enterprise risk policy, an AI Risk Management Policy must address risk characteristics arising from AI-specific capabilities and limitations, including probabilistic outputs, model uncertainty, data dependency, changing behavior, limited explainability, automation, and interactions between AI components and external systems.

The policy should cover risks associated with:

- AI strategy and business adoption.
- AI system design and development.
- Data collection, processing, and governance.
- Model selection, training, testing, and deployment.
- Generative AI and large language models.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).
- AI agents and autonomous workflows.
- Third-party AI providers and model supply chains.
- Cybersecurity, privacy, and intellectual property.
- Operational reliability and business continuity.
- Human oversight and consequential decision-making.
- Legal, regulatory, ethical, and reputational exposure.

The policy should establish a consistent organizational approach without assuming that every AI system has the same risk profile.

For example, an AI assistant used to improve the wording of a non-sensitive internal document may require relatively limited risk-management activities. An AI agent authorized to modify production infrastructure or make consequential customer decisions requires substantially stronger assessment, approval, monitoring, and intervention mechanisms.

The fundamental objective is to ensure that AI adoption remains aligned with organizational objectives, risk appetite, legal obligations, and the organization's capacity to manage foreseeable adverse outcomes.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Risk Management Policy

The purpose of the AI Risk Management Policy is to establish a formal and repeatable process for managing AI-related uncertainty and preventing unacceptable outcomes.

The policy should enable the organization to make informed decisions about whether to adopt, develop, deploy, operate, modify, or retire an AI system.

Its primary objectives include the following.

**Risk identification**

Establish processes for identifying AI-related threats, vulnerabilities, limitations, uncertainties, dependencies, and potential adverse consequences.

**Risk assessment**

Evaluate the likelihood, severity, scope, and potential impact of identified risks using defined assessment criteria.

**Risk treatment**

Select and implement appropriate safeguards, redesign measures, restrictions, monitoring mechanisms, or other treatment options.

**Risk acceptance**

Define who may accept residual risk, within which limits, and subject to what documentation and approval requirements.

**Lifecycle monitoring**

Ensure that risk assessments remain relevant as models, datasets, integrations, users, providers, and operating conditions change.

**Accountability**

Assign risk ownership and establish clear responsibilities for assessment, treatment, oversight, escalation, and assurance.

**Regulatory and business alignment**

Integrate AI risk management with enterprise risk management, information security, privacy, compliance, business continuity, and applicable legal obligations.

**Continuous improvement**

Use incidents, testing, monitoring, audits, and operational feedback to improve the effectiveness of risk-management processes.

The policy should establish a decision framework rather than merely require risk registers or assessment documents to be completed.

A risk assessment is useful only when its findings influence authorization, safeguards, operating conditions, and the decision to proceed with the AI activity.

### 3. AI Risk Management as an Enterprise Governance Control

AI risk management should operate as an integrated component of enterprise risk management rather than as an isolated technical exercise.

The policy should establish how AI risks are identified and evaluated, how they are incorporated into organizational risk reporting, and how decisions are escalated when exposure exceeds approved tolerance.

The framework should connect business objectives, AI use cases, risk assessments, controls, residual-risk decisions, and ongoing monitoring.

The policy should align with the organization's existing risk-management arrangements, including:

- Enterprise risk management.
- Information security risk management.
- AI governance.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Third-party risk management.
- Operational risk management.
- Business continuity and resilience.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Internal audit and assurance.

AI risks should be recorded and escalated through the appropriate enterprise processes when they have material business implications.

For example, an AI system that introduces a material risk of unauthorized disclosure may require both an AI risk assessment and treatment through the organization's information security risk-management process.

The organization should avoid creating disconnected risk records that describe the same underlying exposure differently without clear ownership or reconciliation.

### 4. Scope of the AI Risk Management Policy

The policy should apply to AI systems and AI-enabled activities that the organization develops, acquires, deploys, operates, uses, or materially influences.

Its scope should cover the AI lifecycle, from initial business justification through retirement and decommissioning.

Relevant activities may include:

- AI use-case identification.
- Business and technical feasibility assessment.
- Data acquisition and preparation.
- Model selection and development.
- Fine-tuning and adaptation.
- Security and performance testing.
- Deployment and integration.
- Operational use and monitoring.
- Model and configuration changes.
- Incident response.
- Risk reassessment.
- Retirement and data disposition.

The policy should address internally developed systems, commercial AI products, externally hosted models, embedded AI capabilities, and third-party services.

It should also cover AI agents and automated workflows that interact with enterprise systems or make decisions on behalf of users.

Scope should be determined by the organization's relationship to the AI activity, its access to relevant information, and its ability to influence or control the associated risk.

The organization should define exclusions explicitly and ensure that an exclusion does not inadvertently remove material risks from oversight.

### 5. Organizational Applicability

The AI Risk Management Policy should apply to organizational functions that develop, acquire, authorize, operate, oversee, or use AI systems.

Applicable parties may include:

- Executive management.
- Business owners.
- AI product and system owners.
- Information security.
- Enterprise risk management.
- AI governance.
- Data governance.
- Privacy and legal functions.
- IT and cloud operations.
- Software engineering and MLOps teams.
- Procurement and supplier management.
- Internal audit.
- Employees and contractors.
- Relevant third-party service providers.

Each function should understand its role in identifying and managing AI-related risks.

For example, procurement may assess supplier assurance, information security may evaluate technical threats, privacy may assess personal-data processing, and the business owner may remain accountable for the intended use and resulting business exposure.

The organization should ensure that responsibilities are assigned before an AI system is authorized for material operational use.

Where several functions contribute to a risk assessment, the policy should identify the accountable risk owner and define how conflicting assessments or recommendations are resolved.

### 6. AI Risk Management Policy Ownership

The AI Risk Management Policy should have a formally designated owner responsible for maintaining the policy and coordinating its implementation.

Ownership may reside with the enterprise risk management function, AI governance function, information security governance function, or another formally authorized organizational function.

The policy owner should be responsible for:

- Maintaining the policy framework.
- Defining risk-management procedures.
- Coordinating stakeholder reviews.
- Maintaining risk-assessment requirements.
- Establishing review and escalation criteria.
- Monitoring policy implementation.
- Coordinating material revisions.
- Reporting significant implementation deficiencies.
- Supporting consistency across business functions.

Policy ownership should be distinguished from responsibility for individual AI risks.

The policy owner maintains the governing framework, while business or system owners remain accountable for the risks arising from their specific AI activities.

For example, the AI risk policy owner may define the organization's residual-risk acceptance procedure, but a designated business risk owner remains responsible for ensuring that the procedure is followed for a particular AI deployment.

### 7. AI Risk Management Policy Approval Authority

The AI Risk Management Policy should be approved by an authority with sufficient responsibility to establish mandatory organizational risk-management requirements.

Depending on the organization's structure, approval may involve executive management, the enterprise risk committee, the AI governance committee, or another designated authority.

Approval should confirm that the policy is consistent with the organization's risk appetite, governance model, and applicable obligations.

The approval process should establish:

- Policy owner.
- Approving authority.
- Version number.
- Approval date.
- Effective date.
- Review date.
- Applicable organizational scope.
- Related policies and standards.
- Superseded versions, where applicable.

The policy should also establish who may approve material changes to risk criteria, assessment methods, and acceptance thresholds.

Individual business owners should not be permitted to redefine enterprise risk appetite or approve risks outside their delegated authority.

Material changes should be documented and communicated to affected functions.

### 8. AI Risk Management Principles

The policy should establish principles that guide AI risk decisions consistently across the organization.

**Risk-based governance**

The depth of assessment and control should reflect the nature, likelihood, and potential consequences of the risk.

**Lifecycle accountability**

Risk management should continue throughout the AI lifecycle rather than ending when a system is approved for deployment.

**Proportionality**

Assessment effort and safeguards should be proportionate to the system's capabilities, exposure, and potential impact.

**Human accountability**

A named person or organizational function should remain accountable for risk decisions, even when AI systems perform automated activities.

**Evidence-based decisions**

Risk assessments should rely on relevant information, documented assumptions, appropriate testing, and defensible reasoning.

**Defense in depth**

Material risks should be addressed through complementary safeguards rather than relying on a single control.

**Transparency and traceability**

Material risk decisions, assumptions, approvals, and treatment actions should be documented sufficiently to support review and assurance.

**Continuous monitoring**

Risk assessments should be revisited when operating conditions, system capabilities, threats, or business requirements change.

**Escalation of unacceptable risk**

Activities that exceed approved tolerance should be escalated, restricted, redesigned, or stopped according to established decision authority.

**Independent challenge**

Material risk assessments and acceptance decisions should be subject to appropriate oversight and challenge.

These principles should be translated into practical assessment criteria, approval requirements, and operational procedures.

### 9. Definition of AI Risk

AI risk is the potential for an AI-related event, behavior, decision, or failure to produce an adverse consequence for individuals, the organization, customers, third parties, or society.

Risk may arise from the AI model itself, the data it processes, the application that integrates it, the environment in which it operates, or the way people use and interpret its outputs.

AI risk may involve:

- Confidentiality loss.
- Unauthorized access.
- Data corruption.
- Privacy violations.
- Inaccurate or misleading outputs.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Security compromise.
- Operational disruption.
- Financial loss.
- Legal or regulatory consequences.
- Reputational damage.
- Unsafe automated actions.
- Loss of human control.
- Supplier dependency.
- Failure to meet intended performance.

AI risk should not be equated exclusively with cybersecurity risk.

A model may be secure against unauthorized access yet produce materially inaccurate recommendations. Conversely, a highly accurate model may operate within an insecure environment that exposes confidential information.

The policy should therefore establish an integrated approach that considers technical, operational, human, legal, ethical, and business dimensions where relevant.

### 10. AI Risk Categories

The organization should define a risk taxonomy that enables consistent identification, classification, ownership, and reporting of AI risks.

The taxonomy should reflect the organization's activities and exposure.

| Risk category            | Representative concerns                                               |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strategic                | Misalignment with business objectives or AI strategy                  |
| Governance               | Unclear ownership, authority, or oversight                            |
| Cybersecurity            | Unauthorized access, data leakage, prompt injection, model theft      |
| Data                     | Poor quality, unauthorized processing, poisoning, excessive retention |
| Model                    | Inaccuracy, instability, drift, limited reliability                   |
| Operational              | Service disruption, process failure, inadequate recovery              |
| Privacy                  | Unlawful or inappropriate personal-data processing                    |
| Legal and regulatory     | Noncompliance with applicable obligations                             |
| Ethical and human impact | Discrimination, unfairness, harmful outcomes                          |
| Third-party              | Provider failure, weak assurance, concentration risk                  |
| Financial                | Unexpected cost, fraud, loss, or liability                            |
| Reputational             | Loss of trust or public confidence                                    |
| Agentic and automation   | Unauthorized actions, excessive autonomy, tool misuse                 |
| Intellectual property    | Unauthorized disclosure or infringement                               |
| Resilience               | Inability to maintain or restore critical AI services                 |

Risk categories should support analysis rather than force every risk into a single exclusive classification.

A single event may affect multiple categories. For example, an AI agent that discloses customer records through an unauthorized integration may create cybersecurity, privacy, contractual, operational, and reputational risks simultaneously.

The organization should establish a primary risk owner while identifying the supporting functions responsible for related dimensions.

### 11. AI Risk Versus AI Uncertainty

The organization should distinguish between a risk assessment and the uncertainty that limits confidence in that assessment.

Risk assessment estimates potential adverse outcomes using available information, assumptions, and defined methods.

Uncertainty arises when important information is incomplete, ambiguous, variable, or difficult to validate.

AI uncertainty may result from:

- Limited visibility into model behavior.
- Incomplete training information.
- Uncertain performance in unfamiliar contexts.
- Changing external conditions.
- Inadequate testing coverage.
- Unknown attack paths.
- Unclear provider practices.
- Limited operational history.
- Uncertain human interaction patterns.

The organization should document material uncertainties rather than presenting estimates as more precise than the evidence supports.

Where uncertainty is substantial, the organization may need additional testing, stronger safeguards, restricted deployment, human oversight, or a decision not to proceed.

For example, an AI agent with insufficient evidence of reliable behavior should not receive broad production permissions merely because no incident has yet been observed.

### 12. AI Risk Identification

Risk identification establishes the potential adverse events and conditions that could affect an AI system or its intended use.

The process should consider the complete system rather than focusing exclusively on the model.

Relevant components include:

- Users and administrators.
- Models and model providers.
- Training and inference data.
- Applications and APIs.
- Retrieval systems.
- Plugins and tools.
- Cloud infrastructure.
- External integrations.
- Business workflows.
- Human decision-makers.
- Suppliers and subcontractors.

Risk identification methods may include threat modeling, stakeholder workshops, architecture reviews, data-flow analysis, scenario analysis, security testing, incident analysis, and supplier assessments.

The organization should document the cause, event, and potential consequence of each material risk.

For example:

- **Cause:** An AI agent has excessive permissions.
- **Event:** The agent executes an unauthorized modification.
- **Consequence:** Production disruption, data loss, or unauthorized access.

This structure helps the organization identify where controls can prevent the event, limit its impact, or improve detection and recovery.

### 13. AI Risk Assessment

AI risk assessment evaluates identified risks according to defined criteria and available evidence.

The assessment should consider the likelihood of the relevant event and the severity of its potential consequences, while accounting for uncertainty and the effectiveness of existing controls.

The process should establish:

- Risk scenario.
- Affected assets and stakeholders.
- Inherent risk.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness.
- Residual risk.
- Assumptions and limitations.
- Treatment requirements.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Decision authority.

The assessment method should be appropriate to the risk.

A qualitative assessment may be suitable for an early-stage, low-impact use case, while a high-impact AI system may require more detailed quantitative evidence, specialist assessment, or structured scenario analysis.

Risk scores should support decisions rather than replace professional judgment.

A numerical score alone does not demonstrate that a risk is acceptable, particularly where mandatory obligations or potentially severe consequences are involved.

### 14. AI Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk associated with an AI activity before considering the effect of the relevant controls.

It reflects the characteristics of the use case, the system's capabilities, the data involved, and the potential consequences of failure.

Factors affecting inherent risk may include:

- Sensitivity of processed information.
- Number of affected individuals or systems.
- Degree of automation.
- Scope of access.
- Potential impact of incorrect outputs.
- Exposure to external threats.
- Reversibility of actions.
- Business criticality.
- Regulatory significance.
- Dependency on third-party services.

For example, an AI agent authorized to execute financial transactions may have high inherent risk because an erroneous or manipulated action could create direct financial loss.

Inherent risk should not be reduced simply because the organization has not yet implemented controls.

It provides a baseline for determining the level of safeguards, review, and oversight required.

### 15. AI Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after the relevant controls have been implemented and their effectiveness has been considered.

Residual risk should be evaluated using evidence about the controls rather than assuming that documented safeguards operate as intended.

The assessment should consider:

- Control design.
- Implementation status.
- Operating effectiveness.
- Known limitations.
- Remaining vulnerabilities.
- Human and operational dependencies.
- Uncertainty.
- Potential control failure.
- Recovery capabilities.

For example, an AI agent may initially present substantial risk because it can modify production resources. Least-privilege permissions, transaction limits, approval gates, monitoring, and tested suspension mechanisms may reduce that risk.

However, the risk does not automatically become low merely because these controls exist. Their effectiveness must be evaluated, and important failure scenarios must remain visible.

Residual risk should be compared against approved tolerance and escalated when it exceeds the relevant threshold.

### 16. AI Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is a formal decision by an authorized risk owner or delegated authority to retain a defined level of residual risk.

Acceptance should be based on an informed evaluation of the potential consequences, existing safeguards, business justification, and available alternatives.

A risk-acceptance record should identify:

- Risk description.
- Affected AI system or use case.
- Inherent and residual risk.
- Existing controls.
- Remaining weaknesses.
- Business rationale.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Approval authority.
- Conditions of acceptance.
- Review date.
- Expiration or reassessment trigger where appropriate.

Risk acceptance must remain within delegated authority and cannot override binding legal requirements or mandatory restrictions.

The organization should distinguish accepting residual risk from approving an otherwise unauthorized AI activity. A use case must first satisfy the applicable authorization requirements before it can proceed under an accepted risk decision.

### 17. AI Risk Appetite and Risk Tolerance

Risk appetite defines the broad level and types of risk the organization is willing to pursue or retain in support of its objectives.

Risk tolerance establishes the more specific boundaries within which individual risks may be managed.

The AI Risk Management Policy should translate these concepts into operational criteria.

For example, an organization may tolerate some uncertainty in an AI tool used for internal brainstorming while maintaining very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of regulated information or uncontrolled modification of production infrastructure.

Risk criteria should consider:

- Potential severity.
- Likelihood.
- Business criticality.
- Data sensitivity.
- Impact on individuals.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Reversibility.
- Control effectiveness.
- Uncertainty.

The organization should define escalation thresholds and specify which risks require senior approval or prohibition.

Risk appetite should guide decision-making but must not be used to justify violations of mandatory obligations.

### 18. AI Risk Assessment Methodology

The organization should establish a consistent methodology for assessing AI risks.

The methodology should define the assessment process, scoring criteria, evidence expectations, decision thresholds, and documentation requirements.

A typical assessment process may include:

1. Define the AI system and intended use.
2. Identify relevant stakeholders and affected assets.
3. Establish system boundaries and dependencies.
4. Identify foreseeable risk scenarios.
5. Evaluate inherent risk.
6. Identify existing controls.
7. Evaluate control effectiveness.
8. Determine residual risk.
9. Define treatment actions.
10. Obtain required approvals.
11. Establish monitoring and reassessment criteria.

The methodology should specify how likelihood and impact are evaluated and how uncertainty affects the final decision.

The organization should also define when specialist assessments are required, such as cybersecurity threat modeling, privacy impact assessment, AI impact assessment, or supplier security review.

Consistency should not eliminate professional judgment. Different AI systems may require different assessment techniques while remaining subject to the same overarching governance principles.

### 19. AI Risk Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood assessment estimates how plausibly a defined risk scenario may occur within the relevant operating context.

The assessment should consider the threat environment, exposure, system design, existing safeguards, historical evidence, and opportunities for misuse or failure.

Factors may include:

- Accessibility of the AI system.
- Exposure to external users.
- Attack surface.
- Model and application weaknesses.
- Data quality.
- Complexity of integrations.
- Level of autonomy.
- Frequency of use.
- Threat actor capability.
- Historical incidents.
- Effectiveness of preventive controls.

Where quantitative probabilities are unavailable, the organization may use defined qualitative categories.

The methodology should specify what each category means and what evidence supports the classification.

Likelihood should not be estimated solely from the absence of prior incidents. New AI capabilities may have limited operational history, and weaknesses may remain undetected.

### 20. AI Risk Impact Assessment

Impact assessment evaluates the consequences if a defined AI risk scenario occurs.

The assessment should consider the effect on the organization, affected individuals, customers, third parties, and relevant external stakeholders.

Potential impact dimensions include:

- Financial loss.
- Operational disruption.
- Confidentiality breach.
- Privacy harm.
- Legal liability.
- Regulatory consequences.
- Reputational damage.
- Safety implications.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Loss of critical services.
- Irreversible or difficult-to-reverse actions.

The organization should consider both direct and downstream consequences.

For example, an inaccurate AI-generated recommendation may initially appear to be a minor quality issue. If that recommendation is used in a consequential decision without verification, it may create substantial legal, financial, or human impact.

Impact criteria should reflect the organization's business context and applicable obligations.

### 21. AI Risk Scoring and Classification

Risk scoring provides a structured method for comparing risks and supporting treatment priorities.

A qualitative approach may use defined likelihood and impact levels to determine an overall risk category.

For example, a simplified matrix may classify risks as low, moderate, high, or critical.

```
                    IMPACT
             Low   Medium   High   Critical
Likelihood
Low           Low    Low    Medium   High
Medium        Low   Medium   High    High
High        Medium  High    High    Critical
Very High   Medium  High   Critical  Critical
```

This matrix is illustrative only. The organization should define its own scoring methodology, thresholds, and mandatory escalation criteria.

The scoring process should identify situations where numerical aggregation is inappropriate.

For example, a low likelihood should not automatically make an otherwise unacceptable risk acceptable if the potential consequence is catastrophic or the relevant activity is prohibited.

Risk scores should be supported by documented reasoning and should not obscure severe individual risk dimensions through averaging.

### 22. AI Risk Treatment Options

The organization should define the available options for treating identified AI risks.

Common treatment options include:

**Risk avoidance**

Do not initiate or continue the activity that creates an unacceptable risk.

**Risk mitigation**

Implement safeguards that reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk.

**Risk transfer or sharing**

Allocate certain financial or contractual consequences to another party where appropriate, while recognizing that accountability and legal obligations may remain with the organization.

**Risk acceptance**

Retain the residual risk through a documented decision made by an authorized authority.

Treatment decisions should consider the effectiveness, cost, feasibility, and limitations of the available options.

For example, if an AI agent's broad permissions create unacceptable risk, the organization may reduce permissions, add approval gates, redesign the workflow, or prohibit the use case.

The selected treatment should address the underlying risk rather than merely improve the appearance of the risk score.

### 23. AI Risk Treatment Planning

Each material risk requiring treatment should have a documented treatment plan.

The plan should identify:

- Risk scenario.
- Required treatment.
- Accountable owner.
- Supporting control owners.
- Target completion date.
- Required resources.
- Dependencies.
- Expected residual-risk outcome.
- Validation method.
- Escalation criteria.

Treatment plans should distinguish between planned controls and controls that are already implemented and effective.

For example, a planned DLP rule should not be treated as an operating safeguard until it has been implemented and tested.

High-risk treatment actions should receive appropriate prioritization, and overdue actions should be escalated according to established governance requirements.

Closure should require evidence that the treatment was implemented and that the resulting residual risk was reassessed.

### 24. AI Risk Register

The organization should maintain a risk register for material AI-related risks.

The register provides a structured record of identified risk scenarios, ownership, assessments, treatments, acceptance decisions, and monitoring status.

A practical AI risk register may contain the following fields:

| Field                 | Purpose                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID               | Unique risk identifier                  |
| AI system or use case | Identifies the affected activity        |
| Risk category         | Classifies the primary risk dimension   |
| Risk scenario         | Describes cause, event, and consequence |
| Risk owner            | Identifies accountability               |
| Inherent risk         | Records risk before relevant controls   |
| Existing controls     | Identifies current safeguards           |
| Control effectiveness | Records evidence-based effectiveness    |
| Residual risk         | Records risk after controls             |
| Treatment plan        | Defines required actions                |
| Treatment owner       | Assigns implementation responsibility   |
| Target date           | Establishes expected completion         |
| Acceptance status     | Records formal risk decisions           |
| Review date           | Defines reassessment timing             |
| Status                | Tracks progress and closure             |

The register should support links to relevant assessments, incidents, control evidence, and approvals.

It should be maintained as an active risk-management instrument rather than a static document prepared only for an audit.

### 25. AI Risk Ownership and Accountability

Every material AI risk should have a designated owner with sufficient authority and understanding to oversee its management.

Risk ownership should reflect responsibility for the business activity or system that creates the exposure.

The risk owner should ensure that:

- The risk is adequately understood.
- The assessment is current.
- Required controls are implemented.
- Treatment actions are completed.
- Residual risk is evaluated.
- Acceptance decisions are authorized.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Unacceptable exposure is escalated.

Risk ownership should be distinguished from control ownership.

A business owner may own the overall risk while information security, IT, privacy, or a supplier operates specific controls that contribute to treatment.

Where risks affect multiple functions, supporting responsibilities should be documented to prevent gaps or conflicting decisions.

### 26. AI Risk Control Ownership

Each material control used to treat an AI risk should have a designated owner responsible for its implementation and ongoing operation.

Control ownership should identify:

- Control objective.
- Control description.
- Responsible function.
- Implementation mechanism.
- Operating frequency.
- Required evidence.
- Testing requirements.
- Escalation conditions.
- Remediation responsibilities.

For example, an AI risk may depend on access controls, DLP, model validation, and incident monitoring. Each control should have an identifiable owner, even though a single business owner remains accountable for the overall risk.

Control ownership should include the ability to identify deficiencies and initiate remediation.

The organization should avoid assigning controls to roles that lack the authority, resources, or technical capability necessary to operate them effectively.

### 27. AI Risk Assessment Independence and Challenge

Material AI risk assessments should receive appropriate challenge from qualified individuals or functions that are sufficiently independent of the activity being assessed.

Independent challenge helps identify optimistic assumptions, incomplete scenarios, weak evidence, and conflicts between business objectives and risk requirements.

Challenge may be provided by:

- Enterprise risk management.
- Information security.
- AI governance.
- Privacy and legal specialists.
- Model risk management.
- Independent technical assessors.
- Internal audit, within its assurance mandate.

The degree of independence should be proportionate to the risk and organizational context.

For example, a business team may perform the initial assessment of a new AI use case, while a specialist risk function reviews the assessment before authorization.

Independent challenge should be documented where material decisions depend on it.

### 28. AI Risk Assessment Evidence Requirements

Risk assessments should be supported by evidence appropriate to the significance and complexity of the risk.

Evidence may include:

- Architecture diagrams.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Threat models.
- Security test results.
- Model evaluation results.
- Privacy assessments.
- Supplier assurance documents.
- Configuration records.
- Incident history.
- Operational metrics.
- Expert review.
- Documented assumptions.
- Stakeholder analysis.

The organization should distinguish between verified evidence, management representations, assumptions, and information that remains uncertain.

Evidence should be sufficiently current and relevant to the actual AI system and intended use.

For example, an assessment based on an earlier model version may no longer support a decision if the system has materially changed.

The policy should specify documentation and retention expectations for high-risk decisions.

### 29. AI Risk Monitoring

AI risks should be monitored throughout operation to determine whether the original assessment remains valid.

Monitoring should identify changes in system behavior, threat exposure, control effectiveness, data quality, provider conditions, and business use.

Relevant monitoring signals may include:

- Security alerts.
- Model performance changes.
- Data drift.
- Unexpected outputs.
- Unauthorized access attempts.
- Agent action anomalies.
- Provider incidents.
- Control failures.
- Complaints or harmful outcomes.
- Changes in operating conditions.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the risk and should have defined thresholds, owners, and escalation procedures.

Where monitoring identifies a material deviation, the organization should determine whether the use case requires reassessment, additional safeguards, restricted operation, or suspension.

Monitoring without a defined response process provides limited risk-management value.

### 30. AI Risk Reassessment Triggers

The organization should define circumstances that require an AI risk assessment to be revisited.

Triggers may include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- A new model or model version.
- A change in data sources.
- Increased data sensitivity.
- New integrations.
- Expanded permissions.
- Increased autonomy.
- A material security incident.
- Significant performance deterioration.
- New regulatory obligations.
- Provider changes.
- New evidence of harmful outcomes.
- Failure of a key control.
- A material change in the affected population.

Reassessment should determine whether the existing risk classification, safeguards, approval, and residual-risk decision remain valid.

The organization should not automatically assume that an existing approval covers a materially changed AI system.

Where a change introduces risk beyond approved tolerance, the affected activity should be restricted or paused according to established decision authority while the issue is evaluated.

### 31. Core AI Risk Management Policy Principle

The AI Risk Management Policy establishes a formal, accountable, and evidence-based approach for determining whether AI-related risks are understood, appropriately treated, and acceptable for the intended business purpose.

Its foundation should connect risk identification, assessment, treatment, ownership, acceptance, and ongoing monitoring.

The organization should preserve the distinction between inherent risk and residual risk, between a planned safeguard and an effective control, and between a documented risk assessment and an authorized decision to proceed.

```
AI System and Use Case
          │
          ▼
    Risk Identification
          │
          ▼
     Inherent Risk
          │
          ▼
Existing Control Evaluation
          │
          ▼
     Residual Risk
          │
          ▼
     Treatment Decision
          │
          ▼
Authorized Approval / Restriction
          │
          ▼
Monitoring and Reassessment
          │
          ▼
 Evidence and Improvement
```

The fundamental distinctions are:

- **Risk identification ≠ risk assessment**
- **Risk assessment ≠ risk acceptance**
- **Inherent risk ≠ residual risk**
- **Planned control ≠ implemented control**
- **Implemented control ≠ effective control**
- **Risk owner ≠ control owner**
- **Risk scoring ≠ professional judgment**
- **Absence of incidents ≠ absence of risk**
- **Supplier assurance ≠ elimination of organizational responsibility**
- **Risk acceptance ≠ authorization to violate mandatory requirements**
- **Approval of an AI system ≠ permanent approval of every future configuration**
- **Monitoring ≠ effective risk response**
- **Risk register completion ≠ risk treatment**
- **Documented assumptions ≠ verified evidence**
- **AI capability ≠ acceptable risk**

**Core Principle:**

> **An effective AI Risk Management Policy establishes a continuous, evidence-based decision process that identifies AI-related risks, evaluates their significance, assigns accountable owners, implements proportionate treatment, and ensures that residual risk remains within authorized limits throughout the AI lifecycle.**
>
> # 20.04 — AI Risk Management Policy

## Part 2 — AI Risk Management Policy Controls

### 32. AI Risk Management Control Framework

An AI Risk Management Policy must be supported by a formal control framework that converts policy requirements into repeatable, testable, and auditable activities. The framework defines the minimum controls required to identify, assess, treat, monitor, escalate, and report risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

The framework should establish:

- **Control objectives:** The risk outcomes the organization expects to achieve.
- **Control requirements:** Mandatory activities, safeguards, approvals, and restrictions.
- **Control ownership:** The individuals accountable for operating and overseeing controls.
- **Control evidence:** The records demonstrating that controls were implemented and operated.
- **Control effectiveness criteria:** The conditions used to determine whether controls work as intended.
- **Control escalation:** The process for handling deficiencies, failures, and unacceptable residual risk.

The framework must be proportionate to the organization's AI risk exposure. A low-impact internal productivity assistant may require a streamlined assessment, while an AI system affecting employment, credit, healthcare, critical infrastructure, or safety may require extensive independent review and stronger approval requirements.

A control framework is not simply a checklist. It is an operating mechanism connecting risk identification to management decisions and measurable risk reduction.

### 33. Mandatory AI Risk Assessment Control

Every AI use case must undergo a documented risk assessment before authorization for production use, subject to any formally approved process for limited experimentation.

The assessment must identify the system, its intended purpose, its users, affected stakeholders, data sources, dependencies, operating environment, and foreseeable consequences of failure or misuse.

At a minimum, the assessment must establish:

1. The AI system's business purpose and operational context.
2. The inherent risks before considering controls.
3. The applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements.
4. The existing and proposed risk controls.
5. The expected residual risk after treatment.
6. The accountable risk owner and control owners.
7. The required approval authority.
8. The conditions for monitoring and reassessment.

An assessment is incomplete if it records risks without assigning owners, proposing treatment, or establishing a decision about whether the system may proceed.

The organization must define which changes require reassessment and which activities are prohibited until required approvals are obtained.

### 34. AI System Inventory and Risk Assessment Linkage

AI risk assessments must be linked to the authoritative AI system inventory. The inventory provides the foundation for establishing risk coverage, ownership, lifecycle status, and governance obligations.

Each assessment should reference a unique system or use-case identifier. Where a business process uses several AI components, the organization must determine whether assessments should cover the overall solution, individual components, or both.

The inventory should capture, where applicable:

| Inventory attribute            | Risk management purpose                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| System and use-case identifier | Provides traceability across governance records             |
| Business owner                 | Establishes accountability for business outcomes            |
| Technical owner                | Identifies responsibility for technical operation           |
| Intended purpose               | Defines the context in which risk is evaluated              |
| Model and provider             | Identifies dependencies and third-party exposure            |
| Data classification            | Supports privacy, confidentiality, and security assessments |
| Deployment environment         | Identifies infrastructure and operational exposure          |
| Impact classification          | Supports proportional assessment and approval               |
| Lifecycle status               | Identifies systems requiring review or retirement           |
| Assessment reference           | Connects the inventory to risk evidence and decisions       |

A system without a confirmed owner or documented purpose should be treated as a governance exception until those deficiencies are resolved.

### 35. AI Risk Assessment Scope Control

The assessment scope must be explicitly documented to prevent material risks from being excluded unintentionally.

Scope determination should consider the complete AI-enabled business process, not merely the model or application interface. For example, a generative AI assistant may depend on a retrieval system, document repositories, identity services, external APIs, cloud infrastructure, and human approval workflows.

The assessment should consider:

- The AI model and its supporting components.
- Data collection, preparation, storage, retrieval, and processing.
- Model development, configuration, deployment, and updates.
- User interactions and downstream consumers of outputs.
- External services, suppliers, and integration points.
- Human decisions influenced by AI-generated information.
- Automated actions performed by connected systems or agents.
- Operational support, monitoring, incident response, and recovery.
- Decommissioning, data disposal, and residual obligations.

Scope exclusions must be documented with a rationale and approved by an appropriate authority where they could materially affect the risk conclusion.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risk decisions reflect the actual operating environment and material dependencies of the AI-enabled service.

### 36. AI Risk Criteria and Scoring Control

The organization must establish consistent risk criteria before assessing individual AI use cases. Without predefined criteria, teams may assign risk ratings inconsistently, making prioritization and management reporting unreliable.

Risk criteria should define the meaning of likelihood, impact, risk levels, escalation thresholds, and acceptance limits. The criteria should be aligned with enterprise risk management while accommodating AI-specific characteristics.

A simple scoring model may use:

[\
\text{Inherent Risk Score}=\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Impact}\
]

For a five-point likelihood scale and a five-point impact scale, the resulting score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Score | Illustrative classification | Expected treatment                                                        |
| ----- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1–4   | Low                         | Apply baseline controls and routine monitoring                            |
| 5–9   | Moderate                    | Document treatment and assign an accountable owner                        |
| 10–16 | High                        | Require enhanced mitigation and management review                         |
| 17–25 | Critical                    | Escalate for senior decision-making; restrict deployment until authorized |

These thresholds are illustrative, not universal. The organization must approve its own methodology and define any mandatory prohibitions or regulatory requirements that override a numerical score.

A numerical score must not override a severe consequence, a legal prohibition, or an unacceptable rights or safety impact. Qualitative judgment and documented rationale remain necessary.

### 37. Inherent Risk Assessment Control

Inherent risk must be assessed before giving credit to existing or proposed controls. This establishes the underlying exposure associated with the AI use case.

The assessment should consider the nature of the use case, the sensitivity of its data, the level of autonomy, the affected population, the potential scale of deployment, and the consequences of incorrect or malicious outputs.

Relevant factors include:

- Potential physical, financial, operational, or reputational harm.
- Effects on individuals' rights, access, opportunities, or treatment.
- Exposure of personal, confidential, or regulated information.
- Potential for manipulation, exploitation, or adversarial misuse.
- Degree of human oversight and reversibility of decisions.
- Dependence on external providers and opaque model behavior.
- Scale, speed, and reach of automated actions.
- Difficulty detecting or correcting failures.

The assessor must explain why the assigned likelihood and impact ratings are appropriate. Where reliable evidence is unavailable, uncertainty must be recorded rather than concealed by an apparently precise score.

### 38. Residual Risk Assessment Control

Residual risk is the risk remaining after accounting for the design and demonstrated effectiveness of relevant controls.

The organization must distinguish between controls that are merely planned, controls that have been implemented, and controls whose operating effectiveness has been tested.

A proposed safeguard must not automatically reduce the residual risk rating. Risk reduction should reflect the control's actual status, coverage, reliability, and evidence.

A residual risk assessment should document:

1. The inherent risk rating.
2. The applicable controls and their implementation status.
3. Evidence supporting control effectiveness.
4. Known limitations and dependencies.
5. The remaining likelihood and impact.
6. The residual risk classification.
7. The accountable owner and required decision.

For example, a human-review control may reduce risk only if reviewers have sufficient expertise, access to relevant evidence, adequate time, and genuine authority to reject the AI recommendation.

Residual risk must be reassessed when controls fail, the operating context changes, or monitoring reveals that the original assumptions are no longer valid.

### 39. AI Risk Treatment Selection Control

Each risk requiring treatment must have a documented response selected from the organization's approved risk treatment options.

| Treatment option  | Application                                                                                               |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid             | Do not pursue the AI use case or discontinue the risky activity                                           |
| Mitigate          | Implement controls that reduce likelihood, impact, or both                                                |
| Transfer or share | Allocate specified financial or operational exposure through contracts, insurance, or shared arrangements |
| Accept            | Retain the residual risk through an authorized decision                                                   |
| Restrict          | Limit users, data, functionality, autonomy, or deployment scope                                           |
| Defer             | Postpone implementation until required conditions are met                                                 |

Transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability for legal compliance, responsible use, or risks that remain under its control.

Deferral is not a permanent substitute for treatment. A deferred risk must have an owner, a review date, defined restrictions, and a decision on whether the AI system may continue operating.

The chosen response must be justified against the risk's severity, the feasibility of mitigation, business objectives, stakeholder impacts, and applicable mandatory requirements.

### 40. AI Risk Treatment Plan Control

A treatment plan must convert the selected response into measurable implementation activities.

Each treatment action should specify:

- The risk identifier and risk statement.
- The required control or corrective action.
- The accountable action owner.
- The target completion date.
- Required funding, resources, and dependencies.
- Implementation milestones.
- Verification and acceptance criteria.
- Evidence required for closure.
- Interim restrictions while remediation remains incomplete.

A treatment plan should distinguish remediation of the underlying risk from remediation of a control deficiency. For example, improving access logging may correct a monitoring weakness but may not sufficiently mitigate the underlying risk of an AI agent performing unauthorized transactions.

Overdue actions must be escalated according to severity and organizational risk appetite. Critical actions should not remain overdue without a documented decision on operational restrictions and residual exposure.

### 41. AI Risk Acceptance Authority Control

Risk acceptance must be performed by an individual or body with delegated authority appropriate to the risk level.

The person requesting acceptance should not automatically be the sole decision-maker. Independent challenge is especially important when the request concerns a material business benefit, a high-impact AI system, a significant control deficiency, or a risk that affects external stakeholders.

An acceptance record must contain:

1. The risk and affected AI system.
2. The residual risk rating and supporting rationale.
3. The reasons treatment is not being completed immediately.
4. The expected business benefit and alternatives considered.
5. The decision-maker and authority basis.
6. Any conditions, restrictions, or compensating controls.
7. The acceptance date and expiration or review date.
8. The events that invalidate the acceptance.
9. The evidence supporting the decision.

Risk acceptance cannot authorize an activity prohibited by law, bypass mandatory regulatory safeguards, or remove obligations that cannot lawfully be waived.

### 42. AI Risk Escalation Control

The organization must define mandatory escalation conditions and the recipients responsible for decisions.

Escalation should occur when a risk exceeds approved thresholds, a critical control fails, a treatment action is overdue beyond its permitted tolerance, a material incident occurs, or new information invalidates a previous risk decision.

Additional triggers may include:

- Potential harm to life, physical safety, or fundamental rights.
- Suspected unlawful processing or disclosure of personal data.
- Unauthorized AI actions or privilege escalation.
- Material model behavior changes or unexplained degradation.
- Significant vulnerabilities affecting a model or AI dependency.
- Regulatory notifications or contractual reporting obligations.
- Evidence that the system is being used beyond its approved purpose.
- A risk acceptance decision that has expired or become invalid.

Escalation paths must identify the required notification timeframe, decision authority, interim protective measures, and documentation requirements.

The organization should distinguish ordinary risk escalation from incident response. A newly identified risk may require governance review, whereas an active security incident may require immediate containment and a separate incident-management process.

### 43. AI Risk Control Effectiveness Assessment

Controls must be evaluated for both design adequacy and operating effectiveness.

**Design effectiveness** asks whether a control, if implemented as intended, is capable of addressing the identified risk.

**Operating effectiveness** asks whether the control actually operates consistently, completely, and reliably in practice.

For example, a policy requiring human approval before an AI agent executes a high-impact transaction may be well designed. If the agent can invoke an alternative API that bypasses the approval workflow, the control is not operating effectively across the relevant execution paths.

Testing methods may include:

- Inspection of policies, configurations, and access rules.
- Sampling of approval records and transactions.
- Reperformance of risk assessment calculations.
- Testing of technical restrictions and authorization boundaries.
- Review of monitoring alerts and incident records.
- Adversarial testing and controlled simulations.
- Interviews with control operators and business owners.
- Analysis of control failures, exceptions, and recurring findings.

Control effectiveness conclusions must be supported by evidence and recorded limitations. A control should not be classified as effective solely because a procedure exists.

### 44. AI Risk Evidence and Documentation Control

AI risk decisions must be supported by evidence sufficient for management review, audit, regulatory examination, and subsequent reassessment.

Evidence should establish what was assessed, which assumptions were used, what controls existed at the time, who made the decision, and why the resulting risk conclusion was reasonable.

Evidence may include:

- Completed assessments and approved risk criteria.
- System architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Data classification and privacy analysis.
- Model evaluations and security test results.
- Vendor assurance documentation.
- Control configurations and test records.
- Meeting decisions and approval records.
- Monitoring metrics and incident reports.
- Treatment plans and remediation verification.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.

Evidence must be attributable to the relevant system and assessment version. Where feasible, records should be time-stamped, access-controlled, and protected against unauthorized alteration.

The organization must define retention periods according to legal, regulatory, contractual, audit, and business requirements. Sensitive evidence should be protected from unnecessary disclosure and retained only as permitted by applicable obligations.

### 45. AI Risk Monitoring Control

AI risk management must continue after deployment. An approval decision represents a judgment based on the information available at a particular time; it does not establish that risk will remain unchanged.

Monitoring must be aligned with the risk profile and operating characteristics of the AI system.

Relevant monitoring areas include:

- Security events, unauthorized access, and anomalous behavior.
- Changes in model performance, reliability, and output quality.
- Drift in data, model behavior, or operating conditions.
- Privacy incidents and inappropriate data disclosure.
- Bias, disparate impacts, and other relevant fairness indicators.
- Human override rates and unresolved reviewer concerns.
- Agent actions, tool invocation, and transaction anomalies.
- Third-party incidents and provider changes.
- Control failures, overdue treatment actions, and exceptions.
- Changes in laws, contractual duties, or approved use cases.

Each monitored risk should have defined indicators, thresholds, review frequency, ownership, and escalation procedures. Monitoring frequency must be proportionate to the potential harm and the speed with which the risk can change.

### 46. AI Risk Reassessment Trigger Control

The organization must define events that require an AI risk assessment to be reviewed or repeated.

Reassessment should be triggered when a material change occurs, not merely when the annual review date arrives.

| Trigger                                 | Required review focus                                     |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model replacement or major update       | Changed capabilities, behavior, limitations, and controls |
| New data source or expanded data access | Privacy, confidentiality, provenance, and data quality    |
| New user group or business purpose      | Authorization, impact, and scope                          |
| Increased autonomy or new tools         | Action permissions, supervision, and containment          |
| New external integration                | Trust boundaries, dependencies, and data flows            |
| Significant incident or near miss       | Failed assumptions, root causes, and residual risk        |
| Regulatory or contractual change        | Compliance obligations and required controls              |
| Control failure or repeated exceptions  | Effectiveness and adequacy of treatment                   |
| Material deployment-scale increase      | Exposure, impact, and operational resilience              |
| Retirement or decommissioning           | Residual access, data retention, and disposal risks       |

The reassessment record should identify which previous conclusions remain valid, which require revision, and whether the existing approval remains appropriate.

A material change must not be treated as routine maintenance when it changes the system's intended purpose, risk exposure, or operational authority.

### 47. AI Third-Party Risk Control

Third-party AI services must be assessed according to the risks they introduce and the organization's ability to govern those risks.

The assessment should cover the provider, model, hosting environment, data processing arrangements, subcontractors, support processes, service dependencies, and contractual allocation of responsibilities.

Relevant controls include:

- Provider due diligence and assurance review.
- Identification of the model and service configuration.
- Assessment of data use, retention, and training practices.
- Review of access controls and tenant isolation.
- Evaluation of security testing and vulnerability management.
- Assessment of incident notification and cooperation terms.
- Review of service continuity and exit arrangements.
- Restrictions on unauthorized data reuse or onward disclosure.
- Procedures for material provider or model changes.
- Verification of contractual and regulatory requirements.

The organization must not assume that a supplier's certification or assurance report automatically establishes that the specific AI use case is acceptable. Assurance evidence must be evaluated for scope, date, exclusions, and relevance to the risks being assessed.

### 48. AI Privacy and Data Protection Risk Control

AI risk assessments must address privacy and data protection risks wherever the use case involves personal data or could affect identifiable individuals.

The review should consider the purposes of processing, lawful basis where applicable, data minimization, data accuracy, retention, access, transparency, data subject rights, and international transfers where relevant.

Specific AI-related considerations may include:

- Personal data embedded in prompts or uploaded documents.
- Sensitive information retrieved from connected repositories.
- Memorization or unintended disclosure of training information.
- Inferences that reveal or predict sensitive attributes.
- Re-identification risks associated with supposedly anonymized data.
- Use of personal data for model training or fine-tuning.
- Automated decisions and profiling.
- Cross-border processing and provider access.
- Retention of interaction histories and audit logs.

Where required, the organization must complete a data protection impact assessment or other applicable privacy review. Such an assessment may be linked to the AI risk assessment but should not be assumed to replace it.

Privacy risks must be assigned owners, treated appropriately, and escalated when legal requirements or approved privacy thresholds are not met.

### 49. AI Security Risk Control

The AI risk assessment must consider security threats that arise from the AI system, its supporting infrastructure, its data, and its interactions with users and external services.

Depending on the system, relevant risks may include prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, insecure output handling, data poisoning, model extraction, model theft, unauthorized model access, adversarial manipulation, insecure plugin use, and excessive agent permissions.

Security assessment should also cover conventional enterprise risks such as identity compromise, vulnerable dependencies, cloud misconfiguration, inadequate logging, exposed APIs, weak secrets management, and insufficient backup or recovery capability.

The assessment must link material security risks to specific controls and evidence. A generic statement that the organization follows cybersecurity best practices is not sufficient evidence that an AI-specific threat has been adequately addressed.

Where necessary, security risk treatment should be coordinated with the cybersecurity, application security, cloud security, and incident response functions.

### 50. AI Safety, Reliability, and Human Impact Risk Control

AI risk management must consider harms that may arise even when a system operates as technically designed.

Depending on its purpose, the assessment may need to consider unsafe recommendations, unreliable outputs, automation bias, inappropriate delegation of decisions, discriminatory outcomes, lack of contestability, and inadequate human intervention.

Controls should be proportionate to the potential consequences and may include:

- Defined operational boundaries and prohibited uses.
- Independent evaluation against relevant safety criteria.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Escalation to qualified personnel when confidence is insufficient.
- Mechanisms for correction, appeal, or contestation.
- Restrictions on autonomous execution.
- Testing under foreseeable failure conditions.
- Clear communication of system limitations.
- Safe fallback and shutdown procedures.

Human oversight must be substantive. A nominal approval step does not adequately mitigate risk when the reviewer lacks the information, expertise, time, or authority needed to intervene.

### 51. AI Model and Performance Risk Control

The organization must assess risks associated with model behavior, reliability, suitability, and limitations.

The assessment should determine whether the model is appropriate for its intended purpose and whether its known limitations are compatible with the consequences of its use.

Relevant controls may include:

- Model validation against documented acceptance criteria.
- Evaluation using representative and appropriately governed datasets.
- Testing of edge cases and foreseeable misuse.
- Documentation of limitations and unsupported use cases.
- Monitoring for drift and performance degradation.
- Revalidation after material model or data changes.
- Comparison against alternative models where warranted.
- Defined rollback or replacement criteria.
- Review of model provenance and version changes.

For generative AI, assessment should also consider hallucinations, inconsistent responses, sensitivity to prompt changes, and the risk that fluent outputs may be mistaken for verified facts.

The organization must establish whether performance thresholds are appropriate to the use case. A level of error acceptable for brainstorming may be unacceptable for a safety-critical or regulated decision process.

### 52. AI Human Oversight Control

Where human oversight is required as a risk treatment, the organization must document how that oversight operates in practice.

The control design should identify the reviewer, decision authority, review criteria, information available, intervention options, escalation route, and evidence retained.

Effective oversight may require:

- Clear separation between AI recommendations and final decisions.
- Access to relevant source material and uncertainty information.
- Training appropriate to the reviewer's responsibilities.
- Authority to reject, override, or suspend AI outputs.
- Defined escalation criteria for ambiguous or high-risk cases.
- Workload limits that permit meaningful review.
- Sampling and quality assurance of review decisions.
- Monitoring of overrides, disagreements, and reviewer errors.

The organization should evaluate whether human reviewers can detect the relevant failure modes. Human involvement must not be credited as a strong control merely because a person is present somewhere in the workflow.

### 53. AI Agent and Autonomous Action Risk Control

AI systems capable of invoking tools, interacting with external services, changing records, or executing transactions require explicit assessment of their operational authority.

The organization must identify what the agent can read, write, execute, approve, modify, or transmit, and under which identity and permissions those actions occur.

Required controls should be determined by risk and may include:

- Least-privilege tool and API permissions.
- Allowlisted actions, destinations, and resources.
- Explicit authorization for sensitive operations.
- Human approval for designated high-impact actions.
- Limits on transaction value, volume, or frequency.
- Separation of planning from execution where appropriate.
- Protection against prompt injection and instruction manipulation.
- Restricted access to credentials and secrets.
- Traceable records of requests, decisions, and actions.
- Emergency suspension and recovery mechanisms.

Risk assessments must consider indirect prompt injection, compromised context sources, chained tool calls, unintended actions, and the potential for one compromised component to affect downstream systems.

The agent's autonomy must be restricted where the organization cannot adequately prevent, detect, or recover from unacceptable outcomes.

### 54. AI Change Management Risk Control

Changes to AI models, configurations, data pipelines, prompts, retrieval sources, integrations, and agent permissions must be assessed for risk impact.

The change process should classify changes according to their potential to affect security, privacy, performance, safety, compliance, or business outcomes.

Material changes should require risk review and approval before production deployment. Lower-risk changes may follow an expedited process when the criteria and safeguards have been formally defined.

Change records should capture:

- The description and reason for the change.
- Affected systems, dependencies, and stakeholders.
- Expected benefits and potential adverse effects.
- Assessment of new or changed risks.
- Required testing and approvals.
- Deployment and rollback plans.
- Updated documentation and inventory information.
- Post-deployment verification results.

A change that alters the intended purpose, access privileges, degree of autonomy, or affected population must not be treated as a routine technical update without evaluating the resulting risk.

### 55. AI Risk Remediation and Closure Control

Risk remediation must remain open until the required treatment has been implemented and the closure criteria have been satisfied.

The completion of a task does not necessarily mean the risk has been reduced to an acceptable level. Closure requires verification that the treatment addresses the intended risk and that any remaining exposure has been reassessed.

A remediation record should include the original finding, root cause where known, agreed corrective action, owner, target date, implementation evidence, verification results, and final risk decision.

Closure should be rejected or returned for further action when:

- The control is implemented only partially.
- Testing reveals an unresolved weakness.
- The evidence does not support the claimed improvement.
- The residual risk remains above the authorized threshold.
- Related dependencies or affected systems were omitted.
- A compensating control has not been validated.

Where remediation cannot be completed within the required period, the issue must be escalated and managed through the appropriate risk acceptance or exception process. Overdue remediation must not be concealed by repeatedly changing target dates without justification.

### 56. AI Risk Exception Control

Exceptions may be used when a defined policy or control requirement cannot be met temporarily and an authorized decision-maker determines that a limited deviation is justified.

An exception is not an informal permission to bypass governance. It must be documented, time-bound, risk-assessed, and subject to compensating controls where appropriate.

The exception record should identify:

1. The requirement being waived or modified.
2. The business justification.
3. The affected AI system and operating scope.
4. The risk introduced by the deviation.
5. The compensating controls.
6. The accountable owner and approving authority.
7. The effective date and expiry date.
8. Monitoring and review requirements.
9. The remediation plan or exit condition.
10. Conditions that require early revocation.

Exceptions must not be used to authorize unlawful conduct or override mandatory obligations. Repeated exceptions for the same requirement should trigger an investigation into whether the control design, implementation process, or policy itself requires review.

### 57. AI Risk Reporting Control

AI risk reporting must provide decision-makers with an accurate view of exposure, treatment progress, control performance, and material changes in risk.

Reports should be tailored to the audience. Operational teams need actionable details, while executive committees and boards need a concise view of material exposure, decisions required, and trends.

Reporting should include, as appropriate:

- Distribution of AI systems by risk classification.
- Number of assessments completed and overdue.
- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks accepted outside normal tolerances.
- Overdue treatment actions.
- Material control deficiencies.
- Open exceptions and approaching expirations.
- Significant incidents and emerging risk themes.
- Third-party dependencies and assurance gaps.
- Reassessment completion and material changes.
- Progress against agreed remediation objectives.

Metrics should include context, reporting period, definitions, ownership, and data-quality limitations. Aggregate counts should not conceal a small number of severe risks.

### 58. AI Risk Key Performance Indicators

Key performance indicators (KPIs) measure whether the AI risk management process is being executed as intended.

Examples include:

| KPI                                  | Illustrative measurement                                                      |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessment coverage                  | Percentage of in-scope AI systems with current assessments                    |
| Pre-deployment assessment completion | Percentage assessed before production authorization                           |
| Treatment-plan completion            | Percentage of actions completed by their due dates                            |
| Reassessment timeliness              | Percentage of triggered reassessments completed within the defined period     |
| Risk ownership coverage              | Percentage of material risks with assigned owners                             |
| Evidence completeness                | Percentage of sampled assessments meeting evidence requirements               |
| Control testing coverage             | Percentage of required controls tested within the review period               |
| Training completion                  | Percentage of designated risk and control owners completing required training |

Each KPI should have a defined calculation, data source, reporting frequency, accountable owner, and target.

Targets must be realistic and should encourage substantive risk reduction rather than superficial completion. For example, achieving 100% assessment coverage does not demonstrate effective risk management if assessments are outdated, poorly evidenced, or disconnected from operational controls.

### 59. AI Risk Key Risk Indicators

Key risk indicators (KRIs) provide signals that risk exposure may be increasing or that controls may be deteriorating.

Potential KRIs include:

- Growth in high or critical residual risks.
- Increasing numbers of overdue critical treatment actions.
- Repeated failure of important security or safety controls.
- Rising rates of unauthorized AI tool use.
- Material increases in model error or harmful outputs.
- Increasing rates of human override or unresolved disagreement.
- Repeated incidents involving the same provider or model.
- Exceptions approaching expiry without remediation.
- Unauthorized changes to models, prompts, or permissions.
- Significant gaps in monitoring or risk evidence.

KRIs should have thresholds and escalation rules. A threshold breach should prompt investigation and, where appropriate, containment, reassessment, or escalation to management.

A KRI is not automatically proof that harm has occurred. It is a signal requiring interpretation in context. Conversely, the absence of alerts does not prove that risk is low when monitoring coverage is incomplete.

### 60. AI Risk Review and Approval Control

AI risk assessments must undergo review proportionate to the risk classification and the consequences of the proposed use.

The review process should verify that the assessment is complete, the scoring rationale is consistent, relevant stakeholders have contributed, and treatment plans are credible.

Reviewers should challenge:

- Unsupported likelihood or impact ratings.
- Missing stakeholders or system dependencies.
- Unverified assumptions about model behavior.
- Controls that are planned but not implemented.
- Residual risk reductions unsupported by evidence.
- Unclear ownership or unrealistic treatment dates.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Incomplete third-party or privacy analysis.
- Conflicts with legal or regulatory obligations.
- Requests for acceptance beyond delegated authority.

Approval records must identify the decision, approving authority, relevant conditions, and any limitations on use.

Approval should be conditional where additional safeguards must be completed before deployment. Conditions that are mandatory for safe or lawful operation must be verified before the system is authorized to proceed.

### 61. AI Risk Independent Challenge Control

The organization should establish independent challenge for material AI risk decisions to reduce the possibility that commercial pressure, delivery deadlines, or conflicts of interest undermine the quality of assessment.

Independent challenge may be performed by a risk management function, AI governance committee, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, model risk management, or another appropriately independent reviewer.

The reviewer should have access to sufficient information, competence relevant to the risks, and authority to raise unresolved concerns.

The process should document material disagreements and their resolution. If the business owner and independent reviewer disagree about risk classification or acceptability, the decision must be escalated according to the governance framework rather than resolved solely by the party seeking approval.

The level of independence should reflect risk severity. Not every low-risk use case requires a separate committee review, but high-impact or critical-risk use cases warrant stronger challenge.

### 62. AI Risk Dependencies and Aggregation Control

AI risks must be evaluated both individually and in relation to other systems, processes, and enterprise exposures.

A collection of individually moderate-risk AI systems may create significant aggregate exposure when they depend on the same provider, share sensitive data, influence the same decision process, or execute actions through a common integration.

The organization should identify:

- Shared model and provider dependencies.
- Common data repositories and retrieval services.
- Shared identity, access, and infrastructure components.
- Concentration of critical AI capabilities in one supplier.
- Interdependencies among AI-enabled business processes.
- Common control weaknesses affecting multiple systems.
- Cumulative impacts on individuals or stakeholder groups.

Risk aggregation should not rely exclusively on adding numerical scores. The organization must consider correlated failure, common-cause events, concentration risk, and cascading consequences.

Material aggregated risks should be recorded at the appropriate enterprise level and assigned to an owner who can coordinate treatment across affected systems.

### 63. AI Risk Assumption and Uncertainty Control

AI risk assessments depend on assumptions about data, model behavior, users, operating conditions, provider commitments, and control performance. Those assumptions must be explicit and reviewable.

The organization should distinguish verified facts from estimates, hypotheses, and unresolved uncertainties.

For each material assumption, the record should identify its source, rationale, importance to the risk conclusion, validation method, owner, and review trigger.

Examples include assumptions that:

- A provider does not retain prompts beyond a defined period.
- A model will not perform actions outside its configured permissions.
- Human reviewers can identify specified classes of incorrect output.
- Training or retrieval data is sufficiently representative.
- A control remains effective after a model update.
- A supplier will notify the organization of material incidents.

When an assumption cannot be verified, the organization should consider a more conservative risk rating, additional controls, a restricted deployment, or a decision to defer the use case.

Material uncertainty must not be treated as evidence that the risk is absent.

### 64. AI Risk Scenario Analysis Control

For material AI use cases, scenario analysis should be used to explore plausible adverse events and evaluate whether existing controls can prevent, detect, contain, or recover from them.

Scenarios should be specific to the system's purpose, dependencies, and operating environment.

Examples include:

- An AI assistant discloses confidential information through a response.
- An agent follows malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content.
- A model update introduces unacceptable performance degradation.
- A third-party provider experiences a security incident.
- Incorrect AI recommendations influence a consequential business decision.
- A compromised integration enables unauthorized transactions.
- Monitoring fails to detect a gradual change in model behavior.
- A key provider becomes unavailable during a critical business process.

For each scenario, the assessment should identify the initiating event, threat or failure path, affected assets and stakeholders, potential consequences, existing controls, remaining weaknesses, and required treatment.

Scenario analysis complements likelihood-impact scoring. It provides a causal explanation of how harm could occur and where interventions may reduce the risk.

### 65. AI Risk Control Mapping

Every material AI risk must be mapped to one or more relevant controls, and important controls should be traceable to the risks they are intended to address.

This mapping enables the organization to determine whether risks are untreated, whether controls are duplicated unnecessarily, and whether control failures create exposure across several systems.

A risk-control mapping record should include:

| Field                      | Purpose                                               |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk identifier            | Uniquely identifies the risk                          |
| Risk statement             | Describes the cause, event, and consequence           |
| Control identifier         | References the relevant safeguard                     |
| Control objective          | Explains the intended risk reduction                  |
| Control owner              | Identifies accountability                             |
| Implementation status      | Records whether the control is operational            |
| Effectiveness status       | Records the result of testing                         |
| Evidence reference         | Links supporting records                              |
| Residual risk relationship | Explains the control's contribution to remaining risk |

The organization should avoid treating the presence of a mapped control as proof of risk mitigation. The control must be relevant, appropriately designed, implemented, and effective.

### 66. AI Risk-Control Gap Management

A control gap exists when a required control is absent, inadequately designed, insufficiently implemented, or ineffective in operation.

The organization must distinguish a missing control from a control that exists but fails to achieve its objective. The distinction matters because the corrective action may require new design, improved implementation, operational remediation, or a broader redesign of the AI workflow.

Each material gap should have:

- A description of the deficiency.
- The risks and systems affected.
- The severity and potential consequences.
- The cause, where known.
- The required corrective action.
- An accountable owner and target date.
- Interim safeguards or restrictions.
- Verification criteria.
- An escalation and closure decision.

Where a gap creates unacceptable residual risk, deployment or continued operation must be restricted in accordance with the organization's risk appetite and mandatory requirements.

### 67. AI Risk Communication and Stakeholder Notification Control

Material AI risks must be communicated to the stakeholders who need the information to make decisions, fulfill obligations, or protect affected parties.

Stakeholders may include business owners, system operators, security and privacy teams, legal and compliance functions, senior management, suppliers, customers, regulators, and individuals affected by AI-enabled decisions.

Communication should be accurate, timely, understandable, and proportionate to the stakeholder's role.

The organization should define when risk information must be shared, who is authorized to communicate it, which channels should be used, and how confidentiality and legal restrictions are maintained.

Risk communication must not be confused with public disclosure of sensitive technical details. The objective is to ensure that relevant parties receive the information necessary to act while protecting confidential information and respecting applicable obligations.

### 68. AI Risk Training and Competence Control

Individuals who identify, assess, approve, treat, or monitor AI risks must possess competence appropriate to their responsibilities.

Training should be role-based. A business owner may require knowledge of impact assessment and risk acceptance, while a security assessor may require technical knowledge of AI threats, system architecture, and control testing.

The organization should define competence requirements for:

- Business and risk owners.
- AI developers and system operators.
- AI governance and risk management personnel.
- Cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and compliance reviewers.
- Human reviewers responsible for consequential decisions.
- Internal auditors and independent assurance providers.
- Personnel authorized to accept material residual risk.

Training records should establish completion and, where warranted, competence through assessments, practical exercises, or demonstrated performance.

Training must be refreshed when responsibilities change, significant risks emerge, or the organization's AI governance requirements are materially revised.

### 69. AI Risk Lifecycle Control

Risk management must cover the full AI lifecycle, from initial idea through development, deployment, operation, material change, and retirement.

Different lifecycle stages introduce different risks. Early-stage design decisions may determine data exposure and system authority, while production operation introduces changing users, dependencies, threat conditions, and performance characteristics.

A lifecycle control model should define required risk activities and approval gates for:

1. Use-case proposal and initial screening.
2. Feasibility assessment and system design.
3. Data acquisition and preparation.
4. Model selection, development, or configuration.
5. Validation and pre-deployment assessment.
6. Production authorization.
7. Monitoring and periodic review.
8. Material changes and incident-driven reassessment.
9. Suspension, replacement, or decommissioning.

The organization should define minimum requirements for each applicable stage and document any stage that is not relevant to a particular system.

Lifecycle coverage prevents risk management from becoming a one-time approval activity disconnected from operational reality.

### 70. AI Risk Suspension and Decommissioning Control

The organization must define conditions under which an AI system should be suspended, restricted, replaced, or retired because risk is no longer acceptable.

Triggers may include a critical vulnerability, serious incident, unlawful use, material control failure, loss of required provider assurance, unacceptable performance degradation, or an expired approval that cannot be renewed.

Suspension and decommissioning procedures should address:

- Authority to suspend or disable the system.
- Communication with affected operational teams.
- Protection of dependent business processes.
- Revocation of credentials, tokens, and integrations.
- Preservation of evidence needed for investigation or audit.
- Retention and disposal of data in accordance with obligations.
- Termination of third-party access and services.
- Management of downstream systems and residual dependencies.
- Verification that unauthorized access and execution have ceased.
- Formal closure of outstanding risks and obligations.

A shutdown mechanism must be tested where appropriate. An emergency procedure that exists only in documentation may not provide reliable risk reduction during an actual event.

### 71. AI Risk Control Exceptions and Compensating Measures Review

Where a control cannot be implemented as designed, the organization must determine whether an alternative safeguard can achieve an equivalent or sufficient risk reduction.

A compensating control should be evaluated against the specific control objective, not merely against the convenience of implementation.

For example, if a particular monitoring integration is unavailable, an alternative may provide temporary risk reduction through more frequent manual review and restricted access. The organization must evaluate whether the alternative provides adequate coverage, timeliness, reliability, and evidence.

The review should document:

- The original control objective.
- The reason the standard control is unavailable or unsuitable.
- The proposed compensating measure.
- The risks that remain uncovered.
- The evidence supporting the effectiveness claim.
- The approval authority and duration.
- The conditions for removal or replacement.

Compensating measures must be reassessed when the operating context changes or evidence shows that they are insufficient. They must not be used to disguise an unacceptable residual risk.

### 72. AI Risk Management Assurance Control

The organization must establish assurance activities to determine whether its AI risk management framework is appropriately designed and operating as intended.

Assurance may combine first-line control monitoring, second-line risk and compliance oversight, and independent third-line internal audit. The precise model should align with the organization's governance structure.

Assurance activities may evaluate:

- Completeness and accuracy of the AI inventory.
- Timeliness and quality of risk assessments.
- Consistency of scoring and classification.
- Compliance with risk acceptance authority.
- Effectiveness of material risk controls.
- Quality of evidence and decision records.
- Timeliness of remediation and reassessment.
- Treatment of third-party and aggregated risks.
- Reliability of risk reporting and metrics.
- Adherence to lifecycle approval requirements.

Findings must be assigned owners, prioritized, tracked, and independently verified where warranted. Repeated findings should trigger analysis of underlying causes rather than repeated correction of isolated symptoms.

Assurance results should inform the annual review of the policy, the risk management methodology, control design, and resource allocation.

### 73. AI Risk Register Quality Control

The AI risk register must be maintained as a reliable management record rather than a static spreadsheet that is updated only for reporting purposes.

The organization should establish quality requirements covering completeness, consistency, traceability, timeliness, and ownership.

A risk register entry should include, at a minimum, the risk identifier, affected system, risk statement, category, inherent risk, existing controls, residual risk, owner, treatment decision, action status, review date, and acceptance or escalation references where applicable.

Quality checks should identify:

- Risks without accountable owners.
- Residual ratings inconsistent with control evidence.
- High-risk entries without treatment plans.
- Overdue actions without escalation.
- Expired risk acceptances.
- Duplicate or contradictory records.
- Assessments not linked to the inventory.
- Risks with outdated assumptions or review dates.
- Closed risks without documented verification.

Where automated workflows are available, they should flag missing fields, overdue dates, and invalid status transitions. Automated validation improves consistency but does not replace professional judgment about the substance of a risk.

### 74. AI Risk Decision Traceability Control

Material decisions must be traceable from the original risk through assessment, treatment, approval, monitoring, and subsequent review.

Traceability enables the organization to explain why a system was approved, which safeguards were required, whether those safeguards were implemented, and what changed when the risk profile evolved.

The organization should maintain links among:

- AI inventory records.
- Use-case and impact assessments.
- Risk register entries.
- Control mappings and testing records.
- Treatment plans and remediation evidence.
- Risk acceptance and exception decisions.
- Production approvals and change records.
- Monitoring alerts and incident records.
- Periodic reviews and reassessments.
- Audit findings and corrective actions.

Identifiers and version histories should remain stable enough to support historical reconstruction. When an assessment is revised, the prior decision record should remain available according to retention requirements rather than being silently overwritten.

Traceability is particularly important when a material incident requires the organization to reconstruct the assumptions and approvals that governed the system at the time.

### 75. AI Risk Management Control Review Cycle

The organization must periodically review the effectiveness and suitability of its AI risk management controls.

The review frequency should reflect risk exposure, the pace of system change, regulatory requirements, and the consequences of control failure.

The review should consider whether:

- The risk methodology remains suitable.
- Risk criteria and thresholds remain aligned with appetite.
- Assessments cover the relevant AI inventory.
- Control requirements address emerging threats.
- Risk ownership and approval authorities remain clear.
- Monitoring and escalation operate effectively.
- Third-party assurance remains adequate.
- Risk reports support timely decisions.
- Repeated exceptions reveal systemic weaknesses.
- Lessons from incidents and audits have been incorporated.

The review should result in documented decisions, assigned improvement actions, accountable owners, and target dates.

A policy review that confirms the document's wording without evaluating operational effectiveness is insufficient. The objective is to determine whether the organization can consistently identify and manage AI risks as its technology, business use, and external obligations evolve.

### 76. Core AI Risk Management Control Principle

An effective AI Risk Management Policy must be implemented through controls that are explicit, proportionate, evidence-based, and enforceable. Each material risk must be connected to an accountable owner, an appropriate treatment decision, validated controls, documented residual exposure, and a defined process for monitoring and reassessment.

The organization must distinguish between **a control requirement and an implemented control**, **an implemented control and an effective control**, and **a reduced risk and an accepted risk**. These distinctions prevent procedural completion from being mistaken for actual risk reduction.

The control framework must ensure that risks are not merely recorded but actively managed, escalated when necessary, and kept within authorized limits throughout the AI lifecycle.

**Core Principle:** AI risk controls are effective only when they produce demonstrable risk reduction and support accountable decisions based on reliable evidence.

## Part 3 — AI Risk Management Policy Governance

### 77. AI Risk Management Governance Operating Model

AI risk management requires a formal governance operating model that defines how risk decisions are made, challenged, approved, monitored, and escalated across the organization.

The operating model must connect enterprise risk management with AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, business operations, technology management, and internal audit. These functions must work together without creating ambiguity about accountability or duplicating responsibilities unnecessarily.

The operating model should define:

- Governance bodies and their decision-making authority.
- Executive accountability for AI risk exposure.
- Business ownership of AI use cases and their outcomes.
- Technical responsibility for implementation and operation.
- Independent risk oversight and challenge.
- Assurance responsibilities and audit access.
- Escalation paths for unacceptable or changing risk.
- Reporting requirements and management review cycles.

A governance model must distinguish between responsibility for managing a risk and authority to accept that risk. A system owner may be responsible for implementing controls but may not possess the delegated authority to approve a high residual risk.

The model should be proportionate to organizational size, AI maturity, system criticality, and regulatory exposure. Smaller organizations may combine several functions, provided conflicts of interest are managed and independent challenge remains available for material decisions.

### 78. Three Lines Model for AI Risk Management

The organization should establish clear accountability for AI risk management using a governance structure aligned with the Three Lines Model or an equivalent enterprise risk framework.

The model separates operational responsibility, independent oversight, and independent assurance while recognizing that effective risk management requires cooperation among all three.

| Governance line | Primary responsibility                     | AI risk management examples                                                     |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line      | Own and manage risks                       | Business owners, AI product teams, developers, system operators, control owners |
| Second line     | Provide oversight, guidance, and challenge | Enterprise risk, AI governance, cybersecurity oversight, privacy, compliance    |
| Third line      | Provide independent assurance              | Internal audit or another appropriately independent assurance function          |

The first line is responsible for identifying and managing risks in day-to-day operations. The second line establishes methods, monitors adherence, challenges decisions, and reports material exposure. The third line evaluates whether governance, risk management, and controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

The model does not transfer risk ownership from operational management to the risk function. Nor does it mean that only the third line can test controls. Operational testing and second-line monitoring remain important, while third-line assurance must preserve its independence.

### 79. First-Line AI Risk Ownership

The first line must own the risks associated with the AI systems and business activities it operates.

Business owners are accountable for the intended purpose, business justification, operating boundaries, and consequences of the use case. Technical owners are responsible for relevant implementation and operational safeguards. Control owners must ensure that assigned controls operate as required.

First-line responsibilities include:

1. Registering AI systems and use cases.
2. Identifying risks within the operational context.
3. Participating in risk assessments.
4. Implementing approved risk treatments.
5. Maintaining accurate system and control documentation.
6. Monitoring relevant risk indicators.
7. Reporting incidents, control failures, and material changes.
8. Escalating risks that exceed delegated authority.
9. Maintaining evidence of control operation.
10. Supporting reassessment, audit, and remediation activities.

Business owners must not delegate all AI risk decisions to developers or technology suppliers. Technical expertise is necessary, but business accountability remains essential because the business determines how the AI capability is used and who may be affected.

### 80. Second-Line AI Risk Oversight

The second line provides independent oversight and challenge of the AI risk management activities performed by operational teams.

Its role is to establish consistent methods, verify adherence to governance requirements, challenge unsupported risk conclusions, and escalate material concerns.

Depending on the organizational structure, responsibilities may be distributed across AI governance, enterprise risk management, information security, privacy, legal, compliance, and model risk management.

The second line should:

- Maintain or oversee the risk assessment methodology.
- Define minimum evidence and documentation standards.
- Review risk classifications and material treatment decisions.
- Monitor high and critical residual risks.
- Challenge risk acceptance requests.
- Identify systemic weaknesses and recurring exceptions.
- Monitor regulatory and framework changes.
- Provide management reporting and risk insights.
- Coordinate risk escalation across functions.
- Advise on improvements to the governance framework.

Second-line oversight should not become the default owner of operational remediation. The function responsible for the underlying risk must remain accountable for implementing corrective action.

### 81. Third-Line AI Risk Assurance

The third line provides independent assurance over the adequacy and effectiveness of AI risk governance, risk management, and internal controls.

Internal audit should determine its coverage using a risk-based audit plan that considers the organization's AI inventory, exposure, regulatory obligations, prior findings, and material changes.

Potential audit areas include:

- Completeness and reliability of the AI system inventory.
- Compliance with policy and approval requirements.
- Quality and consistency of risk assessments.
- Authorization and independence of risk acceptance.
- Effectiveness of material controls.
- Management of third-party AI dependencies.
- Accuracy of risk reporting.
- Handling of incidents and exceptions.
- Closure of audit findings and remediation actions.
- Alignment between documented governance and actual practice.

Audit procedures should be designed according to the auditor's mandate, competence, and access to relevant evidence. Specialized technical assessments may require qualified support.

Internal audit should not approve operational AI risk decisions or assume responsibility for designing and operating controls that it is expected to assess independently.

### 82. Executive Accountability for AI Risk

Executive management must ensure that AI risks are managed consistently with enterprise strategy, approved risk appetite, legal obligations, and available organizational capabilities.

Executive accountability includes establishing governance arrangements, assigning sufficient resources, resolving cross-functional conflicts, and ensuring that material risk decisions receive appropriate attention.

Executives should receive information about:

- High and critical residual risks.
- AI systems with significant potential stakeholder impact.
- Material incidents and recurring control failures.
- Risks accepted outside normal management tolerances.
- Significant third-party dependencies.
- Overdue remediation of critical findings.
- Changes in legal or regulatory exposure.
- Areas where risk exceeds available control capability.

The organization must define which executives can authorize risk acceptance at each level and which decisions must be escalated to a board, board committee, or other governing body.

Executive sponsorship does not eliminate the need for documented approval authority. A general instruction to proceed with an AI initiative must not be treated as implicit acceptance of every associated risk.

### 83. AI Risk Management Committee Charter

Where justified by the organization's size and exposure, an AI Risk Management Committee or equivalent governance body should oversee material AI risk decisions and cross-functional dependencies.

The committee charter should define its purpose, scope, membership, authority, meeting frequency, quorum, decision rules, escalation responsibilities, and reporting obligations.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Reviewing the AI risk profile.
- Challenging high and critical risk assessments.
- Reviewing material risk acceptance requests.
- Resolving disagreements between business and control functions.
- Prioritizing cross-functional treatment actions.
- Reviewing material AI incidents and lessons learned.
- Monitoring risk appetite breaches.
- Approving governance standards within delegated authority.
- Escalating decisions beyond its authority.
- Reviewing progress against risk reduction objectives.

The committee should include representatives with appropriate business, technical, risk, security, privacy, legal, and compliance expertise according to the issues under consideration.

Meeting records should document decisions, conditions, dissenting views where material, assigned actions, owners, and deadlines. Committee membership alone does not establish that a decision was properly authorized.

### 84. AI Risk Decision Rights Matrix

The organization must define decision rights so that risk decisions are made by the appropriate authority and cannot be approved informally by individuals without delegated power.

A decision rights matrix should cover the complete risk lifecycle, from initial assessment to acceptance, escalation, and closure.

| Decision                                      | Responsible party                  | Approval or oversight                           |
| --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Initial use-case risk assessment              | Business and technical owners      | Relevant risk or governance review              |
| Risk treatment plan                           | Designated risk and control owners | Business owner or delegated authority           |
| Routine low-risk acceptance                   | Authorized business owner          | According to approved delegation                |
| High residual risk acceptance                 | Designated executive or committee  | Independent risk challenge                      |
| Critical risk or appetite breach              | Relevant executive risk authority  | Board-level escalation where required           |
| Material policy exception                     | Requesting business owner          | Authorized exception approver                   |
| Production authorization                      | Designated service authority       | Required risk and control gates                 |
| Risk closure                                  | Assigned risk owner and verifier   | Independent verification where warranted        |
| Suspension of an unsafe or compromised system | Designated operational authority   | Escalation and review under incident procedures |

This table is illustrative. The organization must adapt it to its actual governance structure, regulatory obligations, and delegated authorities.

No decision matrix should permit an individual to approve an activity that is prohibited by law or outside the organization's authority.

### 85. AI Risk Appetite Statement

The AI Risk Appetite Statement defines the amount and types of AI-related risk the organization is willing to accept while pursuing its objectives.

It should align with enterprise risk appetite and establish clear boundaries for unacceptable exposure. The statement must be sufficiently specific to guide real decisions rather than relying on general commitments to responsible innovation.

It should address the organization's tolerance for risks involving:

- Security and confidentiality.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Safety and operational reliability.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Human rights and discriminatory outcomes.
- Financial loss and service disruption.
- Model reliability and explainability where relevant.
- Third-party concentration and dependency.
- Autonomous actions and consequential decisions.
- Reputational damage and loss of stakeholder trust.

The statement should identify prohibited activities, mandatory safeguards, escalation thresholds, and the authorities responsible for decisions within each risk category.

Risk appetite is not a guarantee that all risks below a numerical threshold are acceptable. Mandatory legal requirements and severe potential harms may require restrictions regardless of the calculated score.

### 86. AI Risk Tolerance and Threshold Governance

Risk tolerance translates the broader risk appetite into operational thresholds and decision rules.

For example, the organization may establish maximum acceptable levels for overdue critical remediation, defined performance failure rates, unauthorized actions, or unresolved high-impact assessment findings.

Thresholds must be linked to measurable indicators, assigned owners, monitoring frequency, and escalation procedures.

The governance process should define:

1. The risk category and threshold.
2. The rationale for the threshold.
3. The source and calculation method for the indicator.
4. The authority responsible for approving the threshold.
5. The consequences of a breach.
6. The notification and escalation timeframe.
7. The required response and review process.

Thresholds should be reviewed when new evidence, incidents, business changes, or regulatory developments indicate that existing limits are no longer appropriate.

An operational threshold must not be interpreted as permission to cause harm up to that limit. It is a management trigger, not a substitute for legal compliance or substantive risk judgment.

### 87. AI Risk Escalation Governance

The organization must establish a governance process for escalating material AI risks to decision-makers with the authority and competence to respond.

Escalation should be based on documented criteria rather than informal relationships or an individual's willingness to raise concerns.

The escalation framework should identify:

- Risk severity and threshold breaches.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The required escalation recipient.
- Notification timeframes.
- Interim protective measures.
- Required decision and approval records.
- The process for unresolved disagreement.
- The authority to restrict or suspend operation.
- Reporting requirements after resolution.

Escalation must occur when a risk exceeds the owner's authority, an acceptance expires, a mandatory control fails, or material information invalidates a prior approval.

The organization should provide a route for employees and contractors to raise concerns without inappropriate retaliation. Concerns involving possible legal violations, serious harm, or deliberate circumvention may require additional reporting channels.

### 88. AI Risk Ownership Model

Every material AI risk must have a clearly identified owner who is accountable for ensuring that the risk is assessed, treated, monitored, and escalated.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority and access to resources to coordinate the response. Where the owner lacks the authority to implement the necessary treatment, the issue must be escalated.

The organization should distinguish the following roles:

- **Business owner:** Accountable for the business use case and its outcomes.
- **Risk owner:** Accountable for managing a specific risk.
- **Control owner:** Responsible for operating a defined control.
- **Action owner:** Responsible for completing a particular treatment activity.
- **Risk approver:** Authorized to accept the relevant residual risk.
- **Independent reviewer:** Provides challenge or verifies the assessment.
- **System owner:** Accountable for the technical service or system, according to organizational definitions.

One person may hold more than one role in a smaller organization, but the combination must not create unacceptable conflicts of interest or bypass required independent review.

### 89. AI Risk and Control Accountability Separation

The governance framework must distinguish accountability for risk from responsibility for controls.

A risk owner is concerned with the overall exposure and whether it remains acceptable. A control owner is responsible for operating a particular safeguard. The action owner is responsible for delivering a corrective action, while the approver determines whether a decision falls within delegated authority.

For example, a business owner may own the risk that an AI assistant exposes confidential information. The identity team may own access controls, the AI platform team may own prompt and retrieval safeguards, and the risk function may challenge the residual risk assessment.

No single control should be assumed to eliminate the full risk unless evidence supports that conclusion.

The organization should document dependencies among controls and identify who is responsible when a risk requires coordinated action across several teams.

### 90. AI Risk Governance Independence and Conflicts of Interest

Risk governance must identify and manage conflicts of interest that could undermine the objectivity of assessment, challenge, or acceptance decisions.

Conflicts may arise when the same individual is responsible for delivering a commercial objective, assessing whether that objective creates unacceptable risk, and approving the resulting exposure.

The organization should define when independent review is required and how conflicts are disclosed, mitigated, and documented.

Possible safeguards include:

- Separate preparation and approval responsibilities.
- Independent review of high-impact assessments.
- Escalation to a governance committee.
- Documented challenge of disputed ratings.
- Recusal from decisions where independence is compromised.
- Independent verification of critical remediation.
- Clear separation between operational ownership and assurance.

Independence should be proportionate to the severity of the decision. Excessive review of routine low-risk activities can create unnecessary delays, while insufficient challenge of material decisions can allow commercial pressure to override risk governance.

### 91. AI Risk Policy Hierarchy and Governance Integration

The AI Risk Management Policy must be integrated with the organization's broader policy and standards hierarchy.

It should establish overarching requirements while relying on supporting policies, standards, procedures, and technical controls for detailed implementation.

Relevant supporting documents may include:

- Enterprise risk management policy.
- AI governance policy.
- AI security policy.
- AI acceptable use policy.
- Information security policy.
- Privacy and data protection policy.
- Third-party risk management policy.
- Secure development and change management standards.
- Incident response and business continuity procedures.
- Model risk management standards.
- Data governance and retention standards.

The hierarchy must identify which document governs when requirements overlap or conflict. Legal and regulatory obligations take precedence over internal policy provisions, and more restrictive requirements may apply to specific regulated or high-impact use cases.

The organization should maintain traceability between policy requirements and supporting controls so that changes to one document do not leave inconsistent or obsolete requirements elsewhere.

### 92. AI Risk Governance Standards and Procedures

The policy must be supported by standards and procedures that translate governance principles into repeatable execution.

Standards define mandatory requirements, while procedures describe how designated activities are performed. Guidance may explain recommended practices but must not be presented as mandatory unless formally designated.

The organization should establish standards or procedures for:

- AI risk classification and scoring.
- Initial and periodic risk assessment.
- Risk treatment planning.
- Risk acceptance and exceptions.
- AI system inventory maintenance.
- Control testing and evidence collection.
- Risk escalation and reporting.
- Material change assessment.
- Third-party risk assessment.
- Incident-driven reassessment.
- Risk closure and assurance.

Each document should have an owner, approval authority, version history, effective date, review schedule, and defined applicability.

Procedures must remain consistent with the approved policy. If operational teams repeatedly cannot comply with a requirement, the organization should investigate whether the process is poorly designed, inadequately resourced, or genuinely incompatible with the required risk outcome.

### 93. AI Risk Regulatory Obligations Governance

The organization must establish a process for identifying, interpreting, assigning, and monitoring legal and regulatory obligations relevant to AI risk.

Applicable obligations depend on jurisdiction, sector, intended purpose, affected individuals, data processing, and the organization's role in the AI value chain.

The governance process should involve legal and compliance specialists when interpretation requires professional judgment. It should also distinguish binding obligations from voluntary frameworks, contractual requirements, and internal commitments.

The process should:

1. Identify potentially applicable laws and regulations.
2. Determine the organization's role and relevant obligations.
3. Map obligations to AI systems, use cases, and controls.
4. Assign accountable owners for implementation.
5. Record evidence supporting compliance conclusions.
6. Monitor changes in applicable requirements.
7. Reassess affected risks and controls.
8. Escalate potential noncompliance.

Frameworks such as ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework can support governance, but using a voluntary framework does not by itself establish compliance with applicable law.

### 94. AI Risk Framework Alignment

The organization should align its AI risk management process with relevant enterprise and AI governance frameworks while retaining a coherent internal decision model.

Depending on the organization's needs, reference points may include ISO 31000 for risk management, ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, applicable cybersecurity frameworks, and sector-specific model risk guidance.

Alignment should focus on the substance of risk management: governance, context, identification, analysis, evaluation, treatment, monitoring, communication, and continual improvement.

The organization should document:

- Which frameworks and requirements are in scope.
- How their concepts map to internal processes.
- Which requirements are mandatory and which are voluntary.
- How overlapping requirements are consolidated.
- How gaps and exceptions are managed.
- Which evidence demonstrates implementation.
- Who maintains the mappings as frameworks evolve.

The purpose of alignment is not to accumulate framework references. It is to create a consistent and auditable management process that satisfies applicable obligations and supports effective decisions.

### 95. AI Risk Methodology Governance

The organization must establish governance over the methodology used to identify, analyze, score, evaluate, and treat AI risks.

The methodology should be documented, approved, version-controlled, and applied consistently across relevant use cases.

Governance should define:

- Risk categories and definitions.
- Likelihood and impact scales.
- Risk scoring and classification rules.
- Mandatory escalation thresholds.
- Inherent and residual risk assessment requirements.
- Treatment and acceptance criteria.
- Requirements for uncertainty and evidence.
- Rules for qualitative overrides.
- Review and validation of the methodology.
- Training and communication requirements.

Methodology changes should be assessed for their effect on existing risk ratings and decisions. If a revised scoring model materially changes the classification of existing systems, the organization should determine whether reassessment or renewed approval is required.

A scoring methodology is a decision aid. It must not replace professional judgment, technical evidence, or mandatory legal analysis.

### 96. AI Risk Classification Governance

Risk classification determines the level of governance, review, controls, and approval required for an AI system or use case.

The classification scheme should consider both the likelihood and consequences of adverse outcomes, as well as relevant legal, operational, security, privacy, and stakeholder-impact factors.

Governance should define:

- Classification criteria and thresholds.
- The responsible assessor.
- Required independent review.
- Minimum controls for each class.
- Approval authority for deployment.
- Monitoring and reassessment frequency.
- Escalation conditions.
- Evidence requirements.
- Circumstances requiring classification override.

The organization should not rely solely on a model's technical complexity or vendor label. A simple AI capability embedded in a consequential decision process may warrant stronger controls than a technically sophisticated tool used for low-impact experimentation.

Classification must be reviewed when the intended purpose, affected population, autonomy, data exposure, or operating environment changes materially.

### 97. AI Risk Acceptance Governance

Risk acceptance governance establishes how the organization authorizes the retention of residual risk and verifies that the decision remains valid.

The process must define approval thresholds, required evidence, independence requirements, expiration rules, and conditions that invalidate acceptance.

Governance should ensure that:

- The risk is accurately described and assessed.
- Treatment options have been considered.
- Mandatory obligations are not waived.
- The decision-maker has delegated authority.
- Relevant control deficiencies are disclosed.
- Compensating controls are documented.
- Acceptance conditions are monitored.
- Expired decisions are escalated.
- Material changes trigger reconsideration.
- Decisions remain traceable for audit and review.

Risk acceptance must not be treated as a substitute for an unavailable control when the exposure is prohibited or exceeds the organization's authority.

The organization should periodically analyze accepted risks to identify recurring patterns that may indicate inadequate control investment, unrealistic delivery commitments, or weaknesses in the risk appetite framework.

### 98. AI Risk Exception Governance

Exception governance must establish a consistent process for temporary or otherwise authorized deviations from internal requirements.

The process should distinguish a policy exception from acceptance of residual risk. A policy exception authorizes a deviation from a defined internal requirement, while risk acceptance authorizes retention of a specified residual risk within the decision-maker's authority. One decision may require both records.

The exception process should define:

- Eligibility criteria.
- Required justification and evidence.
- Risk assessment requirements.
- Approval authority.
- Compensating controls.
- Expiration and renewal rules.
- Monitoring and review requirements.
- Revocation conditions.
- Remediation and closure requirements.
- Reporting of recurring exceptions.

Exceptions should be time-bound where possible and reviewed before expiration. Repeated renewals should trigger additional scrutiny because they may indicate that a supposedly temporary deviation has become a permanent operating model.

### 99. AI Risk Incident Governance

AI incidents must be integrated into the organization's incident management and risk governance processes.

An incident may reveal that an existing risk was underestimated, a control was ineffective, an assumption was invalid, or a new risk was not previously identified.

The governance process should establish how incidents are linked to risk records, how material risk ratings are reconsidered, and how corrective actions are monitored.

Incident governance should coordinate relevant functions, including security, privacy, legal, compliance, business operations, communications, and supplier management, according to the nature of the event.

Required activities may include:

- Immediate containment and harm reduction.
- Preservation of relevant evidence.
- Assessment of affected systems and stakeholders.
- Determination of reporting and notification obligations.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Reassessment of affected risks.
- Revision of controls and procedures.
- Tracking of corrective actions.
- Verification of remediation.
- Communication of lessons learned.

Incident closure must not automatically close the associated risk. The organization must separately determine whether the underlying exposure has been sufficiently reduced.

### 100. AI Risk Privacy Governance

Privacy governance must be integrated into AI risk decisions whenever personal data, profiling, sensitive inferences, or effects on individuals are relevant.

The organization should define the responsibilities of privacy professionals, business owners, data owners, and technical teams in identifying and treating privacy risk.

Governance should address the circumstances requiring privacy review, data protection impact assessment, consultation with legal specialists, and escalation of unresolved privacy concerns.

Privacy risk governance should consider:

- Purpose limitation and data minimization.
- Lawful processing and transparency.
- Data subject rights.
- Sensitive data and inferences.
- Retention and deletion.
- Model training and reuse of personal data.
- Provider and international transfer arrangements.
- Automated decision-making and profiling.
- Re-identification and disclosure risk.
- Privacy incidents and notification duties.

Where applicable law requires a specific assessment or consultation, the organization must complete it according to the relevant legal requirements. An AI risk assessment may provide supporting information but does not automatically replace a legally required privacy process.

### 101. AI Risk Cybersecurity Governance

Cybersecurity governance must ensure that AI-related security risks are managed within the organization's wider information security and technology risk framework.

The cybersecurity function should define relevant security requirements, contribute specialist threat analysis, review material findings, and monitor control effectiveness within its mandate.

Responsibilities may include:

- AI threat modeling and security architecture review.
- Identity and access management.
- Data protection and secrets management.
- Application and API security.
- Cloud and infrastructure security.
- Model and data supply chain security.
- Vulnerability management and security testing.
- Security logging and monitoring.
- AI incident response.
- Security risk reporting and escalation.

The governance model must clarify where AI-specific controls supplement existing security controls and where additional measures are necessary because of model behavior, natural-language interfaces, retrieval, or autonomous execution.

Cybersecurity approval should not be interpreted as acceptance of all business, privacy, safety, or legal risks. Material non-security risks require review by the relevant accountable functions.

### 102. AI Risk Legal and Compliance Governance

Legal and compliance functions should provide oversight of relevant legal obligations, contractual duties, regulatory exposure, and compliance risks associated with AI use.

Their involvement should be proportionate to the use case and the nature of the issues under consideration.

Governance processes should define when legal or compliance review is mandatory, including circumstances involving regulated decisions, sensitive data, intellectual property, consumer protection, employment, safety, or material contractual commitments.

The process should support:

- Identification of applicable obligations.
- Review of intended purpose and permitted use.
- Evaluation of contractual restrictions.
- Assessment of disclosure and transparency requirements.
- Review of regulatory classification where relevant.
- Escalation of potential noncompliance.
- Tracking of remediation and evidence.
- Reassessment following regulatory change.

The organization should not assume that a general legal review covers every operational control. Legal interpretation and operational implementation are related but distinct responsibilities.

### 103. AI Risk Third-Party Governance

Third-party AI risk governance must be integrated with supplier management, procurement, legal, information security, privacy, and business continuity processes.

The organization should establish a lifecycle process covering supplier selection, due diligence, contracting, onboarding, monitoring, material changes, incident response, and exit.

Governance requirements should be proportionate to the supplier's role and the potential consequences of disruption, misuse, or failure.

The process should establish:

- Risk-based supplier classification.
- Pre-contract assessment requirements.
- Required security and privacy assurances.
- Contractual controls and responsibility allocation.
- Monitoring of material supplier changes.
- Incident notification and cooperation requirements.
- Concentration and dependency analysis.
- Contingency and exit planning.
- Evidence retention and assurance access.
- Escalation of unacceptable supplier risk.

A supplier's reputation or certification does not eliminate the need to evaluate the particular service and intended use. The organization remains accountable for its own decisions and obligations, even when a provider operates the underlying model or infrastructure.

### 104. AI Risk Human Impact and Rights Governance

AI governance must identify when a use case could materially affect individuals, groups, communities, or other stakeholders.

The organization should define when specialized human impact, ethics, safety, accessibility, or rights-based review is required.

The review should consider, where relevant:

- Discrimination and disparate impact.
- Effects on access to employment, education, credit, healthcare, or public services.
- Privacy, dignity, and autonomy.
- Transparency and ability to contest decisions.
- Accessibility and effects on vulnerable populations.
- Human oversight and meaningful intervention.
- Potential physical or psychological harm.
- Distribution of benefits and burdens.
- Reversibility and availability of remedies.

A risk assessment should not treat the absence of documented complaints as proof that the system has no adverse impact. The organization should consider the quality of available evidence, the affected population, and whether monitoring can identify relevant harms.

Where applicable law establishes mandatory requirements or prohibitions, those requirements govern regardless of the outcome of an internal scoring model.

### 105. AI Risk Model Governance Integration

Where AI systems use models that require specialized model risk management, the organization should integrate model validation and oversight with the broader AI risk management process.

Model governance may include model inventory, model classification, independent validation, limitations documentation, change approval, performance monitoring, and retirement decisions.

The organization should define how model-level findings influence use-case risk ratings and deployment decisions.

For example, a model may pass a general technical validation but still be unsuitable for a particular business purpose because the consequences of error are too severe or the intended operating conditions differ from the validation environment.

Governance should ensure that:

- Model validation assumptions are documented.
- Validation evidence is relevant to the intended use.
- Model limitations are communicated to decision-makers.
- Material model changes trigger reassessment.
- Independent review is applied where warranted.
- Model findings are linked to risk treatment.
- Performance monitoring supports continued suitability.
- Model retirement and replacement are controlled.

Model validation and enterprise AI risk assessment are complementary processes. Neither should be assumed to replace the other.

### 106. AI Risk Governance for Human Oversight

The organization must define who is accountable for designing, operating, and evaluating human oversight mechanisms in AI-enabled workflows.

Governance should establish the level of human intervention required for different risk classes and specify when a person must review, approve, override, or stop an AI-driven process.

The requirements should consider the consequences of error, system autonomy, reviewer's competence, available information, and the feasibility of meaningful intervention.

The governance model should establish:

- Criteria for mandatory human review.
- Roles and authority of reviewers.
- Training and competence requirements.
- Conditions requiring escalation.
- Records of approvals and overrides.
- Monitoring of review quality.
- Procedures for challenging AI-generated recommendations.
- Review of automation bias and overreliance.
- Authority to suspend the process.

Human oversight must be evaluated as a control with its own failure modes. It should not be presumed effective simply because the workflow includes a human approval step.

### 107. AI Risk Governance for Autonomous Systems

AI systems capable of autonomous or semi-autonomous action require governance over the boundaries of delegated authority.

The organization must establish which actions may be executed automatically, which require approval, which are prohibited, and who can change those permissions.

Governance requirements should address:

- Scope of authorized actions.
- Identity and permissions used by the system.
- Access to tools and external services.
- Restrictions on sensitive transactions.
- Human approval thresholds.
- Monitoring and traceability.
- Emergency suspension authority.
- Recovery and rollback responsibilities.
- Change approval for autonomy expansion.
- Periodic review of delegated authority.

Increasing autonomy should be treated as a potential material change because it may alter the likelihood, speed, scale, and reversibility of harm.

An AI agent's ability to plan or reason does not establish authority to execute every action it can technically perform. Technical capability and authorized permission must remain distinct.

### 108. AI Risk Governance Reporting to the Board

Board-level reporting should provide sufficient information for oversight of material AI risk without overwhelming directors with operational detail.

The report should connect AI risk exposure to enterprise objectives, risk appetite, significant obligations, stakeholder impact, and management action.

Depending on the organization's exposure, board reporting may include:

- AI risk profile and material changes.
- High and critical residual risks.
- Significant incidents and lessons learned.
- Risk appetite breaches and acceptance decisions.
- Material third-party dependencies.
- Key control deficiencies.
- Overdue critical remediation.
- Significant legal or regulatory developments.
- Progress against governance objectives.
- Decisions requiring board or committee attention.

Reporting should distinguish current exposure from future concerns and clearly identify decisions required from the board.

The board should receive information about material uncertainty and limitations in the available data. A dashboard with favorable aggregate metrics must not obscure severe individual risks or unresolved compliance concerns.

### 109. AI Risk Governance Management Reporting

Management reporting should provide actionable information for allocating resources, resolving dependencies, and monitoring the performance of AI risk management.

Compared with board reporting, management reports may contain more operational detail, including risk ownership, treatment progress, control testing, overdue actions, and emerging patterns.

Reports should be designed around decisions and accountability rather than the volume of information collected.

The organization should define:

- Report owners and recipients.
- Reporting frequency.
- Standard risk categories and definitions.
- Required metrics and thresholds.
- Data quality and validation checks.
- Escalation rules for adverse trends.
- Follow-up actions and decision records.
- Requirements for communicating material changes.

Management should review whether reported metrics produce meaningful insight. If a metric encourages superficial completion or can be improved without reducing risk, it should be revised or supplemented with effectiveness measures.

### 110. AI Risk Governance Continuous Improvement

The AI risk governance framework must improve as the organization gains experience, changes its technology, and learns from operational results.

Continuous improvement should use evidence from incidents, control testing, internal audit, regulatory developments, stakeholder feedback, risk assessments, and performance monitoring.

The improvement process should identify whether issues are isolated or indicate systemic weaknesses in governance, methods, resources, or control design.

Typical improvement activities include:

- Revising risk criteria and assessment methods.
- Improving inventory and risk data quality.
- Strengthening role clarity and approval authority.
- Updating control requirements.
- Improving training and competence.
- Automating repeatable governance workflows.
- Reducing recurring exceptions.
- Improving third-party assurance.
- Refining reporting and escalation.
- Testing the effectiveness of corrective actions.

Improvement actions must have owners, target dates, and verification criteria. The organization should confirm that changes produce the intended outcome rather than treating publication of a revised document as proof of improvement.

### 111. AI Risk Governance Maturity Assessment

The organization should periodically evaluate the maturity of its AI risk governance capabilities to identify gaps and prioritize investment.

A maturity assessment should examine whether governance activities are defined, implemented consistently, measured, and improved.

An illustrative maturity scale is:

| Level          | Description                                                                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1 — Ad hoc     | Risk activities are reactive, inconsistent, and dependent on individuals                   |
| 2 — Developing | Basic policies and responsibilities exist but application is uneven                        |
| 3 — Defined    | Standardized processes, ownership, and approval requirements are established               |
| 4 — Managed    | Performance is measured, controls are tested, and reporting supports decisions             |
| 5 — Optimized  | Governance is continuously improved using evidence, feedback, and evolving risk conditions |

Maturity should be evaluated using evidence, not self-assessment alone. A policy may be well documented while actual risk assessments, control operation, or management decisions remain inconsistent.

The organization should prioritize improvements based on risk reduction, regulatory obligations, business criticality, and the feasibility of implementation. The objective is effective governance, not achievement of the highest maturity label for its own sake.

### 112. AI Risk Governance Final Principle

AI risk governance establishes who is accountable for risk, who operates and oversees controls, who can approve residual exposure, and how material decisions are challenged and assured.

An effective governance model connects operational ownership with independent oversight, executive accountability, clear decision rights, and evidence-based reporting. It must ensure that material risks reach the appropriate authority and that decisions remain traceable throughout the AI lifecycle.

The organization must distinguish between **risk ownership and control ownership**, **oversight and independent assurance**, **risk appetite and operational tolerance**, and **management approval and lawful authorization**. These distinctions prevent governance gaps, conflicts of interest, and unauthorized acceptance of unacceptable exposure.

**Core Principle:** AI risk governance is effective when every material risk has accountable ownership, every significant decision is made by an authorized and appropriately informed authority, and independent challenge and assurance support continued alignment with organizational risk appetite and applicable obligations.


# 20.04 — AI Risk Management Policy

## Part 4 — AI Risk Management Policy Architecture

### 113. AI Risk Management Reference Architecture

An enterprise AI Risk Management Policy must be supported by a reference architecture that connects governance decisions, risk assessment, technical controls, operational monitoring, and assurance evidence.

The architecture establishes how risk information moves from AI system discovery through assessment, treatment, authorization, monitoring, and reassessment. It must provide a consistent mechanism for translating enterprise risk requirements into enforceable technical and operational safeguards.

The reference architecture should include the following logical layers:

- **Governance layer:** Policy requirements, risk appetite, delegated authority, approval gates, and escalation rules.
- **Risk management layer:** Risk identification, scoring, treatment planning, acceptance, and reassessment.
- **AI system layer:** Models, applications, retrieval systems, agents, APIs, and supporting services.
- **Control enforcement layer:** Identity, access restrictions, data protection, guardrails, network controls, and approval mechanisms.
- **Monitoring layer:** Telemetry, risk indicators, control testing, anomaly detection, and incident detection.
- **Evidence and assurance layer:** Audit trails, assessment records, control evidence, management reporting, and independent review.

These layers must be connected rather than implemented as isolated tools. A critical risk identified during assessment should influence deployment authorization, technical restrictions, monitoring thresholds, and subsequent management reporting.

```
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|             ENTERPRISE AI GOVERNANCE                  |
| Policy | Risk Appetite | Decision Rights | Escalation  |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
                            |
                            v
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|                AI RISK MANAGEMENT                     |
| Identify -> Assess -> Treat -> Accept -> Reassess      |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
                            |
                            v
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|                  AI SYSTEM ENVIRONMENT                |
| Models | Applications | RAG | Agents | APIs | Data     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
                            |
                            v
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|               CONTROL ENFORCEMENT                    |
| IAM | Data Controls | Guardrails | Approvals | Network |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
                            |
                            v
+-------------------------------------------------------+
|              MONITORING AND ASSURANCE                 |
| Telemetry | Testing | Evidence | Audit | Reporting     |
+---------------------------+---------------------------+
                            |
                            +------> Risk Reassessment
```

**Architecture requirement:** Risk decisions must influence system behavior, and operational evidence must be capable of changing risk decisions.

### 114. AI Risk Governance Control Plane

The AI risk governance control plane provides the central mechanisms through which organizational risk requirements are translated into system-level policies and operational decisions.

It may include an AI governance platform, a risk register, a policy engine, workflow automation, identity integrations, model inventories, approval systems, and monitoring interfaces.

The control plane should support:

- Registration and classification of AI systems.
- Assignment of accountable owners.
- Association of systems with approved use cases.
- Application of risk-based control requirements.
- Workflow-driven assessment and approval.
- Tracking of treatment actions and exceptions.
- Distribution of approved technical policies.
- Monitoring of compliance with risk decisions.
- Centralized reporting and evidence references.

The control plane must not become a single point of failure for all AI operations without appropriate resilience and access safeguards. Its own availability, integrity, authorization, and change management should be assessed.

Where a central platform is unavailable, the organization should define safe fallback procedures for high-risk operations. Those procedures must not silently authorize actions that would otherwise require governance approval.

### 115. AI Risk Management Data Plane

The AI risk management data plane comprises the operational systems and information flows through which AI-related activity occurs.

It includes model inference requests, prompts, retrieved documents, model outputs, tool calls, external integrations, data pipelines, and resulting business transactions.

The risk management architecture must establish how data-plane activities are constrained by approved governance decisions.

Examples include:

- Limiting model access to approved data sources.
- Restricting AI agents to authorized tools.
- Preventing unapproved external destinations.
- Enforcing data classification and disclosure rules.
- Requiring human approval for designated actions.
- Logging material system activity.
- Blocking actions that violate mandatory restrictions.

The data plane must not be assumed to follow governance decisions merely because those decisions are recorded in a risk management platform. Technical enforcement, operational procedures, or a combination of both must implement the relevant requirements.

### 116. AI Risk-Based Architecture Requirements

Architecture requirements should be derived from the system's assessed risks rather than applied identically to every AI deployment.

A low-impact internal summarization tool may require basic access controls, approved data sources, output verification, and logging. An autonomous agent connected to financial systems may require stricter identity isolation, transaction limits, approval gates, comprehensive audit trails, and tested emergency suspension.

The organization should define risk-based architecture profiles that specify minimum requirements for relevant risk classes.

| Risk dimension            | Potential architecture requirement                                      |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sensitive data exposure   | Data minimization, access restrictions, redaction, controlled retention |
| Unauthorized actions      | Least privilege, action allowlists, transaction controls                |
| High-impact decisions     | Human review, validation, appeal or correction mechanisms               |
| External model dependency | Provider assurance, contractual controls, fallback arrangements         |
| Model manipulation        | Input validation, trust-boundary enforcement, adversarial testing       |
| Operational criticality   | Resilience, rollback, recovery, monitoring                              |
| Regulatory exposure       | Traceability, evidence retention, required approvals                    |
| High autonomy             | Restricted execution authority, action monitoring, emergency shutdown   |

The architecture profile should identify mandatory controls, permitted compensating controls, prohibited configurations, and evidence required before deployment.

### 117. AI Risk Trust Boundary Architecture

Trust boundaries define where information, authority, or execution moves between components with different security or governance assumptions.

An AI system may cross boundaries between users, enterprise data sources, model providers, retrieval systems, agent frameworks, external APIs, and business applications.

The architecture must identify these boundaries and assess the risks associated with each transition.

Relevant boundaries include:

- User to AI application.
- Application to model endpoint.
- Model to retrieval system.
- Retrieval system to enterprise repository.
- Agent to external tool.
- Internal service to third-party provider.
- AI-generated output to downstream business process.
- Development environment to production environment.

Each boundary should have explicit controls for authentication, authorization, input validation, data protection, monitoring, and error handling as appropriate.

Natural-language instructions and retrieved content must not automatically inherit the authority of the system that processes them. Untrusted content should be treated as information requiring evaluation, not as permission to change system policy or expand execution privileges.

### 118. AI Identity and Access Risk Architecture

Identity and access management must establish which users, services, models, and agents can access AI capabilities and supporting resources.

The architecture should distinguish human identities, service identities, workload identities, and agent execution identities. Where technically feasible, each identity should have a defined purpose, limited permissions, and attributable activity.

Required controls may include:

- Centralized authentication.
- Role-based or attribute-based authorization.
- Least-privilege permissions.
- Separation of privileged duties.
- Strong authentication for sensitive administrative actions.
- Short-lived credentials where feasible.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Revocation of unused or compromised identities.
- Segregation between development and production.
- Logging of material authorization decisions.

An AI agent should not inherit unrestricted access merely because its human initiator possesses broad privileges. The system's permissions should be limited to the approved operational purpose.

Access requirements must be derived from the risk assessment and validated against the actual execution paths, including indirect tool invocation and service-to-service access.

### 119. AI Privileged Access Risk Architecture

Privileged access creates elevated risk when AI systems can administer infrastructure, modify records, change security settings, or execute sensitive transactions.

The organization should identify privileged capabilities available to models, agents, administrators, and integrated services.

Controls should include, as appropriate:

- Separation of privileged and routine identities.
- Just-in-time or time-limited privileged access.
- Approval for sensitive operations.
- Restrictions on administrative APIs.
- Secure credential storage.
- Monitoring of privileged actions.
- Independent verification of high-impact changes.
- Emergency revocation mechanisms.
- Periodic review of privileged permissions.

Privileged credentials must not be embedded in prompts, model context, source code, or unprotected configuration files.

Where an AI system can initiate privileged operations, the organization must evaluate whether the approval and execution mechanism can be bypassed through alternative interfaces, indirect instructions, or chained tool calls.

### 120. AI Secrets Management Architecture

Secrets management protects credentials, API keys, tokens, certificates, signing keys, and other sensitive authentication material used by AI systems.

The architecture should ensure that secrets are issued to authorized components, stored securely, accessed only when required, rotated according to defined criteria, and revoked when exposure or compromise is suspected.

Relevant controls include:

- Centralized secrets management.
- Workload-specific authorization.
- Separation of secrets from prompts and model context.
- Restricted access to secret-management APIs.
- Credential rotation and expiration.
- Monitoring of secret access.
- Detection of accidental disclosure.
- Revocation and recovery procedures.
- Prevention of secrets appearing in logs or outputs.

A model should not receive a reusable credential simply because a downstream tool requires one. Where possible, the application or tool gateway should perform the authorized operation without exposing the underlying secret to the model.

### 121. AI Network Segmentation and Egress Control

Network architecture should restrict the paths through which AI applications, models, agents, and supporting services can communicate.

The risk assessment should identify required destinations, prohibited destinations, sensitive internal networks, and external dependencies.

Controls may include:

- Network segmentation.
- Private connectivity to model endpoints where supported.
- Egress allowlists.
- API gateways and controlled proxies.
- Restrictions on direct internet access.
- DNS and destination monitoring.
- Isolation of development and production environments.
- Segmentation of high-value data repositories.
- Detection of unauthorized outbound traffic.

Egress restrictions are particularly important when an AI application can generate requests or select destinations dynamically. Without effective restrictions, malicious instructions or compromised components may cause unauthorized disclosure or interaction with external services.

The architecture should validate network controls against actual traffic flows rather than relying solely on documented network diagrams.

### 122. AI API Gateway and Service Integration Risk

API gateways and service integration layers provide enforcement points between AI components and enterprise applications.

They can support authentication, authorization, schema validation, rate limiting, request inspection, logging, and policy enforcement.

The architecture should define which AI components can invoke which APIs and under what conditions.

Controls should include:

- Explicit API registration and ownership.
- Authentication and scoped authorization.
- Request and response validation.
- Rate and transaction limits.
- Protection against replay or duplicate execution where relevant.
- Sensitive data filtering.
- Monitoring of unusual invocation patterns.
- Version and configuration management.
- Failure handling and timeout policies.
- Evidence of sensitive operations.

An AI-generated API request should be treated as a request requiring authorization and validation, not as a trusted instruction. The downstream service must enforce its own authorization rules rather than relying exclusively on the AI application.

### 123. AI Data Governance and Provenance Architecture

Data governance architecture must support traceability of the information used by AI systems and provide safeguards against unauthorized, inaccurate, or inappropriate data use.

The architecture should identify relevant data sources, data owners, classifications, transformations, retention requirements, and permitted purposes.

Controls may include:

- Data cataloging and classification.
- Provenance and lineage records.
- Source authorization.
- Data quality validation.
- Access restrictions.
- Sensitive data detection and redaction.
- Retention and deletion enforcement.
- Versioning of datasets and retrieval indexes.
- Restrictions on unauthorized training or reuse.
- Monitoring for unexpected data movement.

Where an AI output materially influences a decision, the organization should preserve sufficient provenance to investigate the sources and transformations that contributed to that output, subject to applicable privacy, confidentiality, and retention requirements.

Data lineage supports risk analysis, but it does not prove that the source information is accurate or that the resulting model output is correct.

### 124. AI Data Classification Enforcement

Data classification must be translated into operational controls that constrain how AI systems access, process, transmit, and retain information.

The organization should define which data classes may be used with which AI services, under what conditions, and with which safeguards.

For example, confidential information may be restricted to approved enterprise AI services, while highly sensitive data may require additional authorization, private deployment, masking, or prohibition from particular use cases.

Enforcement mechanisms may include:

- Classification labels and metadata.
- Data loss prevention controls.
- Content inspection and redaction.
- Approved-service allowlists.
- Repository-level access controls.
- Prompt and attachment restrictions.
- Retention and deletion controls.
- Monitoring and alerting.
- Exception workflows.

The classification policy must account for derived information. An AI-generated summary, embedding, inference, or output may remain sensitive even if it does not reproduce the original data verbatim.

### 125. AI Model Endpoint and Deployment Risk Architecture

Model endpoints expose inference capabilities to applications, users, or other services and therefore require controls appropriate to their exposure and business purpose.

The architecture should identify endpoint ownership, permitted callers, model version, authentication requirements, data processing conditions, rate limits, and monitoring responsibilities.

Controls may include:

- Authenticated and authorized access.
- Network restrictions.
- Rate limiting and abuse detection.
- Request and output validation.
- Model version tracking.
- Usage quotas and cost controls.
- Monitoring for anomalous request patterns.
- Protection against unauthorized model extraction.
- Safe error handling.
- Controlled deployment and rollback.

Publicly exposed endpoints require particular attention to abuse, denial-of-service, data disclosure, and unauthorized use. Internal endpoints should not be assumed safe merely because they are not publicly reachable.

The organization should evaluate whether endpoint protections remain effective when multiple applications, tenants, or agent workflows share the same model service.

### 126. AI Model Registry and Version Governance

A model registry or equivalent inventory mechanism should maintain authoritative records of models used in development, testing, and production.

Records should identify model provenance, version, intended purpose, owner, validation status, known limitations, deployment status, and relevant risk approvals.

The governance process should prevent unapproved model versions from entering production and ensure that material changes trigger the appropriate review.

Controls may include:

- Model identity and version tracking.
- Approval status and deployment eligibility.
- Provenance and licensing records.
- Validation evidence.
- Known limitations and restrictions.
- Change history.
- Rollback capability.
- Links to risk assessments.
- Retirement and revocation status.

Where a third-party model changes without a corresponding internal deployment event, the organization should determine how it will detect the change and reassess the associated risk.

### 127. AI Model Supply Chain Risk Architecture

The AI model supply chain includes models, datasets, software packages, training pipelines, evaluation tools, plugins, infrastructure, and third-party services used to build or operate AI capabilities.

The organization must assess whether these dependencies introduce risks involving malicious modification, unauthorized access, vulnerable components, licensing restrictions, provenance gaps, or service discontinuity.

Controls may include:

- Supplier and component inventories.
- Provenance verification.
- Integrity checks and signed artifacts where supported.
- Dependency scanning.
- Controlled model and dataset acquisition.
- Secure build pipelines.
- Restricted artifact repositories.
- Review of licenses and usage terms.
- Vulnerability and advisory monitoring.
- Supplier change and incident notification.

The level of assurance should be proportionate to the component's importance and the consequences of compromise.

An artifact's presence in an approved repository does not alone establish that it is trustworthy. Approval should rely on documented acquisition, verification, assessment, and deployment controls.

### 128. AI Training and Fine-Tuning Pipeline Risk Architecture

Training and fine-tuning pipelines may introduce risks through unauthorized data, poisoned examples, insecure storage, compromised dependencies, or unapproved model changes.

The architecture should define how datasets are acquired, validated, transformed, accessed, and linked to resulting model versions.

Controls may include:

- Dataset provenance and authorization checks.
- Separation of training and production environments.
- Restricted access to training data.
- Dataset integrity verification.
- Review of data quality and contamination.
- Controlled experiment tracking.
- Reproducible pipeline configuration where feasible.
- Model evaluation before release.
- Approval of production promotion.
- Protection of intermediate artifacts.

Where training is performed by an external provider, the organization should assess what information is shared, whether it may be reused, and what assurance is available regarding processing and model changes.

A training pipeline should not promote a model solely because the training job completed successfully. Release decisions must consider validation results, intended use, security testing, and residual risk.

### 129. AI Retrieval-Augmented Generation Risk Architecture

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) introduces additional risk because model responses depend on retrieved information, access controls, indexing behavior, and the interaction between retrieved content and model instructions.

The architecture should define how source documents are authorized, indexed, retrieved, ranked, presented to the model, and referenced in outputs.

Controls should include:

- Source-level access enforcement.
- Document provenance and ownership.
- Segregation of data by tenant or access boundary.
- Validation of ingestion sources.
- Protection against unauthorized index modification.
- Filtering of sensitive information.
- Retrieval logging and traceability.
- Handling of malicious or untrusted content.
- Monitoring for unauthorized disclosure.
- Deletion and re-indexing procedures.

The system must not assume that a document is safe to follow merely because it came from an authorized repository. Retrieved content may contain outdated information, malicious instructions, or content that should not influence the system's execution behavior.

Access control must apply during retrieval, not merely when documents are initially uploaded.

### 130. AI Vector Database and Embedding Risk Architecture

Vector databases and embedding stores can expose sensitive information and create risks related to access control, tenant isolation, data provenance, retrieval manipulation, and retention.

The organization should evaluate whether embeddings or associated metadata can reveal confidential or personal information and whether the store is protected according to the sensitivity of its contents.

Controls may include:

- Authenticated access and least privilege.
- Tenant and collection isolation.
- Source document authorization checks.
- Metadata filtering and validation.
- Encryption and key management.
- Restricted administrative operations.
- Monitoring of unusual queries and access.
- Controlled indexing and deletion.
- Backup protection.
- Evidence of data lineage and ownership.

Deleting a source document may not automatically remove all associated embeddings, caches, replicas, or derived artifacts. The architecture should define how deletion and retention requirements propagate through the complete retrieval lifecycle.

### 131. AI Prompt and Context Security Architecture

Prompt and context security governs how system instructions, developer instructions, user inputs, retrieved content, conversation history, and other contextual information influence AI behavior.

The architecture should distinguish trusted configuration from untrusted user or retrieved content and define how each category is handled.

Controls may include:

- Protected system configuration.
- Separation of instructions from untrusted content where supported.
- Input validation and content inspection.
- Restrictions on sensitive context exposure.
- Context minimization.
- Prompt injection testing.
- Output validation.
- Access-controlled conversation history.
- Safe handling of tool instructions.
- Monitoring for attempted policy bypass.

Prompt-based safeguards should not be treated as the only enforcement mechanism for high-impact operations. Sensitive data access, privileged actions, and external transactions should be controlled through authorization and validation mechanisms outside the model's natural-language reasoning process.

### 132. AI Output Validation and Downstream Risk Architecture

AI outputs may be inaccurate, incomplete, biased, manipulated, or unsuitable for the intended purpose. Architecture controls should prevent unverified outputs from automatically causing unacceptable downstream consequences.

The required validation depends on the output type and its use.

Controls may include:

- Schema and format validation.
- Verification of source references.
- Domain-specific rule checks.
- Confidence or uncertainty handling where meaningful.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Restrictions on direct execution of generated code.
- Sanitization of output rendered in applications.
- Validation of generated queries and API requests.
- Rejection of invalid or incomplete results.
- Logging of consequential output decisions.

A syntactically valid output is not necessarily factually correct or safe. Validation should address the relevant failure mode, and the organization should define what happens when an output cannot be verified.

### 133. AI Agent Gateway and Tool Governance Architecture

An AI agent gateway or equivalent enforcement component can mediate interactions between an agent and the tools it is authorized to use.

The gateway should enforce policy independently of the agent's generated instructions.

Relevant controls include:

- Registered and approved tool definitions.
- Tool-specific authorization.
- Input and output schema validation.
- Restriction of sensitive operations.
- Rate and transaction limits.
- Approval requirements for designated actions.
- Logging of tool requests and results.
- Detection of unusual invocation patterns.
- Credential isolation.
- Revocation of tool access.

The gateway should validate the actual operation requested, the identity under which it will execute, and the resources affected. It should not rely solely on the model's explanation of why an action is necessary.

Where an agent can access tools through multiple paths, the organization must ensure that equivalent controls cover all relevant execution routes.

### 134. AI Agent Planning and Execution Separation

Separating planning from execution can reduce the risk that an AI agent's generated reasoning directly triggers sensitive actions without authorization.

The architecture may allow an agent to propose a plan, while a separate policy enforcement component validates the proposed actions against approved permissions and constraints.

A controlled execution flow may include:

1. Receive the request and identify the intended objective.
2. Generate a proposed action plan.
3. Validate the plan against policy and risk restrictions.
4. Require human approval where mandated.
5. Execute authorized actions through controlled tools.
6. Verify the resulting state.
7. Record actions, decisions, and evidence.
8. Stop or escalate if an unexpected result occurs.

This separation is not a guarantee against malicious or incorrect plans. The enforcement layer must validate actual operations and their consequences rather than simply approve the plan as a whole.

### 135. AI Agent Memory and State Management Risk Architecture

Agent memory and persistent state can influence future behavior, retain sensitive information, and create risks when data becomes stale, corrupted, or accessible across users or tenants.

The architecture should define what information may be stored, why it is retained, who can access it, how it is validated, and when it must be deleted.

Controls may include:

- Separation of short-term context and persistent memory.
- User- or tenant-specific isolation.
- Data classification and retention rules.
- Provenance and timestamps.
- Protection against unauthorized memory modification.
- Validation of information before consequential reuse.
- Access controls and audit trails.
- Deletion and correction mechanisms.
- Restrictions on sensitive data retention.
- Recovery from corrupted or manipulated state.

Persistent memory should not be treated as an authoritative source simply because it was recorded during an earlier interaction. Material decisions should use verified sources and current authorization.

### 136. AI Transaction and High-Impact Action Controls

AI systems that initiate financial transactions, modify important records, issue external communications, or perform other consequential actions require explicit transaction controls.

The architecture should define which actions are permitted, which require approval, and which must never be performed autonomously.

Controls may include:

- Transaction allowlists.
- Amount, frequency, and volume limits.
- Separation of initiation and approval.
- Dual authorization for designated operations.
- Validation of recipient and destination.
- Duplicate-action prevention.
- State verification after execution.
- Tamper-resistant transaction logs.
- Emergency suspension.
- Reconciliation and recovery procedures.

The organization should test failure scenarios such as repeated execution, ambiguous instructions, partial completion, compromised context, and unexpected downstream responses.

A system's ability to execute an action quickly does not justify removing approval safeguards. Authorization requirements should reflect the consequences and reversibility of the action.

### 137. AI Human Approval Gateway Architecture

A human approval gateway mediates actions that require a person to make an explicit decision before execution.

The gateway should present the reviewer with enough information to understand the action, its target, its consequences, and any material uncertainty.

The approval mechanism should record:

- The proposed action.
- The AI system and identity requesting it.
- Relevant supporting information.
- Risk classification and applicable restrictions.
- The reviewer's identity and authority.
- Approval, rejection, or requested modification.
- Time of decision.
- Any conditions imposed.
- The final execution result.

Approval must be bound to the specific action and context. A general approval for an agent to operate should not automatically authorize every future transaction.

The architecture should prevent approval reuse, unauthorized alteration of the approved action, and execution after an approval has expired or become invalid.

### 138. AI Logging and Audit Trail Architecture

Logging must provide sufficient evidence to reconstruct material AI activity, investigate incidents, verify control operation, and support risk reassessment.

The logging architecture should identify which events must be recorded, where records are stored, who can access them, how integrity is protected, and how long they are retained.

Relevant events may include:

- Authentication and authorization decisions.
- Model and configuration versions.
- Material requests and responses.
- Retrieval and tool invocation events.
- Human approval decisions.
- Policy enforcement and rejection events.
- Changes to permissions and system configuration.
- Risk acceptance and exception decisions.
- Security alerts and incident actions.
- Control tests and remediation verification.

Logging should be proportionate to risk and must account for privacy, confidentiality, and data minimization. Recording every prompt or output may be inappropriate where it creates excessive sensitive-data exposure.

The organization should define how sensitive values are redacted, how logs are protected against alteration, and how evidence remains available for required investigations and audits.

### 139. AI Observability and Risk Telemetry Architecture

Observability provides information about system behavior, performance, dependencies, and control operation. Risk telemetry uses this information to identify conditions that may require intervention or reassessment.

The architecture should connect relevant operational signals to defined risk indicators and escalation procedures.

Signals may include:

- Model latency and failure rates.
- Output validation failures.
- Unusual token or resource consumption.
- Tool invocation anomalies.
- Authorization denials and privilege changes.
- Human override rates.
- Retrieval failures or unexpected data access.
- Model performance degradation.
- Security alerts and incident indicators.
- Provider availability and dependency failures.

Telemetry should be designed around actionable risk signals rather than collected indiscriminately.

A low rate of alerts is not evidence of low risk if the system lacks adequate instrumentation. The organization should assess telemetry coverage, signal quality, blind spots, and the reliability of the mechanisms used to interpret events.

### 140. AI Security Information and Event Management Integration

AI-related security events should be integrated into the organization's security monitoring architecture where doing so improves detection, investigation, and response.

Security information and event management (SIEM) integration can correlate AI activity with identity events, endpoint telemetry, cloud logs, network activity, and other enterprise signals.

Relevant use cases may include:

- Unusual model endpoint access.
- Unauthorized agent tool invocation.
- Attempts to access restricted repositories.
- Repeated policy enforcement failures.
- Suspicious outbound data movement.
- Unexpected changes to model configuration.
- Abnormal use of privileged credentials.
- Provider or dependency security incidents.
- Attempts to bypass approval workflows.

Integration should define event schemas, severity mapping, ownership, alert routing, and response procedures.

The organization must avoid treating SIEM integration as a substitute for AI-specific validation. Some risks, such as subtle output quality degradation or harmful recommendations, may require application-level monitoring and domain-specific evaluation.

### 141. AI Risk Policy-as-Code Architecture

Policy-as-code enables selected governance requirements to be expressed as machine-enforceable rules.

Examples include prohibiting unapproved model versions, restricting data classifications, enforcing deployment approval gates, limiting agent permissions, or preventing production deployment when mandatory assessment fields are missing.

Policy-as-code can improve consistency and reduce reliance on manual checks, but it must be governed like any other critical control.

The architecture should define:

- Approved policy sources and owners.
- Version control and change approval.
- Testing before release.
- Separation of development and production.
- Enforcement points.
- Exception handling.
- Monitoring of policy decisions.
- Rollback and recovery.
- Evidence retention.
- Periodic effectiveness review.

Machine-enforced policies should be traceable to approved governance requirements. The organization must also define how policy changes are assessed for unintended consequences and how enforcement failures are detected.

### 142. AI Governance Workflow Automation

Workflow automation can support risk assessments, approvals, reassessments, remediation, exceptions, and evidence collection.

The architecture should ensure that automated workflows preserve decision authority and do not convert administrative completion into substantive risk approval.

Automation may be used to:

- Assign assessments based on system classification.
- Require specified reviewers.
- Prevent progression when mandatory fields are missing.
- Notify owners of approaching deadlines.
- Escalate overdue actions.
- Expire temporary exceptions.
- Trigger reassessment after material changes.
- Collect evidence references.
- Generate management reports.

Automated workflows should be tested for authorization errors, incorrect routing, duplicate records, stale approvals, and unintended bypass paths.

A workflow should not automatically accept high residual risk merely because all mandatory form fields have been completed. Substantive decisions must remain with authorized decision-makers.

### 143. AI Risk Detection and Anomaly Response Architecture

The organization should establish mechanisms for detecting unusual activity or conditions that may indicate increasing AI risk.

Detection methods may include threshold rules, statistical analysis, behavioral baselines, security detections, domain-specific validation, and human review.

Potential detection scenarios include:

- Unexpected access to sensitive data.
- Unusual patterns of tool invocation.
- Sudden increases in output rejection rates.
- Material deviations in model behavior.
- Repeated attempts to bypass restrictions.
- Abnormal external communication.
- Unexpected transaction volume.
- Changes in error or override rates.
- Unexplained provider behavior changes.

Detection must be connected to response actions. The organization should define when an alert requires investigation, additional monitoring, restricted operation, suspension, or reassessment.

Detection models and rules can themselves generate false positives and false negatives. Their effectiveness should be evaluated against documented objectives and available evidence.

### 144. AI Risk Intelligence and Threat-Informed Architecture

AI risk management should incorporate relevant threat intelligence, vulnerability information, supplier advisories, incident lessons, and emerging attack techniques.

The organization should determine which information sources are relevant, who evaluates their credibility, and how identified threats affect internal risk assessments.

Threat-informed activities may include:

- Monitoring AI-specific vulnerability disclosures.
- Reviewing attacks against similar AI systems.
- Tracking provider security advisories.
- Evaluating new prompt injection and model abuse techniques.
- Assessing threats to model and data supply chains.
- Updating threat models and control requirements.
- Testing controls against relevant attack scenarios.
- Communicating material changes to system owners.

Threat intelligence should be translated into specific decisions. A newly disclosed technique may require a vulnerability review, additional testing, revised monitoring, or temporary restriction of a system.

The existence of a published threat does not automatically establish that a particular system is vulnerable; applicability should be evaluated using the system's actual architecture and configuration.

### 145. AI Vulnerability and Weakness Management Architecture

The organization must establish processes for identifying, assessing, prioritizing, remediating, and verifying vulnerabilities affecting AI systems and their dependencies.

The process should cover conventional software vulnerabilities as well as weaknesses in model integration, retrieval controls, agent permissions, data pipelines, and AI-specific security safeguards.

Prioritization should consider severity, exploitability, exposure, affected assets, potential impact, available mitigations, and applicable remediation requirements.

Controls may include:

- Asset and dependency identification.
- Vulnerability scanning and advisory monitoring.
- Security testing and penetration testing.
- Risk-based remediation deadlines.
- Temporary mitigations and restrictions.
- Verification of remediation.
- Escalation of overdue critical findings.
- Evidence of closure.
- Reassessment after significant weaknesses are identified.

A vulnerability score alone should not determine remediation priority. A moderate technical weakness affecting a highly consequential AI process may require faster action than a more severe issue in a strongly isolated, low-impact environment.

### 146. AI Red Teaming and Adversarial Testing Architecture

AI red teaming and adversarial testing evaluate whether a system can be manipulated, misused, or caused to produce unacceptable outcomes under realistic threat conditions.

Testing scope should be based on the system's intended purpose, architecture, exposure, autonomy, and potential consequences.

Relevant test scenarios may include:

- Direct and indirect prompt injection.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Unauthorized tool use.
- Retrieval access-control failures.
- Model abuse and service exhaustion.
- Unsafe or invalid output generation.
- Privilege escalation through integrations.
- Manipulation of persistent memory.
- Evasion of approval controls.
- Failure of containment and recovery mechanisms.

The testing process should define authorization, scope, safety constraints, evidence handling, reporting, and remediation requirements.

Findings must be connected to risk records and treatment actions. A successful test campaign does not establish that all risks have been eliminated; the organization must evaluate test coverage, limitations, and changes in the threat environment.

### 147. AI Risk Acceptance Enforcement Architecture

The architecture should enforce the operational consequences of risk acceptance decisions.

A risk acceptance record should specify the system, approved scope, residual risk, authority, duration, conditions, and any required restrictions. Where feasible, those conditions should be represented in the systems that govern deployment, access, and operation.

Examples include:

- Preventing deployment until required approvals exist.
- Limiting an accepted use case to specified users.
- Restricting access to particular data sources.
- Requiring additional human approval.
- Applying temporary transaction limits.
- Generating alerts before acceptance expiry.
- Blocking operation after acceptance becomes invalid.
- Triggering reassessment after material change.

The organization must not rely solely on a document stored in a repository when the decision requires an enforceable operational restriction.

Where technical enforcement is not feasible, compensating procedures and independent verification should be defined and documented.

### 148. AI Risk Exception Enforcement Architecture

Exception governance should be connected to the technical and operational systems affected by the exception.

The architecture should identify which restrictions are temporarily modified, the scope of the permitted deviation, the approving authority, and the expiry conditions.

Controls may include:

- Exception identifiers linked to affected assets.
- Automated expiry notifications.
- Time-limited access or configuration changes.
- Restricted scope and permitted operations.
- Monitoring of exception-related activity.
- Evidence of compensating control operation.
- Revocation or rollback mechanisms.
- Alerts for use outside the approved scope.

The architecture should prevent a temporary exception from silently becoming a permanent configuration. Where possible, expired exceptions should automatically trigger a restriction or escalation rather than relying entirely on manual reminders.

An exception does not remove the need to reassess residual risk or comply with mandatory obligations.

### 149. AI Third-Party Assurance Architecture

Third-party assurance architecture connects supplier due diligence, contractual commitments, provider evidence, operational monitoring, and risk decisions.

The organization should maintain a record of which suppliers support each AI system and which assurance requirements apply to those dependencies.

Assurance information may include security certifications, independent reports, penetration testing summaries, vulnerability management practices, data processing terms, incident notification arrangements, and business continuity capabilities.

The architecture should support:

- Supplier-to-system dependency mapping.
- Assessment of assurance scope and exclusions.
- Tracking of evidence dates and expiry.
- Monitoring of material provider changes.
- Recording of supplier findings.
- Linking findings to internal risks.
- Tracking remediation and contractual actions.
- Escalation of unacceptable supplier exposure.
- Exit and substitution planning.

Supplier assurance should be evaluated against the specific service and risk context. A report covering a provider's general environment may not adequately address the particular model, feature, deployment region, or data-processing arrangement used by the organization.

### 150. AI Shared Responsibility Architecture

When AI services depend on external providers, the organization must establish which security, operational, data, and governance responsibilities belong to each party.

Responsibility allocation varies by service model, deployment configuration, contract, and the organization's role. It should be documented rather than inferred from general descriptions of cloud or AI services.

A shared responsibility matrix should identify responsibility for:

- Infrastructure security.
- Model hosting and maintenance.
- Identity and access controls.
- Data classification and permitted use.
- Prompt and output handling.
- Application-level safeguards.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability remediation.
- Incident notification and response.
- Backup, recovery, and continuity.
- Regulatory obligations and evidence.
- Decommissioning and data disposal.

The matrix should identify shared responsibilities as well as responsibilities assigned to one party.

Outsourcing a model or platform does not automatically transfer the organization's accountability for its own data, users, decisions, and legal obligations. Internal controls must address the responsibilities that remain with the organization.

### 151. AI Operational Resilience and Continuity Architecture

AI systems that support important business processes must be assessed for availability, recovery, and continuity risks.

The architecture should identify critical dependencies, acceptable disruption, recovery objectives, fallback processes, and the consequences of degraded or unavailable AI capabilities.

Controls may include:

- Redundant service components where justified.
- Provider outage monitoring.
- Backup and recovery procedures.
- Alternative models or service providers.
- Manual fallback workflows.
- Defined recovery time and recovery point objectives where applicable.
- Controlled degradation of functionality.
- Data and configuration recovery.
- Dependency failure testing.
- Communication and escalation procedures.

Fallback mechanisms must preserve essential safety, security, and authorization requirements. Switching to an alternative model should not bypass required validation or introduce an unassessed data-processing arrangement.

The organization should periodically test continuity arrangements rather than relying solely on provider availability commitments or documented recovery plans.

### 152. AI Rollback and Recovery Architecture

Rollback and recovery capabilities enable the organization to restore an approved state after a faulty model update, configuration change, compromised dependency, or material control failure.

The architecture should identify which components can be restored, how the previous state is verified, and how recovery affects data, transactions, and downstream systems.

Relevant controls include:

- Versioned model and configuration artifacts.
- Approved deployment packages.
- Reproducible configuration records where feasible.
- Data and index recovery procedures.
- Reversal or reconciliation of consequential actions.
- Integrity checks after recovery.
- Controlled reauthorization before resuming service.
- Evidence preservation.
- Recovery testing.
- Post-recovery risk reassessment.

Rollback is not always sufficient to reverse AI-related harm. A disclosed secret, external communication, or irreversible transaction may require separate containment and remediation.

The recovery process should therefore address both restoration of the system and management of consequences that cannot be technically reversed.

### 153. AI Emergency Suspension and Kill-Switch Architecture

A high-risk AI system may require an emergency mechanism to stop or restrict its operation when continued activity could cause unacceptable harm.

The mechanism should be designed according to the system's architecture and potential failure modes. A single application-level disable button may be insufficient if the system has independent workers, persistent sessions, external tool access, or queued actions.

The design should consider:

- Authority to initiate suspension.
- Technical mechanisms for disabling execution.
- Revocation of credentials and tool permissions.
- Handling of queued or in-progress actions.
- Prevention of unauthorized reactivation.
- Preservation of required evidence.
- Notification and escalation.
- Safe fallback operation.
- Verification that execution has stopped.
- Conditions for controlled restart.

Emergency suspension should be tested in an appropriate environment and at a frequency proportionate to risk.

Restart should require confirmation that the initiating risk has been addressed, relevant controls have been verified, and the appropriate authority has approved resumed operation.

### 154. AI Configuration Management and Drift Control

AI configuration management ensures that deployed systems remain aligned with approved risk assumptions and control requirements.

Relevant configurations may include model versions, system instructions, retrieval sources, tool permissions, network rules, safety settings, data pipelines, and monitoring thresholds.

The architecture should maintain a record of approved configurations and detect unauthorized or material changes.

Controls may include:

- Version control for configuration artifacts.
- Approval of material changes.
- Separation of development and production.
- Configuration baselines.
- Automated drift detection.
- Periodic configuration review.
- Evidence of deployment changes.
- Rollback procedures.
- Restricted administrative access.
- Reassessment when assumptions become invalid.

Configuration drift can create risk even when the underlying model remains unchanged. For example, a new retrieval source or expanded agent permission may materially change data exposure and execution authority.

### 155. AI Evidence Integrity and Provenance Architecture

Evidence used for risk decisions must be sufficiently reliable to support audit, investigation, and management review.

The architecture should preserve the relationship between evidence, the system configuration, the assessment version, and the decision made at the relevant time.

Controls may include:

- Timestamped evidence records.
- Versioned assessments and approvals.
- Access-controlled repositories.
- Integrity protection for critical records.
- Provenance metadata.
- Links to source systems.
- Retention and legal hold procedures.
- Audit trails for evidence changes.
- Separation of evidence creation and approval where warranted.
- Procedures for identifying missing or unreliable records.

The organization should avoid relying exclusively on screenshots or manually prepared summaries when authoritative system records are available and necessary to verify a material claim.

Evidence integrity does not guarantee that evidence is accurate or complete. The assurance process must also evaluate the source, scope, reliability, and limitations of the information.

### 156. AI Risk Observability Coverage Assessment

The organization must assess whether the available telemetry is sufficient to detect the risk conditions it claims to monitor.

For each material risk, the monitoring design should identify the observable signals, detection method, expected coverage, known blind spots, response threshold, and responsible function.

The review should consider:

- Whether relevant events are generated.
- Whether telemetry covers all material components.
- Whether logs can be correlated across services.
- Whether sensitive information is protected.
- Whether alerts reach the appropriate responders.
- Whether thresholds reflect risk appetite.
- Whether false positives and false negatives are evaluated.
- Whether monitoring failures are themselves detected.
- Whether monitoring evidence is retained.
- Whether coverage changes trigger reassessment.

Where a risk cannot be monitored reliably, the organization should consider alternative controls, additional instrumentation, reduced operating scope, or a more conservative residual risk conclusion.

The absence of observed failures is meaningful only when the monitoring process has adequate coverage and sensitivity.

### 157. AI Privacy-Preserving Monitoring Architecture

AI monitoring must balance security and risk visibility with privacy, confidentiality, and data minimization.

The architecture should collect the information necessary to detect and investigate relevant events while avoiding unnecessary retention or disclosure of sensitive prompts, outputs, personal data, or proprietary content.

Potential safeguards include:

- Redaction and tokenization.
- Role-based access to logs.
- Separation of identifying information from operational metrics.
- Purpose-limited access to conversation records.
- Retention schedules and deletion controls.
- Restricted use of monitoring data.
- Auditing of access to sensitive logs.
- Secure handling of incident evidence.
- Privacy review of new monitoring capabilities.

The organization should evaluate whether aggregate or metadata-based telemetry can meet the monitoring objective without retaining full content.

Where detailed content must be retained for a legitimate and authorized purpose, access and retention should be controlled according to the applicable requirements.

### 158. AI Risk Interoperability and Portability Architecture

Interoperability and portability affect the organization's ability to change providers, migrate systems, reproduce assessments, and preserve governance evidence.

The architecture should avoid unnecessary dependence on a single proprietary format when that dependence would materially impair risk management or operational continuity.

Relevant considerations include:

- Exportable inventory and risk register records.
- Portable assessment and approval evidence.
- Documented model and configuration versions.
- Standardized control and risk identifiers.
- Data and retrieval export capabilities where permitted.
- Alternative provider readiness.
- Retention of historical decisions.
- Dependency mapping.
- Contractual exit provisions.
- Secure transfer and deletion verification.

Portability must respect licensing, intellectual property, privacy, and contractual restrictions. The organization should determine which artifacts can be transferred and which may require recreation or replacement.

A migration plan should include reassessment of the destination environment rather than assuming that an approved risk decision automatically transfers to a different provider or architecture.

### 159. AI Risk Retention and Deletion Architecture

The architecture must implement retention and deletion requirements for AI-related data, evidence, and derived artifacts.

The relevant data may include prompts, outputs, uploaded files, retrieval indexes, embeddings, training datasets, model artifacts, logs, cached content, backups, and supplier-held records.

The organization should define retention periods and deletion rules according to legal, regulatory, contractual, security, privacy, and operational requirements.

Controls may include:

- Data lifecycle classification.
- Retention schedules.
- Automated deletion workflows.
- Propagation of deletion requests.
- Management of replicas and caches.
- Backup retention and expiry.
- Provider deletion verification where available.
- Exceptions for legal holds.
- Evidence of deletion.
- Periodic retention compliance reviews.

Deleting a conversation does not necessarily delete every derivative or backup associated with it. The organization must understand the actual storage and processing architecture before making claims about complete deletion.

### 160. AI Decommissioning and End-of-Life Risk Architecture

AI system retirement must be governed to prevent residual access, untracked dependencies, unnecessary data retention, and unsupported services from creating continuing exposure.

The architecture should identify how the system will be disabled, how dependent services will be updated, and how relevant data and evidence will be retained or disposed of.

Decommissioning controls should cover:

- Formal retirement authorization.
- Inventory and dependency updates.
- Revocation of identities and credentials.
- Removal of endpoints and integrations.
- Termination of supplier access and services.
- Disposal or transfer of data and artifacts.
- Retention of required audit evidence.
- Closure of monitoring and alerting dependencies.
- Verification that the system is no longer accessible.
- Closure or transfer of residual risks.

Retirement must be verified technically and administratively. Removing an application from a user interface does not prove that background services, credentials, APIs, or data stores have been disabled.

### 161. AI Architecture Risk Testing and Verification

The organization must verify that the implemented architecture satisfies the controls and risk assumptions documented in the assessment.

Verification should be performed at the level appropriate to the risk, from configuration review and control testing to penetration testing, adversarial exercises, resilience testing, and independent validation.

The verification plan should identify:

- The requirement being tested.
- The risk addressed by the requirement.
- The test method and scope.
- Preconditions and expected outcomes.
- Evidence to be retained.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Deficiencies and corrective actions.
- Retesting requirements.
- The authority responsible for accepting the results.

Tests should include relevant failure paths and bypass attempts. A successful test of the intended workflow does not demonstrate that unauthorized alternative paths are blocked.

The organization should ensure that material findings are resolved or explicitly managed before production authorization.

### 162. AI Architecture Control Failure Management

Architecture controls can fail because of configuration errors, software defects, dependency changes, authorization weaknesses, unavailable services, or unexpected interactions among components.

The organization must define how control failures are detected, assessed, contained, and remediated.

The response should consider whether the failure affects one system or several systems that share a component or control.

Required activities may include:

- Identification of the failed control and affected assets.
- Determination of potential consequences.
- Temporary restriction or suspension.
- Preservation of evidence.
- Notification of relevant owners and responders.
- Assessment of related control dependencies.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Corrective action and retesting.
- Risk reassessment.
- Review of similar systems for the same weakness.

Control failure should not automatically result in continued operation under the original risk rating. Where the failure removes a material safeguard, residual risk must be reassessed and appropriate restrictions applied.

### 163. AI Architecture Dependency and Concentration Risk

AI architecture should account for dependencies that may create common-cause failures, concentration risk, or cascading impacts.

A system may depend on a shared model provider, cloud platform, identity service, retrieval database, API gateway, or monitoring platform. A failure in one dependency may affect multiple AI use cases simultaneously.

The organization should maintain dependency maps and assess:

- Critical shared components.
- Provider concentration.
- Single points of failure.
- Shared identity and access dependencies.
- Common data and retrieval services.
- Cross-system privilege relationships.
- Shared control and monitoring failures.
- Recovery and substitution options.
- Impact of correlated outages.
- Residual risks after mitigation.

Risk aggregation should inform architecture decisions, continuity planning, supplier strategy, and executive reporting.

Redundancy alone does not eliminate concentration risk if the primary and backup services depend on the same provider, identity platform, or control plane.

### 164. AI Architecture Maturity and Capability Assessment

The organization should assess whether its architecture and supporting controls are mature enough for the AI systems it intends to operate.

The assessment should consider the reliability of identity management, data governance, model lifecycle management, control enforcement, observability, resilience, evidence collection, and risk integration.

Architecture maturity should be evaluated using documented evidence rather than the presence of tools or reference diagrams alone.

The review may consider:

- Consistency of architecture standards.
- Enforcement of risk-based requirements.
- Coverage of inventory and dependency records.
- Effectiveness of technical controls.
- Automation and workflow reliability.
- Monitoring and incident response capability.
- Reproducibility of deployments.
- Resilience and recovery testing.
- Evidence quality and traceability.
- Integration with governance decision-making.

The organization should prioritize capability gaps according to the risks they create and the systems affected. A technically sophisticated platform may still be immature if governance requirements are not enforced or operational evidence is unreliable.

### 165. AI Risk Architecture Performance and Effectiveness Metrics

Architecture performance metrics should help determine whether technical safeguards reduce risk and support reliable operation.

The organization should distinguish system performance metrics from control effectiveness and risk outcome metrics.

Examples include:

| Metric                                  | What it helps evaluate                                          |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized action rejection rate      | Whether policy enforcement blocks prohibited operations         |
| Access review completion                | Whether permissions are reviewed as required                    |
| Critical vulnerability remediation time | Whether important weaknesses are addressed promptly             |
| Monitoring coverage                     | Whether material components generate required telemetry         |
| Detection and response time             | Whether relevant events are identified and handled promptly     |
| Approval enforcement rate               | Whether designated actions require the expected authorization   |
| Configuration drift findings            | Whether deployed systems remain aligned with approved baselines |
| Recovery test success                   | Whether recovery procedures work as intended                    |
| Evidence completeness                   | Whether material decisions and controls can be verified         |
| Repeated control failure rate           | Whether remediation addresses underlying weaknesses             |

Metrics should be interpreted in context. A high rejection rate may indicate effective enforcement or excessive legitimate requests being blocked. A low number of detected incidents may indicate low exposure or inadequate detection.

The organization should define metric ownership, data quality checks, thresholds, and actions triggered by adverse trends.

### 166. AI Risk Architecture Blueprint and Documentation

Each material AI system should have architecture documentation sufficient to support risk assessment, control implementation, incident response, and independent review.

The documentation should reflect the deployed configuration rather than an aspirational design that differs from production.

Depending on risk, the blueprint should include:

- Data-flow and trust-boundary diagrams.
- Identity and authorization models.

Before production authorization, the organization should verify that required architecture controls and governance conditions have been satisfied.

Criteria may include:

- Documented limitations and known weaknesses.
- Completed third-party and privacy reviews where applicable.
- Authorized residual risk decision.

The organization should distinguish mandatory release gates from conditions that can be completed after deployment under an explicitly approved plan.

A deployment must not proceed merely because the system is technically functional. Production readiness requires satisfaction of the applicable risk, security, legal, operational, and governance conditions.

### 168. AI Risk Architecture Integration with Enterprise Governance

AI architecture must remain connected to enterprise governance systems so that technical changes and operational events can influence risk decisions.

Integration points may include enterprise risk management, cybersecurity operations, privacy management, vendor risk management, change management, incident management, internal audit, and business continuity.

The integration should establish how architecture findings are recorded, who owns resulting actions, and how material issues reach decision-makers.

Examples include:

- A vulnerability finding triggering a risk reassessment.
- A provider incident updating supplier and system risk records.
- An identity change triggering a review of agent permissions.
- A model update initiating validation and approval workflows.
- A monitoring threshold breach escalating to the risk owner.
- An expired exception restricting the affected operation.
- An audit finding creating a tracked remediation action.

Integration should avoid disconnected repositories that produce contradictory system status, ownership, or risk ratings. Where several systems remain necessary, authoritative sources and synchronization responsibilities must be defined.

### 169. AI Architecture Anti-Patterns and Governance Failure Modes

The organization should identify architecture patterns that repeatedly undermine AI risk controls.

Common anti-patterns include:

- Treating prompts as the sole enforcement mechanism for critical restrictions.
- Giving agents broad permissions without a documented need.
- Allowing direct execution without authorization checks.
- Assuming retrieved content is trusted because it comes from an internal source.
- Logging sensitive information without appropriate controls.
- Treating supplier assurance as proof that every use case is safe.
- Relying on a kill switch that does not stop background execution.
- Approving models without validating their intended use.
- Treating planned controls as effective controls.
- Maintaining risk assessments that do not reflect production configuration.
- Allowing exceptions to expire without enforcement or review.
- Measuring control implementation without testing effectiveness.

The organization should use these anti-patterns in architecture reviews, technical assessments, and internal assurance.

Identifying an anti-pattern does not automatically establish the severity of the resulting risk. The organization should evaluate the actual implementation, exposure, compensating controls, and potential consequences before determining treatment.

### 170. AI Risk Architecture Operationalization

Architecture requirements become effective only when they are incorporated into the processes used to build, acquire, deploy, and operate AI systems.

Operationalization should connect architecture standards to procurement, solution design, development, testing, deployment, monitoring, and change management.

The organization should define:

- Required architecture review checkpoints.
- Risk-based design patterns.
- Approved and prohibited configurations.
- Mandatory control implementation evidence.
- Testing and acceptance criteria.
- Change and exception procedures.
- Monitoring ownership.
- Production approval gates.
- Periodic architecture reviews.
- Retirement and recovery requirements.

Reusable patterns can improve consistency and reduce delivery effort, but they must be evaluated for suitability to the specific use case. A previously approved architecture pattern may require modification when the data sensitivity, autonomy, scale, or impact changes.

### 171. AI Risk Architecture Continuous Improvement

The architecture must evolve as new AI capabilities, threats, dependencies, and operating requirements emerge.

Improvement activities should be informed by technical testing, incidents, control failures, risk reassessments, audit findings, provider changes, and operational feedback.

The organization should evaluate whether:

- Existing controls remain effective.
- New attack paths require additional safeguards.
- Architecture standards remain proportionate.
- Monitoring provides sufficient coverage.
- Recovery mechanisms meet business requirements.
- Dependencies create unacceptable concentration risk.
- Evidence remains reliable and traceable.
- Automation improves consistency without weakening decision authority.
- Technical restrictions reflect current risk appetite.
- Architecture changes produce measurable risk reduction.

Improvement actions must be assigned, prioritized, tracked, and verified. The organization should document the rationale for material architecture changes and update affected risk assessments and operating procedures.

### 172. AI Risk Management Policy Architecture Final Principle

The AI Risk Management Policy must be supported by an architecture that connects governance decisions to enforceable controls, operational monitoring, evidence collection, and recovery mechanisms.

The architecture should ensure that approved risk boundaries are reflected in identity and access controls, data handling, model deployment, retrieval, agent execution, integrations, logging, and incident response. It must also provide a reliable path for operational evidence to trigger reassessment, restriction, or suspension when risk changes.

The organization must distinguish between **documented controls and technically enforced controls**, **model capability and authorized action**, **monitoring activity and effective detection**, and **system restoration and complete risk resolution**.

**Core Principle:** AI risk management architecture is effective when approved governance requirements are translated into verified technical and operational safeguards, material activity is observable, control failures trigger appropriate action, and the organization can demonstrate that residual risk remains within authorized limits.
