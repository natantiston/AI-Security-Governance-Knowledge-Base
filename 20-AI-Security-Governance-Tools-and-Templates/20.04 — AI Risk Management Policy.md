# 20.04 — AI Risk Management Policy

## Part 1 — AI Risk Management Policy Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI Risk Management Policy

An AI Risk Management Policy establishes the organizational principles, responsibilities, decision-making requirements, and mandatory processes for identifying, assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting risks associated with artificial intelligence systems throughout their lifecycle.

The policy defines how the organization determines whether an AI system is suitable for a proposed purpose, which risks require additional safeguards, who may authorize residual risk, and under what circumstances an AI activity must be restricted, suspended, or discontinued.

Unlike a general enterprise risk policy, an AI Risk Management Policy must address risk characteristics arising from AI-specific capabilities and limitations, including probabilistic outputs, model uncertainty, data dependency, changing behavior, limited explainability, automation, and interactions between AI components and external systems.

The policy should cover risks associated with:

- AI strategy and business adoption.
- AI system design and development.
- Data collection, processing, and governance.
- Model selection, training, testing, and deployment.
- Generative AI and large language models.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).
- AI agents and autonomous workflows.
- Third-party AI providers and model supply chains.
- Cybersecurity, privacy, and intellectual property.
- Operational reliability and business continuity.
- Human oversight and consequential decision-making.
- Legal, regulatory, ethical, and reputational exposure.

The policy should establish a consistent organizational approach without assuming that every AI system has the same risk profile.

For example, an AI assistant used to improve the wording of a non-sensitive internal document may require relatively limited risk-management activities. An AI agent authorized to modify production infrastructure or make consequential customer decisions requires substantially stronger assessment, approval, monitoring, and intervention mechanisms.

The fundamental objective is to ensure that AI adoption remains aligned with organizational objectives, risk appetite, legal obligations, and the organization's capacity to manage foreseeable adverse outcomes.

### 2. Purpose of the AI Risk Management Policy

The purpose of the AI Risk Management Policy is to establish a formal and repeatable process for managing AI-related uncertainty and preventing unacceptable outcomes.

The policy should enable the organization to make informed decisions about whether to adopt, develop, deploy, operate, modify, or retire an AI system.

Its primary objectives include the following.

**Risk identification**

Establish processes for identifying AI-related threats, vulnerabilities, limitations, uncertainties, dependencies, and potential adverse consequences.

**Risk assessment**

Evaluate the likelihood, severity, scope, and potential impact of identified risks using defined assessment criteria.

**Risk treatment**

Select and implement appropriate safeguards, redesign measures, restrictions, monitoring mechanisms, or other treatment options.

**Risk acceptance**

Define who may accept residual risk, within which limits, and subject to what documentation and approval requirements.

**Lifecycle monitoring**

Ensure that risk assessments remain relevant as models, datasets, integrations, users, providers, and operating conditions change.

**Accountability**

Assign risk ownership and establish clear responsibilities for assessment, treatment, oversight, escalation, and assurance.

**Regulatory and business alignment**

Integrate AI risk management with enterprise risk management, information security, privacy, compliance, business continuity, and applicable legal obligations.

**Continuous improvement**

Use incidents, testing, monitoring, audits, and operational feedback to improve the effectiveness of risk-management processes.

The policy should establish a decision framework rather than merely require risk registers or assessment documents to be completed.

A risk assessment is useful only when its findings influence authorization, safeguards, operating conditions, and the decision to proceed with the AI activity.

### 3. AI Risk Management as an Enterprise Governance Control

AI risk management should operate as an integrated component of enterprise risk management rather than as an isolated technical exercise.

The policy should establish how AI risks are identified and evaluated, how they are incorporated into organizational risk reporting, and how decisions are escalated when exposure exceeds approved tolerance.

The framework should connect business objectives, AI use cases, risk assessments, controls, residual-risk decisions, and ongoing monitoring.

The policy should align with the organization's existing risk-management arrangements, including:

- Enterprise risk management.
- Information security risk management.
- AI governance.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Third-party risk management.
- Operational risk management.
- Business continuity and resilience.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Internal audit and assurance.

AI risks should be recorded and escalated through the appropriate enterprise processes when they have material business implications.

For example, an AI system that introduces a material risk of unauthorized disclosure may require both an AI risk assessment and treatment through the organization's information security risk-management process.

The organization should avoid creating disconnected risk records that describe the same underlying exposure differently without clear ownership or reconciliation.

### 4. Scope of the AI Risk Management Policy

The policy should apply to AI systems and AI-enabled activities that the organization develops, acquires, deploys, operates, uses, or materially influences.

Its scope should cover the AI lifecycle, from initial business justification through retirement and decommissioning.

Relevant activities may include:

- AI use-case identification.
- Business and technical feasibility assessment.
- Data acquisition and preparation.
- Model selection and development.
- Fine-tuning and adaptation.
- Security and performance testing.
- Deployment and integration.
- Operational use and monitoring.
- Model and configuration changes.
- Incident response.
- Risk reassessment.
- Retirement and data disposition.

The policy should address internally developed systems, commercial AI products, externally hosted models, embedded AI capabilities, and third-party services.

It should also cover AI agents and automated workflows that interact with enterprise systems or make decisions on behalf of users.

Scope should be determined by the organization's relationship to the AI activity, its access to relevant information, and its ability to influence or control the associated risk.

The organization should define exclusions explicitly and ensure that an exclusion does not inadvertently remove material risks from oversight.

### 5. Organizational Applicability

The AI Risk Management Policy should apply to organizational functions that develop, acquire, authorize, operate, oversee, or use AI systems.

Applicable parties may include:

- Executive management.
- Business owners.
- AI product and system owners.
- Information security.
- Enterprise risk management.
- AI governance.
- Data governance.
- Privacy and legal functions.
- IT and cloud operations.
- Software engineering and MLOps teams.
- Procurement and supplier management.
- Internal audit.
- Employees and contractors.
- Relevant third-party service providers.

Each function should understand its role in identifying and managing AI-related risks.

For example, procurement may assess supplier assurance, information security may evaluate technical threats, privacy may assess personal-data processing, and the business owner may remain accountable for the intended use and resulting business exposure.

The organization should ensure that responsibilities are assigned before an AI system is authorized for material operational use.

Where several functions contribute to a risk assessment, the policy should identify the accountable risk owner and define how conflicting assessments or recommendations are resolved.

### 6. AI Risk Management Policy Ownership

The AI Risk Management Policy should have a formally designated owner responsible for maintaining the policy and coordinating its implementation.

Ownership may reside with the enterprise risk management function, AI governance function, information security governance function, or another formally authorized organizational function.

The policy owner should be responsible for:

- Maintaining the policy framework.
- Defining risk-management procedures.
- Coordinating stakeholder reviews.
- Maintaining risk-assessment requirements.
- Establishing review and escalation criteria.
- Monitoring policy implementation.
- Coordinating material revisions.
- Reporting significant implementation deficiencies.
- Supporting consistency across business functions.

Policy ownership should be distinguished from responsibility for individual AI risks.

The policy owner maintains the governing framework, while business or system owners remain accountable for the risks arising from their specific AI activities.

For example, the AI risk policy owner may define the organization's residual-risk acceptance procedure, but a designated business risk owner remains responsible for ensuring that the procedure is followed for a particular AI deployment.

### 7. AI Risk Management Policy Approval Authority

The AI Risk Management Policy should be approved by an authority with sufficient responsibility to establish mandatory organizational risk-management requirements.

Depending on the organization's structure, approval may involve executive management, the enterprise risk committee, the AI governance committee, or another designated authority.

Approval should confirm that the policy is consistent with the organization's risk appetite, governance model, and applicable obligations.

The approval process should establish:

- Policy owner.
- Approving authority.
- Version number.
- Approval date.
- Effective date.
- Review date.
- Applicable organizational scope.
- Related policies and standards.
- Superseded versions, where applicable.

The policy should also establish who may approve material changes to risk criteria, assessment methods, and acceptance thresholds.

Individual business owners should not be permitted to redefine enterprise risk appetite or approve risks outside their delegated authority.

Material changes should be documented and communicated to affected functions.

### 8. AI Risk Management Principles

The policy should establish principles that guide AI risk decisions consistently across the organization.

**Risk-based governance**

The depth of assessment and control should reflect the nature, likelihood, and potential consequences of the risk.

**Lifecycle accountability**

Risk management should continue throughout the AI lifecycle rather than ending when a system is approved for deployment.

**Proportionality**

Assessment effort and safeguards should be proportionate to the system's capabilities, exposure, and potential impact.

**Human accountability**

A named person or organizational function should remain accountable for risk decisions, even when AI systems perform automated activities.

**Evidence-based decisions**

Risk assessments should rely on relevant information, documented assumptions, appropriate testing, and defensible reasoning.

**Defense in depth**

Material risks should be addressed through complementary safeguards rather than relying on a single control.

**Transparency and traceability**

Material risk decisions, assumptions, approvals, and treatment actions should be documented sufficiently to support review and assurance.

**Continuous monitoring**

Risk assessments should be revisited when operating conditions, system capabilities, threats, or business requirements change.

**Escalation of unacceptable risk**

Activities that exceed approved tolerance should be escalated, restricted, redesigned, or stopped according to established decision authority.

**Independent challenge**

Material risk assessments and acceptance decisions should be subject to appropriate oversight and challenge.

These principles should be translated into practical assessment criteria, approval requirements, and operational procedures.

### 9. Definition of AI Risk

AI risk is the potential for an AI-related event, behavior, decision, or failure to produce an adverse consequence for individuals, the organization, customers, third parties, or society.

Risk may arise from the AI model itself, the data it processes, the application that integrates it, the environment in which it operates, or the way people use and interpret its outputs.

AI risk may involve:

- Confidentiality loss.
- Unauthorized access.
- Data corruption.
- Privacy violations.
- Inaccurate or misleading outputs.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Security compromise.
- Operational disruption.
- Financial loss.
- Legal or regulatory consequences.
- Reputational damage.
- Unsafe automated actions.
- Loss of human control.
- Supplier dependency.
- Failure to meet intended performance.

AI risk should not be equated exclusively with cybersecurity risk.

A model may be secure against unauthorized access yet produce materially inaccurate recommendations. Conversely, a highly accurate model may operate within an insecure environment that exposes confidential information.

The policy should therefore establish an integrated approach that considers technical, operational, human, legal, ethical, and business dimensions where relevant.

### 10. AI Risk Categories

The organization should define a risk taxonomy that enables consistent identification, classification, ownership, and reporting of AI risks.

The taxonomy should reflect the organization's activities and exposure.

| Risk category            | Representative concerns                                               |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strategic                | Misalignment with business objectives or AI strategy                  |
| Governance               | Unclear ownership, authority, or oversight                            |
| Cybersecurity            | Unauthorized access, data leakage, prompt injection, model theft      |
| Data                     | Poor quality, unauthorized processing, poisoning, excessive retention |
| Model                    | Inaccuracy, instability, drift, limited reliability                   |
| Operational              | Service disruption, process failure, inadequate recovery              |
| Privacy                  | Unlawful or inappropriate personal-data processing                    |
| Legal and regulatory     | Noncompliance with applicable obligations                             |
| Ethical and human impact | Discrimination, unfairness, harmful outcomes                          |
| Third-party              | Provider failure, weak assurance, concentration risk                  |
| Financial                | Unexpected cost, fraud, loss, or liability                            |
| Reputational             | Loss of trust or public confidence                                    |
| Agentic and automation   | Unauthorized actions, excessive autonomy, tool misuse                 |
| Intellectual property    | Unauthorized disclosure or infringement                               |
| Resilience               | Inability to maintain or restore critical AI services                 |

Risk categories should support analysis rather than force every risk into a single exclusive classification.

A single event may affect multiple categories. For example, an AI agent that discloses customer records through an unauthorized integration may create cybersecurity, privacy, contractual, operational, and reputational risks simultaneously.

The organization should establish a primary risk owner while identifying the supporting functions responsible for related dimensions.

### 11. AI Risk Versus AI Uncertainty

The organization should distinguish between a risk assessment and the uncertainty that limits confidence in that assessment.

Risk assessment estimates potential adverse outcomes using available information, assumptions, and defined methods.

Uncertainty arises when important information is incomplete, ambiguous, variable, or difficult to validate.

AI uncertainty may result from:

- Limited visibility into model behavior.
- Incomplete training information.
- Uncertain performance in unfamiliar contexts.
- Changing external conditions.
- Inadequate testing coverage.
- Unknown attack paths.
- Unclear provider practices.
- Limited operational history.
- Uncertain human interaction patterns.

The organization should document material uncertainties rather than presenting estimates as more precise than the evidence supports.

Where uncertainty is substantial, the organization may need additional testing, stronger safeguards, restricted deployment, human oversight, or a decision not to proceed.

For example, an AI agent with insufficient evidence of reliable behavior should not receive broad production permissions merely because no incident has yet been observed.

### 12. AI Risk Identification

Risk identification establishes the potential adverse events and conditions that could affect an AI system or its intended use.

The process should consider the complete system rather than focusing exclusively on the model.

Relevant components include:

- Users and administrators.
- Models and model providers.
- Training and inference data.
- Applications and APIs.
- Retrieval systems.
- Plugins and tools.
- Cloud infrastructure.
- External integrations.
- Business workflows.
- Human decision-makers.
- Suppliers and subcontractors.

Risk identification methods may include threat modeling, stakeholder workshops, architecture reviews, data-flow analysis, scenario analysis, security testing, incident analysis, and supplier assessments.

The organization should document the cause, event, and potential consequence of each material risk.

For example:

- **Cause:** An AI agent has excessive permissions.
- **Event:** The agent executes an unauthorized modification.
- **Consequence:** Production disruption, data loss, or unauthorized access.

This structure helps the organization identify where controls can prevent the event, limit its impact, or improve detection and recovery.

### 13. AI Risk Assessment

AI risk assessment evaluates identified risks according to defined criteria and available evidence.

The assessment should consider the likelihood of the relevant event and the severity of its potential consequences, while accounting for uncertainty and the effectiveness of existing controls.

The process should establish:

- Risk scenario.
- Affected assets and stakeholders.
- Inherent risk.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness.
- Residual risk.
- Assumptions and limitations.
- Treatment requirements.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Decision authority.

The assessment method should be appropriate to the risk.

A qualitative assessment may be suitable for an early-stage, low-impact use case, while a high-impact AI system may require more detailed quantitative evidence, specialist assessment, or structured scenario analysis.

Risk scores should support decisions rather than replace professional judgment.

A numerical score alone does not demonstrate that a risk is acceptable, particularly where mandatory obligations or potentially severe consequences are involved.

### 14. AI Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk associated with an AI activity before considering the effect of the relevant controls.

It reflects the characteristics of the use case, the system's capabilities, the data involved, and the potential consequences of failure.

Factors affecting inherent risk may include:

- Sensitivity of processed information.
- Number of affected individuals or systems.
- Degree of automation.
- Scope of access.
- Potential impact of incorrect outputs.
- Exposure to external threats.
- Reversibility of actions.
- Business criticality.
- Regulatory significance.
- Dependency on third-party services.

For example, an AI agent authorized to execute financial transactions may have high inherent risk because an erroneous or manipulated action could create direct financial loss.

Inherent risk should not be reduced simply because the organization has not yet implemented controls.

It provides a baseline for determining the level of safeguards, review, and oversight required.

### 15. AI Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after the relevant controls have been implemented and their effectiveness has been considered.

Residual risk should be evaluated using evidence about the controls rather than assuming that documented safeguards operate as intended.

The assessment should consider:

- Control design.
- Implementation status.
- Operating effectiveness.
- Known limitations.
- Remaining vulnerabilities.
- Human and operational dependencies.
- Uncertainty.
- Potential control failure.
- Recovery capabilities.

For example, an AI agent may initially present substantial risk because it can modify production resources. Least-privilege permissions, transaction limits, approval gates, monitoring, and tested suspension mechanisms may reduce that risk.

However, the risk does not automatically become low merely because these controls exist. Their effectiveness must be evaluated, and important failure scenarios must remain visible.

Residual risk should be compared against approved tolerance and escalated when it exceeds the relevant threshold.

### 16. AI Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is a formal decision by an authorized risk owner or delegated authority to retain a defined level of residual risk.

Acceptance should be based on an informed evaluation of the potential consequences, existing safeguards, business justification, and available alternatives.

A risk-acceptance record should identify:

- Risk description.
- Affected AI system or use case.
- Inherent and residual risk.
- Existing controls.
- Remaining weaknesses.
- Business rationale.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Approval authority.
- Conditions of acceptance.
- Review date.
- Expiration or reassessment trigger where appropriate.

Risk acceptance must remain within delegated authority and cannot override binding legal requirements or mandatory restrictions.

The organization should distinguish accepting residual risk from approving an otherwise unauthorized AI activity. A use case must first satisfy the applicable authorization requirements before it can proceed under an accepted risk decision.

### 17. AI Risk Appetite and Risk Tolerance

Risk appetite defines the broad level and types of risk the organization is willing to pursue or retain in support of its objectives.

Risk tolerance establishes the more specific boundaries within which individual risks may be managed.

The AI Risk Management Policy should translate these concepts into operational criteria.

For example, an organization may tolerate some uncertainty in an AI tool used for internal brainstorming while maintaining very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of regulated information or uncontrolled modification of production infrastructure.

Risk criteria should consider:

- Potential severity.
- Likelihood.
- Business criticality.
- Data sensitivity.
- Impact on individuals.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Reversibility.
- Control effectiveness.
- Uncertainty.

The organization should define escalation thresholds and specify which risks require senior approval or prohibition.

Risk appetite should guide decision-making but must not be used to justify violations of mandatory obligations.

### 18. AI Risk Assessment Methodology

The organization should establish a consistent methodology for assessing AI risks.

The methodology should define the assessment process, scoring criteria, evidence expectations, decision thresholds, and documentation requirements.

A typical assessment process may include:

1. Define the AI system and intended use.
2. Identify relevant stakeholders and affected assets.
3. Establish system boundaries and dependencies.
4. Identify foreseeable risk scenarios.
5. Evaluate inherent risk.
6. Identify existing controls.
7. Evaluate control effectiveness.
8. Determine residual risk.
9. Define treatment actions.
10. Obtain required approvals.
11. Establish monitoring and reassessment criteria.

The methodology should specify how likelihood and impact are evaluated and how uncertainty affects the final decision.

The organization should also define when specialist assessments are required, such as cybersecurity threat modeling, privacy impact assessment, AI impact assessment, or supplier security review.

Consistency should not eliminate professional judgment. Different AI systems may require different assessment techniques while remaining subject to the same overarching governance principles.

### 19. AI Risk Likelihood Assessment

Likelihood assessment estimates how plausibly a defined risk scenario may occur within the relevant operating context.

The assessment should consider the threat environment, exposure, system design, existing safeguards, historical evidence, and opportunities for misuse or failure.

Factors may include:

- Accessibility of the AI system.
- Exposure to external users.
- Attack surface.
- Model and application weaknesses.
- Data quality.
- Complexity of integrations.
- Level of autonomy.
- Frequency of use.
- Threat actor capability.
- Historical incidents.
- Effectiveness of preventive controls.

Where quantitative probabilities are unavailable, the organization may use defined qualitative categories.

The methodology should specify what each category means and what evidence supports the classification.

Likelihood should not be estimated solely from the absence of prior incidents. New AI capabilities may have limited operational history, and weaknesses may remain undetected.

### 20. AI Risk Impact Assessment

Impact assessment evaluates the consequences if a defined AI risk scenario occurs.

The assessment should consider the effect on the organization, affected individuals, customers, third parties, and relevant external stakeholders.

Potential impact dimensions include:

- Financial loss.
- Operational disruption.
- Confidentiality breach.
- Privacy harm.
- Legal liability.
- Regulatory consequences.
- Reputational damage.
- Safety implications.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Loss of critical services.
- Irreversible or difficult-to-reverse actions.

The organization should consider both direct and downstream consequences.

For example, an inaccurate AI-generated recommendation may initially appear to be a minor quality issue. If that recommendation is used in a consequential decision without verification, it may create substantial legal, financial, or human impact.

Impact criteria should reflect the organization's business context and applicable obligations.

### 21. AI Risk Scoring and Classification

Risk scoring provides a structured method for comparing risks and supporting treatment priorities.

A qualitative approach may use defined likelihood and impact levels to determine an overall risk category.

For example, a simplified matrix may classify risks as low, moderate, high, or critical.

```
                    IMPACT
             Low   Medium   High   Critical
Likelihood
Low           Low    Low    Medium   High
Medium        Low   Medium   High    High
High        Medium  High    High    Critical
Very High   Medium  High   Critical  Critical
```

This matrix is illustrative only. The organization should define its own scoring methodology, thresholds, and mandatory escalation criteria.

The scoring process should identify situations where numerical aggregation is inappropriate.

For example, a low likelihood should not automatically make an otherwise unacceptable risk acceptable if the potential consequence is catastrophic or the relevant activity is prohibited.

Risk scores should be supported by documented reasoning and should not obscure severe individual risk dimensions through averaging.

### 22. AI Risk Treatment Options

The organization should define the available options for treating identified AI risks.

Common treatment options include:

**Risk avoidance**

Do not initiate or continue the activity that creates an unacceptable risk.

**Risk mitigation**

Implement safeguards that reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk.

**Risk transfer or sharing**

Allocate certain financial or contractual consequences to another party where appropriate, while recognizing that accountability and legal obligations may remain with the organization.

**Risk acceptance**

Retain the residual risk through a documented decision made by an authorized authority.

Treatment decisions should consider the effectiveness, cost, feasibility, and limitations of the available options.

For example, if an AI agent's broad permissions create unacceptable risk, the organization may reduce permissions, add approval gates, redesign the workflow, or prohibit the use case.

The selected treatment should address the underlying risk rather than merely improve the appearance of the risk score.

### 23. AI Risk Treatment Planning

Each material risk requiring treatment should have a documented treatment plan.

The plan should identify:

- Risk scenario.
- Required treatment.
- Accountable owner.
- Supporting control owners.
- Target completion date.
- Required resources.
- Dependencies.
- Expected residual-risk outcome.
- Validation method.
- Escalation criteria.

Treatment plans should distinguish between planned controls and controls that are already implemented and effective.

For example, a planned DLP rule should not be treated as an operating safeguard until it has been implemented and tested.

High-risk treatment actions should receive appropriate prioritization, and overdue actions should be escalated according to established governance requirements.

Closure should require evidence that the treatment was implemented and that the resulting residual risk was reassessed.

### 24. AI Risk Register

The organization should maintain a risk register for material AI-related risks.

The register provides a structured record of identified risk scenarios, ownership, assessments, treatments, acceptance decisions, and monitoring status.

A practical AI risk register may contain the following fields:

| Field                 | Purpose                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID               | Unique risk identifier                  |
| AI system or use case | Identifies the affected activity        |
| Risk category         | Classifies the primary risk dimension   |
| Risk scenario         | Describes cause, event, and consequence |
| Risk owner            | Identifies accountability               |
| Inherent risk         | Records risk before relevant controls   |
| Existing controls     | Identifies current safeguards           |
| Control effectiveness | Records evidence-based effectiveness    |
| Residual risk         | Records risk after controls             |
| Treatment plan        | Defines required actions                |
| Treatment owner       | Assigns implementation responsibility   |
| Target date           | Establishes expected completion         |
| Acceptance status     | Records formal risk decisions           |
| Review date           | Defines reassessment timing             |
| Status                | Tracks progress and closure             |

The register should support links to relevant assessments, incidents, control evidence, and approvals.

It should be maintained as an active risk-management instrument rather than a static document prepared only for an audit.

### 25. AI Risk Ownership and Accountability

Every material AI risk should have a designated owner with sufficient authority and understanding to oversee its management.

Risk ownership should reflect responsibility for the business activity or system that creates the exposure.

The risk owner should ensure that:

- The risk is adequately understood.
- The assessment is current.
- Required controls are implemented.
- Treatment actions are completed.
- Residual risk is evaluated.
- Acceptance decisions are authorized.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Unacceptable exposure is escalated.

Risk ownership should be distinguished from control ownership.

A business owner may own the overall risk while information security, IT, privacy, or a supplier operates specific controls that contribute to treatment.

Where risks affect multiple functions, supporting responsibilities should be documented to prevent gaps or conflicting decisions.

### 26. AI Risk Control Ownership

Each material control used to treat an AI risk should have a designated owner responsible for its implementation and ongoing operation.

Control ownership should identify:

- Control objective.
- Control description.
- Responsible function.
- Implementation mechanism.
- Operating frequency.
- Required evidence.
- Testing requirements.
- Escalation conditions.
- Remediation responsibilities.

For example, an AI risk may depend on access controls, DLP, model validation, and incident monitoring. Each control should have an identifiable owner, even though a single business owner remains accountable for the overall risk.

Control ownership should include the ability to identify deficiencies and initiate remediation.

The organization should avoid assigning controls to roles that lack the authority, resources, or technical capability necessary to operate them effectively.

### 27. AI Risk Assessment Independence and Challenge

Material AI risk assessments should receive appropriate challenge from qualified individuals or functions that are sufficiently independent of the activity being assessed.

Independent challenge helps identify optimistic assumptions, incomplete scenarios, weak evidence, and conflicts between business objectives and risk requirements.

Challenge may be provided by:

- Enterprise risk management.
- Information security.
- AI governance.
- Privacy and legal specialists.
- Model risk management.
- Independent technical assessors.
- Internal audit, within its assurance mandate.

The degree of independence should be proportionate to the risk and organizational context.

For example, a business team may perform the initial assessment of a new AI use case, while a specialist risk function reviews the assessment before authorization.

Independent challenge should be documented where material decisions depend on it.

### 28. AI Risk Assessment Evidence Requirements

Risk assessments should be supported by evidence appropriate to the significance and complexity of the risk.

Evidence may include:

- Architecture diagrams.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Threat models.
- Security test results.
- Model evaluation results.
- Privacy assessments.
- Supplier assurance documents.
- Configuration records.
- Incident history.
- Operational metrics.
- Expert review.
- Documented assumptions.
- Stakeholder analysis.

The organization should distinguish between verified evidence, management representations, assumptions, and information that remains uncertain.

Evidence should be sufficiently current and relevant to the actual AI system and intended use.

For example, an assessment based on an earlier model version may no longer support a decision if the system has materially changed.

The policy should specify documentation and retention expectations for high-risk decisions.

### 29. AI Risk Monitoring

AI risks should be monitored throughout operation to determine whether the original assessment remains valid.

Monitoring should identify changes in system behavior, threat exposure, control effectiveness, data quality, provider conditions, and business use.

Relevant monitoring signals may include:

- Security alerts.
- Model performance changes.
- Data drift.
- Unexpected outputs.
- Unauthorized access attempts.
- Agent action anomalies.
- Provider incidents.
- Control failures.
- Complaints or harmful outcomes.
- Changes in operating conditions.

Monitoring should be proportionate to the risk and should have defined thresholds, owners, and escalation procedures.

Where monitoring identifies a material deviation, the organization should determine whether the use case requires reassessment, additional safeguards, restricted operation, or suspension.

Monitoring without a defined response process provides limited risk-management value.

### 30. AI Risk Reassessment Triggers

The organization should define circumstances that require an AI risk assessment to be revisited.

Triggers may include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- A new model or model version.
- A change in data sources.
- Increased data sensitivity.
- New integrations.
- Expanded permissions.
- Increased autonomy.
- A material security incident.
- Significant performance deterioration.
- New regulatory obligations.
- Provider changes.
- New evidence of harmful outcomes.
- Failure of a key control.
- A material change in the affected population.

Reassessment should determine whether the existing risk classification, safeguards, approval, and residual-risk decision remain valid.

The organization should not automatically assume that an existing approval covers a materially changed AI system.

Where a change introduces risk beyond approved tolerance, the affected activity should be restricted or paused according to established decision authority while the issue is evaluated.

### 31. Core AI Risk Management Policy Principle

The AI Risk Management Policy establishes a formal, accountable, and evidence-based approach for determining whether AI-related risks are understood, appropriately treated, and acceptable for the intended business purpose.

Its foundation should connect risk identification, assessment, treatment, ownership, acceptance, and ongoing monitoring.

The organization should preserve the distinction between inherent risk and residual risk, between a planned safeguard and an effective control, and between a documented risk assessment and an authorized decision to proceed.

```
AI System and Use Case
          │
          ▼
    Risk Identification
          │
          ▼
     Inherent Risk
          │
          ▼
Existing Control Evaluation
          │
          ▼
     Residual Risk
          │
          ▼
     Treatment Decision
          │
          ▼
Authorized Approval / Restriction
          │
          ▼
Monitoring and Reassessment
          │
          ▼
 Evidence and Improvement
```

The fundamental distinctions are:

- **Risk identification ≠ risk assessment**
- **Risk assessment ≠ risk acceptance**
- **Inherent risk ≠ residual risk**
- **Planned control ≠ implemented control**
- **Implemented control ≠ effective control**
- **Risk owner ≠ control owner**
- **Risk scoring ≠ professional judgment**
- **Absence of incidents ≠ absence of risk**
- **Supplier assurance ≠ elimination of organizational responsibility**
- **Risk acceptance ≠ authorization to violate mandatory requirements**
- **Approval of an AI system ≠ permanent approval of every future configuration**
- **Monitoring ≠ effective risk response**
- **Risk register completion ≠ risk treatment**
- **Documented assumptions ≠ verified evidence**
- **AI capability ≠ acceptable risk**

**Core Principle:**

> **An effective AI Risk Management Policy establishes a continuous, evidence-based decision process that identifies AI-related risks, evaluates their significance, assigns accountable owners, implements proportionate treatment, and ensures that residual risk remains within authorized limits throughout the AI lifecycle.**
>
> # 20.04 — AI Risk Management Policy

## Part 2 — AI Risk Management Policy Controls

### 32. AI Risk Management Control Framework

An AI Risk Management Policy must be supported by a formal control framework that converts policy requirements into repeatable, testable, and auditable activities. The framework defines the minimum controls required to identify, assess, treat, monitor, escalate, and report risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

The framework should establish:

- **Control objectives:** The risk outcomes the organization expects to achieve.
- **Control requirements:** Mandatory activities, safeguards, approvals, and restrictions.
- **Control ownership:** The individuals accountable for operating and overseeing controls.
- **Control evidence:** The records demonstrating that controls were implemented and operated.
- **Control effectiveness criteria:** The conditions used to determine whether controls work as intended.
- **Control escalation:** The process for handling deficiencies, failures, and unacceptable residual risk.

The framework must be proportionate to the organization's AI risk exposure. A low-impact internal productivity assistant may require a streamlined assessment, while an AI system affecting employment, credit, healthcare, critical infrastructure, or safety may require extensive independent review and stronger approval requirements.

A control framework is not simply a checklist. It is an operating mechanism connecting risk identification to management decisions and measurable risk reduction.

### 33. Mandatory AI Risk Assessment Control

Every AI use case must undergo a documented risk assessment before authorization for production use, subject to any formally approved process for limited experimentation.

The assessment must identify the system, its intended purpose, its users, affected stakeholders, data sources, dependencies, operating environment, and foreseeable consequences of failure or misuse.

At a minimum, the assessment must establish:

1. The AI system's business purpose and operational context.
2. The inherent risks before considering controls.
3. The applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements.
4. The existing and proposed risk controls.
5. The expected residual risk after treatment.
6. The accountable risk owner and control owners.
7. The required approval authority.
8. The conditions for monitoring and reassessment.

An assessment is incomplete if it records risks without assigning owners, proposing treatment, or establishing a decision about whether the system may proceed.

The organization must define which changes require reassessment and which activities are prohibited until required approvals are obtained.

### 34. AI System Inventory and Risk Assessment Linkage

AI risk assessments must be linked to the authoritative AI system inventory. The inventory provides the foundation for establishing risk coverage, ownership, lifecycle status, and governance obligations.

Each assessment should reference a unique system or use-case identifier. Where a business process uses several AI components, the organization must determine whether assessments should cover the overall solution, individual components, or both.

The inventory should capture, where applicable:

| Inventory attribute            | Risk management purpose                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| System and use-case identifier | Provides traceability across governance records             |
| Business owner                 | Establishes accountability for business outcomes            |
| Technical owner                | Identifies responsibility for technical operation           |
| Intended purpose               | Defines the context in which risk is evaluated              |
| Model and provider             | Identifies dependencies and third-party exposure            |
| Data classification            | Supports privacy, confidentiality, and security assessments |
| Deployment environment         | Identifies infrastructure and operational exposure          |
| Impact classification          | Supports proportional assessment and approval               |
| Lifecycle status               | Identifies systems requiring review or retirement           |
| Assessment reference           | Connects the inventory to risk evidence and decisions       |

A system without a confirmed owner or documented purpose should be treated as a governance exception until those deficiencies are resolved.

### 35. AI Risk Assessment Scope Control

The assessment scope must be explicitly documented to prevent material risks from being excluded unintentionally.

Scope determination should consider the complete AI-enabled business process, not merely the model or application interface. For example, a generative AI assistant may depend on a retrieval system, document repositories, identity services, external APIs, cloud infrastructure, and human approval workflows.

The assessment should consider:

- The AI model and its supporting components.
- Data collection, preparation, storage, retrieval, and processing.
- Model development, configuration, deployment, and updates.
- User interactions and downstream consumers of outputs.
- External services, suppliers, and integration points.
- Human decisions influenced by AI-generated information.
- Automated actions performed by connected systems or agents.
- Operational support, monitoring, incident response, and recovery.
- Decommissioning, data disposal, and residual obligations.

Scope exclusions must be documented with a rationale and approved by an appropriate authority where they could materially affect the risk conclusion.

**Control objective:** Ensure that risk decisions reflect the actual operating environment and material dependencies of the AI-enabled service.

### 36. AI Risk Criteria and Scoring Control

The organization must establish consistent risk criteria before assessing individual AI use cases. Without predefined criteria, teams may assign risk ratings inconsistently, making prioritization and management reporting unreliable.

Risk criteria should define the meaning of likelihood, impact, risk levels, escalation thresholds, and acceptance limits. The criteria should be aligned with enterprise risk management while accommodating AI-specific characteristics.

A simple scoring model may use:

[\
\text{Inherent Risk Score}=\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Impact}\
]

For a five-point likelihood scale and a five-point impact scale, the resulting score ranges from 1 to 25.

| Score | Illustrative classification | Expected treatment                                                        |
| ----- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1–4   | Low                         | Apply baseline controls and routine monitoring                            |
| 5–9   | Moderate                    | Document treatment and assign an accountable owner                        |
| 10–16 | High                        | Require enhanced mitigation and management review                         |
| 17–25 | Critical                    | Escalate for senior decision-making; restrict deployment until authorized |

These thresholds are illustrative, not universal. The organization must approve its own methodology and define any mandatory prohibitions or regulatory requirements that override a numerical score.

A numerical score must not override a severe consequence, a legal prohibition, or an unacceptable rights or safety impact. Qualitative judgment and documented rationale remain necessary.

### 37. Inherent Risk Assessment Control

Inherent risk must be assessed before giving credit to existing or proposed controls. This establishes the underlying exposure associated with the AI use case.

The assessment should consider the nature of the use case, the sensitivity of its data, the level of autonomy, the affected population, the potential scale of deployment, and the consequences of incorrect or malicious outputs.

Relevant factors include:

- Potential physical, financial, operational, or reputational harm.
- Effects on individuals' rights, access, opportunities, or treatment.
- Exposure of personal, confidential, or regulated information.
- Potential for manipulation, exploitation, or adversarial misuse.
- Degree of human oversight and reversibility of decisions.
- Dependence on external providers and opaque model behavior.
- Scale, speed, and reach of automated actions.
- Difficulty detecting or correcting failures.

The assessor must explain why the assigned likelihood and impact ratings are appropriate. Where reliable evidence is unavailable, uncertainty must be recorded rather than concealed by an apparently precise score.

### 38. Residual Risk Assessment Control

Residual risk is the risk remaining after accounting for the design and demonstrated effectiveness of relevant controls.

The organization must distinguish between controls that are merely planned, controls that have been implemented, and controls whose operating effectiveness has been tested.

A proposed safeguard must not automatically reduce the residual risk rating. Risk reduction should reflect the control's actual status, coverage, reliability, and evidence.

A residual risk assessment should document:

1. The inherent risk rating.
2. The applicable controls and their implementation status.
3. Evidence supporting control effectiveness.
4. Known limitations and dependencies.
5. The remaining likelihood and impact.
6. The residual risk classification.
7. The accountable owner and required decision.

For example, a human-review control may reduce risk only if reviewers have sufficient expertise, access to relevant evidence, adequate time, and genuine authority to reject the AI recommendation.

Residual risk must be reassessed when controls fail, the operating context changes, or monitoring reveals that the original assumptions are no longer valid.

### 39. AI Risk Treatment Selection Control

Each risk requiring treatment must have a documented response selected from the organization's approved risk treatment options.

| Treatment option  | Application                                                                                               |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid             | Do not pursue the AI use case or discontinue the risky activity                                           |
| Mitigate          | Implement controls that reduce likelihood, impact, or both                                                |
| Transfer or share | Allocate specified financial or operational exposure through contracts, insurance, or shared arrangements |
| Accept            | Retain the residual risk through an authorized decision                                                   |
| Restrict          | Limit users, data, functionality, autonomy, or deployment scope                                           |
| Defer             | Postpone implementation until required conditions are met                                                 |

Transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability for legal compliance, responsible use, or risks that remain under its control.

Deferral is not a permanent substitute for treatment. A deferred risk must have an owner, a review date, defined restrictions, and a decision on whether the AI system may continue operating.

The chosen response must be justified against the risk's severity, the feasibility of mitigation, business objectives, stakeholder impacts, and applicable mandatory requirements.

### 40. AI Risk Treatment Plan Control

A treatment plan must convert the selected response into measurable implementation activities.

Each treatment action should specify:

- The risk identifier and risk statement.
- The required control or corrective action.
- The accountable action owner.
- The target completion date.
- Required funding, resources, and dependencies.
- Implementation milestones.
- Verification and acceptance criteria.
- Evidence required for closure.
- Interim restrictions while remediation remains incomplete.

A treatment plan should distinguish remediation of the underlying risk from remediation of a control deficiency. For example, improving access logging may correct a monitoring weakness but may not sufficiently mitigate the underlying risk of an AI agent performing unauthorized transactions.

Overdue actions must be escalated according to severity and organizational risk appetite. Critical actions should not remain overdue without a documented decision on operational restrictions and residual exposure.

### 41. AI Risk Acceptance Authority Control

Risk acceptance must be performed by an individual or body with delegated authority appropriate to the risk level.

The person requesting acceptance should not automatically be the sole decision-maker. Independent challenge is especially important when the request concerns a material business benefit, a high-impact AI system, a significant control deficiency, or a risk that affects external stakeholders.

An acceptance record must contain:

1. The risk and affected AI system.
2. The residual risk rating and supporting rationale.
3. The reasons treatment is not being completed immediately.
4. The expected business benefit and alternatives considered.
5. The decision-maker and authority basis.
6. Any conditions, restrictions, or compensating controls.
7. The acceptance date and expiration or review date.
8. The events that invalidate the acceptance.
9. The evidence supporting the decision.

Risk acceptance cannot authorize an activity prohibited by law, bypass mandatory regulatory safeguards, or remove obligations that cannot lawfully be waived.

### 42. AI Risk Escalation Control

The organization must define mandatory escalation conditions and the recipients responsible for decisions.

Escalation should occur when a risk exceeds approved thresholds, a critical control fails, a treatment action is overdue beyond its permitted tolerance, a material incident occurs, or new information invalidates a previous risk decision.

Additional triggers may include:

- Potential harm to life, physical safety, or fundamental rights.
- Suspected unlawful processing or disclosure of personal data.
- Unauthorized AI actions or privilege escalation.
- Material model behavior changes or unexplained degradation.
- Significant vulnerabilities affecting a model or AI dependency.
- Regulatory notifications or contractual reporting obligations.
- Evidence that the system is being used beyond its approved purpose.
- A risk acceptance decision that has expired or become invalid.

Escalation paths must identify the required notification timeframe, decision authority, interim protective measures, and documentation requirements.

The organization should distinguish ordinary risk escalation from incident response. A newly identified risk may require governance review, whereas an active security incident may require immediate containment and a separate incident-management process.

### 43. AI Risk Control Effectiveness Assessment

Controls must be evaluated for both design adequacy and operating effectiveness.

**Design effectiveness** asks whether a control, if implemented as intended, is capable of addressing the identified risk.

**Operating effectiveness** asks whether the control actually operates consistently, completely, and reliably in practice.

For example, a policy requiring human approval before an AI agent executes a high-impact transaction may be well designed. If the agent can invoke an alternative API that bypasses the approval workflow, the control is not operating effectively across the relevant execution paths.

Testing methods may include:

- Inspection of policies, configurations, and access rules.
- Sampling of approval records and transactions.
- Reperformance of risk assessment calculations.
- Testing of technical restrictions and authorization boundaries.
- Review of monitoring alerts and incident records.
- Adversarial testing and controlled simulations.
- Interviews with control operators and business owners.
- Analysis of control failures, exceptions, and recurring findings.

Control effectiveness conclusions must be supported by evidence and recorded limitations. A control should not be classified as effective solely because a procedure exists.

### 44. AI Risk Evidence and Documentation Control

AI risk decisions must be supported by evidence sufficient for management review, audit, regulatory examination, and subsequent reassessment.

Evidence should establish what was assessed, which assumptions were used, what controls existed at the time, who made the decision, and why the resulting risk conclusion was reasonable.

Evidence may include:

- Completed assessments and approved risk criteria.
- System architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Data classification and privacy analysis.
- Model evaluations and security test results.
- Vendor assurance documentation.
- Control configurations and test records.
- Meeting decisions and approval records.
- Monitoring metrics and incident reports.
- Treatment plans and remediation verification.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.

Evidence must be attributable to the relevant system and assessment version. Where feasible, records should be time-stamped, access-controlled, and protected against unauthorized alteration.

The organization must define retention periods according to legal, regulatory, contractual, audit, and business requirements. Sensitive evidence should be protected from unnecessary disclosure and retained only as permitted by applicable obligations.

### 45. AI Risk Monitoring Control

AI risk management must continue after deployment. An approval decision represents a judgment based on the information available at a particular time; it does not establish that risk will remain unchanged.

Monitoring must be aligned with the risk profile and operating characteristics of the AI system.

Relevant monitoring areas include:

- Security events, unauthorized access, and anomalous behavior.
- Changes in model performance, reliability, and output quality.
- Drift in data, model behavior, or operating conditions.
- Privacy incidents and inappropriate data disclosure.
- Bias, disparate impacts, and other relevant fairness indicators.
- Human override rates and unresolved reviewer concerns.
- Agent actions, tool invocation, and transaction anomalies.
- Third-party incidents and provider changes.
- Control failures, overdue treatment actions, and exceptions.
- Changes in laws, contractual duties, or approved use cases.

Each monitored risk should have defined indicators, thresholds, review frequency, ownership, and escalation procedures. Monitoring frequency must be proportionate to the potential harm and the speed with which the risk can change.

### 46. AI Risk Reassessment Trigger Control

The organization must define events that require an AI risk assessment to be reviewed or repeated.

Reassessment should be triggered when a material change occurs, not merely when the annual review date arrives.

| Trigger                                 | Required review focus                                     |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model replacement or major update       | Changed capabilities, behavior, limitations, and controls |
| New data source or expanded data access | Privacy, confidentiality, provenance, and data quality    |
| New user group or business purpose      | Authorization, impact, and scope                          |
| Increased autonomy or new tools         | Action permissions, supervision, and containment          |
| New external integration                | Trust boundaries, dependencies, and data flows            |
| Significant incident or near miss       | Failed assumptions, root causes, and residual risk        |
| Regulatory or contractual change        | Compliance obligations and required controls              |
| Control failure or repeated exceptions  | Effectiveness and adequacy of treatment                   |
| Material deployment-scale increase      | Exposure, impact, and operational resilience              |
| Retirement or decommissioning           | Residual access, data retention, and disposal risks       |

The reassessment record should identify which previous conclusions remain valid, which require revision, and whether the existing approval remains appropriate.

A material change must not be treated as routine maintenance when it changes the system's intended purpose, risk exposure, or operational authority.

### 47. AI Third-Party Risk Control

Third-party AI services must be assessed according to the risks they introduce and the organization's ability to govern those risks.

The assessment should cover the provider, model, hosting environment, data processing arrangements, subcontractors, support processes, service dependencies, and contractual allocation of responsibilities.

Relevant controls include:

- Provider due diligence and assurance review.
- Identification of the model and service configuration.
- Assessment of data use, retention, and training practices.
- Review of access controls and tenant isolation.
- Evaluation of security testing and vulnerability management.
- Assessment of incident notification and cooperation terms.
- Review of service continuity and exit arrangements.
- Restrictions on unauthorized data reuse or onward disclosure.
- Procedures for material provider or model changes.
- Verification of contractual and regulatory requirements.

The organization must not assume that a supplier's certification or assurance report automatically establishes that the specific AI use case is acceptable. Assurance evidence must be evaluated for scope, date, exclusions, and relevance to the risks being assessed.

### 48. AI Privacy and Data Protection Risk Control

AI risk assessments must address privacy and data protection risks wherever the use case involves personal data or could affect identifiable individuals.

The review should consider the purposes of processing, lawful basis where applicable, data minimization, data accuracy, retention, access, transparency, data subject rights, and international transfers where relevant.

Specific AI-related considerations may include:

- Personal data embedded in prompts or uploaded documents.
- Sensitive information retrieved from connected repositories.
- Memorization or unintended disclosure of training information.
- Inferences that reveal or predict sensitive attributes.
- Re-identification risks associated with supposedly anonymized data.
- Use of personal data for model training or fine-tuning.
- Automated decisions and profiling.
- Cross-border processing and provider access.
- Retention of interaction histories and audit logs.

Where required, the organization must complete a data protection impact assessment or other applicable privacy review. Such an assessment may be linked to the AI risk assessment but should not be assumed to replace it.

Privacy risks must be assigned owners, treated appropriately, and escalated when legal requirements or approved privacy thresholds are not met.

### 49. AI Security Risk Control

The AI risk assessment must consider security threats that arise from the AI system, its supporting infrastructure, its data, and its interactions with users and external services.

Depending on the system, relevant risks may include prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, insecure output handling, data poisoning, model extraction, model theft, unauthorized model access, adversarial manipulation, insecure plugin use, and excessive agent permissions.

Security assessment should also cover conventional enterprise risks such as identity compromise, vulnerable dependencies, cloud misconfiguration, inadequate logging, exposed APIs, weak secrets management, and insufficient backup or recovery capability.

The assessment must link material security risks to specific controls and evidence. A generic statement that the organization follows cybersecurity best practices is not sufficient evidence that an AI-specific threat has been adequately addressed.

Where necessary, security risk treatment should be coordinated with the cybersecurity, application security, cloud security, and incident response functions.

### 50. AI Safety, Reliability, and Human Impact Risk Control

AI risk management must consider harms that may arise even when a system operates as technically designed.

Depending on its purpose, the assessment may need to consider unsafe recommendations, unreliable outputs, automation bias, inappropriate delegation of decisions, discriminatory outcomes, lack of contestability, and inadequate human intervention.

Controls should be proportionate to the potential consequences and may include:

- Defined operational boundaries and prohibited uses.
- Independent evaluation against relevant safety criteria.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Escalation to qualified personnel when confidence is insufficient.
- Mechanisms for correction, appeal, or contestation.
- Restrictions on autonomous execution.
- Testing under foreseeable failure conditions.
- Clear communication of system limitations.
- Safe fallback and shutdown procedures.

Human oversight must be substantive. A nominal approval step does not adequately mitigate risk when the reviewer lacks the information, expertise, time, or authority needed to intervene.

### 51. AI Model and Performance Risk Control

The organization must assess risks associated with model behavior, reliability, suitability, and limitations.

The assessment should determine whether the model is appropriate for its intended purpose and whether its known limitations are compatible with the consequences of its use.

Relevant controls may include:

- Model validation against documented acceptance criteria.
- Evaluation using representative and appropriately governed datasets.
- Testing of edge cases and foreseeable misuse.
- Documentation of limitations and unsupported use cases.
- Monitoring for drift and performance degradation.
- Revalidation after material model or data changes.
- Comparison against alternative models where warranted.
- Defined rollback or replacement criteria.
- Review of model provenance and version changes.

For generative AI, assessment should also consider hallucinations, inconsistent responses, sensitivity to prompt changes, and the risk that fluent outputs may be mistaken for verified facts.

The organization must establish whether performance thresholds are appropriate to the use case. A level of error acceptable for brainstorming may be unacceptable for a safety-critical or regulated decision process.

### 52. AI Human Oversight Control

Where human oversight is required as a risk treatment, the organization must document how that oversight operates in practice.

The control design should identify the reviewer, decision authority, review criteria, information available, intervention options, escalation route, and evidence retained.

Effective oversight may require:

- Clear separation between AI recommendations and final decisions.
- Access to relevant source material and uncertainty information.
- Training appropriate to the reviewer's responsibilities.
- Authority to reject, override, or suspend AI outputs.
- Defined escalation criteria for ambiguous or high-risk cases.
- Workload limits that permit meaningful review.
- Sampling and quality assurance of review decisions.
- Monitoring of overrides, disagreements, and reviewer errors.

The organization should evaluate whether human reviewers can detect the relevant failure modes. Human involvement must not be credited as a strong control merely because a person is present somewhere in the workflow.

### 53. AI Agent and Autonomous Action Risk Control

AI systems capable of invoking tools, interacting with external services, changing records, or executing transactions require explicit assessment of their operational authority.

The organization must identify what the agent can read, write, execute, approve, modify, or transmit, and under which identity and permissions those actions occur.

Required controls should be determined by risk and may include:

- Least-privilege tool and API permissions.
- Allowlisted actions, destinations, and resources.
- Explicit authorization for sensitive operations.
- Human approval for designated high-impact actions.
- Limits on transaction value, volume, or frequency.
- Separation of planning from execution where appropriate.
- Protection against prompt injection and instruction manipulation.
- Restricted access to credentials and secrets.
- Traceable records of requests, decisions, and actions.
- Emergency suspension and recovery mechanisms.

Risk assessments must consider indirect prompt injection, compromised context sources, chained tool calls, unintended actions, and the potential for one compromised component to affect downstream systems.

The agent's autonomy must be restricted where the organization cannot adequately prevent, detect, or recover from unacceptable outcomes.

### 54. AI Change Management Risk Control

Changes to AI models, configurations, data pipelines, prompts, retrieval sources, integrations, and agent permissions must be assessed for risk impact.

The change process should classify changes according to their potential to affect security, privacy, performance, safety, compliance, or business outcomes.

Material changes should require risk review and approval before production deployment. Lower-risk changes may follow an expedited process when the criteria and safeguards have been formally defined.

Change records should capture:

- The description and reason for the change.
- Affected systems, dependencies, and stakeholders.
- Expected benefits and potential adverse effects.
- Assessment of new or changed risks.
- Required testing and approvals.
- Deployment and rollback plans.
- Updated documentation and inventory information.
- Post-deployment verification results.

A change that alters the intended purpose, access privileges, degree of autonomy, or affected population must not be treated as a routine technical update without evaluating the resulting risk.

### 55. AI Risk Remediation and Closure Control

Risk remediation must remain open until the required treatment has been implemented and the closure criteria have been satisfied.

The completion of a task does not necessarily mean the risk has been reduced to an acceptable level. Closure requires verification that the treatment addresses the intended risk and that any remaining exposure has been reassessed.

A remediation record should include the original finding, root cause where known, agreed corrective action, owner, target date, implementation evidence, verification results, and final risk decision.

Closure should be rejected or returned for further action when:

- The control is implemented only partially.
- Testing reveals an unresolved weakness.
- The evidence does not support the claimed improvement.
- The residual risk remains above the authorized threshold.
- Related dependencies or affected systems were omitted.
- A compensating control has not been validated.

Where remediation cannot be completed within the required period, the issue must be escalated and managed through the appropriate risk acceptance or exception process. Overdue remediation must not be concealed by repeatedly changing target dates without justification.

### 56. AI Risk Exception Control

Exceptions may be used when a defined policy or control requirement cannot be met temporarily and an authorized decision-maker determines that a limited deviation is justified.

An exception is not an informal permission to bypass governance. It must be documented, time-bound, risk-assessed, and subject to compensating controls where appropriate.

The exception record should identify:

1. The requirement being waived or modified.
2. The business justification.
3. The affected AI system and operating scope.
4. The risk introduced by the deviation.
5. The compensating controls.
6. The accountable owner and approving authority.
7. The effective date and expiry date.
8. Monitoring and review requirements.
9. The remediation plan or exit condition.
10. Conditions that require early revocation.

Exceptions must not be used to authorize unlawful conduct or override mandatory obligations. Repeated exceptions for the same requirement should trigger an investigation into whether the control design, implementation process, or policy itself requires review.

### 57. AI Risk Reporting Control

AI risk reporting must provide decision-makers with an accurate view of exposure, treatment progress, control performance, and material changes in risk.

Reports should be tailored to the audience. Operational teams need actionable details, while executive committees and boards need a concise view of material exposure, decisions required, and trends.

Reporting should include, as appropriate:

- Distribution of AI systems by risk classification.
- Number of assessments completed and overdue.
- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks accepted outside normal tolerances.
- Overdue treatment actions.
- Material control deficiencies.
- Open exceptions and approaching expirations.
- Significant incidents and emerging risk themes.
- Third-party dependencies and assurance gaps.
- Reassessment completion and material changes.
- Progress against agreed remediation objectives.

Metrics should include context, reporting period, definitions, ownership, and data-quality limitations. Aggregate counts should not conceal a small number of severe risks.

### 58. AI Risk Key Performance Indicators

Key performance indicators (KPIs) measure whether the AI risk management process is being executed as intended.

Examples include:

| KPI                                  | Illustrative measurement                                                      |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessment coverage                  | Percentage of in-scope AI systems with current assessments                    |
| Pre-deployment assessment completion | Percentage assessed before production authorization                           |
| Treatment-plan completion            | Percentage of actions completed by their due dates                            |
| Reassessment timeliness              | Percentage of triggered reassessments completed within the defined period     |
| Risk ownership coverage              | Percentage of material risks with assigned owners                             |
| Evidence completeness                | Percentage of sampled assessments meeting evidence requirements               |
| Control testing coverage             | Percentage of required controls tested within the review period               |
| Training completion                  | Percentage of designated risk and control owners completing required training |

Each KPI should have a defined calculation, data source, reporting frequency, accountable owner, and target.

Targets must be realistic and should encourage substantive risk reduction rather than superficial completion. For example, achieving 100% assessment coverage does not demonstrate effective risk management if assessments are outdated, poorly evidenced, or disconnected from operational controls.

### 59. AI Risk Key Risk Indicators

Key risk indicators (KRIs) provide signals that risk exposure may be increasing or that controls may be deteriorating.

Potential KRIs include:

- Growth in high or critical residual risks.
- Increasing numbers of overdue critical treatment actions.
- Repeated failure of important security or safety controls.
- Rising rates of unauthorized AI tool use.
- Material increases in model error or harmful outputs.
- Increasing rates of human override or unresolved disagreement.
- Repeated incidents involving the same provider or model.
- Exceptions approaching expiry without remediation.
- Unauthorized changes to models, prompts, or permissions.
- Significant gaps in monitoring or risk evidence.

KRIs should have thresholds and escalation rules. A threshold breach should prompt investigation and, where appropriate, containment, reassessment, or escalation to management.

A KRI is not automatically proof that harm has occurred. It is a signal requiring interpretation in context. Conversely, the absence of alerts does not prove that risk is low when monitoring coverage is incomplete.

### 60. AI Risk Review and Approval Control

AI risk assessments must undergo review proportionate to the risk classification and the consequences of the proposed use.

The review process should verify that the assessment is complete, the scoring rationale is consistent, relevant stakeholders have contributed, and treatment plans are credible.

Reviewers should challenge:

- Unsupported likelihood or impact ratings.
- Missing stakeholders or system dependencies.
- Unverified assumptions about model behavior.
- Controls that are planned but not implemented.
- Residual risk reductions unsupported by evidence.
- Unclear ownership or unrealistic treatment dates.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Incomplete third-party or privacy analysis.
- Conflicts with legal or regulatory obligations.
- Requests for acceptance beyond delegated authority.

Approval records must identify the decision, approving authority, relevant conditions, and any limitations on use.

Approval should be conditional where additional safeguards must be completed before deployment. Conditions that are mandatory for safe or lawful operation must be verified before the system is authorized to proceed.

### 61. AI Risk Independent Challenge Control

The organization should establish independent challenge for material AI risk decisions to reduce the possibility that commercial pressure, delivery deadlines, or conflicts of interest undermine the quality of assessment.

Independent challenge may be performed by a risk management function, AI governance committee, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, model risk management, or another appropriately independent reviewer.

The reviewer should have access to sufficient information, competence relevant to the risks, and authority to raise unresolved concerns.

The process should document material disagreements and their resolution. If the business owner and independent reviewer disagree about risk classification or acceptability, the decision must be escalated according to the governance framework rather than resolved solely by the party seeking approval.

The level of independence should reflect risk severity. Not every low-risk use case requires a separate committee review, but high-impact or critical-risk use cases warrant stronger challenge.

### 62. AI Risk Dependencies and Aggregation Control

AI risks must be evaluated both individually and in relation to other systems, processes, and enterprise exposures.

A collection of individually moderate-risk AI systems may create significant aggregate exposure when they depend on the same provider, share sensitive data, influence the same decision process, or execute actions through a common integration.

The organization should identify:

- Shared model and provider dependencies.
- Common data repositories and retrieval services.
- Shared identity, access, and infrastructure components.
- Concentration of critical AI capabilities in one supplier.
- Interdependencies among AI-enabled business processes.
- Common control weaknesses affecting multiple systems.
- Cumulative impacts on individuals or stakeholder groups.

Risk aggregation should not rely exclusively on adding numerical scores. The organization must consider correlated failure, common-cause events, concentration risk, and cascading consequences.

Material aggregated risks should be recorded at the appropriate enterprise level and assigned to an owner who can coordinate treatment across affected systems.

### 63. AI Risk Assumption and Uncertainty Control

AI risk assessments depend on assumptions about data, model behavior, users, operating conditions, provider commitments, and control performance. Those assumptions must be explicit and reviewable.

The organization should distinguish verified facts from estimates, hypotheses, and unresolved uncertainties.

For each material assumption, the record should identify its source, rationale, importance to the risk conclusion, validation method, owner, and review trigger.

Examples include assumptions that:

- A provider does not retain prompts beyond a defined period.
- A model will not perform actions outside its configured permissions.
- Human reviewers can identify specified classes of incorrect output.
- Training or retrieval data is sufficiently representative.
- A control remains effective after a model update.
- A supplier will notify the organization of material incidents.

When an assumption cannot be verified, the organization should consider a more conservative risk rating, additional controls, a restricted deployment, or a decision to defer the use case.

Material uncertainty must not be treated as evidence that the risk is absent.

### 64. AI Risk Scenario Analysis Control

For material AI use cases, scenario analysis should be used to explore plausible adverse events and evaluate whether existing controls can prevent, detect, contain, or recover from them.

Scenarios should be specific to the system's purpose, dependencies, and operating environment.

Examples include:

- An AI assistant discloses confidential information through a response.
- An agent follows malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content.
- A model update introduces unacceptable performance degradation.
- A third-party provider experiences a security incident.
- Incorrect AI recommendations influence a consequential business decision.
- A compromised integration enables unauthorized transactions.
- Monitoring fails to detect a gradual change in model behavior.
- A key provider becomes unavailable during a critical business process.

For each scenario, the assessment should identify the initiating event, threat or failure path, affected assets and stakeholders, potential consequences, existing controls, remaining weaknesses, and required treatment.

Scenario analysis complements likelihood-impact scoring. It provides a causal explanation of how harm could occur and where interventions may reduce the risk.

### 65. AI Risk Control Mapping

Every material AI risk must be mapped to one or more relevant controls, and important controls should be traceable to the risks they are intended to address.

This mapping enables the organization to determine whether risks are untreated, whether controls are duplicated unnecessarily, and whether control failures create exposure across several systems.

A risk-control mapping record should include:

| Field                      | Purpose                                               |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk identifier            | Uniquely identifies the risk                          |
| Risk statement             | Describes the cause, event, and consequence           |
| Control identifier         | References the relevant safeguard                     |
| Control objective          | Explains the intended risk reduction                  |
| Control owner              | Identifies accountability                             |
| Implementation status      | Records whether the control is operational            |
| Effectiveness status       | Records the result of testing                         |
| Evidence reference         | Links supporting records                              |
| Residual risk relationship | Explains the control's contribution to remaining risk |

The organization should avoid treating the presence of a mapped control as proof of risk mitigation. The control must be relevant, appropriately designed, implemented, and effective.

### 66. AI Risk-Control Gap Management

A control gap exists when a required control is absent, inadequately designed, insufficiently implemented, or ineffective in operation.

The organization must distinguish a missing control from a control that exists but fails to achieve its objective. The distinction matters because the corrective action may require new design, improved implementation, operational remediation, or a broader redesign of the AI workflow.

Each material gap should have:

- A description of the deficiency.
- The risks and systems affected.
- The severity and potential consequences.
- The cause, where known.
- The required corrective action.
- An accountable owner and target date.
- Interim safeguards or restrictions.
- Verification criteria.
- An escalation and closure decision.

Where a gap creates unacceptable residual risk, deployment or continued operation must be restricted in accordance with the organization's risk appetite and mandatory requirements.

### 67. AI Risk Communication and Stakeholder Notification Control

Material AI risks must be communicated to the stakeholders who need the information to make decisions, fulfill obligations, or protect affected parties.

Stakeholders may include business owners, system operators, security and privacy teams, legal and compliance functions, senior management, suppliers, customers, regulators, and individuals affected by AI-enabled decisions.

Communication should be accurate, timely, understandable, and proportionate to the stakeholder's role.

The organization should define when risk information must be shared, who is authorized to communicate it, which channels should be used, and how confidentiality and legal restrictions are maintained.

Risk communication must not be confused with public disclosure of sensitive technical details. The objective is to ensure that relevant parties receive the information necessary to act while protecting confidential information and respecting applicable obligations.

### 68. AI Risk Training and Competence Control

Individuals who identify, assess, approve, treat, or monitor AI risks must possess competence appropriate to their responsibilities.

Training should be role-based. A business owner may require knowledge of impact assessment and risk acceptance, while a security assessor may require technical knowledge of AI threats, system architecture, and control testing.

The organization should define competence requirements for:

- Business and risk owners.
- AI developers and system operators.
- AI governance and risk management personnel.
- Cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and compliance reviewers.
- Human reviewers responsible for consequential decisions.
- Internal auditors and independent assurance providers.
- Personnel authorized to accept material residual risk.

Training records should establish completion and, where warranted, competence through assessments, practical exercises, or demonstrated performance.

Training must be refreshed when responsibilities change, significant risks emerge, or the organization's AI governance requirements are materially revised.

### 69. AI Risk Lifecycle Control

Risk management must cover the full AI lifecycle, from initial idea through development, deployment, operation, material change, and retirement.

Different lifecycle stages introduce different risks. Early-stage design decisions may determine data exposure and system authority, while production operation introduces changing users, dependencies, threat conditions, and performance characteristics.

A lifecycle control model should define required risk activities and approval gates for:

1. Use-case proposal and initial screening.
2. Feasibility assessment and system design.
3. Data acquisition and preparation.
4. Model selection, development, or configuration.
5. Validation and pre-deployment assessment.
6. Production authorization.
7. Monitoring and periodic review.
8. Material changes and incident-driven reassessment.
9. Suspension, replacement, or decommissioning.

The organization should define minimum requirements for each applicable stage and document any stage that is not relevant to a particular system.

Lifecycle coverage prevents risk management from becoming a one-time approval activity disconnected from operational reality.

### 70. AI Risk Suspension and Decommissioning Control

The organization must define conditions under which an AI system should be suspended, restricted, replaced, or retired because risk is no longer acceptable.

Triggers may include a critical vulnerability, serious incident, unlawful use, material control failure, loss of required provider assurance, unacceptable performance degradation, or an expired approval that cannot be renewed.

Suspension and decommissioning procedures should address:

- Authority to suspend or disable the system.
- Communication with affected operational teams.
- Protection of dependent business processes.
- Revocation of credentials, tokens, and integrations.
- Preservation of evidence needed for investigation or audit.
- Retention and disposal of data in accordance with obligations.
- Termination of third-party access and services.
- Management of downstream systems and residual dependencies.
- Verification that unauthorized access and execution have ceased.
- Formal closure of outstanding risks and obligations.

A shutdown mechanism must be tested where appropriate. An emergency procedure that exists only in documentation may not provide reliable risk reduction during an actual event.

### 71. AI Risk Control Exceptions and Compensating Measures Review

Where a control cannot be implemented as designed, the organization must determine whether an alternative safeguard can achieve an equivalent or sufficient risk reduction.

A compensating control should be evaluated against the specific control objective, not merely against the convenience of implementation.

For example, if a particular monitoring integration is unavailable, an alternative may provide temporary risk reduction through more frequent manual review and restricted access. The organization must evaluate whether the alternative provides adequate coverage, timeliness, reliability, and evidence.

The review should document:

- The original control objective.
- The reason the standard control is unavailable or unsuitable.
- The proposed compensating measure.
- The risks that remain uncovered.
- The evidence supporting the effectiveness claim.
- The approval authority and duration.
- The conditions for removal or replacement.

Compensating measures must be reassessed when the operating context changes or evidence shows that they are insufficient. They must not be used to disguise an unacceptable residual risk.

### 72. AI Risk Management Assurance Control

The organization must establish assurance activities to determine whether its AI risk management framework is appropriately designed and operating as intended.

Assurance may combine first-line control monitoring, second-line risk and compliance oversight, and independent third-line internal audit. The precise model should align with the organization's governance structure.

Assurance activities may evaluate:

- Completeness and accuracy of the AI inventory.
- Timeliness and quality of risk assessments.
- Consistency of scoring and classification.
- Compliance with risk acceptance authority.
- Effectiveness of material risk controls.
- Quality of evidence and decision records.
- Timeliness of remediation and reassessment.
- Treatment of third-party and aggregated risks.
- Reliability of risk reporting and metrics.
- Adherence to lifecycle approval requirements.

Findings must be assigned owners, prioritized, tracked, and independently verified where warranted. Repeated findings should trigger analysis of underlying causes rather than repeated correction of isolated symptoms.

Assurance results should inform the annual review of the policy, the risk management methodology, control design, and resource allocation.

### 73. AI Risk Register Quality Control

The AI risk register must be maintained as a reliable management record rather than a static spreadsheet that is updated only for reporting purposes.

The organization should establish quality requirements covering completeness, consistency, traceability, timeliness, and ownership.

A risk register entry should include, at a minimum, the risk identifier, affected system, risk statement, category, inherent risk, existing controls, residual risk, owner, treatment decision, action status, review date, and acceptance or escalation references where applicable.

Quality checks should identify:

- Risks without accountable owners.
- Residual ratings inconsistent with control evidence.
- High-risk entries without treatment plans.
- Overdue actions without escalation.
- Expired risk acceptances.
- Duplicate or contradictory records.
- Assessments not linked to the inventory.
- Risks with outdated assumptions or review dates.
- Closed risks without documented verification.

Where automated workflows are available, they should flag missing fields, overdue dates, and invalid status transitions. Automated validation improves consistency but does not replace professional judgment about the substance of a risk.

### 74. AI Risk Decision Traceability Control

Material decisions must be traceable from the original risk through assessment, treatment, approval, monitoring, and subsequent review.

Traceability enables the organization to explain why a system was approved, which safeguards were required, whether those safeguards were implemented, and what changed when the risk profile evolved.

The organization should maintain links among:

- AI inventory records.
- Use-case and impact assessments.
- Risk register entries.
- Control mappings and testing records.
- Treatment plans and remediation evidence.
- Risk acceptance and exception decisions.
- Production approvals and change records.
- Monitoring alerts and incident records.
- Periodic reviews and reassessments.
- Audit findings and corrective actions.

Identifiers and version histories should remain stable enough to support historical reconstruction. When an assessment is revised, the prior decision record should remain available according to retention requirements rather than being silently overwritten.

Traceability is particularly important when a material incident requires the organization to reconstruct the assumptions and approvals that governed the system at the time.

### 75. AI Risk Management Control Review Cycle

The organization must periodically review the effectiveness and suitability of its AI risk management controls.

The review frequency should reflect risk exposure, the pace of system change, regulatory requirements, and the consequences of control failure.

The review should consider whether:

- The risk methodology remains suitable.
- Risk criteria and thresholds remain aligned with appetite.
- Assessments cover the relevant AI inventory.
- Control requirements address emerging threats.
- Risk ownership and approval authorities remain clear.
- Monitoring and escalation operate effectively.
- Third-party assurance remains adequate.
- Risk reports support timely decisions.
- Repeated exceptions reveal systemic weaknesses.
- Lessons from incidents and audits have been incorporated.

The review should result in documented decisions, assigned improvement actions, accountable owners, and target dates.

A policy review that confirms the document's wording without evaluating operational effectiveness is insufficient. The objective is to determine whether the organization can consistently identify and manage AI risks as its technology, business use, and external obligations evolve.

### 76. Core AI Risk Management Control Principle

An effective AI Risk Management Policy must be implemented through controls that are explicit, proportionate, evidence-based, and enforceable. Each material risk must be connected to an accountable owner, an appropriate treatment decision, validated controls, documented residual exposure, and a defined process for monitoring and reassessment.

The organization must distinguish between **a control requirement and an implemented control**, **an implemented control and an effective control**, and **a reduced risk and an accepted risk**. These distinctions prevent procedural completion from being mistaken for actual risk reduction.

The control framework must ensure that risks are not merely recorded but actively managed, escalated when necessary, and kept within authorized limits throughout the AI lifecycle.

**Core Principle:** AI risk controls are effective only when they produce demonstrable risk reduction and support accountable decisions based on reliable evidence.
