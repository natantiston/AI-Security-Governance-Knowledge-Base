# 20.11 — AI Security Questionnaire

## Part 1 — Foundations, Scope, Respondent Context, and Core Security Governance

### 1. Introduction to the AI Security Questionnaire

An AI Security Questionnaire is a structured assessment instrument used to collect information about the security controls, governance arrangements, technical safeguards, operational procedures, and risk management practices associated with an artificial intelligence system.

Organizations use AI Security Questionnaires to evaluate AI systems developed internally, purchased from external vendors, embedded in SaaS platforms, integrated into business applications, or deployed through third-party AI services.

Unlike a general IT security questionnaire, an AI Security Questionnaire must address risks arising from AI-specific capabilities and dependencies, including:

- Model behavior and limitations.
- Training, fine-tuning, and inference data.
- Prompt injection and indirect prompt injection.
- Sensitive information disclosure through model outputs.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and knowledge-source security.
- Model and AI application supply-chain dependencies.
- Excessive permissions granted to AI agents.
- Unauthorized or unsafe tool execution.
- Model extraction, adversarial manipulation, and abuse.
- AI-generated decisions that affect people or business operations.
- AI system drift, configuration changes, and performance degradation.
- Inadequate human oversight and failure containment.

The questionnaire provides a repeatable mechanism for collecting information from system owners, developers, platform teams, cybersecurity teams, AI providers, and other relevant stakeholders.

However, **a completed questionnaire does not, by itself, prove that an AI system is secure**. Responses must be evaluated against documented requirements, supporting evidence, technical validation, and the organization's risk acceptance criteria.

For example, a supplier might answer that its AI platform encrypts sensitive information. That response is a security claim. A documented encryption standard, configuration evidence, independent assurance report, and validation of the relevant deployment provide progressively stronger evidence of implementation and effectiveness.

The purpose of the questionnaire is therefore not simply to collect answers. It is to establish a defensible basis for security decisions.

### 2. Purpose and Objectives of the AI Security Questionnaire

An enterprise AI Security Questionnaire should support several interconnected objectives.

| Objective | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Security control identification | Identify which controls exist and which are missing. | Determine whether an AI assistant restricts access to confidential documents. |
| AI-specific threat assessment | Identify threats that are not adequately addressed by conventional IT questionnaires. | Assess indirect prompt injection through retrieved documents. |
| Governance validation | Establish whether accountability, policies, approvals, and escalation mechanisms exist. | Confirm that a designated owner approves production deployment. |
| Data protection assessment | Evaluate how information is collected, processed, retained, disclosed, and deleted. | Determine whether prompts containing personal data are retained by an external provider. |
| Technical security assessment | Examine architecture, identity, APIs, model interfaces, and execution boundaries. | Verify that an AI agent cannot invoke administrative APIs without authorization. |
| Third-party risk management | Evaluate external AI providers and inherited security dependencies. | Assess the provider's access controls and incident notification commitments. |
| Regulatory and contractual alignment | Collect information needed to assess applicable obligations. | Determine whether the deployment requires additional privacy, legal, or regulatory review. |
| Risk prioritization | Identify deficiencies requiring remediation or escalation. | Prioritize an unrestricted agent capable of modifying production records. |
| Evidence collection | Establish a traceable record supporting the assessment. | Request a penetration-test report or configuration evidence. |
| Approval support | Inform decisions to approve, conditionally approve, restrict, or reject a deployment. | Permit a pilot only after sensitive-data controls are implemented. |

The questionnaire should support decisions throughout the AI lifecycle, including:

1. Initial concept and use-case approval.
2. Architecture and solution design.
3. AI provider and vendor selection.
4. Development and integration.
5. Testing and security validation.
6. Production deployment.
7. Periodic reassessment.
8. Significant model or architecture changes.
9. Incident investigation.
10. Retirement and decommissioning.

The organization should determine which objectives apply to each assessment. A low-impact internal productivity assistant may need a proportionate review, while an AI agent that can access customer records and initiate financial transactions requires substantially stronger assurance.

The assessment process should be risk-based rather than treating every AI system as equally dangerous.

### 3. The Questionnaire as Evidence Collection Versus Security Assurance

One of the most important distinctions in AI security governance is the difference between collecting an answer and establishing that a security control is effective.

Questionnaires frequently produce a false sense of assurance when reviewers interpret positive responses as proof of implementation.

Consider the following question:

**Question:** Does the AI application enforce role-based access control?

A respondent answers: "Yes."

That answer does not establish whether:

- Role-based access control is enabled in production.
- Permissions are assigned according to least privilege.
- Access restrictions apply to retrieved documents as well as application screens.
- AI-generated requests inherit the correct user permissions.
- Service identities have excessive privileges.
- Access restrictions are tested regularly.
- Privilege escalation is prevented.
- Access failures are logged and investigated.

A robust assessment therefore separates the respondent's statement from the evidence supporting it.

#### 3.1 Five Levels of Assessment Confidence

The following model can be used to classify the strength of available assurance.

| Level | Evidence state | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 0 | Unknown | The organization cannot establish whether the control exists. | The supplier does not know whether prompts are retained. |
| Level 1 | Claimed | The respondent states that the control exists, without sufficient supporting evidence. | The supplier answers "Yes" to encryption requirements. |
| Level 2 | Documented | A policy, standard, design document, or procedure describes the control. | An approved document defines encryption requirements. |
| Level 3 | Implemented | Evidence demonstrates that the control has been deployed in the relevant environment. | Configuration records demonstrate that encryption is enabled. |
| Level 4 | Validated | Testing or independent assurance demonstrates that the control operates as intended within the assessed scope. | Testing confirms that unauthorized users cannot retrieve restricted documents. |

This is a proposed internal assurance model, not a universal rating system mandated by an AI standard.

An organization may adopt a different scale, provided the definitions are clear and applied consistently.

Even Level 4 requires interpretation. A test performed six months ago in a development environment does not necessarily demonstrate that the current production deployment is secure.

The reviewer should therefore consider the evidence's:

- Scope.
- Authenticity.
- Date.
- Completeness.
- Independence.
- Relevance to the assessed AI system.
- Applicability to the actual production configuration.

#### 3.2 Evidence Is Not the Same as Control Effectiveness

A document can be genuine but insufficient.

For example, a penetration-test report might be valid but cover only the web application interface. It may not examine the AI model interface, RAG permissions, agent tools, or external integrations.

Similarly, an ISO certification may demonstrate that a management system has been assessed within a defined scope. It does not automatically prove that every AI-specific threat has been tested or that every control is effective for the proposed use case.

The assessor must evaluate what the evidence actually demonstrates rather than relying solely on document titles, certification logos, or supplier assurances.

#### 3.3 Questionnaire Assessment Principle

The questionnaire should establish three separate conclusions:

1. **Control existence:** Is the control defined or claimed to exist?
2. **Control implementation:** Has the control been implemented in the assessed environment?
3. **Control effectiveness:** Does the control adequately reduce the relevant risk?

A control may exist but be poorly implemented. It may be implemented but ineffective against the threat being assessed. It may also be effective against one threat while leaving other risks unaddressed.

The assessment must preserve these distinctions.

### 4. Scope and Applicability of the AI Security Questionnaire

The questionnaire should be tailored to the architecture, capabilities, data sensitivity, business purpose, and risk profile of the AI system under review.

A single generic questionnaire may be useful as a baseline, but it should not be applied identically to every deployment.

#### 4.1 AI Systems Within Scope

The questionnaire may apply to the following categories.

| AI system category | Example | Important assessment considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Generative AI applications | Internal writing assistant | Prompt confidentiality, output handling, data retention, and access controls. |
| Large language model applications | Customer-support chatbot | Prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, output validation, and abuse prevention. |
| Retrieval-augmented generation systems | Assistant connected to internal knowledge repositories | Retrieval authorization, document permissions, indexing, and source integrity. |
| AI-enabled SaaS platforms | CRM platform with embedded AI functionality | Tenant isolation, provider controls, configuration, integrations, and contractual terms. |
| Machine learning prediction systems | Fraud detection model | Training-data integrity, feature security, model manipulation, and decision monitoring. |
| Computer vision systems | Automated image inspection | Image-data protection, access control, model robustness, and output reliability. |
| AI agents | Agent that reads records and updates business applications | Tool authorization, action boundaries, approval gates, and transaction safety. |
| AI development platforms | Managed model-training environment | Training data, experiment access, artifact protection, and supply-chain security. |
| AI APIs and model endpoints | External model inference API | Authentication, rate limits, data handling, abuse prevention, and availability. |
| AI-assisted decision systems | Recruitment or credit-support application | Decision impact, human oversight, fairness, explainability requirements, and appeal mechanisms. |
| AI embedded in enterprise software | AI features within ERP or collaboration tools | Feature configuration, permissions, data flows, and inherited provider controls. |
| Fine-tuned or customized models | Organization-specific model adaptation | Training-data governance, model artifacts, testing, and deployment security. |

#### 4.2 System Boundary

The questionnaire must define what is included in the assessment.

An AI application is rarely limited to its model. Its security depends on the surrounding application, infrastructure, data sources, identity services, APIs, monitoring systems, and external providers.

For a RAG-based enterprise assistant, the assessment boundary may include:

- User interface.
- Application backend.
- Model gateway.
- Foundation model provider.
- Embedding model.
- Vector database.
- Enterprise document repositories.
- Identity provider.
- API integrations.
- Logging and monitoring platform.
- Administrative interfaces.
- Supporting cloud infrastructure.

The assessor should document which components are directly controlled by the organization, which are managed by suppliers, and which are shared responsibilities.

#### 4.3 In-Scope and Out-of-Scope Components

Every assessment should explicitly record exclusions.

| Component | Example scope decision | Required treatment |
|---|---|---|
| AI application | In scope | Assess application security and AI-specific controls. |
| Foundation model | Externally managed | Assess provider assurance and available model-level safeguards. |
| Vector database | In scope | Assess access controls, isolation, data protection, and retrieval behavior. |
| Corporate identity service | Shared dependency | Assess relevant authentication and authorization integrations. |
| Supplier's internal infrastructure | Not directly accessible | Evaluate available independent assurance and contractual commitments. |
| Unrelated enterprise systems | Out of scope | Document exclusion and identify any material dependencies. |

An out-of-scope component should not automatically be treated as risk-free.

If a material dependency cannot be assessed directly, the reviewer should document the limitation and obtain alternative evidence where possible.

If the evidence is insufficient, the appropriate conclusion may be that the risk remains unverified.

### 5. Distinguishing an AI Security Questionnaire from Other Assessments

Organizations often use several assessment instruments during AI governance. Their purposes overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

| Assessment instrument | Primary question | Typical focus | Main output |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI Security Questionnaire | What security controls exist, and what evidence supports them? | Security architecture, technical safeguards, operational security, and control implementation. | Security control findings and evidence gaps. |
| AI Vendor Assessment | Can we rely on this external AI provider? | Supplier governance, security assurance, subcontractors, contractual obligations, and provider resilience. | Vendor risk rating and procurement recommendations. |
| AI Use-Case Assessment | Should we use AI for this business purpose? | Intended use, business value, affected stakeholders, risk, proportionality, and approval. | Use-case approval or rejection decision. |
| AI Impact Assessment | What consequences could this AI system have for people, society, or the organization? | Impacts, affected parties, rights, fairness, and mitigation. | Impact findings and mitigation plan. |
| AI Risk Assessment | What can go wrong, how serious is it, and how should the risk be treated? | Threat scenarios, likelihood, impact, controls, and residual risk. | Risk register and treatment decisions. |
| AI Architecture Review | Is the proposed architecture sufficiently secure and appropriate? | Trust boundaries, component design, identity, data flows, integrations, and failure containment. | Architecture findings and design actions. |
| AI Model Risk Assessment | Is the model appropriate, reliable, and sufficiently controlled for its intended purpose? | Model limitations, performance, robustness, drift, and validation. | Model risk findings and validation requirements. |
| AI Compliance Assessment | Which applicable obligations are satisfied or unmet? | Legal requirements, standards, contractual commitments, and control traceability. | Compliance gaps and remediation actions. |
| AI Penetration Test or Red-Team Assessment | Can relevant security protections be bypassed under defined test conditions? | Adversarial testing, exploitation paths, and technical weaknesses. | Test findings and validated attack scenarios. |

#### 5.1 How the Assessments Work Together

Consider a company introducing an AI-powered customer-support assistant.

The use-case assessment establishes why the assistant is needed and what it is permitted to do.

The vendor assessment examines the external AI provider and its contractual commitments.

The security questionnaire gathers information about the assistant's controls.

The architecture review examines the application, model gateway, knowledge sources, and identity integrations.

The risk assessment evaluates scenarios such as disclosure of confidential customer information.

Security testing attempts to validate whether relevant safeguards resist realistic attacks.

The compliance assessment maps the resulting evidence to applicable obligations.

Together, these activities support a defensible approval decision. No single questionnaire should be expected to replace the complete governance process.

### 6. Standards, Frameworks, and Regulatory Context

An AI Security Questionnaire should support the organization's chosen governance framework and applicable legal obligations.

It should not assume that every framework applies to every AI system, nor should it claim that completing a questionnaire establishes compliance.

The following references can inform questionnaire design.

| Reference | Relevance to the questionnaire | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI management-system governance, responsibilities, planning, operation, evaluation, and improvement. | Certification or alignment does not independently prove every AI security control is effective. |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information security management, risk treatment, and security control governance. | General information security controls may need AI-specific supplementation. |
| ISO/IEC 27002 | Information security control guidance. | Controls must be interpreted in the context of the AI architecture and threat model. |
| ISO/IEC 23894 | AI risk management guidance. | Risk management guidance does not replace system-specific security testing. |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework | AI risk governance, mapping, measurement, and management. | It is a risk-management framework, not a universal certification checklist. |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework | Cybersecurity governance and outcomes across organizational security activities. | AI-specific threats require additional questions and tailored assessment criteria. |
| NIST AI 600-1, Generative AI Profile | Generative AI risk considerations and risk-management practices. | Applicability depends on the system and deployment context. |
| OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications | Practical threat categories for LLM-enabled applications. | A threat list is not, by itself, a complete enterprise governance framework. |
| OWASP Machine Learning Security Top Ten | Machine-learning security threat considerations. | The relevant threats depend on the model type, lifecycle, and system architecture. |
| EU AI Act | Applicable legal requirements determined by the AI system's role, classification, and use. | A security questionnaire alone cannot establish legal compliance or determine every applicable obligation. |
| GDPR | Personal-data processing obligations where the regulation applies. | Security controls are only one part of data protection compliance. |
| DORA and sector-specific requirements | Additional obligations may apply to certain regulated entities and ICT arrangements. | Applicability must be assessed against the organization's activities and legal status. |

#### 6.1 Mapping Questions to Requirements

Each substantive questionnaire item should have a documented rationale.

A control-mapping record may contain:

- Question ID.
- Security objective.
- Threat or risk scenario.
- Control requirement.
- Relevant framework reference.
- Applicability conditions.
- Required evidence.
- Assessment criteria.
- Finding severity rules.
- Control owner.

For example:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Question ID | AISQ-DATA-004 |
| Security objective | Prevent unauthorized disclosure of confidential information. |
| Risk scenario | An AI response exposes a confidential document to an unauthorized user. |
| Control requirement | Retrieved content must be subject to effective authorization checks. |
| Evidence | Architecture documentation, access-control configuration, and test results. |
| Assessment criterion | Unauthorized users cannot retrieve protected content through the AI application. |
| Finding severity | Based on data sensitivity, exposure, exploitability, and business impact. |

The mapping should be version-controlled because standards, regulations, internal requirements, and AI architectures can change.

### 7. Respondent Instructions and Evidence Expectations

The quality of an assessment depends partly on the quality of the instructions given to respondents.

Ambiguous questions produce inconsistent answers. Questions without evidence requirements encourage unsupported assurances. Questions that combine multiple requirements make it difficult to identify specific control deficiencies.

The questionnaire should therefore establish a common response protocol before assessment begins.

#### 7.1 Standard Response Options

A recommended response model is:

| Response | Meaning | Required follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Yes — Implemented | The control is implemented within the stated scope. | Provide supporting evidence and identify the implementation boundary. |
| Partially implemented | Some, but not all, requirements are satisfied. | Identify gaps, affected components, compensating controls, and remediation dates. |
| No — Not implemented | The control is absent or does not satisfy the requirement. | Explain the gap and propose a treatment plan. |
| Not applicable | The requirement genuinely does not apply to the assessed system. | Provide a rationale for non-applicability. |
| Unknown | The respondent cannot establish the control's status. | Identify the information gap, accountable owner, and verification action. |
| Planned | Implementation is intended but not yet complete. | Provide an owner, target date, dependencies, and interim safeguards. |
| Unable to disclose | The respondent cannot provide the requested information. | Explain the restriction and identify alternative assurance evidence. |

A planned control must not be counted as an implemented control.

Similarly, "Not applicable" should not be accepted without review. A respondent may misunderstand the question or overlook a relevant attack path.

#### 7.2 Evidence Requirements

For each question, the assessor should specify the expected evidence type.

Examples include:

- Approved policies and standards.
- Architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Configuration exports or screenshots.
- Access-control matrices.
- Technical test reports.
- Penetration-test and red-team findings.
- Vulnerability management records.
- Incident response procedures.
- Logging and monitoring configurations.
- Data-processing agreements.
- Supplier assurance reports.
- Model evaluation results.
- AI system inventory records.
- Change management approvals.
- Records of human oversight and intervention.

Evidence should be proportionate to the risk. A low-impact pilot may not require the same volume of documentation as an AI system that handles highly sensitive information or can perform consequential actions.

#### 7.3 Evidence Quality Rules

Respondents should be instructed to:

1. Identify the system and environment covered by the evidence.
2. Provide the date and version of relevant documents.
3. Explain any material exclusions.
4. Distinguish implemented controls from planned improvements.
5. Identify whether evidence is produced internally or by an independent party.
6. Avoid sharing unnecessary personal data, credentials, secrets, or exploitable technical details.
7. Explain when a requested artifact cannot be provided.
8. Identify the owner responsible for confirming the response.

Confidential evidence should be exchanged through approved channels rather than attached indiscriminately to an email or stored in an unrestricted repository.

### 8. Assessment Owner and Respondent Responsibilities

An AI Security Questionnaire is a cross-functional assessment. Its accuracy depends on assigning the right questions to people who understand the relevant controls.

The business owner should not be expected to independently answer every technical question. Likewise, a security engineer should not be expected to determine every legal, privacy, or business-impact requirement.

#### 8.1 Core Roles

| Role | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Assessment owner | Coordinates the assessment, defines scope, tracks completion, and maintains the assessment record. |
| AI system or product owner | Confirms intended purpose, business context, users, dependencies, and operational ownership. |
| AI or ML engineering lead | Provides information about models, pipelines, evaluation, deployment, and technical limitations. |
| Application owner | Explains application logic, integrations, access controls, and production operations. |
| Cybersecurity architect | Reviews architecture, trust boundaries, attack paths, and security requirements. |
| Security operations team | Explains monitoring, alerting, incident handling, and security telemetry. |
| Data owner | Confirms data classification, authorized use, retention, and access requirements. |
| Privacy or data protection team | Evaluates personal-data processing and applicable privacy obligations. |
| Legal and compliance team | Determines relevant legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements. |
| Procurement or third-party risk team | Coordinates supplier assurance and contractual evidence. |
| AI governance function | Ensures alignment with the organization's AI governance process. |
| Independent assessor or auditor | Challenges responses, examines evidence, and evaluates control effectiveness within the assigned mandate. |
| Executive or delegated risk owner | Makes risk acceptance decisions within the person's documented authority. |

#### 8.2 Responsibility Boundaries

The questionnaire should make clear that answering a question does not automatically confer authority to approve the associated risk.

For example:

- An engineer can confirm that a technical safeguard has been implemented.
- A security assessor can evaluate whether the safeguard adequately addresses a threat.
- A business owner can explain the operational consequences of restricting the system.
- A privacy specialist can advise on personal-data processing.
- An authorized risk owner can accept a residual risk within delegated authority.

These responsibilities should be reflected in the assessment workflow and approval matrix.

### 9. Assessment Metadata and Record Identification

Every questionnaire should have a unique identifier and sufficient metadata to establish exactly what was assessed, when it was assessed, and who was responsible.

Without this information, the organization may be unable to determine whether a previous assessment applies to a changed model, a new deployment, a different vendor, or an additional use case.

#### 9.1 Recommended Metadata Fields

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Unique identifier for the assessment record. |
| Questionnaire version | Version of the questions and assessment criteria used. |
| AI system ID | Reference to the organization's AI system inventory. |
| Use-case ID | Reference to the approved or proposed business use case. |
| Vendor ID | Reference to the supplier record, if applicable. |
| Assessment title | Short, descriptive name. |
| Assessment type | Internal system, supplier, application, model, agent, or another defined category. |
| Business unit | Organization responsible for the system or use case. |
| Business owner | Person accountable for the business purpose. |
| Technical owner | Person accountable for the technical implementation. |
| Assessment owner | Person coordinating the questionnaire. |
| Respondent organization | Internal team or external supplier completing the assessment. |
| Environment | Development, testing, staging, production, or another defined environment. |
| Deployment model | Cloud, SaaS, on-premises, hybrid, or another deployment model. |
| Assessment start date | Date the assessment begins. |
| Target completion date | Planned completion date. |
| Assessment status | Draft, in progress, evidence review, remediation, approved, rejected, or closed. |
| Data classification | Highest relevant classification of data processed. |
| Preliminary risk tier | Initial risk category based on organizational criteria. |
| Review date | Date of the latest substantive review. |
| Approval reference | Link or identifier for the final decision record. |

#### 9.2 Version Control

The questionnaire version must be distinguished from the assessed system version.

For example:

- Questionnaire version: AISQ 1.3.
- AI application version: 4.2.
- Model version: Provider Model Release B.
- Assessment date: 15 October.
- Production configuration reviewed: Configuration revision 27.

This distinction helps establish whether the conclusions remain applicable after a change.

If a provider changes its model, data-retention terms, or inference infrastructure, the organization may need to reassess affected controls even when the application's version number remains unchanged.

### 10. AI System and Product Identification

Before evaluating security controls, the assessor must understand what the AI system actually is.

Product names alone are insufficient because one product may contain several models, services, data-processing components, and optional capabilities.

The questionnaire should establish the system's functional and technical identity.

#### 10.1 Identification Questions

**AISQ-SYS-001 — What is the name and unique identifier of the AI system?**

Record the product name, internal system identifier, owner, and inventory reference.

**AISQ-SYS-002 — What business purpose does the system serve?**

Describe the business problem, intended users, expected outcomes, and permitted use boundaries.

**AISQ-SYS-003 — Which AI capabilities are enabled?**

Identify relevant capabilities, such as text generation, summarization, classification, image analysis, predictive scoring, document retrieval, autonomous planning, or tool execution.

**AISQ-SYS-004 — Which models and model providers are involved?**

Identify foundation models, embedding models, reranking models, classifiers, fine-tuned models, and other relevant components.

**AISQ-SYS-005 — Which application components support the AI functionality?**

Identify the frontend, backend, model gateway, retrieval services, data stores, orchestration components, and external integrations.

**AISQ-SYS-006 — Is the system developed internally, purchased, externally hosted, or assembled from multiple services?**

Describe the development and service delivery model.

**AISQ-SYS-007 — Which environments contain the system?**

Identify development, testing, staging, production, and disaster recovery environments, as applicable.

**AISQ-SYS-008 — Is the system capable of taking actions beyond generating an output?**

Identify whether it can invoke APIs, modify records, send messages, create tickets, execute code, initiate transactions, or trigger other business processes.

**AISQ-SYS-009 — Which other systems depend on its outputs?**

Identify downstream applications, workflows, automated decisions, and human decision-makers.

**AISQ-SYS-010 — Has the system undergone material changes since its previous assessment?**

Identify changes to models, prompts, permissions, data sources, hosting arrangements, integrations, and intended use.

#### 10.2 Why Capability Identification Matters

A system that generates draft text generally has a different operational risk profile from an AI agent authorized to update customer records.

The agent's additional permissions create potential consequences beyond the content of its responses.

The assessment must therefore identify not only the model's technical characteristics but also the actions the complete system can perform.

### 11. AI Capabilities and Model Types

The questionnaire should identify the AI techniques and capabilities present in the system because different technologies introduce different security concerns.

| Capability or model type | Description | Security considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Large language model | Generates or transforms language. | Prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, unsafe output, and abuse. |
| Embedding model | Converts information into vector representations. | Data exposure, access boundaries, embedding pipeline integrity, and retrieval behavior. |
| Reranking model | Reorders retrieved results according to relevance. | Manipulated ranking, inappropriate retrieval prioritization, and dependence on untrusted content. |
| Predictive machine learning | Estimates a value, category, or outcome. | Feature manipulation, data integrity, model drift, and prediction reliability. |
| Computer vision | Interprets images or video. | Input manipulation, sensitive image data, robustness, and output interpretation. |
| Speech recognition or synthesis | Processes or generates audio. | Voice-data protection, spoofing, unauthorized recording, and output misuse. |
| Multimodal model | Processes combinations of text, images, audio, or other modalities. | Cross-modal prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, and inconsistent safety behavior. |
| Retrieval-augmented generation | Combines model generation with external information retrieval. | Retrieval authorization, document poisoning, source integrity, and unauthorized context disclosure. |
| Fine-tuned model | Has been adapted using additional training. | Training-data governance, artifact security, evaluation, and update controls. |
| AI agent | Uses a model to plan and perform actions through tools. | Excessive permissions, unsafe actions, untrusted instructions, and insufficient approval gates. |

A system may combine several of these capabilities.

For example, an enterprise AI assistant may use a language model, an embedding model, a vector database, and an agent framework. The questionnaire should cover each material component rather than selecting only one model category.

The assessment should also record whether the organization has access to model configuration, model weights, training details, safety settings, and relevant telemetry.

When the provider does not disclose those details, the assessor should record the limitation and evaluate alternative assurance measures.

### 12. Intended Purpose and Approved Use

The questionnaire must establish the purpose for which the AI system is authorized to operate.

Security controls cannot be evaluated adequately without knowing how the system is intended to be used.

For example, an AI assistant approved to summarize public marketing material should not automatically be authorized to process employee medical records or make employment decisions.

#### 12.1 Intended-Purpose Questions

**AISQ-USE-001 — What is the formally approved intended purpose of the AI system?**

Record the approved business purpose and link it to the use-case assessment.

**AISQ-USE-002 — Which user groups are authorized to use the system?**

Identify employees, administrators, customers, contractors, partners, and other relevant groups.

**AISQ-USE-003 — Which activities are permitted?**

Describe the allowed operations and the boundaries of authorized use.

**AISQ-USE-004 — Which activities are prohibited or restricted?**

Identify uses that are explicitly disallowed, require additional approval, or are outside the assessed scope.

**AISQ-USE-005 — Can the system be used to make or materially influence decisions affecting individuals?**

If so, identify the decisions and the applicable oversight and review requirements.

**AISQ-USE-006 — Can the system execute actions automatically?**

Identify the actions, permission boundaries, approval requirements, and rollback mechanisms.

**AISQ-USE-007 — Can users connect additional data sources, tools, plugins, or external services?**

Describe how additions are authorized, assessed, and monitored.

**AISQ-USE-008 — How is unauthorized use detected and handled?**

Describe technical restrictions, monitoring, escalation, and disciplinary or contractual procedures where relevant.

#### 12.2 Intended Use Versus Actual Use

An organization may approve a system for one purpose while users gradually adopt it for additional activities.

This creates a governance gap.

For example, an internal summarization tool might later be used to generate customer-facing legal commitments. The original assessment may not have considered the accuracy, approval, or liability implications of that use.

The questionnaire should therefore establish whether actual use is monitored against the approved purpose.

Material deviations should trigger reassessment rather than being treated as ordinary user behavior.

### 13. Deployment and Hosting Model

The hosting arrangement affects which controls the organization can implement directly and which depend on a provider.

The questionnaire should identify where each major component operates and who is responsible for securing it.

#### 13.1 Deployment Categories

| Deployment model | Description | Main assessment focus |
|---|---|---|
| On-premises | AI components operate on infrastructure managed by the organization. | Infrastructure hardening, model artifacts, local access, network segmentation, and operations. |
| Public cloud | AI components run on cloud infrastructure. | Cloud IAM, network controls, service configuration, logging, and shared responsibility. |
| Managed AI platform | A provider manages AI infrastructure and model services. | Provider assurance, tenant isolation, service configuration, data handling, and contractual controls. |
| SaaS application | AI functionality is embedded in a provider-operated application. | Tenant configuration, identity integration, data processing, access control, and provider dependencies. |
| Hybrid deployment | Components operate across organizational and provider-managed environments. | Trust boundaries, identity federation, data transfers, and shared-control responsibilities. |
| Self-hosted model | The organization deploys model software or weights in its own environment. | Artifact integrity, infrastructure security, patching, model access, and deployment operations. |
| External API | The organization sends requests to an externally managed AI service. | API authentication, data disclosure, provider retention, availability, and usage restrictions. |

#### 13.2 Shared Responsibility

The organization should not assume that using a managed AI service transfers all security responsibilities to the provider.

The provider may secure the underlying infrastructure while the customer remains responsible for identity configuration, access permissions, data selection, application logic, and monitoring.

The exact allocation depends on the service and contractual arrangement.

A responsibility matrix should identify:

- Controls implemented by the provider.
- Controls implemented by the customer.
- Controls requiring joint implementation.
- Controls for which assurance is insufficient.
- Responsibilities that are not clearly allocated.

#### 13.3 Deployment Questions

**AISQ-DEP-001 — Where are the AI application and supporting components hosted?**

Identify cloud accounts, regions, data centers, or provider services as appropriate.

**AISQ-DEP-002 — Which party manages the underlying infrastructure?**

Identify the provider and the organizational team responsible for oversight.

**AISQ-DEP-003 — Which security controls are managed by the provider, the customer, or both?**

Provide the relevant shared-responsibility documentation.

### 14. Data Categories and Sensitivity

Data security is a central component of AI security because AI systems may process information through prompts, retrieved documents, model context, embeddings, logs, training pipelines, and generated outputs.

The questionnaire should identify the types of information handled at every material stage.

#### 14.1 Data Categories

| Data category | Examples | Assessment considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Public information | Published product descriptions and public documentation | Integrity, poisoning, and unauthorized modification. |
| Internal information | Internal procedures and operational documentation | Access restrictions and inappropriate disclosure. |
| Confidential business information | Contracts, commercial plans, and internal financial information | Data leakage, retention, provider access, and unauthorized use. |

#### 15.3 Data-Flow Risk Indicators

The following conditions warrant further investigation:

- Sensitive information is sent to an external provider without a clear approved purpose.
- The organization cannot determine where data is processed.
- The provider's retention practices are unknown.
- Retrieved documents are not subject to the original access permissions.
- Sensitive prompts are copied into unrestricted logs.
- New integrations can be enabled without security review.
- Data deletion does not cover secondary stores.
- The system sends generated outputs to downstream applications without authorization or validation.

These conditions do not automatically prove a security breach. They indicate potential control weaknesses requiring risk assessment and appropriate evidence.

### 20. AI Risk Assessment Methodology

The questionnaire should connect control responses to the organization's formal AI risk management process.

A list of control deficiencies is useful, but it does not automatically establish which risks are most important.

Risk assessment requires the organization to consider the relevant threat scenario, the likelihood of occurrence, the potential impact, existing controls, and residual exposure.

#### 20.1 Risk Scenario Structure

A practical risk scenario can be expressed as:

**Threat or event → Vulnerability or control weakness → Affected asset or stakeholder → Consequence → Business impact**

Example:

An attacker submits a crafted document containing indirect prompt injection. The AI retrieval system processes the document without adequately separating untrusted instructions from trusted application instructions. The AI assistant follows the malicious instructions and attempts to retrieve or disclose confidential business information. The organization may experience a data breach, contractual consequences, regulatory exposure, and loss of trust.

The questionnaire helps establish whether relevant safeguards exist, but the complete risk assessment determines the significance of the scenario.

#### 20.2 Risk Assessment Questions

**AISQ-RISK-001 — Has the AI system undergone a documented security risk assessment?**

Identify the assessment date, scope, methodology, and accountable owner.

**AISQ-RISK-002 — Does the assessment cover AI-specific threats relevant to the system?**

Identify the threats considered and explain significant exclusions.

**AISQ-RISK-003 — Are risks assessed across the AI lifecycle?**

Confirm coverage of development, deployment, operation, change, and retirement, as applicable.

**AISQ-RISK-004 — Are inherent risks distinguished from residual risks?**

Explain how existing controls influence the final risk rating.

**AISQ-RISK-005 — Are identified risks assigned to accountable owners?**

Provide the risk register or equivalent record.

**AISQ-RISK-006 — Are remediation actions tracked to completion?**

Identify the action owner, target date, status, and closure evidence.

**AISQ-RISK-007 — Are residual risks accepted by authorized individuals?**

Provide the relevant decision record and any conditions attached to acceptance.

**AISQ-RISK-008 — Are risk assessments updated following material changes or incidents?**

Explain the reassessment triggers and review process.

#### 20.3 Preliminary Risk Indicators

The following conditions may indicate elevated risk:

- No accountable owner.
- No documented assessment.
- Sensitive data processed without clear authorization boundaries.
- Broad agent permissions.
- No documented production approval.
- Unverified external provider data-handling practices.
- Inadequate logging or incident response.
- No process for reviewing material model changes.
- Critical security findings without an approved treatment plan.
- Residual risks accepted by individuals without appropriate authority.

These indicators should prompt investigation. The final rating must consider the actual exposure, affected assets, available controls, and business context.

### 23. Identity and Access Controls

Identity and access management are fundamental to AI security because AI applications may combine user permissions, service identities, model-provider credentials, retrieval access, and tool-execution privileges.

#### 23.1 Identity and Access Questions

**AISQ-IAM-003 — Are permissions assigned according to least privilege?**

**AISQ-IAM-010 — Are permissions revalidated before consequential AI actions are executed?**

#### 23.2 The Critical Difference Between User Authentication and Action Authorization

Authentication establishes an identity. Authorization determines what that identity may do.

An AI model may receive a request from an authenticated user but still generate a suggestion that exceeds the user's permissions.

The application must not treat model-generated instructions as proof of authorization.

For example, if a customer-support agent is permitted to view a customer's service history but not issue refunds, the AI application must enforce that restriction through the relevant backend authorization mechanism.

The model's apparent confidence or instruction to perform a refund must not bypass the business application's authorization rules.

#### 24.2 Agent Permission Boundaries

- Which tools are available.
- Which actions each tool can perform.
- Which resources each tool can access.
- Whether the agent can write, delete, or modify data.
- Whether it can initiate external communication.
- Whether actions require human approval.
- Whether limits are enforced outside the model.
- Whether emergency revocation is available.

Permissions should be enforced by the application, identity system, API, or other appropriate technical mechanism.

A prompt that tells an agent not to perform a dangerous action is not equivalent to a technical permission boundary.

#### 25.2 Cryptographic Protection

The questionnaire should identify how sensitive information and relevant artifacts are protected in transit and at rest.

The assessor should distinguish between:

- Encryption implemented by the application.
- Encryption implemented by a cloud or storage provider.
- Key management controlled by the organization.
- Key management controlled by a provider.
- Additional controls required for especially sensitive information.

The presence of encryption does not automatically prevent unauthorized disclosure through an authorized application, a compromised account, excessive permissions, or unsafe model behavior.

Cryptographic protection must be evaluated alongside access control, application security, data governance, and operational controls.

An externally exposed model endpoint may be vulnerable to excessive requests, unauthorized use, automated abuse, or attempts to extract information.

The questionnaire should establish whether the organization has defined acceptable usage, capacity limits, monitoring, and abuse response procedures.

For sensitive deployments, it may also be necessary to evaluate whether callers can infer information about other users or tenants through the endpoint.

The appropriate controls depend on the endpoint's exposure, the provider's capabilities, the data processed, and the potential impact of misuse.

### 27. Data Protection and Encryption

The questionnaire should determine how information is protected while being transmitted, stored, processed, and retained.

The assessment should cover not only the primary database but also the complete AI data lifecycle.

#### 27.1 Data Protection Questions

**AISQ-DP-001 — Is sensitive information encrypted in transit?**

Identify relevant communication paths and the applicable protection requirements.

**AISQ-DP-002 — Is sensitive information encrypted at rest where required?**

Identify applicable storage components, including databases, file stores, vector databases, and backups.

**AISQ-DP-003 — Are encryption keys managed according to approved security requirements?**

Describe key ownership, access restrictions, and rotation.

**AISQ-DP-004 — Are data access and processing restricted according to classification and authorization?**

Provide the relevant technical and procedural controls.

**AISQ-DP-005 — Are retention periods defined for prompts, outputs, retrieved content, logs, and other relevant records?**

Identify retention requirements and responsible owners.

**AISQ-DP-006 — Can information be deleted from relevant secondary stores and provider-managed services?**

Explain the deletion process and its documented limitations.

**AISQ-DP-007 — Are personal data and other restricted data processed according to applicable requirements?**

Identify relevant reviews, agreements, and safeguards.

**AISQ-DP-008 — Are data-loss prevention or equivalent controls applied where appropriate?**

Describe how unauthorized transfers or disclosures are prevented or detected.

**AISQ-DP-009 — Are data transfers to external providers documented and approved?**

Provide the data-flow and provider records.

**AISQ-DP-010 — Are data protection controls tested or otherwise validated?**

Provide relevant evidence of implementation and effectiveness.

#### 27.2 The AI Data Leakage Problem

AI systems can disclose information through several channels:

- Directly in a generated response.
- Through retrieval of unauthorized documents.
- Through logs or debugging records.
- Through exposed prompts or traces.
- Through connected tools.
- Through inappropriate output forwarding.
- Through provider retention or secondary processing.
- Through compromised credentials or excessive permissions.

No single safeguard addresses every leakage path.

A robust assessment should evaluate the controls associated with the actual data flows and trust boundaries.

### 28. Logging, Monitoring, and Security Visibility

An organization needs sufficient visibility to detect misuse, investigate incidents, and demonstrate that important controls operate as intended.

AI systems introduce additional monitoring requirements because relevant events may include prompts, model requests, retrieval operations, tool calls, policy violations, and generated actions.

Logging must be designed carefully to avoid creating a secondary repository of sensitive information.

#### 28.1 Logging and Monitoring Questions

**AISQ-MON-001 — Are security-relevant AI application events logged?**

Identify the events captured and the systems receiving them.

**AISQ-MON-002 — Are authentication and authorization events recorded?**

Explain how successful access, denied requests, and privileged operations are tracked.

**AISQ-MON-003 — Are AI agent tool calls and consequential actions recorded?**

Identify the information available to reconstruct what the system attempted and what actually occurred.

**AISQ-MON-004 — Are prompt injection attempts or suspicious input patterns monitored where feasible?**

Describe detection mechanisms and known limitations.

**AISQ-MON-005 — Are unusual usage patterns and resource consumption monitored?**

Identify relevant thresholds, alerts, and response actions.

**AISQ-MON-006 — Are logs protected against unauthorized access and modification?**

Describe access restrictions, integrity controls, and retention.

**AISQ-MON-007 — Are monitoring alerts assigned to an accountable operational team?**

Identify ownership and escalation procedures.

**AISQ-MON-008 — Can investigators reconstruct a material AI security incident?**

Explain how events across the application, model gateway, identity service, retrieval layer, and connected systems are correlated.

**AISQ-MON-009 — Are sensitive prompts and outputs excluded, redacted, or otherwise protected where necessary?**

Explain the balance between investigative visibility and data minimization.

**AISQ-MON-010 — Are monitoring controls periodically tested?**

Provide relevant test records or operational evidence.

#### 28.2 Logging Design Considerations

Logging should be sufficient to answer important investigative questions:

- Which user or service initiated the operation?
- Which AI system and model configuration were involved?
- What data sources were accessed?
- Which tools were invoked?
- What authorization decisions occurred?
- What downstream actions were attempted?
- Did the system enforce relevant restrictions?
- Which alerts were generated?
- What response actions followed?

The organization may not need to retain complete raw prompts or model outputs for every interaction. In some cases, storing such content would increase privacy and confidentiality risks.

The appropriate logging design should balance security monitoring, accountability, data minimization, retention requirements, and investigative needs.

### 29. Incident Response and Operational Resilience

An AI system must have a defined response process for security incidents, unsafe behavior, data disclosure, service disruption, and other material failures.

The organization should be able to investigate incidents and contain the affected capability without necessarily shutting down unrelated business services.

#### 29.1 Incident Response Questions

**AISQ-IR-001 — Is the AI system covered by an approved incident response process?**

Identify the applicable procedure and responsible team.

**AISQ-IR-002 — Are AI-specific incident scenarios included?**

Consider prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, unauthorized tool execution, model compromise, provider incidents, and unsafe outputs.

**AISQ-IR-003 — Are escalation thresholds and notification responsibilities defined?**

Identify internal contacts and relevant contractual or legal notification requirements.

**AISQ-IR-004 — Can access to the AI system or its integrations be restricted quickly?**

Describe emergency access revocation and containment mechanisms.

**AISQ-IR-005 — Can individual tools or capabilities be disabled without disabling the entire application?**

Explain the available containment options.

**AISQ-IR-006 — Are incident logs and relevant evidence preserved?**

Describe evidence handling, access restrictions, and retention.

**AISQ-IR-007 — Are incidents involving external AI providers covered by defined escalation procedures?**

Identify provider contacts, contractual notification requirements, and coordination arrangements.

**AISQ-IR-008 — Are recovery procedures tested?**

Provide evidence of exercises, recovery tests, or other validation.

#### 29.2 Safe Degradation and Recovery

Operational resilience requires more than restoring service availability.

The organization should consider how the AI system behaves when:

- The model provider becomes unavailable.
- The model produces unreliable outputs.
- A retrieval source is compromised.
- An agent begins performing unauthorized actions.
- A critical security vulnerability is discovered.
- A data-protection control fails.
- An external integration becomes unavailable.

Possible responses include disabling selected capabilities, reverting to a previous approved configuration, restricting access to sensitive data, requiring human review, switching to a safe fallback process, or suspending the AI function.

The appropriate response depends on the business process and the consequences of failure.

An AI feature should not continue performing high-impact actions merely because its underlying service remains operational.

### 30. Part 1 Core Principle — A Questionnaire Must Establish the Basis for Security Assurance

An AI Security Questionnaire begins by defining the system, its intended purpose, its data, its architecture, its dependencies, and its governance responsibilities.

It then establishes a structured process for collecting control information and supporting evidence.

The central principle is that **security claims must be distinguished from documented controls, implemented safeguards, and validated control effectiveness**.

A defensible questionnaire should therefore:

1. Define a clear assessment boundary.
2. Identify the AI system, its models, and material dependencies.
3. Establish the approved use and operational context.
4. Identify the data categories and processing flows.
5. Assign questions to appropriate respondents and control owners.
6. Require evidence proportionate to the risk.
7. Map questions to relevant threats and control requirements.
8. Connect findings to the formal risk management process.
9. Record limitations, unknowns, and exclusions.
10. Establish a traceable basis for subsequent review and approval.

The questionnaire is not a substitute for threat modeling, technical testing, privacy assessment, vendor due diligence, or formal risk acceptance. It provides a structured foundation that helps these activities work together.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Questionnaire response | What a respondent reports about a control. | That the statement is correct. |
| Supporting evidence | Information relevant to the control claim. | That the evidence is complete or applicable. |
| Implementation verification | That a control has been deployed within the assessed scope. | That the control is effective against every relevant threat. |
| Control effectiveness validation | Evidence that a control operates as intended under defined conditions. | That the entire AI system is secure. |
| Risk assessment | The significance of identified threats, weaknesses, and consequences. | Automatic authorization to deploy the system. |
| Risk acceptance | A formally authorized decision to tolerate specified residual risk. | Elimination of the underlying risk or exemption from applicable obligations. |

# 20.11 — AI Security Questionnaire

## Part 2 — Technical Security Controls, Model Protection, Data Security, and Operational Safeguards

### 31. Detailed AI Security Control Assessment Methodology

Part 1 established the foundations of an AI Security Questionnaire: assessment scope, system identification, data flows, governance, risk methodology, and evidence expectations.

Part 2 converts those foundations into specific security questions that can be used to assess AI systems in enterprise environments.

The central objective is to determine whether the system has appropriate technical and operational safeguards against the threats identified during assessment.

Each question should be designed to produce an assessable result rather than a vague statement of intent.

For example, the question "Is the AI application secure?" is too broad. It does not identify the control being assessed, the threat being addressed, or the evidence required.

A stronger question is:

**AISQ-CTRL-001 — Does the application enforce server-side authorization for every consequential action requested by the AI model, independently of the model's generated instructions?**

This question identifies a specific security requirement and allows the assessor to request architecture documentation, authorization logic, configuration evidence, and relevant test results.

#### 31.1 Standard Question Structure

Every substantive questionnaire item should contain the following fields.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Question ID | Provides a unique reference for tracking responses and findings. |
| Security domain | Identifies the control area being assessed. |
| Assessment question | States the security requirement clearly. |
| Risk addressed | Identifies the threat or undesirable outcome the control is intended to reduce. |
| Applicability | Defines when the question applies. |
| Expected response | Specifies acceptable response options. |
| Required evidence | Identifies evidence needed to support the answer. |
| Assessment criteria | Defines how the response and evidence will be evaluated. |
| Finding severity | Provides the basis for prioritizing a deficiency. |
| Control owner | Identifies the accountable person or team. |
| Remediation requirement | Defines the expected action if the control is insufficient. |

#### 31.2 Example of a Fully Structured Question

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Question ID | AISQ-CTRL-002 |
| Security domain | AI agent security |
| Assessment question | Are agent tool permissions restricted to explicitly approved actions and resources? |
| Risk addressed | An agent performs unauthorized operations through excessive tool privileges. |
| Applicability | AI systems that invoke tools, APIs, scripts, or business applications. |
| Expected response | Yes, partially implemented, no, not applicable, unknown, or planned. |
| Required evidence | Tool inventory, permission configuration, architecture documentation, and authorization tests. |
| Assessment criteria | The agent cannot perform actions beyond the permissions enforced by the underlying systems. |
| Finding severity | Determined by the available privileges, potential impact, exposure, and effectiveness of compensating controls. |
| Control owner | AI application owner and application security team. |
| Remediation requirement | Restrict tool permissions, implement server-side authorization, and validate enforcement. |

#### 31.3 Assessment Rules

The assessor should apply the following principles:

1. A positive response must be supported by evidence appropriate to the risk.
2. Planned controls must not be treated as implemented controls.
3. Compensating controls must be documented and evaluated.
4. Not-applicable responses require a defensible rationale.
5. Evidence must correspond to the assessed system and environment.
6. Security findings must be linked to identifiable risks.
7. Critical deficiencies must be escalated through the organization's defined process.
8. A supplier's inability to disclose evidence must be recorded as an assurance limitation, not automatically treated as proof that the control is absent.

The result should be a structured assessment that supports risk decisions rather than a simple count of positive responses.

### 32. Model Security and Model Lifecycle Controls

Model security covers the protection, integrity, configuration, evaluation, and controlled use of AI models.

The required controls depend on whether the organization trains models, fine-tunes existing models, hosts open-weight models, or consumes externally managed inference services.

An organization using a managed foundation model may not have direct access to the model's internal weights or training pipeline. Its assessment should therefore focus on available provider assurance, contractual commitments, configuration controls, application-level protections, and testing of the complete service.

#### 32.1 Model Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-MOD-001 — Are all models used by the AI system identified in an authoritative inventory?**

Record the model name, provider, version or release identifier where available, purpose, deployment location, and accountable owner.

**AISQ-MOD-002 — Is the source and provenance of each model documented?**

Identify whether the model is internally developed, externally hosted, obtained from a public repository, or supplied through a managed service.

**AISQ-MOD-003 — Are model versions and updates controlled?**

Explain how changes are identified, assessed, tested, approved, and deployed.

**AISQ-MOD-004 — Are model artifacts protected against unauthorized modification?**

For self-hosted or internally developed models, provide evidence of access controls, artifact integrity protections, and controlled deployment. For managed services, provide the available provider assurance.

**AISQ-MOD-005 — Is access to model files, weights, checkpoints, and configuration restricted?**

Identify the permissions and storage controls protecting relevant artifacts.

**AISQ-MOD-006 — Are model capabilities and known limitations documented?**

Provide available model documentation, evaluation results, intended-use restrictions, and material limitations.

**AISQ-MOD-007 — Are model changes evaluated for security regressions?**

Explain how new versions are assessed for changes in refusal behavior, information disclosure, instruction following, and tool-use behavior where relevant.

**AISQ-MOD-008 — Are model-related vulnerabilities and provider advisories monitored?**

Describe the process for identifying, evaluating, and responding to relevant disclosures.

**AISQ-MOD-009 — Are models obtained from external repositories subject to provenance and integrity checks?**

Identify the approval process and safeguards against malicious or tampered artifacts.

**AISQ-MOD-010 — Can the organization disable or replace a model when a material security issue is identified?**

Explain the available containment, rollback, and provider escalation mechanisms.

#### 32.2 Model Integrity and Provenance

Model integrity concerns whether the model being used is the approved model and whether its artifacts and configuration have been modified without authorization.

Relevant safeguards may include:

- Approved model registries.
- Controlled artifact repositories.
- Integrity verification.
- Restricted model deployment permissions.
- Version-controlled configuration.
- Signed artifacts where supported.
- Controlled promotion between environments.
- Documented model provenance.
- Approval of model updates.
- Monitoring for unauthorized changes.

Not every control applies equally to every deployment model. For a managed service, the organization may depend on provider-controlled processes and must evaluate the evidence available.

#### 32.3 Model Replacement and Rollback

A model update can change behavior even when the application code remains unchanged.

A replacement model may respond differently to instructions, expose information in new ways, or behave differently when invoking tools.

The organization should establish whether a model change triggers:

1. Change identification.
2. Impact assessment.
3. Security and functional evaluation.
4. Approval according to risk.
5. Controlled deployment.
6. Post-deployment monitoring.
7. Rollback or disablement if acceptance criteria are not met.

**Control objective:** The organization must be able to identify which model configuration is in use and respond appropriately when its security or suitability is called into question.

### 33. Prompt Security and Instruction Hierarchy

Prompts are inputs that influence model behavior. They may include system instructions, developer instructions, user requests, retrieved content, tool outputs, and other contextual information.

Prompt security concerns arise when untrusted content influences the model in ways that conflict with the application's intended purpose, data restrictions, or permitted actions.

Prompt wording alone should not be treated as a complete security boundary. Critical permissions must be enforced by the surrounding application and connected systems.

#### 33.1 Prompt Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-PRM-001 — Are system and developer instructions managed through an approved change process?**

Identify the owner, version-control mechanism, approval process, and deployment controls.

**AISQ-PRM-002 — Are untrusted user inputs distinguished from trusted application instructions?**

Explain how the architecture treats untrusted content and limits its influence.

**AISQ-PRM-003 — Has the application been evaluated against direct prompt injection?**

Provide test coverage and findings relevant to the intended use.

**AISQ-PRM-004 — Has the application been evaluated against indirect prompt injection through documents, websites, emails, or retrieved content?**

Identify applicable attack scenarios and the results of testing.

**AISQ-PRM-005 — Are security-critical restrictions enforced outside the model?**

Explain how authorization, transaction limits, and prohibited operations are enforced by application logic or connected services.

**AISQ-PRM-006 — Are prompts evaluated for unintended disclosure of sensitive instructions or information?**

Provide relevant test results and explain how sensitive information is protected.

**AISQ-PRM-007 — Are prompt changes subject to regression testing?**

Identify the test process and the criteria for approving a change.

**AISQ-PRM-008 — Are prompt templates and related configuration protected from unauthorized modification?**

Describe access controls and audit records.

**AISQ-PRM-009 — Are user-visible restrictions and intended-use boundaries communicated appropriately?**

Explain how users are informed about permitted use and relevant limitations.

**AISQ-PRM-010 — Can prompt-related security findings be reported, investigated, and remediated?**

Identify the responsible team and tracking mechanism.

#### 33.2 Prompt Injection Is Not Merely a Keyword-Filtering Problem

Prompt injection occurs when untrusted content influences the model's behavior in an unintended or unauthorized way.

Simple keyword filters may be bypassed through paraphrasing, obfuscation, multilingual content, or instructions embedded in otherwise legitimate documents.

A stronger defense uses multiple layers:

- Minimize the authority granted to model-generated instructions.
- Separate untrusted content from privileged application instructions where feasible.
- Limit the data supplied to the model.
- Restrict tool permissions.
- Enforce authorization at the point of access.
- Validate outputs before consequential use.
- Require approval for sensitive actions.
- Test realistic attack scenarios.
- Monitor relevant security events.

These measures reduce exposure but do not guarantee that every prompt-injection attempt will be detected or prevented.

#### 33.3 Example: Prompt Injection Through Retrieved Content

An employee asks an AI assistant to summarize a supplier document. The document contains malicious instructions intended to make the assistant retrieve confidential internal files.

A secure architecture should not rely solely on telling the model to ignore malicious instructions.

The retrieval service must enforce the employee's access rights, and the application must prevent the model from accessing information or tools that the employee is not authorized to use.

Even if the model follows the malicious instructions, the underlying authorization controls should prevent unauthorized retrieval.

### 34. Input Validation and Untrusted Content Handling

AI applications often accept free-text prompts, uploaded files, web content, emails, database records, images, and tool responses.

These inputs may contain malicious instructions, malformed data, oversized payloads, executable content, or information that should not be processed.

Input validation should be applied at appropriate application boundaries.

#### 34.1 Input Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-INP-001 — Are inputs validated before processing?**

Describe validation of file types, data structures, sizes, encoding, and other relevant constraints.

**AISQ-INP-002 — Are file uploads scanned or inspected according to organizational requirements?**

Identify applicable malware scanning, content validation, and quarantine mechanisms.

**AISQ-INP-003 — Are untrusted external documents treated as untrusted data?**

Explain how content from external sources is handled within prompts and retrieval workflows.

**AISQ-INP-004 — Are oversized or malformed inputs restricted?**

Describe limits and protections against resource exhaustion.

**AISQ-INP-005 — Are dangerous or unsupported input formats rejected or processed through controlled mechanisms?**

Identify the applicable policy and technical enforcement.

**AISQ-INP-006 — Are external content sources authenticated or validated where appropriate?**

Explain how source identity, integrity, and trust are assessed.

**AISQ-INP-007 — Are input-handling controls tested against malicious or unexpected content?**

Provide relevant test results.

**AISQ-INP-008 — Are input validation failures logged and handled safely?**

Explain error handling and monitoring.

#### 34.2 Input Validation Limitations

Syntactically valid input is not necessarily safe input.

A document may pass malware scanning while containing instructions intended to manipulate an AI model. A valid API request may ask an agent to perform an unauthorized operation.

Input validation must therefore be combined with AI-specific threat modeling, authorization, output validation, and execution controls.

### 35. Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Knowledge-Source Security

Retrieval-augmented generation systems obtain information from external or internal knowledge sources and supply selected content to a model as context.

RAG can improve the relevance of responses, but it creates security dependencies involving source permissions, indexing, retrieval logic, data integrity, and context construction.

A retrieval system must preserve the access restrictions associated with the underlying information.

#### 35.1 RAG Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-RAG-001 — Are all knowledge sources identified and approved?**

Maintain an inventory of document repositories, databases, websites, APIs, and other retrieval sources.

**AISQ-RAG-002 — Are source permissions preserved during retrieval?**

Explain how the system prevents unauthorized documents from being returned to a user.

**AISQ-RAG-003 — Are access restrictions enforced before retrieved content is supplied to the model?**

Provide architecture documentation and authorization test results.

**AISQ-RAG-004 — Are indexing and embedding pipelines protected against unauthorized modification?**

Describe access controls, pipeline security, and integrity safeguards.

**AISQ-RAG-005 — Are changes to knowledge sources tracked?**

Explain how document additions, removals, permission changes, and source updates are processed.

**AISQ-RAG-006 — Are stale or revoked documents removed from retrieval systems according to defined requirements?**

Identify synchronization, deletion, and validation mechanisms.

**AISQ-RAG-007 — Are retrieved sources identified or traceable where appropriate?**

Explain how the application supports source attribution and investigation.

**AISQ-RAG-008 — Are retrieved documents evaluated for prompt-injection and data-poisoning risks?**

Describe the applicable controls and their limitations.

**AISQ-RAG-009 — Are vector databases and embedding stores protected against unauthorized access?**

Provide relevant access-control and data-protection evidence.

**AISQ-RAG-010 — Are retrieval results tested for cross-user and cross-tenant data leakage?**

Provide testing evidence relevant to the deployment.

#### 35.2 Document-Level Authorization

Suppose a knowledge repository contains documents for three departments:

- Human Resources.
- Finance.
- Legal.

An employee from Human Resources should not receive confidential Finance or Legal documents simply because the AI application uses a shared vector database.

Authorization must be enforced in the retrieval process or through another robust design that prevents unauthorized content from reaching the model.

Filtering the final response after the model has already received restricted context may be insufficient because the information has already crossed the intended security boundary.

#### 35.3 RAG Data Lifecycle

The assessment should consider:

1. Source approval.
2. Data ingestion.
3. Document validation.
4. Chunking and transformation.
5. Embedding generation.
6. Index storage.
7. Retrieval authorization.
8. Context assembly.
9. Model inference.
10. Output generation.
11. Source updates and revocation.
12. Retention and deletion.

Security controls should cover the complete retrieval pipeline rather than only the vector database.

### 36. AI Agent Tool Security and Action Boundaries

AI agents may use tools to search systems, read documents, update records, execute code, send messages, or trigger business workflows.

The principal security concern is that the agent may combine model-generated plans with privileges that allow consequential actions.

A secure implementation must constrain what the agent can do independently of what the model proposes.

#### 36.1 Agent Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-AGT-001 — Is there an inventory of all tools and actions available to the agent?**

Identify each tool, its purpose, permissions, owner, and permitted operations.

**AISQ-AGT-002 — Are tool permissions restricted to the minimum required for the approved use case?**

Provide permission definitions and validation evidence.

**AISQ-AGT-003 — Are consequential actions authorized by the underlying application or service?**

Explain how server-side authorization is enforced.

**AISQ-AGT-004 — Are write, delete, financial, administrative, and external communication actions subject to appropriate restrictions?**

Identify approval requirements and technical limits.

**AISQ-AGT-005 — Can an agent invoke tools that were not explicitly approved?**

Describe how tool registration, discovery, and invocation are controlled.

**AISQ-AGT-006 — Are tool inputs validated before execution?**

Explain parameter validation, operation restrictions, and error handling.

**AISQ-AGT-007 — Are agent actions logged with sufficient information for investigation?**

Identify the user, agent, tool, authorization result, and action outcome where available.

**AISQ-AGT-008 — Can an agent be stopped or have individual tools disabled quickly?**

Describe emergency containment mechanisms.

**AISQ-AGT-009 — Are high-impact actions subject to human approval or other independent safeguards?**

Identify the actions requiring approval and how the requirement is enforced.

**AISQ-AGT-010 — Are agent workflows tested for unauthorized actions and privilege escalation?**

Provide relevant testing evidence.

#### 36.2 Agent Permission Matrix

A practical assessment should identify the agent's permitted actions explicitly.

| Tool or integration | Read | Create | Modify | Delete | External action | Required safeguard |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Internal knowledge search | Yes | No | No | No | No | Document-level authorization. |
| CRM customer lookup | Yes | No | No | No | No | Customer-record access restrictions. |
| CRM case creation | Yes | Yes | Limited | No | No | Input validation and duplicate prevention. |
| Customer email service | Limited | Yes | No | No | Yes | Recipient validation and approval where required. |
| Billing adjustment API | Limited | Limited | Yes | No | Yes | Transaction limits and independent authorization. |
| Administrative configuration API | Restricted | Limited | Yes | Limited | No | Privileged access and explicit approval. |

The table is illustrative. Actual permissions must reflect the system's approved business purpose.

#### 36.3 Human Approval Is Not a Substitute for Technical Authorization

Human approval may be appropriate for consequential actions, but it should not be the only protection against unauthorized access.

The application must still verify that the action is permitted, the target resource is authorized, and the requested operation satisfies applicable rules.

For example, an approval workflow should not allow an AI agent to bypass a user's access restrictions simply because someone approved a proposed action without understanding its technical consequences.

### 37. Output Validation and Safe Consumption of AI Results

AI-generated output may be inaccurate, maliciously influenced, malformed, or unsuitable for the operation that consumes it.

The risks increase when output is passed directly to another system, used to make a decision, rendered as active content, or interpreted as an instruction.

Output validation should be proportionate to the consequences of the downstream operation.

#### 37.1 Output Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-OUT-001 — Are AI outputs validated before being used by downstream applications?**

Identify the validation mechanisms and the output types covered.

**AISQ-OUT-002 — Are generated values checked against expected schemas and permitted ranges?**

Provide the applicable validation rules.

**AISQ-OUT-003 — Is generated content prevented from being executed as code or interpreted as active content without appropriate safeguards?**

Explain the relevant encoding, sandboxing, or execution controls.

**AISQ-OUT-004 — Are generated instructions prevented from bypassing application authorization?**

Describe how permissions are enforced independently of model output.

**AISQ-OUT-005 — Are high-impact outputs reviewed by an authorized person or validated through independent controls?**

Identify the affected decisions or actions.

**AISQ-OUT-006 — Are outputs checked for sensitive information disclosure where appropriate?**

Explain the detection and prevention mechanisms and their limitations.

**AISQ-OUT-007 — Are generated recommendations distinguished from verified facts or approved decisions where required?**

Describe how the interface communicates uncertainty and limitations.

**AISQ-OUT-008 — Are output-handling controls tested against malicious and malformed responses?**

Provide relevant test results.

#### 37.2 Structured Output Is Not Automatically Safe

A model may produce valid JSON that requests an unauthorized transaction. A generated SQL statement may be syntactically correct but dangerous. A valid HTML response may contain content that creates a cross-site scripting risk if rendered unsafely.

Schema validation confirms that output follows an expected structure. It does not necessarily confirm that the requested action is authorized or safe.

For consequential operations, the application should validate the action's meaning, target, parameters, permissions, and applicable business rules.

### 38. AI Application Security and Secure Integration

AI systems inherit conventional application security risks while introducing additional complexity through model interfaces, orchestration layers, and generated outputs.

The questionnaire should assess the security of the complete application rather than focusing exclusively on the model.

#### 38.1 Application Security Questionnaire

**AISQ-APP-001 — Is the AI application developed and maintained under an approved secure development process?**

Identify the applicable standards and evidence.

**AISQ-APP-002 — Are application components and external dependencies inventoried?**

Provide the dependency inventory or software bill of materials where appropriate.

**AISQ-APP-003 — Are security testing and code review performed before production release?**

Describe the process and acceptance criteria.

**AISQ-APP-004 — Are application secrets protected from unauthorized disclosure?**

Identify the relevant secrets-management controls.

**AISQ-APP-005 — Are errors handled without exposing sensitive implementation details?**

Provide the relevant design or testing evidence.

**AISQ-APP-006 — Are application interfaces protected against relevant injection and authorization vulnerabilities?**

Identify testing scope and results.

**AISQ-APP-007 — Are external integrations restricted and reviewed?**

Explain how integrations are approved, authenticated, and monitored.

**AISQ-APP-008 — Are application changes traceable to approved change records?**

Provide relevant repository or release-management evidence.

**AISQ-APP-009 — Are production configurations protected against unauthorized modification?**

Describe configuration management and privileged access controls.

**AISQ-APP-010 — Are application vulnerabilities tracked through remediation and verified closure?**

Identify the vulnerability management process.

#### 38.2 Integration Risk

The AI model may be only one component in a larger business workflow.

For example, a customer-service assistant might connect to a CRM, billing platform, identity verification service, and email system.

Each integration introduces its own security requirements:

- Authentication and authorization.
- Data minimization.
- Input and output validation.
- Error handling.
- Transaction integrity.
- Rate limits.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Failure handling.
- Change management.

The questionnaire should identify which system is authoritative for each security decision.

A model should not be permitted to override a billing system's transaction rules or an identity service's authorization decision simply because its output appears reasonable.

### 39. Data Poisoning and Training Pipeline Security

Data poisoning occurs when malicious or inappropriate data influences an AI model or related data-processing pipeline in a way that compromises its intended behavior.

The risk is especially relevant when organizations train or fine-tune models, continuously ingest data, or rely on knowledge repositories that can be modified by untrusted parties.

Not every AI application has a training pipeline under the customer's control. The questionnaire should distinguish training-data security from retrieval-source security and identify the controls applicable to each.

#### 39.1 Training Pipeline Questionnaire

**AISQ-TRN-001 — Does the organization train or fine-tune models using its own data?**

Identify the relevant pipelines, datasets, owners, and environments.

**AISQ-TRN-002 — Are training and fine-tuning datasets sourced through approved processes?**

Describe source validation, provenance, and authorization.

**AISQ-TRN-003 — Are datasets protected against unauthorized modification?**

Identify access restrictions, version control, and integrity checks.

**AISQ-TRN-004 — Are dataset changes traceable?**

**AISQ-TRN-005 — Are training and evaluation datasets separated where appropriate?**

**AISQ-TRN-006 — Are training pipelines protected against unauthorized execution or modification?**

Provide relevant pipeline security and access-control evidence.

**AISQ-TRN-007 — Are model outputs evaluated for unexpected behavior following material dataset changes?**

Identify relevant validation and release criteria.

**AISQ-TRN-008 — Are model artifacts protected after training?**

Describe artifact storage, access control, integrity protection, and deployment procedures.

**AISQ-TRN-009 — Are third-party datasets subject to security and licensing review where applicable?**

Identify the approval and documentation process.

**AISQ-TRN-010 — Are suspected data-poisoning incidents investigated through a defined process?**

#### 39.2 Training Data Versus RAG Knowledge Sources

These two areas must not be confused.

| Dimension | Training or fine-tuning data | RAG knowledge source |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Influences model parameters during training or adaptation. | Supplies contextual information during inference. |
| Typical security concern | Poisoned or unauthorized data alters model behavior. | Malicious or unauthorized content influences retrieved context. |
| Common control focus | Dataset provenance, integrity, access control, and evaluation. | Source approval, retrieval authorization, indexing integrity, and access restrictions. |
| Typical remediation | Investigate and correct the dataset or training process; evaluate whether retraining is required. | Remove or correct affected content, update the index, and validate retrieval behavior. |

A single AI system may require both assessments.

### 40. Model Evaluation and Security Testing

AI security testing should assess the behavior of the complete system under realistic operating conditions.

Traditional application security testing remains necessary, but it may not adequately assess prompt injection, unauthorized retrieval, unsafe agent behavior, or model-specific information disclosure.

**AISQ-TST-006 — Are generated outputs tested before they are consumed by downstream applications?**

**AISQ-TST-007 — Are tests repeated after material model, prompt, architecture, or permission changes?**

**AISQ-TST-009 — Are test environments and datasets controlled to prevent unintended exposure?**

**AISQ-TST-010 — Are unresolved high-risk findings subject to explicit release decisions?**

#### 40.2 Testing Dimensions

| Test dimension | Example |
|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Attempt to retrieve information that the current user is not authorized to access. |
| Integrity | Attempt to manipulate retrieved content or cause unauthorized changes. |
| Availability | Test rate limits and resilience against excessive requests. |
| Authorization | Attempt to invoke a tool beyond the user's permissions. |
| Prompt injection | Test direct and indirect instruction manipulation. |
| Output handling | Test malicious or malformed output passed to downstream components. |
| Data protection | Examine whether sensitive information appears in logs or unauthorized responses. |
| Change regression | Compare security behavior before and after a model or prompt update. |
| Recovery | Verify that unsafe tools or compromised integrations can be disabled. |
| Monitoring | Confirm that defined security events generate actionable alerts. |

#### 40.3 Testing Limitations

Passing a set of AI security tests does not prove that the system is immune to attack.

Model behavior may vary with context, configuration, input, and provider updates. Testing should therefore be treated as one component of a broader assurance program.

The assessment should document test scope, limitations, findings, and the conditions under which the results remain valid.

- Controlled change procedures.
- Periodic comparison against the baseline.
- Detection of unauthorized changes.
- Investigation and remediation.
- Reassessment when changes affect the risk profile.

For managed AI platforms, some configuration controls may be operated by the provider. The organization should identify those boundaries and evaluate the available assurance.

#### 44.2 Egress Restrictions as a Defense Layer

Consider an AI agent that processes untrusted documents and has access to an external communication tool.

If the agent is manipulated into sending confidential information to an unauthorized destination, egress controls may reduce the available attack path.

Egress controls do not replace application-level authorization or tool restrictions. They provide an additional containment layer.

The appropriate design may include approved destination lists, restricted service identities, controlled API gateways, network monitoring, and explicit approval for sensitive external transfers.

Privacy and security overlap, but they are not identical.

An AI system may be technically secure while processing personal data for an inappropriate purpose. Conversely, a lawful processing purpose does not eliminate the need for strong technical safeguards.

The questionnaire should identify personal-data risks and route applicable legal or privacy questions to the appropriate specialists.

**AISQ-PRI-005 — Are personal data transfers to external providers documented and assessed?**

**AISQ-PRI-006 — Are sensitive data disclosures through generated outputs assessed?**

Describe relevant testing and safeguards.

**AISQ-PRI-007 — Are data-subject rights and applicable deletion requirements considered in the system design?**

Explain the relevant process and technical limitations.

**AISQ-PRI-008 — Are privacy-related incidents incorporated into incident response and escalation procedures?**

Identify the responsible teams and notification assessment process.

#### 45.2 Privacy Questions Requiring Specialist Review

The questionnaire should not attempt to resolve every legal issue through a simple yes-or-no answer.

Depending on the system, specialist review may be required to determine:

- Whether a lawful basis exists for processing.
- Whether a data protection impact assessment is required.
- Whether special-category data is involved.
- Whether international transfer restrictions apply.
- Whether automated decision-making rules are relevant.
- Whether transparency or additional rights mechanisms are required.
- Whether the provider's processing terms are appropriate.

The security assessor should record the relevant dependency and ensure that unresolved privacy issues are escalated.

### 46. AI Output Reliability and Decision Safeguards

Security is not limited to preventing unauthorized access. An AI system may create material operational risk when users rely on inaccurate, fabricated, outdated, or misleading outputs.

The questionnaire should assess how the system's intended purpose determines the level of verification required.

A low-impact drafting assistant may permit users to review and correct its output. A system influencing financial, employment, healthcare, or other consequential decisions may require substantially stronger validation and human oversight.

#### 46.1 Reliability and Decision Questions

Explain the required review or independent validation mechanism.

**AISQ-REL-005 — Can users challenge, correct, or escalate an unreliable output?**

Describe the available process.

**AISQ-REL-006 — Are material changes in output quality or behavior monitored?**

Identify relevant metrics, thresholds, and escalation mechanisms.

**AISQ-REL-007 — Are human reviewers competent and authorized to perform the required review?**

Explain the training, responsibilities, and decision authority.

**AISQ-REL-008 — Are failures and near misses recorded and analyzed?**

Identify the reporting and corrective-action process.

#### 46.2 Human Oversight Must Be Meaningful

A human approval step is not effective merely because a person clicks a button.

The reviewer should have sufficient information, time, authority, and competence to identify unacceptable outcomes and intervene.

For high-impact decisions, the organization should consider whether reviewers can:

- Understand the relevant limitations.
- Examine the underlying evidence.
- Reject the recommendation.
- Request additional analysis.
- Escalate unusual cases.
- Override an automated recommendation where appropriate.
- Record the basis for consequential decisions.

The required oversight should reflect the actual risks rather than being treated as a universal control that makes every AI use acceptable.

5. Logs and monitoring are functioning.
6. Data integrity has been checked.
7. The system meets the applicable conditions for returning to service.

|---|---|---|

### 51. Security Findings and Remediation Tracking

The questionnaire should produce findings that can be assigned, prioritized, remediated, and verified.

A response such as "Partially implemented" is not a sufficient remediation record. The organization must understand the gap, the risk it creates, and the action needed to address it.

#### 51.1 Finding Record Structure

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | Unique reference. |
| Questionnaire ID | Link to the assessment. |
| Question ID | Link to the control requirement. |
| Finding title | Concise description of the deficiency. |
| Observation | What the assessor found. |
| Evidence reference | Supporting evidence or documented limitation. |
| Risk scenario | Potential consequence of the deficiency. |
| Severity | Priority based on risk and organizational criteria. |
| Affected component | AI system, model, data source, API, or other dependency. |
| Remediation owner | Person accountable for the corrective action. |
| Remediation plan | Actions required to address the finding. |
| Target date | Agreed completion deadline. |
| Interim control | Temporary safeguard, if applicable. |
| Status | Open, in progress, blocked, pending validation, or closed. |
| Closure evidence | Evidence demonstrating that the corrective action is complete. |
| Validation result | Independent or designated confirmation that the issue has been addressed. |

#### 51.2 Example Finding

**Finding ID:** AISQ-FND-014

**Title:** AI retrieval does not enforce document-level authorization.

**Observation:** The AI assistant retrieves documents from a shared knowledge index, but the assessment could not establish that each retrieved document is filtered according to the requesting user's permissions.

**Risk scenario:** A user may obtain confidential information through AI-generated responses despite lacking direct access to the underlying documents.

**Severity:** To be determined using the organization's risk methodology, considering the sensitivity of the documents, the exposure, the likelihood of exploitation, and existing safeguards.

**Required remediation:** Implement and validate authorization enforcement before protected content is supplied to the model.

**Interim safeguards:** Consider restricting access to the affected knowledge source or disabling the affected retrieval capability until adequate controls are established.

**Closure evidence:** Architecture update, access-control configuration, and test results demonstrating that unauthorized users cannot retrieve restricted documents.

The finding should remain open until the corrective action has been validated according to the organization's closure criteria.

### 52. Compensating Controls and Alternative Safeguards

**AISQ-CMP-004 — Does the compensating control address the same threat or reduce the risk through another defensible mechanism?**

**AISQ-CMP-005 — Has the exception or alternative approach been approved by an authorized risk owner?**

Provide the approval record.

**AISQ-CMP-006 — Is the compensating control monitored and periodically reviewed?**

#### 52.2 Example

Suppose a managed AI provider does not permit the customer to configure certain internal model-serving controls.

Possible compensating measures may include restricting sensitive data sent to the provider, applying application-level authorization, reducing the available capabilities, using an approved private connectivity option where supported, or selecting a different provider.

The assessor must determine whether these measures sufficiently reduce the actual risk.

A contractual statement alone may not compensate for an architecture that permits unauthorized disclosure of highly sensitive information.

### 58. Assessment Prioritization and Risk-Based Depth

Not every AI system requires the same assessment depth.

The organization should determine the appropriate questionnaire scope according to the system's intended use, data sensitivity, autonomy, exposure, criticality, and potential consequences.

A proportional approach improves efficiency without allowing high-risk systems to avoid necessary scrutiny.

#### 58.1 Assessment Dimensions

| Dimension | Questions to consider |
|---|---|
| Data sensitivity | Does the system process confidential, personal, regulated, or authentication information? |
| Autonomy | Can the system execute actions without human approval? |
| Business criticality | Would a failure materially affect essential operations? |
| External exposure | Is the system accessible to customers, partners, or the public? |
| Privilege level | Can it access sensitive records or perform privileged operations? |
| Decision impact | Can its outputs materially affect individuals or consequential business decisions? |
| Provider dependency | Does the organization depend on an external provider whose controls cannot be directly inspected? |
| Integration complexity | How many systems and trust boundaries are involved? |
| Uncertainty | Are the model's behavior, limitations, or provider arrangements insufficiently understood? |
| Change frequency | How frequently do models, prompts, tools, or data sources change? |

#### 58.2 Example Assessment Tiers

The following tiers are an illustrative internal model, not a mandatory classification imposed by a universal standard.

| Tier | Typical characteristics | Assessment approach |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Lower exposure | Limited functionality, low-sensitivity data, no consequential autonomous actions. | Core questionnaire and proportionate evidence review. |
| Tier 2 — Moderate exposure | Internal business data, enterprise integrations, or meaningful operational dependencies. | Expanded technical questionnaire, architecture review, and targeted testing. |
| Tier 3 — High exposure | Sensitive information, external users, extensive integrations, or consequential decisions. | Detailed questionnaire, stronger evidence requirements, independent review, and targeted security testing. |
| Tier 4 — Critical exposure | Broad privileged access, high-impact autonomous actions, or potentially severe consequences from failure. | Enhanced assurance, strict release criteria, independent validation, explicit risk authorization, and continuous monitoring. |

An organization should define its own criteria and map them to the applicable risk methodology.

A high-risk designation does not necessarily mean that the system must be rejected. It means that the system requires appropriate safeguards, assurance, and authorized decision-making.

### 59. Part 2 Core Principle — Every Security Claim Must Lead to an Assessable Control

Part 2 establishes the technical and operational dimensions of the AI Security Questionnaire.

The assessment must move beyond general statements and identify the specific safeguards that protect the model, application, data, integrations, and business operations.

A well-designed questionnaire examines whether controls are appropriate for the actual architecture and whether their implementation is supported by evidence.

The key principles are:

1. **Model security:** Identify model provenance, configuration, versioning, and integrity requirements.
2. **Prompt security:** Treat untrusted input as a potential threat and enforce critical restrictions outside the model.
3. **Retrieval security:** Preserve source permissions and prevent unauthorized information from entering model context.
4. **Agent security:** Restrict tool permissions and enforce authorization at the point of action.
5. **Application security:** Secure the complete application and its integrations, not just the AI model.
6. **Data protection:** Assess the entire information lifecycle, including prompts, outputs, retrieval stores, logs, and provider processing.
7. **Testing and vulnerability management:** Validate relevant controls and track deficiencies through verified remediation.
8. **Operational resilience:** Ensure that unsafe capabilities can be contained and that recovery preserves security.
9. **Change management:** Reassess material changes that can alter the system's risk profile.
10. **Traceability:** Connect questionnaire responses to evidence, findings, risk treatment, and approval decisions.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| Prompt instruction | Influences model behavior but does not independently enforce authorization. |
| Input validation | Checks whether input satisfies defined constraints; it does not eliminate all prompt-injection risks. |
| Retrieval authorization | Determines which information may be retrieved for a user or service. |
| Output validation | Checks generated content before downstream use; it does not automatically establish that an action is authorized. |
| Human approval | Adds oversight but must be supported by meaningful review and technical safeguards. |
| Security testing | Provides evidence about behavior under defined test conditions; it does not prove universal security. |
| Compensating control | Reduces a defined risk through an alternative safeguard; its adequacy must be assessed. |
| Security exception | Documents an authorized deviation; it does not automatically eliminate risk or waive legal obligations. |
| Continuous monitoring | Detects defined events or conditions; it is useful only when linked to response and escalation. |
| Security assurance | A conclusion based on scope, evidence, testing, limitations, and risk context—not merely a positive questionnaire response. |

### 60. AI Security Questionnaire Governance and Operating Model

Part 1 established the questionnaire's foundations, including scope, system identification, respondent instructions, and evidence expectations. Part 2 translated those foundations into technical and operational security controls.

Part 3 establishes how the questionnaire operates within the organization's Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) framework.

A questionnaire is not effective simply because security questions have been answered. The organization must determine who is accountable for the responses, who validates the evidence, how deficiencies are evaluated, who can approve residual risk, and how the results influence procurement, development, deployment, and ongoing operations.

The questionnaire should function as a formal assurance mechanism within the AI governance operating model.

#### 60.1 Governance Objectives

The AI Security Questionnaire governance model should establish:

- Clear ownership of the assessment process.
- Defined responsibilities for respondents and reviewers.
- Consistent evaluation criteria.
- Appropriate independence between control implementation and assurance.
- Traceability between responses, evidence, findings, and risks.
- Defined escalation thresholds.
- Authorized risk acceptance.
- Controlled exceptions and remediation.
- Management reporting.
- Periodic reassessment.

The objective is not to centralize every security decision within one team. It is to ensure that decisions are made by the appropriate accountable individuals and that security concerns cannot be bypassed simply because the questionnaire has been completed.

### 62. Assessment Independence and the Three Lines Model

The questionnaire process should distinguish between implementing controls, providing risk oversight, and delivering independent assurance.

A useful reference is the Three Lines Model, which separates operational management responsibilities from risk and compliance oversight and independent internal audit.

The model should be adapted to the organization's structure rather than applied as a rigid organizational chart.

#### 62.1 First Line — Operational Management

The first line includes the teams that develop, operate, procure, or use the AI system.

Their responsibilities include:

- Providing accurate questionnaire responses.
- Implementing security controls.
- Maintaining technical documentation.
- Producing supporting evidence.
- Managing remediation actions.
- Reporting incidents and control failures.
- Keeping the assessment current.

The first line remains accountable for managing the risks arising from its activities.

#### 62.2 Second Line — Risk, Security Governance, and Compliance Oversight

The second line establishes standards, provides oversight, challenges risk assessments, and evaluates whether the control framework is operating as intended.

Responsibilities may include:

- Defining questionnaire methodology.
- Establishing scoring criteria.
- Reviewing high-risk assessments.
- Challenging unsupported responses.
- Evaluating exceptions.
- Monitoring remediation commitments.
- Escalating significant deficiencies.
- Reporting portfolio-level risk trends.

Depending on the organization, some security testing may be performed by specialist teams that sit within operational security or independent assurance functions. Their placement should not compromise the credibility of the review.

#### 62.3 Third Line — Independent Internal Audit

Internal audit evaluates governance, risk management, and control effectiveness independently within its approved mandate.

It may examine whether:

- The questionnaire process is appropriately designed.
- Responses are supported by evidence.
- Findings are recorded accurately.
- Risk acceptance follows delegated authority.
- Exceptions are monitored.
- Remediation is validated.
- Governance reporting is reliable.

Internal audit should not become the routine owner of management's risk decisions or the primary implementer of the controls it later audits.

#### 62.4 Questionnaire Independence Questions

**AISQ-GOV-007 — Is the assessment reviewed independently of the teams responsible for implementing the relevant controls when the risk warrants it?**

**AISQ-GOV-010 — Are conflicts of interest identified and managed during assessment and approval?**

The required degree of independence should be proportionate to the system's risk, complexity, and potential consequences.

### 63. Questionnaire Scope, Applicability, and Exclusions Governance

An organization may maintain a standard questionnaire containing hundreds of questions. Applying every question to every AI system can create unnecessary work, while allowing teams to exclude controls without justification can leave material risks unexamined.

The governance process should establish how applicability is determined and who can approve exclusions.

#### 63.1 Applicability Decision Process

For each question, the assessment should determine whether it is:

1. Applicable and implemented.
2. Applicable but partially implemented.
3. Applicable but not implemented.
4. Applicable, but implementation evidence is unavailable.
5. Not applicable with a documented rationale.
6. Planned but not yet implemented.
7. Unable to be determined because information is insufficient.

These categories should be distinct. In particular, **unknown is not equivalent to not applicable**, and a planned control must not be treated as an existing safeguard.

#### 63.2 Applicability Questions

**AISQ-SCP-001 — Is the applicability of each material control determined using documented criteria?**

**AISQ-SCP-002 — Are exclusions supported by a system-specific rationale?**

**AISQ-SCP-003 — Are exclusions reviewed by an authorized assessor when they affect material risks?**

**AISQ-SCP-004 — Are unresolved applicability questions escalated rather than automatically excluded?**

**AISQ-SCP-005 — Are previously approved exclusions reconsidered after material system changes?**

#### 63.3 Example

A questionnaire asks whether training datasets are protected against unauthorized modification.

An organization uses a managed foundation model and does not train or fine-tune the model itself.

The training-pipeline question may be classified as not applicable to the customer's direct operations. However, the organization should still evaluate applicable provider assurance and other risks relating to its own data, application, retrieval sources, and model configuration.

The exclusion must not be interpreted as evidence that the complete AI service has no model-related risk.

### 65. AI Security Questionnaire Scoring Methodology

A scoring methodology helps the organization compare assessments, identify control deficiencies, and prioritize remediation.

However, scoring must not obscure material risks. A high overall score can coexist with a single critical weakness that makes the system unacceptable for its intended purpose.

The organization should use scoring as a decision-support mechanism, not as a substitute for risk analysis.

#### 65.1 Response Scoring

One possible response scale is shown below.

| Response | Illustrative score | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Fully implemented and evidenced | 4 | The control is implemented and adequate evidence supports its operation. |
| Implemented with minor gaps | 3 | The control is substantially implemented, but limited deficiencies remain. |
| Partially implemented | 2 | Some required safeguards exist, but material gaps remain. |
| Not implemented | 1 | The required control is absent or materially ineffective. |
| Unknown or insufficient evidence | Not scored as compliant | Assurance is insufficient to conclude that the control is effective. |
| Not applicable | Excluded from denominator only after approval | The control is demonstrably outside the assessed scope. |
| Planned | Not scored as implemented | The control has not yet been demonstrated to be operational. |

This scale is illustrative. An organization may choose a different scale, including a simpler compliant/partially compliant/non-compliant approach.

The key requirement is consistent interpretation and clear separation between implementation status and assurance confidence.

#### 65.2 Weighted Control Score

Where a numerical score is useful, the organization may assign weights to questions according to their importance.

A simplified calculation is:

\[
\text{Control Coverage Score} =
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i s_i}
{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i s_{\max}}
\times 100
\]

Where:

- \(w_i\) = the weight assigned to control \(i\).
- \(s_i\) = the achieved score for control \(i\).
- \(s_{\max}\) = the maximum score for that control.
- \(n\) = the number of applicable controls included in the calculation.

The organization must define how unknown responses, missing evidence, and partially applicable controls affect the calculation. Missing information must not silently improve the score.

#### 65.3 Example

Suppose an assessment contains three applicable controls:

| Control | Weight | Maximum score | Achieved score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Authentication and access control | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| Prompt-injection testing | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| Logging and monitoring | 1 | 4 | 3 |

The weighted numerator is:

\[
(3 \times 4) + (2 \times 2) + (1 \times 3) = 19
\]

The maximum weighted score is:

\[
(3 \times 4) + (2 \times 4) + (1 \times 4) = 24
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{Control Coverage Score} = \frac{19}{24} \times 100 \approx 79.2\%
\]

This result summarizes the selected controls but does not establish that the AI system is safe or acceptable.

If the prompt-injection weakness allows an agent to perform unauthorized financial transactions, the system may require restriction regardless of its aggregate score.

#### 65.4 Scoring Governance Questions

**AISQ-SCR-001 — Is the scoring methodology formally documented and approved?**

**AISQ-SCR-002 — Are control weights based on defined risk criteria?**

**AISQ-SCR-003 — Are missing or unsupported responses prevented from being treated as compliant?**

**AISQ-SCR-004 — Are critical control failures escalated independently of the aggregate score?**

**AISQ-SCR-005 — Is the scoring model periodically reviewed for consistency and effectiveness?**

### 66. Control Effectiveness and Evidence Confidence

A control can exist on paper without operating effectively. Conversely, a control may be operating but supported by incomplete or outdated evidence.

The questionnaire should distinguish control implementation from confidence in the assessment conclusion.

#### 66.1 Two-Dimensional Evaluation

A practical approach is to evaluate:

1. **Control effectiveness:** How well does the safeguard address the identified risk?
2. **Evidence confidence:** How strong is the evidence supporting that conclusion?

| Control effectiveness | Evidence confidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Effective | High | Evidence supports the conclusion that the control is implemented and operating as intended. |
| Effective | Low | The control may be adequate, but additional evidence is required to substantiate the conclusion. |
| Ineffective | High | Evidence clearly demonstrates a control deficiency. |
| Ineffective | Low | The control appears deficient, but further investigation is required to establish the precise condition and impact. |
| Unknown | Low | The available information is insufficient for a defensible conclusion. |

The organization may use different labels, but the distinction is important.

#### 66.2 Evidence Confidence Questions

**AISQ-EVC-001 — Is the evidence directly relevant to the control being assessed?**

**AISQ-EVC-002 — Does the evidence cover the applicable environment and scope?**

**AISQ-EVC-003 — Is the evidence current enough to support the assessment conclusion?**

**AISQ-EVC-004 — Has the evidence been corroborated where the risk warrants additional verification?**

**AISQ-EVC-005 — Are material limitations in evidence coverage documented?**

#### 66.3 Examples of Evidence

| Evidence type | Potential value | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Policy or standard | Demonstrates defined requirements and intent. | Does not prove implementation. |
| Architecture diagram | Demonstrates documented design and trust boundaries. | May not reflect the deployed configuration. |
| Configuration export | Demonstrates selected technical settings. | May not prove effectiveness across all environments. |
| Test report | Demonstrates behavior under defined conditions. | Does not establish protection against every attack. |
| Independent assurance report | Provides evidence about controls within the report's scope. | May exclude the specific service, period, or control being assessed. |
| Operational log | Demonstrates recorded events. | May be incomplete or fail to show that alerts were acted upon. |
| Management attestation | Establishes accountability for a statement. | Does not replace independent verification where required. |

### 67. AI Security Risk Rating and Finding Severity

Questionnaire findings should be prioritized according to risk rather than treated as equally important administrative gaps.

A finding's severity should reflect the potential consequences of exploitation or failure, the exposure of the affected system, the feasibility of the attack, and the effectiveness of existing safeguards.

The organization's approved risk methodology should determine the final rating.

#### 67.1 Risk Evaluation Dimensions

| Dimension | Assessment consideration |
|---|---|
| Impact | Potential harm to confidentiality, integrity, availability, individuals, operations, or regulatory obligations. |
| Likelihood | Plausibility of the threat scenario under the assessed conditions. |
| Exposure | Accessibility of the affected component or function. |
| Privilege | Capabilities available to an attacker or compromised AI component. |
| Data sensitivity | Sensitivity and quantity of information potentially affected. |
| Exploitability | Practical difficulty of exploiting the weakness. |
| Control strength | Effectiveness of preventive, detective, and corrective safeguards. |
| Blast radius | Number of users, systems, records, or business processes potentially affected. |
| Recoverability | Ability to contain the event and restore a secure state. |
| Uncertainty | Degree to which missing information limits confidence in the assessment. |

#### 67.2 Example Finding Prioritization

Consider two findings:

**Finding A:** An internal AI assistant has a minor documentation gap in a low-impact function. The relevant technical control is independently verified.

**Finding B:** An AI agent has access to a privileged billing API, and the organization cannot demonstrate that the API enforces authorization independently of the agent's instructions.

Finding B is likely to require substantially greater attention because the weakness may permit unauthorized consequential actions.

The final rating should still follow the organization's defined methodology and the evidence available.

#### 67.3 Severity Questions

**AISQ-RAT-001 — Is each material finding evaluated using an approved risk-rating methodology?**

**AISQ-RAT-002 — Does the severity reflect the actual threat scenario rather than the questionnaire score alone?**

**AISQ-RAT-003 — Are potentially critical weaknesses escalated promptly?**

**AISQ-RAT-004 — Are uncertainties and evidence limitations considered in the decision?**

**AISQ-RAT-005 — Are finding ratings reviewed when new evidence or changed conditions materially affect the risk?**

### 68. Risk Acceptance and Decision Authority

The AI Security Questionnaire may identify risks that cannot be eliminated immediately. The organization must determine whether those risks can be treated, whether the system should be restricted, or whether the residual risk can be accepted by an authorized decision-maker.

Risk acceptance is a governance decision, not an automatic outcome of questionnaire completion.

#### 68.1 Risk Acceptance Requirements

A defensible risk acceptance decision should identify:

- The risk scenario.
- The affected AI system and business process.
- The inherent and residual risk ratings.
- The controls currently in place.
- The known control deficiencies.
- The business justification.
- The available treatment options.
- The potential consequences of acceptance.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approval authority.
- Any conditions or restrictions.
- The review or expiry date where appropriate.

#### 68.2 Risk Acceptance Questions

**AISQ-RA-001 — Are residual risks documented in the organization's risk register or approved equivalent?**

**AISQ-RA-002 — Is the proposed risk acceptance supported by a documented business rationale?**

**AISQ-RA-003 — Is the decision made by an individual with sufficient delegated authority?**

**AISQ-RA-004 — Are mandatory legal, contractual, and policy requirements considered before acceptance?**

**AISQ-RA-005 — Are conditions, compensating controls, and monitoring requirements documented?**

**AISQ-RA-006 — Are risk acceptances reviewed when circumstances materially change?**

**AISQ-RA-007 — Are acceptance decisions traceable to the relevant assessment and supporting evidence?**

#### 68.3 Risk Acceptance Is Not a Universal Waiver

An authorized business owner may accept certain business risks within the organization's delegated authority. However, that authority does not necessarily permit the owner to waive legal obligations, contractual requirements, or mandatory internal controls.

Where a risk cannot legitimately be accepted, the organization must pursue another response, such as additional mitigation, restriction, redesign, or rejection of the proposed use case.

### 69. Risk Escalation and Approval Thresholds

Not every questionnaire finding requires executive attention. However, material risks must reach the appropriate decision-makers before they create unacceptable exposure.

The organization should establish explicit escalation criteria.

#### 69.1 Escalation Triggers

Potential triggers include:

- A critical security control is absent or ineffective.
- The system can access highly sensitive information without adequate authorization.
- An AI agent can perform privileged actions without independent enforcement.
- A material data-protection issue remains unresolved.
- A supplier cannot provide sufficient assurance for a critical dependency.
- A security incident indicates that existing assumptions are invalid.
- A proposed use exceeds the approved risk appetite.
- A material legal or contractual issue remains unresolved.
- A high-risk exception is overdue.
- The system's intended purpose or autonomy changes materially.
- A required approval is missing.
- A material risk cannot be reduced to the organization's acceptable level.

#### 69.2 Escalation Questions

**AISQ-ESC-001 — Are escalation thresholds formally documented?**

**AISQ-ESC-002 — Are critical findings escalated before the relevant deployment or continued operation decision?**

**AISQ-ESC-003 — Are escalation recipients identified by risk category and severity?**

**AISQ-ESC-004 — Are urgent escalation and emergency containment procedures available?**

**AISQ-ESC-005 — Are escalation decisions and outcomes recorded?**

#### 69.3 Example Escalation Matrix

| Condition | Typical escalation route | Potential decision |
|---|---|---|
| Minor documentation gap | Assessment owner and control owner | Correct documentation. |
| Moderate control weakness | Security team and system owner | Remediate within an agreed period. |
| High residual risk | CISO or designated risk authority and business owner | Mitigate, restrict, defer, or consider authorized acceptance. |
| Critical exposure | Security leadership, executive authority, and relevant incident or risk governance body | Contain, suspend, reject, or pursue urgent remediation. |
| Legal or regulatory concern | Legal, privacy, compliance, and relevant management authority | Determine obligations and permissible next steps. |
| Material supplier assurance failure | Procurement, third-party risk, security leadership, and business owner | Seek further assurance, impose conditions, select an alternative, or suspend procurement. |

The exact routes depend on the organization's governance structure, risk appetite, and delegated authority.

### 70. Remediation Governance and Corrective Action Management

Identifying a control gap is only the beginning of remediation.

The organization must assign ownership, agree on a corrective action, establish a target date, monitor progress, and verify that the change reduces the relevant risk.

#### 70.1 Remediation Lifecycle

        |

        |
        v
[Remediation Owner Assigned]
        |

#### 70.2 Remediation Questions

**AISQ-REM-001 — Does every material finding have an accountable owner?**

**AISQ-REM-002 — Is the remediation plan linked to the identified risk scenario?**

**AISQ-REM-003 — Are target dates based on severity and organizational requirements?**

**AISQ-REM-004 — Are interim controls established where remediation cannot be completed immediately?**

**AISQ-REM-005 — Are overdue actions escalated?**

**AISQ-REM-006 — Is closure supported by evidence that the corrective action has been implemented?**

**AISQ-REM-007 — Is control effectiveness revalidated when the deficiency is material?**

**AISQ-REM-008 — Are recurring findings analyzed for underlying systemic causes?**

#### 70.3 Remediation Versus Risk Acceptance

Remediation and risk acceptance must not be confused.

- **Remediation** addresses the underlying deficiency.
- **Mitigation** reduces the likelihood or impact of the risk.
- **Compensating controls** provide alternative safeguards.
- **Risk acceptance** authorizes continued exposure within defined authority.
- **Risk avoidance** eliminates the exposure by not proceeding with the activity or by removing the affected capability.

A finding should not be marked remediated merely because a risk owner has accepted the residual risk. The record should reflect the actual disposition.

### 71. Security Exceptions and Temporary Deviations

Exceptions permit controlled deviation from defined security requirements. They should be governed consistently across AI systems and connected to the organization's risk management process.

The questionnaire should establish whether exceptions are justified, authorized, monitored, and periodically reviewed.

#### 71.1 Exception Register Fields

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Exception ID | Unique reference. |
| AI system | System affected by the deviation. |
| Requirement | Control, policy, or standard being waived or varied. |
| Justification | Reason the requirement cannot currently be met. |
| Risk assessment | Associated threat scenario and residual risk. |
| Compensating controls | Alternative safeguards. |
| Risk owner | Individual accountable for the risk. |
| Approver | Person authorized to approve the exception. |
| Effective date | Date the exception becomes active. |
| Review or expiry date | Date for reassessment or expiry, where applicable. |
| Remediation plan | Corrective action and milestones, if required. |
| Status | Proposed, approved, active, expired, rejected, or closed. |
| Evidence | Supporting documentation and approvals. |

#### 71.2 Exception Governance Questions

**AISQ-EXG-001 — Are exceptions linked to the relevant questionnaire finding and risk record?**

**AISQ-EXG-002 — Are exceptions approved by the appropriate authority?**

**AISQ-EXG-003 — Are exceptions time-bound or subject to defined review conditions where appropriate?**

**AISQ-EXG-004 — Are compensating controls monitored?**

**AISQ-EXG-005 — Are expired or overdue exceptions escalated?**

**AISQ-EXG-006 — Are repeated exceptions analyzed to identify weaknesses in the underlying architecture or control framework?**

#### 71.3 Repeated Exceptions as a Governance Signal

A large number of exceptions in the same control domain may indicate that the organization has a systemic problem.

For example, repeated exceptions for inadequate AI logging may indicate that logging requirements are not incorporated into the standard platform architecture.

Instead of repeatedly approving individual exceptions, the organization should consider a broader corrective initiative, such as improving the shared logging platform or updating the AI development standard.

### 72. Third-Party AI Security Assurance

Organizations frequently consume AI capabilities through external providers, SaaS applications, APIs, embedded AI features, and outsourced development services.

Supplier assurance must address the actual service and its role in the organization's architecture.

A provider's general security certification may be valuable, but it does not automatically establish that every AI-specific risk has been addressed.

#### 72.1 Supplier Assurance Objectives

The organization should establish whether the supplier:

- Has appropriate security governance.
- Protects customer data.
- Maintains access controls.
- Manages vulnerabilities.
- Protects model and service integrity.
- Controls subcontractors and subprocessors.
- Maintains incident response capabilities.
- Supports business continuity.
- Provides relevant assurance evidence.
- Notifies customers of material security incidents and changes according to agreed terms.
- Meets applicable contractual and regulatory requirements.

#### 72.2 Supplier Questionnaire Questions

**AISQ-TPR-001 — Has the supplier's identity and the exact service being assessed been established?**

**AISQ-TPR-002 — Has the supplier's security assurance been evaluated for scope, coverage, and relevance?**

**AISQ-TPR-003 — Does the supplier explain how customer data is processed, retained, and protected?**

**AISQ-TPR-004 — Are customer-data use restrictions documented, including any relevant model training or service-improvement uses?**

**AISQ-TPR-005 — Are access controls and tenant-isolation arrangements documented or supported by appropriate assurance?**

**AISQ-TPR-006 — Are subcontractors and subprocessors identified where relevant?**

**AISQ-TPR-007 — Are security incident notification and cooperation obligations defined?**

**AISQ-TPR-008 — Are vulnerability management and material service changes covered by available assurance?**

**AISQ-TPR-009 — Are business continuity, service availability, and exit arrangements assessed?**

**AISQ-TPR-010 — Are unresolved supplier risks assigned to an accountable internal owner?**

#### 72.3 Supplier Assurance Limitations

The organization may not be able to inspect the provider's internal model-serving infrastructure or obtain every requested technical detail.

In such cases, the assessment should document:

- What information was requested.
- What information was provided.
- Which assurance mechanisms were available.
- What remains unverified.
- Whether the limitation affects a material risk.
- What alternative safeguards or contractual measures are available.
- Whether the residual uncertainty is acceptable.

A supplier's refusal or inability to provide evidence does not automatically prove that the service is insecure. However, it may prevent the organization from establishing sufficient assurance for a particular use case.

### 73. Supplier Evidence Validation and Contractual Alignment

Supplier security documents must be assessed for relevance rather than accepted solely because they appear authoritative.

The organization should confirm that the evidence covers the actual provider, service, deployment model, and period under review.

#### 73.1 Evidence Validation Questions

**AISQ-TPR-011 — Does the assurance report cover the specific service or environment being used?**

**AISQ-TPR-012 — Is the report current and relevant to the assessment period?**

**AISQ-TPR-013 — Are material exclusions, qualifications, and exceptions identified?**

**AISQ-TPR-014 — Are complementary customer controls identified and implemented where required?**

**AISQ-TPR-015 — Are contractual commitments consistent with the organization's data-protection and security requirements?**

**AISQ-TPR-016 — Are material changes to the provider's service or assurance status monitored?**

**AISQ-TPR-017 — Is the supplier's assurance conclusion documented with its limitations?**

#### 73.2 Contractual Areas for Review

Depending on the service and applicable obligations, procurement, legal, security, and privacy teams may need to review:

- Permitted data processing.
- Customer-data retention and deletion.
- Use of customer information for training or service improvement.
- Confidentiality.
- Security incident notification.
- Audit and assurance rights.
- Subcontractor management.
- Data location and international transfers.
- Service availability.
- Vulnerability management.
- Material service changes.
- Service termination and data return.
- Exit assistance and transition requirements.

The questionnaire should identify where contractual review is required. It should not replace legal analysis or assume that a generic supplier statement establishes compliance.

### 74. Shared Responsibility and Inherited Controls

AI services often operate through a shared-responsibility model. The provider operates certain infrastructure or platform controls, while the customer remains responsible for its own configuration, users, data, applications, and use of the service.

The precise boundary varies by deployment model.

#### 74.1 Shared-Responsibility Questions

**AISQ-SHR-001 — Is the division of security responsibilities between the provider and customer documented?**

**AISQ-SHR-002 — Are customer-owned controls explicitly assigned to internal teams?**

**AISQ-SHR-003 — Are provider-owned controls supported by appropriate assurance evidence?**

**AISQ-SHR-004 — Are dependencies on inherited controls recorded in the risk assessment?**

**AISQ-SHR-005 — Are changes to the responsibility boundary reviewed?**

**AISQ-SHR-006 — Are gaps between provider and customer responsibilities identified and addressed?**

#### 74.2 Responsibility Matrix

| Control domain | Provider may be responsible for | Customer typically remains responsible for |
|---|---|---|
| Physical security | Provider facilities and physical infrastructure. | Evaluating provider assurance and contractual suitability. |
| Managed service infrastructure | Underlying service infrastructure within the provider's scope. | Secure use and configuration of the service. |
| Identity | Provider platform capabilities. | Customer identities, roles, credentials, and access policies. |
| Data | Provider controls over data it processes. | Data classification, permitted use, minimization, and customer-side access restrictions. |
| Application | Managed platform components within the service boundary. | Customer application code, integrations, and business logic. |
| AI configuration | Provider-managed defaults and available platform controls. | Approved model selection, prompts, tool permissions, and application configuration where exposed. |
| Monitoring | Provider telemetry and service-level monitoring. | Customer-side monitoring, incident detection, and integration with internal response processes. |
| Incident response | Provider investigation and cooperation within the agreed service scope. | Internal escalation, business response, customer obligations, and risk decisions. |
| Compliance | Provider evidence and controls within its service scope. | Assessing whether the complete processing activity meets applicable requirements. |

This table is illustrative. The actual responsibility boundary must be validated against the service documentation and contractual terms.

#### 74.3 Inherited Control Risk

A customer may rely on a provider's access-control mechanism, but the customer must still configure permissions appropriately.

A provider may offer comprehensive logging, but the customer may need to enable the relevant logs, retain them, integrate them with monitoring, and assign responders.

Inherited controls should therefore be assessed for both provider assurance and customer-side implementation.

### 75. AI Security Questionnaire Quality Assurance

A standardized questionnaire can still produce inconsistent results if assessors interpret questions differently, accept different levels of evidence, or apply scoring criteria inconsistently.

Quality assurance helps maintain the reliability and comparability of assessments.

#### 75.1 Quality Assurance Questions

**AISQ-QA-001 — Are assessment instructions and evaluation criteria documented?**

**AISQ-QA-002 — Are assessors trained in the methodology and relevant AI security concepts?**

**AISQ-QA-003 — Are high-risk assessments subject to a second review?**

**AISQ-QA-004 — Are inconsistent interpretations of questions identified and resolved?**

**AISQ-QA-005 — Are sampling or independent validation procedures applied where appropriate?**

**AISQ-QA-006 — Are scoring differences and recurring assessment errors analyzed?**

**AISQ-QA-007 — Are lessons from completed assessments incorporated into the questionnaire?**

**AISQ-QA-008 — Are questionnaire versions and changes controlled?**

#### 75.2 Quality Assurance Techniques

Potential techniques include:

- Independent review of selected assessments.
- Calibration sessions among assessors.
- Standardized evidence acceptance criteria.
- Review of not-applicable decisions.
- Sampling of closed findings.
- Comparison of similar systems.
- Analysis of recurring control weaknesses.
- Testing of automated scoring logic.
- Periodic review of the questionnaire's relevance to current threats.

Quality assurance should improve consistency without discouraging assessors from identifying unusual or previously unrecognized risks.

### 76. Questionnaire Version Control and Change Governance

Security questionnaires must evolve as AI technologies, organizational requirements, and threat patterns change.

However, uncontrolled changes can undermine comparability between assessments and make it difficult to establish which requirements applied when an approval was granted.

#### 76.1 Version Control Requirements

The organization should maintain:

- A unique questionnaire identifier.
- A version number.
- An effective date.
- A document owner.
- An approval authority.
- A change history.
- The rationale for material changes.
- Mapping to relevant internal controls or external frameworks.
- Defined migration or reassessment rules for existing systems.

#### 76.2 Version Control Questions

**AISQ-VER-001 — Is the approved questionnaire version identifiable?**

**AISQ-VER-002 — Are changes reviewed and approved before becoming effective?**

**AISQ-VER-003 — Are changes to questions, scoring, and evidence requirements documented?**

**AISQ-VER-004 — Are existing assessments evaluated to determine whether reassessment is necessary after a material update?**

**AISQ-VER-005 — Can historical assessments be reconstructed using the version and criteria that applied at the time?**

#### 76.3 Version Change Example

Suppose the organization adds a new mandatory question covering authorization enforcement for AI agents that modify customer records.

The governance owner should determine:

1. Which AI systems use agents with write access.
2. Whether existing assessments covered the same risk.
3. Which systems require reassessment.
4. Whether the new question changes the approval criteria.
5. How open findings should be updated.
6. Whether management reporting needs to reflect the new requirement.

The organization should not assume that every questionnaire update requires every AI system to undergo a complete reassessment. Reassessment should be based on the change's significance and the affected risk profile.

### 77. Assessment Records, Evidence Repository, and Audit Trail

The organization should maintain a controlled record of completed assessments and their supporting evidence.

This enables management to understand the basis for an approval, allows assessors to avoid repeating work unnecessarily, and supports internal and external assurance activities.

#### 77.1 Assessment Record Contents

A completed assessment record should include:

- Assessment identifier.
- AI system identifier.
- System owner and business owner.
- Assessment scope.
- Questionnaire version.
- Respondents and reviewers.
- Submission and review dates.
- Responses and applicability decisions.
- Evidence references.
- Findings and risk ratings.
- Remediation actions.
- Exceptions and compensating controls.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Approval outcome.
- Conditions attached to approval.
- Reassessment triggers and next review date.
- Relevant change history.

#### 77.2 Repository Governance Questions

**AISQ-REC-001 — Are completed questionnaires stored in an approved repository?**

**AISQ-REC-002 — Is access to assessment records restricted according to business need?**

**AISQ-REC-003 — Are evidence artifacts protected against unauthorized modification?**

**AISQ-REC-004 — Are assessment records linked to the relevant AI system inventory and risk register?**

**AISQ-REC-005 — Are retention and disposal requirements defined?**

**AISQ-REC-006 — Can the organization retrieve the evidence supporting a historical approval?**

**AISQ-REC-007 — Are material changes to assessment records traceable?**

#### 77.3 Evidence Minimization

A complete audit trail does not require copying every sensitive artifact into a single repository.

Where appropriate, the assessment can reference an existing controlled evidence source rather than duplicate sensitive logs, source code, customer data, or provider documents.

The organization should maintain sufficient traceability while applying access restrictions, data minimization, retention controls, and secure evidence handling.

### 78. AI Security Approval Workflow and Decision Gates

The questionnaire should feed a formal decision process that establishes whether an AI system may proceed to procurement, pilot deployment, production release, or continued operation.

Different lifecycle stages may require different evidence and approval thresholds.

#### 78.1 Recommended Decision Gates

| Gate | Purpose | Typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| Gate 1 — Initial screening | Determine scope, intended use, and preliminary risk. | Proceed to detailed assessment, request more information, or reject the proposal. |
| Gate 2 — Design assessment | Evaluate architecture, threat scenarios, data flows, and planned controls. | Approve design subject to requirements or require redesign. |
| Gate 3 — Supplier assurance | Assess external service and dependency risks. | Accept supplier assurance, impose conditions, or reject the supplier for the intended use. |
| Gate 4 — Technical validation | Verify that required controls have been implemented and tested. | Proceed to release review or require remediation. |
| Gate 5 — Production approval | Review residual risks, mandatory requirements, and operational readiness. | Approve, restrict, defer, or reject deployment. |
| Gate 6 — Operational review | Evaluate continuing control effectiveness and changes. | Continue operation, require corrective action, restrict capabilities, or suspend use. |
| Gate 7 — Retirement | Verify access revocation, data handling, and decommissioning. | Confirm controlled retirement and close the relevant records. |

Not every system requires seven separate approval meetings. The gates can be integrated into existing governance processes while preserving their decision objectives.

#### 78.2 Approval Workflow Questions

**AISQ-APR-001 — Are required approval gates defined for the AI system's risk category?**

**AISQ-APR-002 — Are mandatory security requirements evaluated before the relevant gate is passed?**

**AISQ-APR-003 — Are approval decisions made by authorized individuals?**

**AISQ-APR-004 — Are restrictions and conditions recorded in a form that can be enforced?**

**AISQ-APR-005 — Are approval decisions linked to the assessed system version and configuration?**

**AISQ-APR-006 — Can the organization suspend or withdraw approval when material conditions change?**

#### 78.3 Approval Is Specific to the Assessed Scope

An approval should not be interpreted as permission to use the system for any purpose.

Approval may depend on the model version, user population, data categories, permitted integrations, autonomy level, and defined business purpose.

A change to those assumptions may require reassessment or additional authorization.

### 79. AI Security Governance Metrics and Management Reporting

Questionnaire results can provide useful information about the organization's AI security posture.

However, metrics should reflect risk and control effectiveness rather than simply measuring the number of completed forms.

#### 79.1 Recommended Governance Metrics

| Metric | What it measures | Potential management use |
|---|---|---|
| Assessment completion rate | Proportion of required assessments completed. | Identifies assessment backlogs. |
| Evidence sufficiency rate | Proportion of applicable control responses supported by acceptable evidence. | Highlights assurance weaknesses. |
| High-risk finding count | Number of unresolved high-risk findings. | Identifies material exposure. |
| Critical finding age | Time critical findings remain unresolved. | Supports urgent escalation. |
| Overdue remediation rate | Proportion of corrective actions past their target dates. | Identifies remediation delays. |
| Exception age | Duration of active exceptions. | Identifies prolonged deviations. |
| Supplier assurance coverage | Proportion of relevant suppliers with adequate assurance for their assessed scope. | Supports third-party risk oversight. |
| Reassessment compliance | Proportion of systems reassessed when required. | Measures lifecycle governance. |
| Repeat finding rate | Frequency of recurring control deficiencies. | Identifies systemic weaknesses. |
| Approval traceability | Proportion of decisions linked to required assessment and risk records. | Supports auditability. |

The organization should define each metric's numerator, denominator, reporting period, exclusions, and accountable owner.

#### 79.2 Metrics Questions

**AISQ-MET-001 — Are questionnaire metrics formally defined and consistently calculated?**

**AISQ-MET-002 — Are material findings reported according to severity and age?**

**AISQ-MET-003 — Are recurring weaknesses analyzed for systemic causes?**

**AISQ-MET-004 — Are management reports linked to actionable remediation or governance decisions?**

**AISQ-MET-005 — Are metrics reviewed for unintended incentives or misleading interpretations?**

#### 79.3 Avoiding Misleading Metrics

A 100% assessment completion rate does not mean the organization has a secure AI portfolio.

Similarly, a high average control score may conceal a small number of severe deficiencies.

Management reporting should therefore combine process metrics with risk-based indicators, evidence quality, unresolved findings, and material changes.

### 80. Regulatory, Legal, and Framework Mapping

The AI Security Questionnaire may support compliance and assurance activities, but it should not be represented as automatically demonstrating compliance with every applicable law or framework.

Different standards and regulations have different scopes, obligations, terminology, and evidence requirements.

The organization should map questionnaire controls to relevant requirements and document where additional assessment is needed.

#### 80.1 Framework Mapping Questions

**AISQ-MAP-001 — Are relevant internal policies and external requirements identified for the assessed system?**

**AISQ-MAP-002 — Are questionnaire questions mapped to the applicable requirements where useful?**

**AISQ-MAP-003 — Are gaps between questionnaire coverage and applicable obligations documented?**

**AISQ-MAP-004 — Are legal and regulatory interpretations reviewed by appropriate specialists?**

**AISQ-MAP-005 — Are changes to relevant requirements monitored and assessed for impact?**

**AISQ-MAP-006 — Are claims of conformity or compliance supported by the required evidence and evaluation process?**

#### 80.2 Common Mapping Areas

Depending on the organization and use case, relevant references may include:

- ISO/IEC 27001 for information security management.
- ISO/IEC 42001 for AI management systems.
- ISO/IEC 23894 for AI risk management guidance.
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework.
- OWASP guidance for AI application and LLM security.
- The EU AI Act where applicable.
- GDPR where personal-data processing is involved.
- Applicable sector-specific security and regulatory requirements.

These references should not be treated as interchangeable. Each has its own scope and purpose.

The organization should determine applicability based on the system, jurisdiction, contractual context, and intended use.

### 81. Management of Material Assessment Changes

An assessment is based on a defined system and a set of assumptions. When those assumptions change, the original conclusions may no longer be reliable.

The organization should define which changes require a limited review and which require a full or substantially renewed assessment.

#### 81.1 Reassessment Triggers

Examples include:

- A new AI model or provider.
- A change in the intended purpose.
- A material increase in user population.
- New categories of personal or confidential data.
- A new external integration.
- Expanded agent permissions.
- Removal of a required security control.
- A significant supplier incident.
- A major vulnerability affecting the service.
- A change in data-retention or training terms.
- A new regulatory obligation.
- Evidence that an existing control is ineffective.
- A change in the system's business criticality.

#### 81.2 Change Assessment Questions

**AISQ-CHG-001 — Are material changes evaluated against the original assessment assumptions?**

**AISQ-CHG-002 — Are affected risks and controls identified before the change is approved where practicable?**

**AISQ-CHG-003 — Is the required reassessment scope documented?**

**AISQ-CHG-004 — Are previous approval conditions revalidated after relevant changes?**

**AISQ-CHG-005 — Are changes that create unacceptable exposure restricted or escalated?**

The organization should integrate these requirements with the existing change-management process rather than establish a disconnected AI-specific process where an existing control can satisfy the same objective.

### 82. AI Security Questionnaire Governance Maturity

Organizations may use a maturity model to assess how effectively their questionnaire process operates.

The model should evaluate the quality of governance, consistency of assessments, evidence management, and use of findings—not simply whether a questionnaire document exists.

#### 82.1 Illustrative Maturity Levels

| Level | Description | Typical characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 — Ad hoc | Assessments are informal and inconsistent. | No standardized questionnaire, unclear ownership, limited evidence retention. |
| Level 2 — Repeatable | Basic procedures and templates exist. | Some assigned owners, recurring assessments, limited consistency. |
| Level 3 — Defined | A documented organization-wide process is established. | Standard methodology, approved questionnaire, defined roles, evidence requirements, and escalation. |
| Level 4 — Managed | The process is measured and actively governed. | Risk-based assessment depth, metrics, quality assurance, integrated remediation, and management reporting. |
| Level 5 — Optimized | The process improves continuously using operational evidence and lessons learned. | Automated workflows where appropriate, systemic finding analysis, proactive reassessment, and continuous refinement. |

This is an internal maturity model, not an externally mandated certification scale.

#### 82.2 Maturity Assessment Questions

**AISQ-MAT-001 — Is the questionnaire process standardized across relevant business units?**

**AISQ-MAT-002 — Are roles, approval rights, and escalation paths documented?**

**AISQ-MAT-003 — Are assessments supported by consistent evidence criteria?**

**AISQ-MAT-004 — Are findings and remediation integrated with enterprise risk management?**

**AISQ-MAT-005 — Are process metrics used to identify weaknesses and improve effectiveness?**

**AISQ-MAT-006 — Are automation and integration introduced with appropriate validation and oversight?**

### 83. Governance Automation and Workflow Integration

Automation can reduce repetitive work in questionnaire distribution, evidence tracking, response validation, reminders, reporting, and reassessment scheduling.

However, automation should not convert an uncertain answer into a false assurance conclusion or make an unauthorized risk decision.

#### 83.1 Appropriate Automation Opportunities

Potential use cases include:

- Automatic assignment of questions to responsible teams.
- Reminder notifications.
- Detection of missing responses.
- Evidence-reference validation.
- Mapping questions to control libraries.
- Calculation of approved scoring formulas.
- Linking findings to risk registers.
- Tracking remediation deadlines.
- Generating management reports.
- Triggering reassessment after defined changes.
- Maintaining assessment version history.

#### 83.2 Automation Governance Questions

**AISQ-AUT-001 — Are automated questionnaire workflows approved and documented?**

**AISQ-AUT-002 — Are scoring rules and decision logic tested before deployment?**

**AISQ-AUT-003 — Are automated outputs reviewed where judgment or high-impact decisions are required?**

**AISQ-AUT-004 — Are changes to workflow rules and scoring models controlled?**

**AISQ-AUT-005 — Are automated errors detectable and correctable?**

**AISQ-AUT-006 — Are permissions for modifying assessment results appropriately restricted?**

**AISQ-AUT-007 — Are automated decisions and workflow events traceable?**

#### 83.3 AI-Assisted Questionnaire Analysis

An organization may use an AI assistant to summarize supplier responses, identify missing evidence, compare answers, or propose findings.

Such assistance should remain subject to suitable controls:

1. The assistant should use the approved assessment materials.
2. Source references should be retained for material conclusions.
3. The output should distinguish supplier assertions from verified facts.
4. Uncertainty should be disclosed.
5. Security-sensitive information should be handled according to policy.
6. Automated scoring should follow approved rules.
7. High-impact risk and approval decisions should remain with authorized personnel unless a separately approved process explicitly establishes otherwise.
8. The organization should validate the assistant's output before using it as formal assurance evidence.

An AI-generated summary is not itself proof that a supplier's control is effective.

### 84. Governance Disputes and Conflicting Assessment Results

Different stakeholders may disagree about a questionnaire response, control interpretation, finding severity, or residual risk.

The organization should provide a defined mechanism for resolving disagreements without suppressing legitimate security concerns.

#### 84.1 Dispute Categories

Common disagreements include:

- Whether a question applies.
- Whether submitted evidence is sufficient.
- Whether a control is effective.
- Whether a finding is material.
- Whether a remediation deadline is reasonable.
- Whether a supplier's assurance is adequate.
- Whether a risk is within the organization's appetite.
- Whether a proposed exception is permissible.
- Whether a deployment should proceed.

#### 84.2 Dispute Resolution Questions

**AISQ-DSP-001 — Is there a documented process for challenging assessment conclusions?**

**AISQ-DSP-002 — Are disputed responses supported by evidence and recorded reasoning?**

**AISQ-DSP-003 — Can material disputes be escalated to an independent or appropriately senior authority?**

**AISQ-DSP-004 — Are final decisions documented with the rationale and accountable decision-maker?**

**AISQ-DSP-005 — Are unresolved disputes prevented from being treated as approved merely because the assessment deadline has passed?**

#### 84.3 Example

The engineering team states that an AI agent cannot execute unauthorized transactions because the system prompt prohibits them.

The security assessor determines that this is insufficient because the connected transaction API does not independently enforce the relevant authorization rules.

The disagreement should be resolved by examining the architecture and evidence. The control should not be marked effective merely because the engineering team believes the prompt instructions will prevent misuse.

Where the evidence demonstrates a material deficiency, the finding should remain open until remediation or an authorized risk decision resolves the issue.

### 85. Suspension, Restriction, and Withdrawal of Approval

A previously approved AI system may become unacceptable if a material vulnerability emerges, an incident occurs, a provider changes its service, or a control is found to be ineffective.

The governance process should establish when an existing approval must be reconsidered.

#### 85.1 Suspension Questions

**AISQ-SUS-001 — Are conditions for restricting or suspending an AI system defined?**

**AISQ-SUS-002 — Can the organization disable affected tools, integrations, or capabilities promptly?**

**AISQ-SUS-003 — Are emergency suspension decisions assigned to authorized roles?**

**AISQ-SUS-004 — Are suspension actions documented and communicated to affected stakeholders?**

**AISQ-SUS-005 — Are criteria for restoring approval defined?**

**AISQ-SUS-006 — Is a reassessment required before resuming use when material risk conditions have changed?**

#### 85.2 Possible Restrictive Actions

Depending on the situation, the organization may:

- Disable a particular agent tool.
- Restrict access to a sensitive data source.
- Reduce the permitted user population.
- Require human approval for previously automated actions.
- Disable external access.
- Suspend a model or provider integration.
- Limit the system to non-sensitive information.
- Roll back a recent change.
- Suspend the complete service.
- Retire the system if the risk cannot be reduced adequately.

The action should be proportionate to the exposure and supported by an appropriate decision process. Emergency containment should not be delayed solely because the normal questionnaire workflow is incomplete.

### 86. Part 3 Core Principle — Questionnaire Assurance Must Produce Accountable Decisions

Part 3 establishes the governance mechanisms that transform questionnaire responses into defensible security and business decisions.

The questionnaire is not an independent authority. It is an assurance instrument within a larger governance process that assigns responsibility, evaluates evidence, prioritizes risk, authorizes exceptions, and controls deployment.

The organization must be able to explain not only what the questionnaire concluded, but also **who made the decision, what evidence supported it, which risks remained, and what conditions govern continued operation**.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Correct interpretation |
|---|---|
| Respondent | Provides accurate information and supporting evidence for assigned questions. |
| Assessment owner | Coordinates the assessment and ensures that the methodology is applied. |
| Control owner | Is accountable for implementing and maintaining the relevant safeguard. |
| Risk owner | Is accountable for the risk and its treatment within delegated authority. |
| Independent assurance | Provides an additional level of confidence through review or testing independent of routine control operation. |
| Attestation | Establishes accountability for a statement but does not replace verification. |
| Control score | Summarizes defined assessment results but does not independently establish that risk is acceptable. |
| Evidence confidence | Indicates the strength and relevance of the evidence supporting an assessment conclusion. |
| Risk acceptance | Authorizes continued exposure within the decision-maker's authority; it does not automatically eliminate the risk. |
| Exception | Records an approved deviation from a requirement and its associated conditions. |
| Supplier assurance | Evaluates evidence about a third party and the specific service being used. |
| Inherited control | A safeguard operated by another party on which the organization relies, subject to its own responsibility and configuration requirements. |
| Approval | Authorizes a defined scope of use under stated conditions, not unrestricted use of the system. |
| Reassessment | Re-evaluates risks and controls when time, changes, incidents, or new evidence make the existing conclusion insufficient. |
| Governance maturity | Measures the effectiveness of the process, not merely the existence of templates or completed assessments. |
