# 20.05 — AI System Inventory Template

## Part 1 — AI System Inventory Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI System Inventory

An AI System Inventory is a structured, authoritative record of the artificial intelligence systems, models, applications, services, and AI-enabled use cases that an organization develops, acquires, deploys, operates, or materially relies upon.

The inventory provides the foundational visibility required to govern AI across the enterprise. Without an accurate inventory, an organization cannot reliably determine which AI systems are in use, who owns them, what information they process, which risks they introduce, or whether they comply with internal policies and external obligations.

AI systems may exist in many forms, including:

- Internally developed machine learning models.
- Third-party generative AI services.
- Large language models integrated into enterprise applications.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) solutions.
- AI agents capable of invoking tools or executing actions.
- AI-powered decision support and prediction systems.
- AI capabilities embedded in purchased software.
- AI services accessed through APIs.
- Experimental AI solutions and proof-of-concept deployments.
- Unauthorized or unregistered AI applications discovered through monitoring.

The inventory must cover more than formally approved production systems. Discovery should also identify experimental deployments, departmental solutions, embedded AI features, and shadow AI where relevant to the organization's risk exposure.

An inventory is not merely a list of products. It is a governance control that connects AI assets to business purpose, accountable ownership, data usage, technical dependencies, risk assessments, control requirements, approvals, and lifecycle status.

### 2. Purpose of the AI System Inventory

The purpose of the AI System Inventory is to establish a reliable source of information for AI governance, security, risk management, compliance, and operational oversight.

The inventory enables the organization to answer fundamental questions:

1. Which AI systems and use cases exist?
2. Who is accountable for each system?
3. Why is each system being used?
4. Which models, providers, datasets, and integrations does it depend on?
5. What information does it process?
6. Who may be affected by its outputs or actions?
7. What is its inherent and residual risk?
8. Which policies, controls, and approvals apply?
9. Is the system authorized for its current purpose?
10. When must it be reassessed, restricted, or retired?

The inventory also supports risk-based prioritization. An organization with hundreds of AI-enabled capabilities cannot apply identical oversight to every system. It needs reliable attributes that distinguish low-impact productivity tools from systems with significant security, privacy, safety, regulatory, or human-impact implications.

The inventory should therefore support both operational management and executive oversight. Operational teams need sufficiently detailed records to maintain individual systems, while management needs aggregated information about exposure, risk concentration, ownership gaps, and governance coverage.

### 3. AI System Inventory as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI System Inventory must be treated as a formal governance control rather than an optional documentation exercise.

The control establishes visibility over AI assets and enables other governance processes to operate consistently. Risk assessments, security reviews, vendor assessments, impact assessments, and production approvals should reference identifiable inventory records.

The inventory should be governed by a defined process that establishes:

- An accountable inventory owner.
- A designated authoritative repository.
- Mandatory registration requirements.
- Minimum data fields.
- Data quality and validation rules.
- Update and review responsibilities.
- Access and change permissions.
- Reconciliation with other enterprise records.
- Escalation for missing or inaccurate information.
- Retirement and archival requirements.

An inventory that contains outdated ownership, incorrect system status, or missing high-risk use cases may create a false impression of governance coverage.

The organization should therefore assess not only whether an inventory exists but also whether it is complete, accurate, current, traceable, and actively used in risk decisions.

### 4. Scope of the AI System Inventory

The inventory scope must be defined according to the organization's AI exposure, operating model, regulatory obligations, and risk management objectives.

At a minimum, the organization should determine whether the inventory covers:

- AI systems developed internally.
- AI systems purchased from external suppliers.
- AI services accessed through enterprise subscriptions.
- AI functionality embedded in third-party products.
- AI models accessed through APIs.
- Internally hosted or externally hosted models.
- AI-enabled workflows and business processes.
- AI agents and autonomous or semi-autonomous systems.
- Experimental systems and pilot deployments.
- AI systems operated by contractors or service providers.
- AI capabilities used by subsidiaries or business units.
- Shadow AI discovered outside approved procurement processes.

The organization should also define whether the inventory tracks individual models, complete applications, business use cases, or multiple related records.

For example, a single enterprise application may use several models, while one model may support multiple business use cases. Recording only the application name may conceal differences in intended purpose, affected populations, data access, and risk classification.

The inventory design must therefore support relationships between systems, models, deployments, and use cases where those distinctions materially affect governance.

### 5. Definition of an AI System for Inventory Purposes

The organization must establish a practical definition of an AI system that is sufficiently consistent for inventory registration and governance.

The definition should align with applicable legal requirements, relevant standards, and the organization's internal AI governance framework. Where a formal regulatory definition applies, the organization must use the appropriate interpretation for that context.

For operational inventory purposes, an AI system may be recorded as a solution that uses an AI model or AI-enabled capability to generate predictions, recommendations, content, classifications, decisions, or actions.

The definition should account for the complete solution, including relevant software, data, interfaces, models, integrations, and human processes.

The organization should distinguish among:

| Record type    | Description                                          | Example                                 |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| AI use case    | The business purpose or activity supported by AI     | Customer support assistance             |
| AI application | The application through which the capability is used | Internal support assistant              |
| AI model       | The model generating predictions or outputs          | An approved language model              |
| AI service     | A hosted or managed AI capability                    | External inference service              |
| AI component   | A technical element supporting the system            | Embedding model or retrieval component  |
| AI deployment  | A specific operating instance or environment         | Production deployment in a cloud region |

These records may be linked rather than duplicated. The organization should define the level at which risk is assessed and ensure that related records remain traceable.

### 6. AI System Inventory Ownership

The organization must assign accountability for maintaining the AI System Inventory.

Inventory ownership involves defining the registration process, maintaining data quality requirements, coordinating updates, resolving record conflicts, and reporting completeness or accuracy issues.

Responsibilities may be distributed across AI governance, enterprise architecture, IT asset management, technology risk, or another designated function. The selected arrangement should reflect the organization's operating model.

The inventory owner should be responsible for:

- Maintaining the inventory schema and data dictionary.
- Defining mandatory fields and permitted values.
- Coordinating registration and update workflows.
- Establishing validation and reconciliation rules.
- Monitoring missing or inconsistent records.
- Managing access and modification rights.
- Coordinating periodic certification.
- Maintaining links to risk and compliance records.
- Reporting inventory quality and coverage.
- Coordinating archival and retirement processes.

The inventory owner is not automatically the owner of every AI system or every risk recorded in the inventory. Those responsibilities must be assigned separately to the relevant business, technical, and risk owners.

### 7. AI System Registration Requirements

Every in-scope AI system or use case must be registered through a defined process.

Registration should occur before production authorization and early enough in the development or acquisition lifecycle to support meaningful risk assessment. Limited experimentation may follow a separate registration process, but the organization should define its boundaries and minimum safeguards.

The registration process should capture sufficient information to identify the system, establish accountability, determine its intended purpose, and initiate risk classification.

A standard registration workflow should include:

1. Submission of an AI system or use-case registration request.
2. Assignment of a unique inventory identifier.
3. Identification of the business and technical owners.
4. Documentation of intended purpose and operating context.
5. Identification of models, providers, and key dependencies.
6. Initial data classification and impact screening.
7. Preliminary risk classification.
8. Identification of required assessments and approvals.
9. Validation of mandatory inventory fields.
10. Assignment of lifecycle status and next review requirements.

Registration does not constitute approval to deploy the system. It establishes the record needed for the subsequent governance process.

The organization should define how unregistered AI systems are handled when discovered. Depending on risk, the response may include registration, assessment, restriction, or suspension.

### 8. Unique AI System Identifier

Each inventory record must have a unique, stable identifier that supports traceability across governance systems.

The identifier should not depend solely on a product name, supplier name, model version, or informal project title because these attributes may change over time.

The identifier should be used to link the inventory record to:

- Risk assessments.
- Risk register entries.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy and impact assessments.
- Vendor assessments.
- Architecture reviews.
- Production approvals.
- Change records.
- Incidents and exceptions.
- Audit findings.
- Monitoring and assurance evidence.
- Retirement records.

A model used by multiple applications may require a separate model identifier linked to several application or use-case identifiers. Likewise, multiple deployments of the same application may require distinct deployment records where configuration or risk differs materially.

The organization should define identifier conventions, uniqueness rules, and record-merging procedures. Retired identifiers should not be casually reassigned to unrelated systems because doing so can break historical traceability.

### 9. AI System Inventory Data Model

The inventory should use a structured data model that defines the entities, attributes, and relationships required for governance.

A useful data model separates core system information from related records that have their own lifecycle and ownership.

For example, a system record may link to a model record, a vendor record, several risk assessments, multiple control mappings, and one or more deployments.

```
                   +----------------------+
                   |   AI BUSINESS USE    |
                   | Purpose / Stakeholders|
                   +----------+-----------+
                              |
                              v
+----------------+   +----------------------+   +----------------+
| AI MODEL       |<->| AI SYSTEM / APP       |<->| AI PROVIDER    |
| Version        |   | Unique ID             |   | Contract       |
| Provenance     |   | Owner / Status        |   | Assurance      |
+----------------+   +----------+-----------+   +----------------+
                                |
             +------------------+------------------+
             |                  |                  |
             v                  v                  v
   +----------------+  +----------------+  +----------------+
   | Risk Records   |  | Assessments     |  | Deployments    |
   | Inherent/Risk  |  | Security/Impact |  | Environment    |
   +----------------+  +----------------+  +----------------+
                                |
                                v
                     +----------------------+
                     | Controls and Evidence|
                     | Approvals / Reviews  |
                     +----------------------+
```

The data model should support relationships without forcing every attribute into a single oversized table.

The organization should also define the authoritative source for each attribute. For example, supplier information may be maintained in procurement systems, while vulnerability findings may reside in security tooling. The inventory can reference those systems where they remain authoritative.

### 10. AI System Inventory Mandatory Fields

The organization must define a minimum set of mandatory fields for all in-scope AI records.

Mandatory fields establish the baseline information needed to identify the system, assign responsibility, determine applicability, and initiate risk management.

An illustrative minimum schema is:

| Field                       | Description                                                               | Requirement                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| AI System ID                | Unique and stable identifier                                              | Mandatory                  |
| System name                 | Recognizable name of the AI capability                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Use-case description        | Business purpose and intended function                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Business owner              | Accountable business representative                                       | Mandatory                  |
| Technical owner             | Responsible technical representative                                      | Mandatory                  |
| Lifecycle status            | Proposed, development, pilot, production, suspended, or retired           | Mandatory                  |
| Deployment type             | Internal, externally hosted, embedded, or hybrid                          | Mandatory                  |
| AI capability type          | Generative, predictive, classification, agentic, or other applicable type | Mandatory                  |
| Model or provider           | Known model, supplier, or service                                         | Mandatory where applicable |
| Data classification         | Highest relevant data sensitivity                                         | Mandatory                  |
| Initial risk classification | Preliminary risk level                                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Registration date           | Date the record was created                                               | Mandatory                  |
| Last review date            | Most recent inventory validation                                          | Mandatory once reviewed    |
| Next review date            | Scheduled review or certification date                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Assessment references       | Links to relevant risk and impact assessments                             | Required as applicable     |

The organization should distinguish a genuinely non-applicable field from a missing field. For example, an internally developed model may not have an external model provider, but its development owner and provenance information remain relevant.

The data dictionary must define the meaning, format, allowed values, validation rules, and ownership of each field.

### 11. AI System Name and Business Description

The inventory must provide a clear system name and a business description that enables reviewers to understand what the AI capability does and why it exists.

The name should be recognizable to operational teams and distinguish the system from similar applications or model instances.

The business description should explain:

- The problem the system addresses.
- The business process it supports.
- The intended users.
- The outputs it produces.
- The decisions or actions it influences.
- The boundaries of its approved purpose.
- Important limitations or dependencies.

Descriptions such as “AI tool,” “chatbot,” or “automation project” are insufficient for meaningful risk assessment because they do not explain the actual business function.

For example, “AI assistant for internal policy search using approved enterprise documents” is more useful than “AI chatbot” because it identifies the intended function and an important data boundary.

The description should be updated when the use case changes materially. A system originally used for internal information retrieval may require reassessment if it is later used to generate customer-facing advice or influence consequential decisions.

### 12. AI Business Owner and Technical Owner Fields

The inventory must identify both business accountability and technical responsibility.

The **business owner** is accountable for the intended purpose, business justification, user population, operational outcomes, and appropriate use of the AI capability.

The **technical owner** is responsible for the technical service or implementation according to the organization's operating model, including relevant configuration, integration, maintenance, and operational safeguards.

The record should identify each owner using an authoritative organizational identity or directory reference where possible.

It should also capture relevant contact or escalation information through approved enterprise systems rather than embedding unnecessary personal information in broadly accessible inventory records.

If an owner changes role or leaves the organization, the record must be updated. Systems without accountable ownership should be flagged for remediation because risk decisions cannot be managed effectively without a responsible party.

Where several teams share technical responsibility, the inventory should identify the primary technical owner and link to additional component owners as necessary.

### 13. AI System Lifecycle Status

The inventory must record the current lifecycle status of each AI system.

Lifecycle status supports governance decisions by distinguishing systems under consideration from systems that are actively operating or no longer authorized for use.

An illustrative lifecycle model is:

| Status                  | Meaning                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Proposed                | Use case submitted but not yet approved for development    |
| In development          | Design, build, or configuration activities are underway    |
| Pilot                   | Limited testing under defined conditions                   |
| Approved for production | Required approvals have been granted                       |
| Production              | Operating within an approved scope                         |
| Suspended               | Operation has been restricted or temporarily stopped       |
| Under review            | Reassessment or material change review is in progress      |
| Retiring                | Controlled withdrawal is underway                          |
| Retired                 | Use has ended and required closure activities are complete |
| Rejected                | The proposed use case was not authorized                   |

The organization should define allowed transitions between statuses and the approval requirements for each transition.

For example, a pilot should not automatically become production merely because testing has finished. Production authorization must satisfy the applicable risk assessment, security, privacy, operational, and approval requirements.

Suspension should be distinguishable from retirement because a suspended system may retain data, credentials, integrations, and dependencies that require active management.

### 14. AI System Deployment Model

The inventory should record how and where the AI system is deployed.

Deployment information is important because hosting models create different security, privacy, operational, and third-party risks.

Deployment models may include:

- Internally hosted on-premises systems.
- Private cloud deployments.
- Public cloud AI services.
- Third-party SaaS AI services.
- AI features embedded in enterprise software.
- Hybrid deployments involving internal and external components.
- Locally executed or edge AI systems.
- API-based access to external models.

The record should identify the relevant environment, hosting provider, deployment region where material, and important operational dependencies.

Deployment information should be sufficiently detailed to support risk analysis without duplicating the full configuration database. Detailed infrastructure records may remain in cloud asset inventories or configuration management databases, with links maintained from the AI inventory.

A change in deployment model can materially affect data handling, access boundaries, provider dependency, resilience, and regulatory obligations. Such changes should trigger review where required by the organization's risk management process.

### 15. AI Capability and System Type Classification

The inventory should classify the type of AI capability used by the system.

Classification helps determine which risks and assessments are relevant. Different AI capabilities may require different control requirements, although categories can overlap.

Illustrative types include:

- Predictive analytics and forecasting.
- Classification and recommendation.
- Computer vision.
- Speech recognition and synthesis.
- Natural language processing.
- Generative AI.
- Retrieval-augmented generation.
- AI agents and tool-using systems.
- Autonomous or semi-autonomous decision systems.
- Embedded AI functionality in commercial products.

The inventory should permit multiple classifications where appropriate. For example, a customer support solution may combine generative AI, retrieval, speech processing, and agent-based actions.

Classification should be based on actual functionality rather than marketing terminology alone. The organization should record enough detail to determine whether the system generates content, influences decisions, accesses sensitive information, or performs actions.

The classification should support assessment routing, control selection, and reporting without implying that a capability category alone determines the final risk rating.

### 16. AI Model Identification and Provenance

The inventory should identify the model or models on which the AI system depends.

Relevant information may include the model name, provider, version, source, deployment method, intended use, known limitations, and validation status.

Where the model is developed internally, provenance information may include the development team, relevant training or fine-tuning records, model artifacts, and approved release identifier.

For third-party models, the inventory should capture the provider and available model identifiers and link to relevant supplier and assurance records.

The organization should distinguish between a named model family and the actual model version or service configuration used in production. Providers may change models, routing, or capabilities over time, and such changes may affect the risk assessment.

Where a provider does not disclose all technical details, the inventory should record the information available, the limitation, and the associated uncertainty. Missing transparency should not be represented as verified model provenance.

### 17. AI Provider and Supplier Identification

The inventory must identify external providers that materially support each AI system.

Providers may include model developers, hosted inference services, SaaS vendors, cloud platforms, data suppliers, integration providers, and managed service operators.

The inventory should link each provider to the systems and use cases that depend on it.

Relevant attributes may include:

- Supplier identifier.
- Service or product name.
- Contract or procurement reference.
- Service owner.
- Data processing role.
- Hosting or processing region where relevant.
- Assurance status.
- Material service dependencies.
- Exit or substitution considerations.
- Related vendor risk assessment.

A system may depend on several suppliers, and one supplier may support many systems. The inventory should preserve these relationships rather than storing a single supplier name when that would conceal important dependencies.

Provider identification supports concentration risk analysis, incident coordination, contractual oversight, and assessment of the consequences of service disruption or provider change.

### 18. AI System Data Classification

The inventory should record the highest relevant data classification associated with the AI system, including data accessed, processed, stored, transmitted, or generated where appropriate.

The classification should follow the organization's information classification scheme.

Relevant categories may include:

- Public information.
- Internal information.
- Confidential business information.
- Restricted or highly sensitive information.
- Personal data.
- Special-category or otherwise sensitive personal data, where applicable.
- Regulated or contractually protected information.
- Credentials, secrets, or security-sensitive information.

The inventory should identify whether the system uses personal data, confidential documents, customer information, employee information, intellectual property, or other sensitive content.

A system that generates outputs from confidential sources may still create confidentiality risks even if it does not retain the original documents directly.

The recorded classification should support data access restrictions, privacy assessment, supplier review, retention decisions, and AI service approval. Detailed data lineage and processing records may be maintained in linked data governance systems.

### 19. AI System Data Flow and Processing Summary

The inventory should provide a concise summary of the system's major data flows.

The purpose is to establish where information originates, how it enters the AI system, which components process it, where it may be stored or transmitted, and how outputs are used.

The summary should consider:

- User prompts and uploaded content.
- Enterprise repositories and retrieval sources.
- Training or fine-tuning data where relevant.
- External model or inference services.
- Application logs and interaction history.
- Generated outputs and downstream systems.
- Third-party integrations.
- Retention and deletion mechanisms.

A high-level data-flow diagram may be linked to the inventory record when the system's complexity or risk justifies it.

```
[Users / Business Process]
            |
            v
[AI Application / API]
            |
            +------> [Approved Data Sources]
            |                   |
            |                   v
            |             [Retrieval / Data Layer]
            |                   |
            v                   v
       [AI Model / Service]
            |
            v
      [Output Validation]
            |
            v
[Human Review / Downstream Action]
            |
            v
 [Logs, Retention, Monitoring]
```

The diagram is illustrative and should be adapted to the actual architecture. It must not imply that every AI system uses retrieval, human review, or the same data path.

### 20. AI System Integration and Dependency Mapping

The inventory should identify important technical and business dependencies that can affect AI risk.

Dependencies may include identity providers, enterprise applications, data repositories, APIs, cloud services, model endpoints, vector databases, workflow engines, monitoring platforms, and external suppliers.

The record should distinguish dependencies that are operationally essential from optional integrations.

Dependency information supports the assessment of:

- Common points of failure.
- Cross-system privilege exposure.
- Unauthorized data access paths.
- Third-party concentration risk.
- Cascading operational impacts.
- Recovery and continuity requirements.
- Security monitoring coverage.
- Change and decommissioning impacts.

Where the organization maintains a configuration management database, cloud asset inventory, or service catalog, the AI inventory should reference those authoritative records instead of duplicating detailed infrastructure data.

Material dependency changes should trigger an inventory update and, where required, a risk reassessment.

### 21. AI System Intended Purpose and Approved Use

The inventory must describe the intended purpose of the AI system and the boundaries of its approved use.

Intended purpose is important because the same technical model may create different risks depending on how it is deployed, who uses it, and what decisions or actions depend on its outputs.

The record should identify:

- The business objective.
- The permitted activities.
- The intended user groups.
- The expected outputs.
- The decisions or processes influenced.
- The degree of human involvement.
- Prohibited or out-of-scope uses.
- Important operating assumptions.

For example, an AI assistant approved to summarize internal documents should not automatically be considered approved to provide external legal advice, evaluate employee performance, or execute financial transactions.

Changes to intended purpose should be reviewed through the organization's use-case change and risk assessment process.

The inventory should distinguish the approved purpose from observed usage where monitoring reveals that actual use differs from the documented scope.

### 22. AI System User and Affected Stakeholder Classification

The inventory should identify the principal user groups and stakeholders affected by the AI system.

Users are those who directly interact with the capability. Affected stakeholders may include individuals or groups whose opportunities, rights, safety, access to services, or treatment are influenced by AI outputs even when they never interact with the system directly.

Relevant groups may include:

- Employees and contractors.
- Customers and prospective customers.
- Suppliers and business partners.
- Applicants and job candidates.
- Patients or service recipients.
- Members of the public.
- Operators responsible for downstream decisions.
- Individuals whose data is processed.
- Other affected groups identified through impact assessment.

The inventory should record the nature of the interaction and the potential consequences for affected parties.

This information helps determine whether additional privacy, human impact, accessibility, fairness, transparency, or regulatory assessments are required.

The organization should avoid recording unnecessary personal information about individual stakeholders. The inventory should capture relevant stakeholder categories and impacts rather than become a repository of personal data.

### 23. AI Autonomy and Decision Authority Classification

The inventory should record the level of autonomy and decision authority exercised by the AI system.

This information is important because an AI system that provides suggestions presents a different risk profile from one that automatically changes records, communicates externally, or initiates transactions.

An illustrative classification is:

| Level                        | Operating behavior                                            | Example                                       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Informational            | Produces information without directly changing business state | Document summarization                        |
| 2 — Advisory                 | Recommends actions for a person to decide                     | Decision support                              |
| 3 — Human-approved execution | Prepares actions that require explicit approval               | Drafting a transaction for review             |
| 4 — Bounded automation       | Executes predefined actions within enforced limits            | Automated ticket categorization and routing   |
| 5 — High-autonomy operation  | Performs multi-step actions with limited intervention         | Agent coordinating several enterprise systems |

These levels are illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's architecture and risk methodology.

The classification should also record the scope of permitted actions, approval requirements, and restrictions. Autonomy level alone does not determine risk: a narrowly bounded automated action may be low impact, while an advisory system may create substantial risk if its recommendations influence consequential decisions.

### 24. AI System Business Criticality

The inventory should identify the business criticality of each AI system.

Criticality describes the operational importance of the system and the consequences of disruption, failure, or unavailability. It is related to, but distinct from, AI risk classification.

A system may have high business criticality because it supports an essential service, even if its AI-specific risk is relatively low. Conversely, an AI system with limited operational dependency may still present high privacy or human-impact risk.

Criticality assessment should consider:

- Business process dependency.
- Service availability requirements.
- Potential financial or operational losses.
- Safety and stakeholder consequences.
- Availability of manual alternatives.
- Recovery requirements.
- Dependency on external providers.
- Impact of degraded functionality.

The classification should support resilience planning, recovery requirements, monitoring priorities, and escalation decisions.

The organization should document the methodology used to assign criticality and review the rating when the business process or dependency profile changes.

### 25. AI System Regulatory and Compliance Classification

The inventory should record the regulatory and compliance considerations that apply to each AI system, based on the organization's legal and compliance assessment process.

Relevant considerations may include sector-specific requirements, privacy and data protection obligations, contractual restrictions, consumer protection, employment requirements, safety obligations, and applicable AI-specific legislation.

For EU deployments, the inventory may need to support analysis under the EU AI Act, including the organization's role, intended purpose, and applicable risk classification where relevant.

The inventory should not assign a definitive legal classification solely through an automated label or general-purpose checklist when specialist interpretation is required.

Relevant fields may include:

- Potentially applicable jurisdictions.
- Relevant regulatory regimes.
- Organizational role in the AI value chain.
- Compliance assessment reference.
- Applicable obligations and control requirements.
- Responsible legal or compliance owner.
- Review date and known uncertainties.

The inventory should link to supporting compliance assessments and evidence rather than attempting to contain every legal analysis within the system record.

Regulatory classification should be reassessed when the intended purpose, deployment context, affected population, or applicable law changes materially.

### 26. AI System Risk Classification Field

The inventory must include an AI risk classification that reflects the organization's approved risk assessment methodology.

The initial classification may be preliminary, but it should identify the level of scrutiny required before the system proceeds.

Depending on the methodology, classification may consider:

- Potential impact on individuals and stakeholders.
- Security and privacy exposure.
- Data sensitivity.
- Level of autonomy.
- Business criticality.
- Regulatory applicability.
- Scale of deployment.
- Model and supplier uncertainty.
- Reversibility of adverse outcomes.
- Effectiveness of existing controls.

The inventory should distinguish initial or inherent risk classification from residual risk after controls have been evaluated.

Where the full assessment has not been completed, the inventory must not present the preliminary classification as a final approval decision.

A high-risk classification should trigger the applicable assessment, review, approval, and monitoring requirements. It should not automatically imply that the system is prohibited, nor should a low numerical score override mandatory restrictions or serious potential harm.

### 27. AI System Assessment and Approval References

The inventory should link to the records that demonstrate whether required AI assessments and approvals have been completed.

Relevant records may include:

- AI use-case assessments.
- AI risk assessments.
- AI impact assessments.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- Vendor assessments.
- Architecture reviews.
- Model validation records.
- Human impact assessments.
- Production approval records.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.

For each relevant assessment, the inventory should identify its status, version or reference, completion date, and next review date where applicable.

The inventory should distinguish among assessments that are not required, not yet started, in progress, completed, expired, or rejected.

A completed assessment does not automatically mean that a system has been approved for production. Approval status should be recorded separately from assessment completion.

This distinction supports accurate governance reporting and prevents incomplete or rejected systems from being incorrectly represented as authorized.

### 28. AI System Security and Control Profile

The inventory should identify the security control profile applicable to each AI system.

The profile should be based on the system's risk classification, architecture, deployment model, data sensitivity, autonomy, and relevant organizational standards.

The inventory may reference controls covering:

- Identity and access management.
- Data protection and confidentiality.
- Network and API security.
- Model and data provenance.
- Secure development and deployment.
- Prompt and retrieval security.
- Agent permissions and action restrictions.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability management.
- Incident response.
- Backup and recovery.
- Third-party assurance.

Detailed control configurations and test evidence should normally remain in their authoritative technical or governance repositories, with traceable references maintained in the inventory.

The inventory should indicate whether the required control assessment is complete and whether material deficiencies remain open.

A control profile must not be interpreted as proof that controls are implemented or effective. Those conclusions require appropriate verification evidence.

### 29. AI System Inventory Review and Certification

The organization must establish a periodic review process to confirm that inventory records remain accurate, complete, and aligned with the actual operating environment.

Review frequency should reflect system risk, rate of change, regulatory obligations, and organizational policy.

The review should confirm:

- The system still exists and remains in scope.
- Ownership and escalation contacts are current.
- Intended purpose and user groups remain accurate.
- Model and provider information is current.
- Data classifications and dependencies are correct.
- Lifecycle status matches actual operation.
- Risk and assessment references remain valid.
- Required approvals have not expired.
- Material changes have been captured.
- Retirement records and residual obligations are complete.

Certification may be performed by the business owner, technical owner, or another designated accountable person, with additional review for material or high-risk systems.

Failure to complete certification should trigger escalation according to defined rules. High-risk systems with unresolved inventory discrepancies may require restricted operation or additional assessment.

### 30. AI System Inventory Data Quality and Validation

Inventory quality must be managed through defined validation rules and reconciliation activities.

The organization should assess data quality across several dimensions:

| Quality dimension | Meaning                                                     |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness      | Required fields and in-scope systems are present            |
| Accuracy          | Recorded information reflects the actual system             |
| Consistency       | Values align across related records and repositories        |
| Timeliness        | Changes are recorded within defined timeframes              |
| Uniqueness        | Duplicate or conflicting system records are controlled      |
| Traceability      | Records link to relevant evidence and decisions             |
| Validity          | Values conform to the approved schema and permitted formats |

Validation may include automated checks, comparison with procurement and asset records, cloud discovery, identity and API inventories, software catalogs, user reporting, and targeted reviews.

Automated discovery can reveal AI usage that has not been registered, but it may not reliably establish the intended purpose, ownership, or legal classification of a system. Those attributes may require human validation.

Data quality findings should be assigned owners and tracked to resolution. Material discrepancies should be reflected in governance reporting and, where relevant, risk assessments.

### 31. Core AI System Inventory Foundations Principle

The AI System Inventory establishes the foundation for AI governance by creating a reliable, traceable record of the systems and use cases for which the organization is accountable.

The inventory must identify what exists, why it is used, who owns it, which models and providers it depends on, what information it processes, and which risk and approval processes apply. It must also remain connected to the actual operating environment through defined ownership, updates, validation, and periodic certification.

The organization must distinguish between **system registration and deployment authorization**, **business ownership and technical ownership**, **initial risk classification and residual risk**, and **inventory completeness and inventory accuracy**.

**Core Principle:** An AI System Inventory is effective only when it provides an accurate and maintained view of the organization's AI exposure and serves as a trusted foundation for risk assessment, control implementation, governance decisions, and lifecycle oversight.
