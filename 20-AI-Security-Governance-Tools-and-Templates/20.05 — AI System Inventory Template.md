# 20.05 — AI System Inventory Template

## Part 1 — AI System Inventory Foundations

### 1. Introduction to the AI System Inventory

An AI System Inventory is a structured, authoritative record of the artificial intelligence systems, models, applications, services, and AI-enabled use cases that an organization develops, acquires, deploys, operates, or materially relies upon.

The inventory provides the foundational visibility required to govern AI across the enterprise. Without an accurate inventory, an organization cannot reliably determine which AI systems are in use, who owns them, what information they process, which risks they introduce, or whether they comply with internal policies and external obligations.

AI systems may exist in many forms, including:

- Internally developed machine learning models.
- Third-party generative AI services.
- Large language models integrated into enterprise applications.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) solutions.
- AI agents capable of invoking tools or executing actions.
- AI-powered decision support and prediction systems.
- AI capabilities embedded in purchased software.
- AI services accessed through APIs.
- Experimental AI solutions and proof-of-concept deployments.
- Unauthorized or unregistered AI applications discovered through monitoring.

The inventory must cover more than formally approved production systems. Discovery should also identify experimental deployments, departmental solutions, embedded AI features, and shadow AI where relevant to the organization's risk exposure.

An inventory is not merely a list of products. It is a governance control that connects AI assets to business purpose, accountable ownership, data usage, technical dependencies, risk assessments, control requirements, approvals, and lifecycle status.

### 2. Purpose of the AI System Inventory

The purpose of the AI System Inventory is to establish a reliable source of information for AI governance, security, risk management, compliance, and operational oversight.

The inventory enables the organization to answer fundamental questions:

1. Which AI systems and use cases exist?
2. Who is accountable for each system?
3. Why is each system being used?
4. Which models, providers, datasets, and integrations does it depend on?
5. What information does it process?
6. Who may be affected by its outputs or actions?
7. What is its inherent and residual risk?
8. Which policies, controls, and approvals apply?
9. Is the system authorized for its current purpose?
10. When must it be reassessed, restricted, or retired?

The inventory also supports risk-based prioritization. An organization with hundreds of AI-enabled capabilities cannot apply identical oversight to every system. It needs reliable attributes that distinguish low-impact productivity tools from systems with significant security, privacy, safety, regulatory, or human-impact implications.

The inventory should therefore support both operational management and executive oversight. Operational teams need sufficiently detailed records to maintain individual systems, while management needs aggregated information about exposure, risk concentration, ownership gaps, and governance coverage.

### 3. AI System Inventory as an Enterprise Governance Control

The AI System Inventory must be treated as a formal governance control rather than an optional documentation exercise.

The control establishes visibility over AI assets and enables other governance processes to operate consistently. Risk assessments, security reviews, vendor assessments, impact assessments, and production approvals should reference identifiable inventory records.

The inventory should be governed by a defined process that establishes:

- An accountable inventory owner.
- A designated authoritative repository.
- Mandatory registration requirements.
- Minimum data fields.
- Data quality and validation rules.
- Update and review responsibilities.
- Access and change permissions.
- Reconciliation with other enterprise records.
- Escalation for missing or inaccurate information.
- Retirement and archival requirements.

An inventory that contains outdated ownership, incorrect system status, or missing high-risk use cases may create a false impression of governance coverage.

The organization should therefore assess not only whether an inventory exists but also whether it is complete, accurate, current, traceable, and actively used in risk decisions.

### 4. Scope of the AI System Inventory

The inventory scope must be defined according to the organization's AI exposure, operating model, regulatory obligations, and risk management objectives.

At a minimum, the organization should determine whether the inventory covers:

- AI systems developed internally.
- AI systems purchased from external suppliers.
- AI services accessed through enterprise subscriptions.
- AI functionality embedded in third-party products.
- AI models accessed through APIs.
- Internally hosted or externally hosted models.
- AI-enabled workflows and business processes.
- AI agents and autonomous or semi-autonomous systems.
- Experimental systems and pilot deployments.
- AI systems operated by contractors or service providers.
- AI capabilities used by subsidiaries or business units.
- Shadow AI discovered outside approved procurement processes.

The organization should also define whether the inventory tracks individual models, complete applications, business use cases, or multiple related records.

For example, a single enterprise application may use several models, while one model may support multiple business use cases. Recording only the application name may conceal differences in intended purpose, affected populations, data access, and risk classification.

The inventory design must therefore support relationships between systems, models, deployments, and use cases where those distinctions materially affect governance.

### 5. Definition of an AI System for Inventory Purposes

The organization must establish a practical definition of an AI system that is sufficiently consistent for inventory registration and governance.

The definition should align with applicable legal requirements, relevant standards, and the organization's internal AI governance framework. Where a formal regulatory definition applies, the organization must use the appropriate interpretation for that context.

For operational inventory purposes, an AI system may be recorded as a solution that uses an AI model or AI-enabled capability to generate predictions, recommendations, content, classifications, decisions, or actions.

The definition should account for the complete solution, including relevant software, data, interfaces, models, integrations, and human processes.

The organization should distinguish among:

| Record type    | Description                                          | Example                                 |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| AI use case    | The business purpose or activity supported by AI     | Customer support assistance             |
| AI application | The application through which the capability is used | Internal support assistant              |
| AI model       | The model generating predictions or outputs          | An approved language model              |
| AI service     | A hosted or managed AI capability                    | External inference service              |
| AI component   | A technical element supporting the system            | Embedding model or retrieval component  |
| AI deployment  | A specific operating instance or environment         | Production deployment in a cloud region |

These records may be linked rather than duplicated. The organization should define the level at which risk is assessed and ensure that related records remain traceable.

### 6. AI System Inventory Ownership

The organization must assign accountability for maintaining the AI System Inventory.

Inventory ownership involves defining the registration process, maintaining data quality requirements, coordinating updates, resolving record conflicts, and reporting completeness or accuracy issues.

Responsibilities may be distributed across AI governance, enterprise architecture, IT asset management, technology risk, or another designated function. The selected arrangement should reflect the organization's operating model.

The inventory owner should be responsible for:

- Maintaining the inventory schema and data dictionary.
- Defining mandatory fields and permitted values.
- Coordinating registration and update workflows.
- Establishing validation and reconciliation rules.
- Monitoring missing or inconsistent records.
- Managing access and modification rights.
- Coordinating periodic certification.
- Maintaining links to risk and compliance records.
- Reporting inventory quality and coverage.
- Coordinating archival and retirement processes.

The inventory owner is not automatically the owner of every AI system or every risk recorded in the inventory. Those responsibilities must be assigned separately to the relevant business, technical, and risk owners.

### 7. AI System Registration Requirements

Every in-scope AI system or use case must be registered through a defined process.

Registration should occur before production authorization and early enough in the development or acquisition lifecycle to support meaningful risk assessment. Limited experimentation may follow a separate registration process, but the organization should define its boundaries and minimum safeguards.

The registration process should capture sufficient information to identify the system, establish accountability, determine its intended purpose, and initiate risk classification.

A standard registration workflow should include:

1. Submission of an AI system or use-case registration request.
2. Assignment of a unique inventory identifier.
3. Identification of the business and technical owners.
4. Documentation of intended purpose and operating context.
5. Identification of models, providers, and key dependencies.
6. Initial data classification and impact screening.
7. Preliminary risk classification.
8. Identification of required assessments and approvals.
9. Validation of mandatory inventory fields.
10. Assignment of lifecycle status and next review requirements.

Registration does not constitute approval to deploy the system. It establishes the record needed for the subsequent governance process.

The organization should define how unregistered AI systems are handled when discovered. Depending on risk, the response may include registration, assessment, restriction, or suspension.

### 8. Unique AI System Identifier

Each inventory record must have a unique, stable identifier that supports traceability across governance systems.

The identifier should not depend solely on a product name, supplier name, model version, or informal project title because these attributes may change over time.

The identifier should be used to link the inventory record to:

- Risk assessments.
- Risk register entries.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy and impact assessments.
- Vendor assessments.
- Architecture reviews.
- Production approvals.
- Change records.
- Incidents and exceptions.
- Audit findings.
- Monitoring and assurance evidence.
- Retirement records.

A model used by multiple applications may require a separate model identifier linked to several application or use-case identifiers. Likewise, multiple deployments of the same application may require distinct deployment records where configuration or risk differs materially.

The organization should define identifier conventions, uniqueness rules, and record-merging procedures. Retired identifiers should not be casually reassigned to unrelated systems because doing so can break historical traceability.

### 9. AI System Inventory Data Model

The inventory should use a structured data model that defines the entities, attributes, and relationships required for governance.

A useful data model separates core system information from related records that have their own lifecycle and ownership.

For example, a system record may link to a model record, a vendor record, several risk assessments, multiple control mappings, and one or more deployments.

```
                   +----------------------+
                   |   AI BUSINESS USE    |
                   | Purpose / Stakeholders|
                   +----------+-----------+
                              |
                              v
+----------------+   +----------------------+   +----------------+
| AI MODEL       |<->| AI SYSTEM / APP       |<->| AI PROVIDER    |
| Version        |   | Unique ID             |   | Contract       |
| Provenance     |   | Owner / Status        |   | Assurance      |
+----------------+   +----------+-----------+   +----------------+
                                |
             +------------------+------------------+
             |                  |                  |
             v                  v                  v
   +----------------+  +----------------+  +----------------+
   | Risk Records   |  | Assessments     |  | Deployments    |
   | Inherent/Risk  |  | Security/Impact |  | Environment    |
   +----------------+  +----------------+  +----------------+
                                |
                                v
                     +----------------------+
                     | Controls and Evidence|
                     | Approvals / Reviews  |
                     +----------------------+
```

The data model should support relationships without forcing every attribute into a single oversized table.

The organization should also define the authoritative source for each attribute. For example, supplier information may be maintained in procurement systems, while vulnerability findings may reside in security tooling. The inventory can reference those systems where they remain authoritative.

### 10. AI System Inventory Mandatory Fields

The organization must define a minimum set of mandatory fields for all in-scope AI records.

Mandatory fields establish the baseline information needed to identify the system, assign responsibility, determine applicability, and initiate risk management.

An illustrative minimum schema is:

| Field                       | Description                                                               | Requirement                |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| AI System ID                | Unique and stable identifier                                              | Mandatory                  |
| System name                 | Recognizable name of the AI capability                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Use-case description        | Business purpose and intended function                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Business owner              | Accountable business representative                                       | Mandatory                  |
| Technical owner             | Responsible technical representative                                      | Mandatory                  |
| Lifecycle status            | Proposed, development, pilot, production, suspended, or retired           | Mandatory                  |
| Deployment type             | Internal, externally hosted, embedded, or hybrid                          | Mandatory                  |
| AI capability type          | Generative, predictive, classification, agentic, or other applicable type | Mandatory                  |
| Model or provider           | Known model, supplier, or service                                         | Mandatory where applicable |
| Data classification         | Highest relevant data sensitivity                                         | Mandatory                  |
| Initial risk classification | Preliminary risk level                                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Registration date           | Date the record was created                                               | Mandatory                  |
| Last review date            | Most recent inventory validation                                          | Mandatory once reviewed    |
| Next review date            | Scheduled review or certification date                                    | Mandatory                  |
| Assessment references       | Links to relevant risk and impact assessments                             | Required as applicable     |

The organization should distinguish a genuinely non-applicable field from a missing field. For example, an internally developed model may not have an external model provider, but its development owner and provenance information remain relevant.

The data dictionary must define the meaning, format, allowed values, validation rules, and ownership of each field.

### 11. AI System Name and Business Description

The inventory must provide a clear system name and a business description that enables reviewers to understand what the AI capability does and why it exists.

The name should be recognizable to operational teams and distinguish the system from similar applications or model instances.

The business description should explain:

- The problem the system addresses.
- The business process it supports.
- The intended users.
- The outputs it produces.
- The decisions or actions it influences.
- The boundaries of its approved purpose.
- Important limitations or dependencies.

Descriptions such as “AI tool,” “chatbot,” or “automation project” are insufficient for meaningful risk assessment because they do not explain the actual business function.

For example, “AI assistant for internal policy search using approved enterprise documents” is more useful than “AI chatbot” because it identifies the intended function and an important data boundary.

The description should be updated when the use case changes materially. A system originally used for internal information retrieval may require reassessment if it is later used to generate customer-facing advice or influence consequential decisions.

### 12. AI Business Owner and Technical Owner Fields

The inventory must identify both business accountability and technical responsibility.

The **business owner** is accountable for the intended purpose, business justification, user population, operational outcomes, and appropriate use of the AI capability.

The **technical owner** is responsible for the technical service or implementation according to the organization's operating model, including relevant configuration, integration, maintenance, and operational safeguards.

The record should identify each owner using an authoritative organizational identity or directory reference where possible.

It should also capture relevant contact or escalation information through approved enterprise systems rather than embedding unnecessary personal information in broadly accessible inventory records.

If an owner changes role or leaves the organization, the record must be updated. Systems without accountable ownership should be flagged for remediation because risk decisions cannot be managed effectively without a responsible party.

Where several teams share technical responsibility, the inventory should identify the primary technical owner and link to additional component owners as necessary.

### 13. AI System Lifecycle Status

The inventory must record the current lifecycle status of each AI system.

Lifecycle status supports governance decisions by distinguishing systems under consideration from systems that are actively operating or no longer authorized for use.

An illustrative lifecycle model is:

| Status                  | Meaning                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Proposed                | Use case submitted but not yet approved for development    |
| In development          | Design, build, or configuration activities are underway    |
| Pilot                   | Limited testing under defined conditions                   |
| Approved for production | Required approvals have been granted                       |
| Production              | Operating within an approved scope                         |
| Suspended               | Operation has been restricted or temporarily stopped       |
| Under review            | Reassessment or material change review is in progress      |
| Retiring                | Controlled withdrawal is underway                          |
| Retired                 | Use has ended and required closure activities are complete |
| Rejected                | The proposed use case was not authorized                   |

The organization should define allowed transitions between statuses and the approval requirements for each transition.

For example, a pilot should not automatically become production merely because testing has finished. Production authorization must satisfy the applicable risk assessment, security, privacy, operational, and approval requirements.

Suspension should be distinguishable from retirement because a suspended system may retain data, credentials, integrations, and dependencies that require active management.

### 14. AI System Deployment Model

The inventory should record how and where the AI system is deployed.

Deployment information is important because hosting models create different security, privacy, operational, and third-party risks.

Deployment models may include:

- Internally hosted on-premises systems.
- Private cloud deployments.
- Public cloud AI services.
- Third-party SaaS AI services.
- AI features embedded in enterprise software.
- Hybrid deployments involving internal and external components.
- Locally executed or edge AI systems.
- API-based access to external models.

The record should identify the relevant environment, hosting provider, deployment region where material, and important operational dependencies.

Deployment information should be sufficiently detailed to support risk analysis without duplicating the full configuration database. Detailed infrastructure records may remain in cloud asset inventories or configuration management databases, with links maintained from the AI inventory.

A change in deployment model can materially affect data handling, access boundaries, provider dependency, resilience, and regulatory obligations. Such changes should trigger review where required by the organization's risk management process.

### 15. AI Capability and System Type Classification

The inventory should classify the type of AI capability used by the system.

Classification helps determine which risks and assessments are relevant. Different AI capabilities may require different control requirements, although categories can overlap.

Illustrative types include:

- Predictive analytics and forecasting.
- Classification and recommendation.
- Computer vision.
- Speech recognition and synthesis.
- Natural language processing.
- Generative AI.
- Retrieval-augmented generation.
- AI agents and tool-using systems.
- Autonomous or semi-autonomous decision systems.
- Embedded AI functionality in commercial products.

The inventory should permit multiple classifications where appropriate. For example, a customer support solution may combine generative AI, retrieval, speech processing, and agent-based actions.

Classification should be based on actual functionality rather than marketing terminology alone. The organization should record enough detail to determine whether the system generates content, influences decisions, accesses sensitive information, or performs actions.

The classification should support assessment routing, control selection, and reporting without implying that a capability category alone determines the final risk rating.

### 16. AI Model Identification and Provenance

The inventory should identify the model or models on which the AI system depends.

Relevant information may include the model name, provider, version, source, deployment method, intended use, known limitations, and validation status.

Where the model is developed internally, provenance information may include the development team, relevant training or fine-tuning records, model artifacts, and approved release identifier.

For third-party models, the inventory should capture the provider and available model identifiers and link to relevant supplier and assurance records.

The organization should distinguish between a named model family and the actual model version or service configuration used in production. Providers may change models, routing, or capabilities over time, and such changes may affect the risk assessment.

Where a provider does not disclose all technical details, the inventory should record the information available, the limitation, and the associated uncertainty. Missing transparency should not be represented as verified model provenance.

### 17. AI Provider and Supplier Identification

The inventory must identify external providers that materially support each AI system.

Providers may include model developers, hosted inference services, SaaS vendors, cloud platforms, data suppliers, integration providers, and managed service operators.

The inventory should link each provider to the systems and use cases that depend on it.

Relevant attributes may include:

- Supplier identifier.
- Service or product name.
- Contract or procurement reference.
- Service owner.
- Data processing role.
- Hosting or processing region where relevant.
- Assurance status.
- Material service dependencies.
- Exit or substitution considerations.
- Related vendor risk assessment.

A system may depend on several suppliers, and one supplier may support many systems. The inventory should preserve these relationships rather than storing a single supplier name when that would conceal important dependencies.

Provider identification supports concentration risk analysis, incident coordination, contractual oversight, and assessment of the consequences of service disruption or provider change.

### 18. AI System Data Classification

The inventory should record the highest relevant data classification associated with the AI system, including data accessed, processed, stored, transmitted, or generated where appropriate.

The classification should follow the organization's information classification scheme.

Relevant categories may include:

- Public information.
- Internal information.
- Confidential business information.
- Restricted or highly sensitive information.
- Personal data.
- Special-category or otherwise sensitive personal data, where applicable.
- Regulated or contractually protected information.
- Credentials, secrets, or security-sensitive information.

The inventory should identify whether the system uses personal data, confidential documents, customer information, employee information, intellectual property, or other sensitive content.

A system that generates outputs from confidential sources may still create confidentiality risks even if it does not retain the original documents directly.

The recorded classification should support data access restrictions, privacy assessment, supplier review, retention decisions, and AI service approval. Detailed data lineage and processing records may be maintained in linked data governance systems.

### 19. AI System Data Flow and Processing Summary

The inventory should provide a concise summary of the system's major data flows.

The purpose is to establish where information originates, how it enters the AI system, which components process it, where it may be stored or transmitted, and how outputs are used.

The summary should consider:

- User prompts and uploaded content.
- Enterprise repositories and retrieval sources.
- Training or fine-tuning data where relevant.
- External model or inference services.
- Application logs and interaction history.
- Generated outputs and downstream systems.
- Third-party integrations.
- Retention and deletion mechanisms.

A high-level data-flow diagram may be linked to the inventory record when the system's complexity or risk justifies it.

```
[Users / Business Process]
            |
            v
[AI Application / API]
            |
            +------> [Approved Data Sources]
            |                   |
            |                   v
            |             [Retrieval / Data Layer]
            |                   |
            v                   v
       [AI Model / Service]
            |
            v
      [Output Validation]
            |
            v
[Human Review / Downstream Action]
            |
            v
 [Logs, Retention, Monitoring]
```

The diagram is illustrative and should be adapted to the actual architecture. It must not imply that every AI system uses retrieval, human review, or the same data path.

### 20. AI System Integration and Dependency Mapping

The inventory should identify important technical and business dependencies that can affect AI risk.

Dependencies may include identity providers, enterprise applications, data repositories, APIs, cloud services, model endpoints, vector databases, workflow engines, monitoring platforms, and external suppliers.

The record should distinguish dependencies that are operationally essential from optional integrations.

Dependency information supports the assessment of:

- Common points of failure.
- Cross-system privilege exposure.
- Unauthorized data access paths.
- Third-party concentration risk.
- Cascading operational impacts.
- Recovery and continuity requirements.
- Security monitoring coverage.
- Change and decommissioning impacts.

Where the organization maintains a configuration management database, cloud asset inventory, or service catalog, the AI inventory should reference those authoritative records instead of duplicating detailed infrastructure data.

Material dependency changes should trigger an inventory update and, where required, a risk reassessment.

### 21. AI System Intended Purpose and Approved Use

The inventory must describe the intended purpose of the AI system and the boundaries of its approved use.

Intended purpose is important because the same technical model may create different risks depending on how it is deployed, who uses it, and what decisions or actions depend on its outputs.

The record should identify:

- The business objective.
- The permitted activities.
- The intended user groups.
- The expected outputs.
- The decisions or processes influenced.
- The degree of human involvement.
- Prohibited or out-of-scope uses.
- Important operating assumptions.

For example, an AI assistant approved to summarize internal documents should not automatically be considered approved to provide external legal advice, evaluate employee performance, or execute financial transactions.

Changes to intended purpose should be reviewed through the organization's use-case change and risk assessment process.

The inventory should distinguish the approved purpose from observed usage where monitoring reveals that actual use differs from the documented scope.

### 22. AI System User and Affected Stakeholder Classification

The inventory should identify the principal user groups and stakeholders affected by the AI system.

Users are those who directly interact with the capability. Affected stakeholders may include individuals or groups whose opportunities, rights, safety, access to services, or treatment are influenced by AI outputs even when they never interact with the system directly.

Relevant groups may include:

- Employees and contractors.
- Customers and prospective customers.
- Suppliers and business partners.
- Applicants and job candidates.
- Patients or service recipients.
- Members of the public.
- Operators responsible for downstream decisions.
- Individuals whose data is processed.
- Other affected groups identified through impact assessment.

The inventory should record the nature of the interaction and the potential consequences for affected parties.

This information helps determine whether additional privacy, human impact, accessibility, fairness, transparency, or regulatory assessments are required.

The organization should avoid recording unnecessary personal information about individual stakeholders. The inventory should capture relevant stakeholder categories and impacts rather than become a repository of personal data.

### 23. AI Autonomy and Decision Authority Classification

The inventory should record the level of autonomy and decision authority exercised by the AI system.

This information is important because an AI system that provides suggestions presents a different risk profile from one that automatically changes records, communicates externally, or initiates transactions.

An illustrative classification is:

| Level                        | Operating behavior                                            | Example                                       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Informational            | Produces information without directly changing business state | Document summarization                        |
| 2 — Advisory                 | Recommends actions for a person to decide                     | Decision support                              |
| 3 — Human-approved execution | Prepares actions that require explicit approval               | Drafting a transaction for review             |
| 4 — Bounded automation       | Executes predefined actions within enforced limits            | Automated ticket categorization and routing   |
| 5 — High-autonomy operation  | Performs multi-step actions with limited intervention         | Agent coordinating several enterprise systems |

These levels are illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's architecture and risk methodology.

The classification should also record the scope of permitted actions, approval requirements, and restrictions. Autonomy level alone does not determine risk: a narrowly bounded automated action may be low impact, while an advisory system may create substantial risk if its recommendations influence consequential decisions.

### 24. AI System Business Criticality

The inventory should identify the business criticality of each AI system.

Criticality describes the operational importance of the system and the consequences of disruption, failure, or unavailability. It is related to, but distinct from, AI risk classification.

A system may have high business criticality because it supports an essential service, even if its AI-specific risk is relatively low. Conversely, an AI system with limited operational dependency may still present high privacy or human-impact risk.

Criticality assessment should consider:

- Business process dependency.
- Service availability requirements.
- Potential financial or operational losses.
- Safety and stakeholder consequences.
- Availability of manual alternatives.
- Recovery requirements.
- Dependency on external providers.
- Impact of degraded functionality.

The classification should support resilience planning, recovery requirements, monitoring priorities, and escalation decisions.

The organization should document the methodology used to assign criticality and review the rating when the business process or dependency profile changes.

### 25. AI System Regulatory and Compliance Classification

The inventory should record the regulatory and compliance considerations that apply to each AI system, based on the organization's legal and compliance assessment process.

Relevant considerations may include sector-specific requirements, privacy and data protection obligations, contractual restrictions, consumer protection, employment requirements, safety obligations, and applicable AI-specific legislation.

For EU deployments, the inventory may need to support analysis under the EU AI Act, including the organization's role, intended purpose, and applicable risk classification where relevant.

The inventory should not assign a definitive legal classification solely through an automated label or general-purpose checklist when specialist interpretation is required.

Relevant fields may include:

- Potentially applicable jurisdictions.
- Relevant regulatory regimes.
- Organizational role in the AI value chain.
- Compliance assessment reference.
- Applicable obligations and control requirements.
- Responsible legal or compliance owner.
- Review date and known uncertainties.

The inventory should link to supporting compliance assessments and evidence rather than attempting to contain every legal analysis within the system record.

Regulatory classification should be reassessed when the intended purpose, deployment context, affected population, or applicable law changes materially.

### 26. AI System Risk Classification Field

The inventory must include an AI risk classification that reflects the organization's approved risk assessment methodology.

The initial classification may be preliminary, but it should identify the level of scrutiny required before the system proceeds.

Depending on the methodology, classification may consider:

- Potential impact on individuals and stakeholders.
- Security and privacy exposure.
- Data sensitivity.
- Level of autonomy.
- Business criticality.
- Regulatory applicability.
- Scale of deployment.
- Model and supplier uncertainty.
- Reversibility of adverse outcomes.
- Effectiveness of existing controls.

The inventory should distinguish initial or inherent risk classification from residual risk after controls have been evaluated.

Where the full assessment has not been completed, the inventory must not present the preliminary classification as a final approval decision.

A high-risk classification should trigger the applicable assessment, review, approval, and monitoring requirements. It should not automatically imply that the system is prohibited, nor should a low numerical score override mandatory restrictions or serious potential harm.

### 27. AI System Assessment and Approval References

The inventory should link to the records that demonstrate whether required AI assessments and approvals have been completed.

Relevant records may include:

- AI use-case assessments.
- AI risk assessments.
- AI impact assessments.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- Vendor assessments.
- Architecture reviews.
- Model validation records.
- Human impact assessments.
- Production approval records.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.

For each relevant assessment, the inventory should identify its status, version or reference, completion date, and next review date where applicable.

The inventory should distinguish among assessments that are not required, not yet started, in progress, completed, expired, or rejected.

A completed assessment does not automatically mean that a system has been approved for production. Approval status should be recorded separately from assessment completion.

This distinction supports accurate governance reporting and prevents incomplete or rejected systems from being incorrectly represented as authorized.

### 28. AI System Security and Control Profile

The inventory should identify the security control profile applicable to each AI system.

The profile should be based on the system's risk classification, architecture, deployment model, data sensitivity, autonomy, and relevant organizational standards.

The inventory may reference controls covering:

- Identity and access management.
- Data protection and confidentiality.
- Network and API security.
- Model and data provenance.
- Secure development and deployment.
- Prompt and retrieval security.
- Agent permissions and action restrictions.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability management.
- Incident response.
- Backup and recovery.
- Third-party assurance.

Detailed control configurations and test evidence should normally remain in their authoritative technical or governance repositories, with traceable references maintained in the inventory.

The inventory should indicate whether the required control assessment is complete and whether material deficiencies remain open.

A control profile must not be interpreted as proof that controls are implemented or effective. Those conclusions require appropriate verification evidence.

### 29. AI System Inventory Review and Certification

The organization must establish a periodic review process to confirm that inventory records remain accurate, complete, and aligned with the actual operating environment.

Review frequency should reflect system risk, rate of change, regulatory obligations, and organizational policy.

The review should confirm:

- The system still exists and remains in scope.
- Ownership and escalation contacts are current.
- Intended purpose and user groups remain accurate.
- Model and provider information is current.
- Data classifications and dependencies are correct.
- Lifecycle status matches actual operation.
- Risk and assessment references remain valid.
- Required approvals have not expired.
- Material changes have been captured.
- Retirement records and residual obligations are complete.

Certification may be performed by the business owner, technical owner, or another designated accountable person, with additional review for material or high-risk systems.

Failure to complete certification should trigger escalation according to defined rules. High-risk systems with unresolved inventory discrepancies may require restricted operation or additional assessment.

### 30. AI System Inventory Data Quality and Validation

Inventory quality must be managed through defined validation rules and reconciliation activities.

The organization should assess data quality across several dimensions:

| Quality dimension | Meaning                                                     |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness      | Required fields and in-scope systems are present            |
| Accuracy          | Recorded information reflects the actual system             |
| Consistency       | Values align across related records and repositories        |
| Timeliness        | Changes are recorded within defined timeframes              |
| Uniqueness        | Duplicate or conflicting system records are controlled      |
| Traceability      | Records link to relevant evidence and decisions             |
| Validity          | Values conform to the approved schema and permitted formats |

Validation may include automated checks, comparison with procurement and asset records, cloud discovery, identity and API inventories, software catalogs, user reporting, and targeted reviews.

Automated discovery can reveal AI usage that has not been registered, but it may not reliably establish the intended purpose, ownership, or legal classification of a system. Those attributes may require human validation.

Data quality findings should be assigned owners and tracked to resolution. Material discrepancies should be reflected in governance reporting and, where relevant, risk assessments.

### 31. Core AI System Inventory Foundations Principle

The AI System Inventory establishes the foundation for AI governance by creating a reliable, traceable record of the systems and use cases for which the organization is accountable.

The inventory must identify what exists, why it is used, who owns it, which models and providers it depends on, what information it processes, and which risk and approval processes apply. It must also remain connected to the actual operating environment through defined ownership, updates, validation, and periodic certification.

The organization must distinguish between **system registration and deployment authorization**, **business ownership and technical ownership**, **initial risk classification and residual risk**, and **inventory completeness and inventory accuracy**.

**Core Principle:** An AI System Inventory is effective only when it provides an accurate and maintained view of the organization's AI exposure and serves as a trusted foundation for risk assessment, control implementation, governance decisions, and lifecycle oversight.

# 20.05 — AI System Inventory Template

## Part 2 — AI System Inventory Controls

### 32. AI System Inventory Control Framework

The AI System Inventory must operate within a formal control framework that defines how AI systems are discovered, registered, classified, maintained, validated, and retired.

The framework should establish mandatory requirements for inventory completeness, data quality, ownership, access, change management, and evidence retention. These requirements must be integrated with existing enterprise processes rather than managed as an isolated administrative activity.

The inventory control framework should define:

- **Control objectives:** The governance outcomes expected from inventory management.
- **Registration requirements:** The conditions under which AI systems and use cases must be recorded.
- **Data requirements:** Mandatory attributes, formats, permitted values, and validation rules.
- **Ownership requirements:** Accountability for inventory maintenance and record accuracy.
- **Lifecycle controls:** Procedures for registration, approval, change, suspension, and retirement.
- **Verification requirements:** Methods for validating inventory completeness and accuracy.
- **Escalation requirements:** Actions for missing, inaccurate, or unauthorized records.
- **Evidence requirements:** Records demonstrating that inventory controls operate effectively.

The framework should distinguish between maintaining the inventory and managing the risks of the systems recorded in it. Inventory controls establish visibility and traceability; other governance controls determine whether a system is acceptable for its intended use.

### 33. Mandatory AI System Registration Control

All AI systems and use cases within the organization's defined inventory scope must be registered through an approved process.

Registration must occur early enough to enable risk classification and assessment before consequential development, procurement, or deployment decisions are made.

The registration control should require:

1. Identification of the proposed system or use case.
2. Assignment of a unique inventory identifier.
3. Identification of business and technical owners.
4. Documentation of intended purpose.
5. Identification of known models, providers, and dependencies.
6. Preliminary data classification.
7. Initial risk screening.
8. Assignment of lifecycle status.
9. Identification of required assessments.
10. Validation of mandatory fields.

The organization should define the registration deadline for each lifecycle stage. For example, an externally acquired AI service should not be introduced into a production business process before the relevant registration and approval requirements are satisfied.

Registration must not be interpreted as permission to operate. The inventory record should distinguish registered systems from systems that have received all required authorizations.

### 34. AI Inventory Scope Enforcement Control

The organization must define mechanisms for ensuring that all in-scope AI systems are captured in the inventory.

Relying exclusively on voluntary declarations by business units may result in incomplete coverage, particularly when AI functionality is embedded in existing software or accessed through external services.

Scope enforcement should use multiple discovery methods, including:

- Procurement and supplier records.
- Software asset inventories.
- Cloud service inventories.
- Application and API catalogs.
- Enterprise architecture records.
- Approved AI service subscriptions.
- Identity and access records.
- Security monitoring and network telemetry.
- Developer and platform team reporting.
- Periodic business-unit attestations.
- Reports of unauthorized or unregistered AI usage.

Discovery results must be evaluated before being treated as confirmed AI inventory records. A detected endpoint or application name may indicate potential AI usage without establishing the intended purpose, ownership, or deployment status.

The organization should maintain a process for investigating discoveries, assigning ownership, registering confirmed systems, and escalating unresolved cases.

### 35. AI System Inventory Data Dictionary Control

The inventory must use a documented data dictionary that defines the meaning and permitted use of each attribute.

Without a consistent data dictionary, different teams may interpret fields differently, producing inconsistent classifications and unreliable reports.

For every inventory field, the data dictionary should specify:

- Field name and unique identifier, where applicable.
- Business definition.
- Data type and accepted format.
- Mandatory or conditional status.
- Permitted values.
- Validation rules.
- Authoritative source.
- Accountable data owner.
- Update frequency.
- Handling of unavailable or non-applicable information.

For example, the field `Lifecycle Status` should use an approved set of values rather than unrestricted text such as “active,” “ongoing,” “live,” or “in progress” without defined meanings.

Changes to the data dictionary should be version-controlled and assessed for their impact on existing records, integrations, reports, and governance workflows.

### 36. AI Inventory Record Uniqueness Control

The organization must prevent duplicate or conflicting inventory records from undermining system visibility and accountability.

Each record should have a stable identifier independent of changeable attributes such as application name, supplier, or model version.

The inventory process should define rules for identifying and resolving duplicates, including situations in which:

- The same AI service is registered by multiple departments.
- A product is recorded under several commercial names.
- One application uses several models.
- One model supports several business use cases.
- A replacement system is mistakenly recorded as the original system.
- A pilot is converted into a production deployment.
- Multiple deployments share an application but differ materially in configuration.

The organization should distinguish between duplicate records and legitimate relationships. A single model supporting several applications should normally be represented through linked records rather than by deleting valid application-level entries.

Merging records must preserve relevant historical assessments, approvals, incidents, and audit evidence. The organization should retain a traceable record of the merge and identify the surviving authoritative identifier.

### 37. AI Inventory Ownership Validation Control

The organization must verify that every in-scope AI system has an accountable business owner and an identified technical owner or equivalent responsible role.

Ownership information should be validated during registration, periodic review, material change, and organizational restructuring.

The control should identify:

- The accountable business function.
- The named or role-based business owner.
- The technical owner or service owner.
- The relevant escalation route.
- The date of the last ownership confirmation.
- The process for replacing an unavailable owner.

Where ownership is disputed, the inventory governance function should assign an interim accountable function and escalate the issue for resolution.

A system without a confirmed owner should not remain indefinitely in an approved state without review. Depending on its risk, the organization may require a remediation deadline, restricted use, or suspension until accountability is established.

### 38. AI Inventory Data Quality Control

The organization must establish controls to maintain the completeness, accuracy, consistency, validity, uniqueness, and timeliness of inventory records.

Data quality requirements should be measurable and supported by validation procedures.

| Quality dimension | Example control                                              |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Completeness      | Identify missing mandatory attributes                        |
| Accuracy          | Confirm recorded details against authoritative sources       |
| Consistency       | Compare lifecycle status across linked systems               |
| Timeliness        | Measure delay between a material change and inventory update |
| Uniqueness        | Detect duplicate identifiers and potential duplicate records |
| Validity          | Reject values outside approved formats or classifications    |
| Traceability      | Verify links to assessments and approvals                    |

Automated validation should be used where practical, but human review remains necessary for attributes requiring contextual judgment.

Data quality findings should be assigned to accountable owners with defined resolution dates. Material errors that affect risk classification, legal applicability, or production authorization must be escalated promptly.

### 39. AI Inventory Update and Change Control

Inventory records must be updated when material information about an AI system changes.

The organization should define which changes require an update, who must initiate it, and how quickly the update must occur.

Triggers may include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- A new model or material model-version change.
- A new provider or hosting arrangement.
- A change in data classification.
- New integrations or external destinations.
- Expanded user access.
- Increased autonomy or new agent capabilities.
- A material change in deployment environment.
- A change in business or technical ownership.
- A suspension, replacement, or retirement decision.

The change process should determine whether the inventory update also requires risk reassessment, security review, privacy review, architecture review, or renewed production approval.

Changes must be traceable to the person or process that initiated them. The organization should retain the previous record values or an appropriate change history to support audit and incident reconstruction.

### 40. AI Inventory Lifecycle Status Validation Control

The organization must verify that the recorded lifecycle status reflects the actual state of the AI system.

Status changes should follow defined transition rules and require appropriate authorization where the transition affects risk or operating permission.

For example, moving from development to pilot may require limited-scope approval, while moving from pilot to production may require completed assessments and formal production authorization.

The control should identify:

- Permitted status transitions.
- Required approvals for each transition.
- Evidence needed to support the transition.
- Ownership of status updates.
- Conditions requiring suspension.
- Retirement and closure requirements.
- Procedures for resolving inconsistent status records.

The organization should compare inventory status with operational evidence where feasible. A system recorded as retired but still accessible through an active API or account represents a control deficiency requiring investigation.

### 41. AI Model and Version Update Control

The inventory must maintain sufficient model identification and version information to support risk assessment and change traceability.

Where the model provider exposes a version identifier, deployment teams should record the version or other authoritative reference relevant to the deployed configuration.

The control should establish how the organization handles:

- New model versions.
- Provider-managed model changes.
- Model replacement.
- Fine-tuning or customization.
- Changes to inference settings.
- Changes to routing between models.
- Model deprecation or withdrawal.
- Unknown or insufficiently disclosed model changes.

A model version change does not automatically require a complete new assessment in every case. The organization should apply documented materiality criteria to determine whether targeted validation, a focused reassessment, or a full review is required.

Where the provider can change model behavior without adequate advance notice, the organization should record the resulting uncertainty and establish monitoring or contractual safeguards appropriate to the risk.

### 42. AI Provider and Supplier Record Validation Control

The inventory must maintain accurate relationships between AI systems and their external providers.

Supplier records should be reconciled with procurement, contract management, vendor risk management, and service ownership records where those systems exist.

Validation should confirm:

- The correct provider identity.
- The relevant product or service.
- The contractual or procurement reference.
- The relationship between provider and AI system.
- The data processing or hosting role, where applicable.
- The current supplier assessment status.
- The relevant service owner.
- Material dependencies and exit considerations.

Where a system uses multiple providers, the inventory should capture each material dependency rather than recording only the primary commercial supplier.

A supplier change must trigger a review of the affected inventory relationships and determine whether existing risk assessments and approvals remain valid.

### 43. AI Data Classification Validation Control

The organization must validate the data classification recorded for each AI system against the information it actually processes or can access.

The assessment should consider input data, retrieved content, model context, generated outputs, persistent memory, logs, and downstream information flows where relevant.

Validation methods may include:

- Review of approved data sources.
- Data-flow analysis.
- Data classification metadata checks.
- Privacy and data protection reviews.
- Security testing.
- Review of access permissions.
- Inspection of retention and logging configurations.
- Confirmation by data owners.

The organization should not assume that a system processes only low-sensitivity data because its main user interface appears to handle general questions. Connected repositories, plugins, APIs, or agent tools may expose more sensitive information.

Material discrepancies between recorded and actual data classification should trigger inventory correction and risk reassessment.

### 44. AI System Data Flow Documentation Control

The inventory must reference a current data-flow description for systems whose complexity or risk warrants formal documentation.

The data-flow record should identify relevant sources, processing components, destinations, external providers, storage locations, and significant trust boundaries.

The control should define:

- Minimum documentation requirements by risk level.
- Ownership of the data-flow record.
- Required review and approval.
- Version control.
- Update triggers.
- Links to architecture and privacy assessments.
- Verification of material data paths.
- Escalation for undocumented processing.

A diagram must reflect the actual system rather than a generic reference architecture. If the deployed system includes additional retrieval sources, logging services, or external APIs, those components should be represented where they affect risk.

The organization should periodically verify important data paths and update documentation when architecture changes alter the processing of sensitive information.

### 45. AI Integration and Dependency Record Control

The inventory must maintain records of material AI system dependencies and integrations.

Dependency records should identify the connected service, its purpose, the direction of information flow, the responsible owner, and the significance of the dependency to system operation.

Relevant dependencies may include:

- Identity and authentication services.
- Enterprise data repositories.
- Model endpoints.
- Retrieval and vector database services.
- External APIs.
- Workflow engines.
- Cloud infrastructure.
- Logging and monitoring platforms.
- Third-party AI providers.
- Downstream business applications.

The organization should identify dependencies that create elevated risk because they provide access to sensitive data, enable consequential actions, or represent a single point of failure.

Changes to material dependencies should be captured through change management and evaluated for potential impacts on security, privacy, availability, and risk classification.

### 46. AI Intended-Purpose and Use-Case Change Control

The inventory must preserve the intended purpose and approved use of each AI system.

A change in business purpose may materially alter the risks associated with an unchanged model or application.

The organization should require review when a system is proposed for a new activity, user group, decision process, or operational environment.

Examples include:

- Moving from internal drafting to external customer communications.
- Using an assistant for employee evaluation.
- Expanding from decision support to automated decisions.
- Connecting a model to sensitive business repositories.
- Enabling an agent to execute transactions.
- Using a general-purpose assistant in a regulated workflow.

The review should determine whether the revised purpose remains within the existing approval and assessment scope.

The inventory should distinguish the approved purpose from observed usage. Where actual use departs materially from the approved scope, the organization should initiate investigation, correction, and reassessment.

### 47. AI User and Stakeholder Classification Control

The organization must maintain sufficiently accurate information about the user groups and affected stakeholders associated with each AI system.

The control should identify changes in the population that uses the system or may be affected by its outputs.

Validation should consider:

- Changes in user groups.
- Expansion to external customers.
- New employee or contractor access.
- Use in decisions affecting individuals.
- Changes in affected populations.
- Introduction of vulnerable or otherwise materially affected groups.
- New downstream consumers of AI-generated outputs.

The purpose is to ensure that impact assessments, privacy reviews, training, transparency measures, and human oversight requirements remain appropriate.

The inventory should capture stakeholder categories and relevant impacts without unnecessarily collecting individual-level personal information.

### 48. AI Autonomy and Action Authority Validation Control

The inventory must accurately record the extent to which an AI system can recommend, initiate, or execute actions.

The control should validate the documented autonomy level against actual system permissions and operational workflows.

Verification may include:

- Reviewing tool and API permissions.
- Inspecting execution paths.
- Testing approval gates.
- Reviewing transaction limits.
- Confirming human intervention points.
- Checking for indirect or alternative execution paths.
- Examining logs of consequential actions.
- Reviewing changes to agent capabilities.

A system documented as advisory should not have unrestricted technical access to execute consequential actions without the appropriate authorization controls.

If actual capabilities exceed the recorded scope, the organization must correct the inventory and assess whether immediate restrictions are required.

### 49. AI Business Criticality Validation Control

The organization must review the business criticality rating when the operational role of an AI system changes.

Criticality should reflect the consequences of system disruption or failure, not simply the number of users or the technical complexity of the solution.

Validation should consider:

- Changes in business process dependency.
- Expansion into essential operations.
- Increased transaction volume.
- Reduced availability of manual alternatives.
- Changes to recovery requirements.
- New downstream dependencies.
- Increased consequences of degraded output quality.
- Changes in supplier or infrastructure dependency.

A system may become more critical over time as business processes become dependent on it. The inventory must reflect this evolution so that resilience, monitoring, support, and recovery arrangements remain proportionate.

### 50. AI Regulatory Applicability and Compliance Reference Control

The inventory must maintain links to relevant regulatory and compliance assessments.

The purpose is to ensure that the organization can identify which AI systems require specialized review and which obligations or control requirements apply.

The control should define:

- Who determines regulatory applicability.
- Which fields must be recorded.
- How assessments are linked.
- How uncertainty is documented.
- When specialist legal or compliance review is required.
- How regulatory changes trigger reassessment.
- How compliance evidence is referenced.
- How obsolete conclusions are corrected.

The inventory should not substitute a simplified classification field for a full legal assessment where the circumstances require specialist interpretation.

Where regulatory applicability is uncertain, the record should show that the determination is pending and identify the owner and target date for resolution.

### 51. AI Risk Classification and Assessment Linkage Control

The inventory must link each in-scope AI system to the appropriate risk assessment records.

The risk classification shown in the inventory should be traceable to the approved assessment methodology and supporting evidence.

The control should distinguish:

- Preliminary risk screening.
- Inherent risk classification.
- Residual risk classification.
- Approval status.
- Risk acceptance status.
- Outstanding assessment actions.

These attributes must not be collapsed into a single status field because they represent different decisions.

For example, a system may have a completed risk assessment but remain unauthorized for production because mandatory safeguards have not been implemented.

Inventory reports should make these distinctions visible so that management does not interpret assessment completion as evidence of acceptable residual risk or production authorization.

### 52. AI Assessment Expiry and Review-Date Control

The inventory must track review dates and assessment validity requirements for relevant AI systems.

The organization should define how periodic review dates are determined according to risk, regulatory obligations, system characteristics, and materiality of change.

The control should generate notifications for upcoming reviews and escalate overdue assessments according to established thresholds.

It should also distinguish scheduled reviews from event-driven reassessments. A system that experiences a material security incident may require immediate review even if its next scheduled assessment is months away.

The inventory should record:

- Last review date.
- Next scheduled review date.
- Required assessment type.
- Responsible reviewer or owner.
- Review status.
- Overdue duration, where applicable.
- Escalation and resulting decision.

Expired or overdue records should not automatically be assumed to invalidate a system's authorization in every case. The organization must define the consequences based on the relevant policy and risk level.

### 53. AI Production Approval Status Control

The inventory must maintain an explicit record of production authorization.

Production approval should be separate from registration, risk assessment completion, and technical readiness.

The control should verify that required approvals and conditions have been satisfied before a system enters production.

Relevant information may include:

- Approval decision and reference.
- Authorized scope and purpose.
- Approving authority.
- Approval date.
- Applicable conditions.
- Required safeguards.
- Expiry or review date where applicable.
- Links to supporting assessments.
- Conditions for suspension or withdrawal.

Where approval is conditional, the inventory should distinguish conditions that must be met before launch from actions permitted after launch under a documented authorization.

A system must not be marked as approved merely because it is available in a software catalog or because a supplier has made the service accessible.

### 54. AI System Control Profile and Control Mapping

The inventory should reference the controls applicable to each AI system.

The control profile should be derived from the system's risk classification, deployment model, data sensitivity, autonomy, and relevant requirements.

The control mapping should connect the system to relevant policy controls, technical safeguards, control owners, and evidence repositories.

The inventory process should verify that:

- Required controls have been identified.
- Control owners are assigned.
- Applicable controls have defined implementation status.
- Material deficiencies are recorded.
- Evidence references are available.
- Exceptions are approved and current.
- Control changes trigger relevant updates.

A mapped control is not necessarily implemented or effective. The inventory should distinguish required controls, implemented controls, tested controls, and controls with unresolved deficiencies.

### 55. AI System Security Assessment Linkage Control

The inventory must link to security assessments required by the organization's governance framework.

Security assessments should evaluate relevant threats, architecture weaknesses, access controls, data exposure, integrations, monitoring, and incident response capabilities.

The inventory should record the assessment reference, status, date, responsible reviewer, and outstanding material findings where appropriate.

The control should ensure that a system's security assessment is updated when material changes occur, including changes to model endpoints, agent permissions, retrieval sources, data sensitivity, or external integrations.

Security approval should not automatically be interpreted as approval of all AI risks. Privacy, safety, business, legal, and stakeholder-impact risks may require separate assessments or approvals.

### 56. AI System Privacy and Impact Assessment Linkage Control

The inventory must identify systems that require privacy, data protection, or broader impact assessment.

The assessment requirement should be based on the nature of the processing, intended purpose, affected stakeholders, potential consequences, and applicable obligations.

The control should ensure that the inventory references:

- Privacy assessments.
- Data protection impact assessments where required.
- Human impact assessments where applicable.
- Relevant safety or ethical assessments.
- Decisions concerning assessment applicability.
- Outstanding findings and treatment actions.

A completed privacy assessment does not necessarily establish that the system is acceptable from a broader AI risk perspective. The inventory should preserve the relationship between the relevant assessments without treating them as interchangeable.

When a material change affects personal data processing or stakeholder impact, the organization should reassess whether existing conclusions remain valid.

### 57. AI Vendor Assessment Linkage Control

The inventory must link externally provided AI capabilities to the appropriate supplier risk records.

Vendor assessment requirements should reflect the supplier's role, data access, criticality, contractual commitments, service dependency, and potential consequences of failure.

The control should capture whether relevant supplier due diligence has been completed and whether material findings remain unresolved.

It should also support traceability between a supplier and all dependent AI systems so that one provider incident or assurance gap can be evaluated across the affected estate.

Where a provider is not subject to the organization's standard procurement process, the organization should define an alternative review path rather than treating the missing procurement record as evidence that supplier risk is not applicable.

### 58. AI Inventory Access Control

Access to the inventory must be restricted according to business need and the sensitivity of its contents.

Although inventory records are often administrative, they may reveal sensitive architecture details, supplier dependencies, high-value systems, risk weaknesses, or security controls.

The organization should define roles for:

- Inventory administration.
- Record creation and modification.
- Business ownership certification.
- Risk and compliance review.
- Technical record maintenance.
- Read-only reporting.
- Audit and assurance access.

Controls should include authentication, authorization, least privilege, access reviews, and logging of material changes.

The organization should consider whether different audiences require different views. Broad management reports may contain aggregated risk information, while detailed technical dependency records may be restricted to authorized personnel.

Access restrictions must not prevent legitimate risk oversight, audit, or incident response.

### 59. AI Inventory Change History and Audit Trail Control

The inventory must maintain a change history sufficient to explain how material records have evolved.

The history should identify the affected record, changed attributes, change date, initiating identity or process, and approval reference where applicable.

The organization should preserve historical values for material attributes such as:

- System owner.
- Intended purpose.
- Model and provider.
- Deployment status.
- Data classification.
- Risk classification.
- Production approval.
- Lifecycle status.
- Assessment references.
- Retirement decision.

Change history supports audit, incident investigation, and reconstruction of the system configuration and governance status at a particular time.

The organization should protect change records against unauthorized alteration and define retention requirements.

A current inventory snapshot alone may be insufficient when the organization needs to establish what was known or approved before a material incident.

### 60. AI Inventory Reconciliation Control

The organization should periodically reconcile the AI inventory with authoritative enterprise records and available technical discovery sources.

Reconciliation can identify unregistered systems, outdated supplier relationships, inactive applications, ownership gaps, and discrepancies between declared and observed usage.

Potential reconciliation sources include:

- Procurement and contract management.
- IT asset management.
- Enterprise application catalogs.
- Cloud asset inventories.
- API management platforms.
- Identity and access management systems.
- Security monitoring.
- Model registries.
- Software development repositories.
- Service management platforms.

The reconciliation process should define matching criteria, exception handling, record ownership, and resolution deadlines.

Differences should be investigated rather than automatically resolved by overwriting one source with another. The appropriate authoritative source may differ by attribute, and technical discovery may not establish business purpose or approval status.

### 61. AI Shadow Usage Discovery and Remediation Control

The organization must establish a process for identifying and handling AI use that occurs outside approved governance channels.

Shadow AI may include personal accounts used for business data, unapproved SaaS services, unauthorized model APIs, embedded AI features activated without review, or departmental tools that have not been registered.

Discovery methods may include user declarations, procurement reviews, access telemetry, network monitoring, endpoint controls, API usage analysis, and targeted assessments.

When shadow usage is identified, the organization should determine:

- Whether the activity involves business information.
- Which data types are exposed.
- Whether credentials or sensitive information were disclosed.
- Whether the service retains or reuses submitted data.
- Whether the use case has a legitimate business need.
- Whether the service can be approved with appropriate safeguards.
- Whether immediate containment is necessary.
- Whether an incident or regulatory review is required.

The response should be proportionate to the exposure. The organization should not assume that every unregistered AI interaction is a major security incident, but it must not ignore unauthorized processing or disclosure.

Where a legitimate need exists, a controlled onboarding path may reduce future shadow usage.

### 62. AI System Retirement and Decommissioning Control

The inventory must support the controlled retirement of AI systems and the closure of their associated governance obligations.

A system should not be marked as retired solely because a project has ended or a user interface is no longer visible.

Retirement validation should consider:

- Confirmation that production use has ended.
- Removal or disabling of endpoints.
- Revocation of identities and credentials.
- Closure of integrations and tool access.
- Supplier contract or subscription status.
- Data retention and deletion requirements.
- Preservation of required evidence.
- Update of dependent-system records.
- Closure or transfer of outstanding risks.
- Verification that unauthorized access is no longer possible.

The inventory should record the retirement decision, effective date, responsible owner, and relevant closure evidence.

Retired records should remain available for historical traceability in accordance with retention requirements. Removing a record entirely may destroy the evidence needed to understand prior risk decisions or investigate historical incidents.

### 63. AI Inventory Exception Management Control

Exceptions to inventory requirements must be documented and approved through a defined process.

Examples may include temporary inability to obtain provider details, incomplete technical documentation during a limited pilot, or a justified delay in updating a non-critical attribute.

An exception should identify:

- The requirement not being met.
- The reason for the deviation.
- The affected system or record.
- The associated risk.
- The compensating measure.
- The approving authority.
- The effective and expiry dates.
- The remediation owner and target date.
- The conditions requiring early escalation.

Exceptions should not be used to permanently avoid registering high-risk systems, assigning accountable owners, or recording mandatory approvals.

Where missing information prevents reliable risk assessment or legal classification, the organization should apply an appropriate restriction until the uncertainty is resolved.

### 64. AI Inventory Incident-Driven Update Control

Material incidents must trigger review of the relevant inventory records.

An incident may reveal that a system's actual purpose, data flows, dependencies, permissions, or operational status differs from the recorded information.

The incident process should identify whether inventory corrections are required and ensure that those corrections are linked to the incident and any resulting risk reassessment.

Examples include:

- Discovery of an undocumented external integration.
- Evidence that an AI agent has broader permissions than recorded.
- Disclosure of information from an unlisted data source.
- Identification of an unregistered model or service.
- Discovery that a supposedly retired endpoint remains accessible.
- A provider change that was not reflected in the inventory.

The organization should assess whether similar systems may share the same deficiency. An incident involving one AI system may reveal an inventory control weakness affecting the broader environment.

### 65. AI Inventory Governance Metrics

The organization should establish metrics to evaluate inventory coverage, quality, and operational effectiveness.

Metrics should have defined calculation methods, data sources, owners, reporting frequencies, and targets.

Illustrative metrics include:

| Metric                       | Purpose                                                                   |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Inventory coverage           | Measures the proportion of identified in-scope AI systems registered      |
| Mandatory-field completeness | Measures records with all required attributes populated                   |
| Ownership coverage           | Measures systems with confirmed business and technical owners             |
| Registration timeliness      | Measures how quickly identified systems are registered                    |
| Review completion            | Measures systems reviewed within the required period                      |
| Assessment linkage           | Measures systems linked to required assessment records                    |
| Duplicate rate               | Measures potential duplicate or conflicting records                       |
| Change-update timeliness     | Measures whether material changes are recorded on time                    |
| Retirement verification      | Measures retired systems with completed closure evidence                  |
| Reconciliation findings      | Tracks unresolved differences between inventory and authoritative sources |

Metrics should be interpreted carefully. A high registration rate does not prove that every system has been correctly identified or classified.

The organization should supplement numerical measures with sampling, independent validation, and investigation of recurring data quality issues.

### 66. AI Inventory Reporting and Escalation Control

Inventory reporting must communicate material coverage gaps, quality issues, and governance dependencies to the appropriate management audience.

Operational reporting may focus on missing fields, overdue reviews, unassigned owners, and unresolved reconciliation findings.

Executive reporting should focus on material exposure, unregistered high-risk systems, systemic ownership gaps, and the organization's ability to establish reliable oversight of its AI estate.

Escalation should be triggered by defined conditions, such as:

- A high-risk system remains unregistered.
- Ownership cannot be established.
- Production authorization cannot be verified.
- Material data classification is unknown.
- A required assessment is missing or overdue.
- An expired exception remains unresolved.
- A retired system remains technically accessible.
- Reconciliation reveals significant unauthorized usage.

Reports should identify the accountable owner, required corrective action, target date, and decision required. Repeated reporting without effective remediation should trigger management escalation.

### 67. AI Inventory Control Testing

Inventory controls must be tested to determine whether they are designed appropriately and operate effectively.

Testing should evaluate both procedural controls and technical mechanisms used to maintain inventory accuracy.

Possible tests include:

- Sampling AI systems and verifying mandatory fields.
- Comparing registered systems with technical discovery sources.
- Confirming business and technical ownership.
- Checking whether material changes are reflected in records.
- Verifying links to required assessments and approvals.
- Testing lifecycle status accuracy.
- Reviewing access and change permissions.
- Confirming retirement closure evidence.
- Testing duplicate detection and reconciliation.
- Reviewing exception approval and expiry handling.

Testing should document the objective, population, sample selection, procedures, evidence, findings, and conclusion.

A failed test should result in a tracked corrective action and assessment of whether the deficiency affects additional systems. Critical inventory failures may undermine the reliability of broader AI risk reporting and should be escalated accordingly.

### 68. AI Inventory Control Deficiency Management

Deficiencies in inventory controls must be recorded, prioritized, assigned, and resolved.

A deficiency may involve a missing system, inaccurate ownership, outdated model information, incomplete data classification, invalid assessment links, or a failure to capture a material change.

The organization should assess the deficiency's impact on governance decisions. A missing optional descriptive field may be low impact, while an unregistered high-risk AI system may represent a significant governance failure.

Each material deficiency should include:

- Description and affected records.
- Root cause where known.
- Potential consequences.
- Severity and priority.
- Corrective action.
- Accountable owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards where needed.
- Verification criteria.
- Closure evidence.

Closure must confirm that the deficiency has been corrected and that related systems or processes have been evaluated for similar weaknesses.

### 69. AI Inventory Evidence and Record Retention Control

The organization must retain evidence demonstrating that inventory controls have operated as required.

Evidence may include registration submissions, validation results, ownership attestations, reconciliation reports, change histories, review records, exception approvals, and retirement confirmations.

The retention process should define:

- Evidence categories.
- Required retention periods.
- Authoritative repositories.
- Access and confidentiality requirements.
- Version and integrity protections.
- Legal hold procedures.
- Disposal authorization.
- Links between evidence and inventory records.

Retention requirements should reflect applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and operational obligations.

The organization should avoid retaining sensitive information indefinitely without a defined purpose. Where detailed technical evidence is held in another system, the inventory should maintain a reliable reference rather than duplicating the full record unnecessarily.

### 70. AI Inventory Integration with Enterprise Systems

The AI System Inventory should integrate with existing enterprise systems where integration improves data quality, traceability, and operational efficiency.

Relevant systems may include:

- IT asset management.
- Configuration management databases.
- Enterprise architecture repositories.
- Procurement and contract management.
- Vendor risk management.
- Identity and access management.
- Cloud asset management.
- Model registries.
- Risk and compliance platforms.
- Security incident management.
- Change management and service management.
- Data catalogs and privacy management tools.

The organization must identify which system is authoritative for each data element and how updates are synchronized.

Integration should prevent conflicting ownership, lifecycle status, supplier information, or approval records. Where real-time integration is unavailable, the organization should define reconciliation frequency and exception handling.

Integration failures must be monitored because stale or incomplete synchronization can create the appearance of accurate governance while operational records have diverged.

### 71. AI Inventory Automation and Workflow Control

Automation may be used to improve registration, validation, reconciliation, review, and reporting.

Automated workflows can reduce manual errors and ensure that required steps are consistently performed.

Potential automation includes:

- Generating unique identifiers.
- Validating mandatory fields.
- Detecting potential duplicates.
- Routing records to designated owners.
- Triggering assessment workflows.
- Notifying owners of upcoming reviews.
- Detecting lifecycle inconsistencies.
- Flagging missing approvals.
- Escalating overdue updates.
- Producing coverage and quality reports.

Automation must be governed and tested. Incorrect matching rules, stale integrations, or misconfigured workflows can create false records, overlook real systems, or incorrectly report that controls have been satisfied.

The organization should define exception handling and human verification for material decisions. Automated completion of an inventory workflow must not be interpreted as approval of the AI system itself.

### 72. AI Inventory Governance and Assurance Review

The inventory governance framework must undergo periodic review to determine whether it remains suitable for the organization's AI environment.

The review should evaluate:

- Whether the inventory scope remains complete.
- Whether mandatory attributes remain adequate.
- Whether discovery mechanisms identify relevant systems.
- Whether ownership and update responsibilities are effective.
- Whether data quality controls detect material errors.
- Whether integrations remain reliable.
- Whether reporting supports management decisions.
- Whether exceptions are properly controlled.
- Whether retirement procedures close residual exposure.
- Whether audit findings have been resolved.

The review should consider changes in AI capabilities, enterprise architecture, supplier arrangements, regulatory obligations, and organizational structure.

Improvements should be prioritized according to the consequences of inventory failure and the importance of the affected systems.

### 73. Core AI System Inventory Control Principle

The AI System Inventory must operate as a controlled, continuously maintained governance record. Registration, validation, ownership, change management, assessment linkage, reconciliation, and retirement must be supported by defined responsibilities and verifiable evidence.

The organization must distinguish between **a registered system and an authorized system**, **a recorded attribute and a verified attribute**, **a completed assessment and an approved deployment**, and **a retired record and a technically decommissioned system**.

**Core Principle:** AI inventory controls are effective only when the organization can reliably identify its in-scope AI systems, maintain accurate records of their purpose and dependencies, connect those records to risk and approval decisions, and detect material discrepancies before they undermine governance.

### 74. AI System Inventory Governance Operating Model

The AI System Inventory must operate within a formal governance model that establishes accountability for inventory completeness, data accuracy, lifecycle management, and the use of inventory information in enterprise risk decisions.

The operating model should define how business units, technology teams, AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, enterprise architecture, and risk management collaborate to maintain a reliable view of the organization's AI estate.

The governance model should establish:

- An accountable inventory governance owner.
- Business and technical ownership for individual systems.
- Data stewardship and validation responsibilities.
- Registration and approval workflows.
- Escalation paths for missing or inaccurate records.
- Coordination with enterprise asset and service management.
- Oversight of unregistered and unauthorized AI usage.
- Reporting responsibilities and review frequency.
- Independent assurance arrangements.
- Continuous improvement mechanisms.

The model must distinguish between responsibility for maintaining inventory records and accountability for the underlying AI systems. The inventory governance function ensures that records meet established requirements; business and technical owners remain accountable for their respective systems.

The operating model should be proportionate to the organization's size, AI maturity, risk exposure, and regulatory obligations. Combining responsibilities may be appropriate in smaller organizations, provided accountability remains explicit and material decisions receive suitable independent challenge.

### 75. Three Lines Model for AI Inventory Governance

The organization should establish clear responsibilities for operating, overseeing, and independently assuring AI inventory controls.

The Three Lines Model provides a useful structure for separating operational ownership, independent oversight, and assurance.

| Governance line | Inventory responsibility                     | Typical activities                                                         |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line      | Maintain accurate system records             | Registration, ownership updates, change reporting, periodic certification  |
| Second line     | Establish standards and challenge compliance | Inventory policy, quality monitoring, reconciliation oversight, escalation |
| Third line      | Independently assess control effectiveness   | Audit testing, coverage validation, review of evidence and remediation     |

The first line should maintain the information needed to govern its AI systems. The second line should establish minimum inventory requirements and challenge material gaps. The third line should independently evaluate whether inventory controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

The inventory governance function must not become the sole source of truth for operational details that business and technology owners are better positioned to maintain.

Similarly, internal audit should not assume responsibility for creating or maintaining the inventory that it is expected to assess independently.

### 76. Executive Accountability for AI Inventory Coverage

Executive management must ensure that the organization has sufficient visibility into its AI estate to support risk management, compliance, and operational oversight.

Executives should establish the authority, resources, and reporting arrangements needed to maintain inventory coverage across business units and technology environments.

Executive accountability should include:

- Approving or sponsoring the inventory governance framework.
- Assigning responsibility for inventory completeness.
- Resolving cross-functional ownership disputes.
- Ensuring appropriate discovery and reconciliation capabilities.
- Reviewing material inventory coverage gaps.
- Addressing persistent unauthorized AI usage.
- Prioritizing remediation of significant data quality deficiencies.
- Ensuring that inventory information supports enterprise risk decisions.
- Reviewing material audit findings.
- Requiring improvement when inventory reliability is inadequate.

Executive management should receive reporting on material omissions and systemic weaknesses rather than relying solely on a headline inventory count.

An organization may report thousands of registered AI assets and still lack effective governance if significant systems remain undiscovered, records are inaccurate, or ownership and approval information cannot be verified.

### 77. AI Inventory Governance Committee and Charter

- Escalating significant inventory risks to executive management.

The committee should include appropriate business, technology, risk, security, privacy, and compliance representation according to the organization's needs.

Meeting records should document material decisions, assigned actions, responsible owners, deadlines, and unresolved concerns. The committee's existence does not remove the responsibility of individual system owners to maintain accurate records.

### 78. AI Inventory Decision Rights Matrix

### 79. AI Inventory Data Ownership and Stewardship

The organization should establish clear ownership for the data recorded in the inventory.

Inventory stewardship ensures that each attribute has a defined meaning, an authoritative source, and a responsible party for maintaining its accuracy.

Different attributes may require different data owners. For example, the business owner may maintain the intended purpose, the technical owner may maintain deployment information, procurement may maintain contract references, and the privacy function may oversee relevant privacy assessment information.

The stewardship model should define:

- Data domains and their accountable owners.
- Authoritative sources for each field.
- Data quality requirements.
- Update and validation responsibilities.
- Access and modification permissions.
- Reconciliation rules.
- Escalation for disputed information.
- Retention and change history requirements.

Where several systems hold related information, the organization should specify which source governs each attribute and how inconsistencies are resolved.

The inventory governance owner remains accountable for the integrity of the inventory process, but should not silently override authoritative data maintained by another responsible function.

### 80. AI Inventory Certification and Attestation Governance

The organization should require periodic certification of inventory records by designated owners.

Certification is a formal confirmation that the record has been reviewed and that the information remains accurate to the best of the certifier's knowledge.

The certification process should specify:

- Which records require certification.
- Who must certify them.
- The review frequency.
- The information that must be verified.
- The evidence required.
- The consequences of non-response.
- The escalation process.
- How corrections are recorded.

Certification should cover relevant attributes such as system purpose, ownership, deployment status, model and provider information, data classification, dependencies, assessment references, and production authorization.

Certification must not be reduced to an automatic acknowledgment without meaningful review. The organization should use risk-based sampling or independent verification where warranted.

Failure to certify should be visible in management reporting. For material systems, unresolved certification failures may require additional review or operating restrictions under the organization's governance policy.

### 81. AI Inventory Governance Independence and Conflict Management

The inventory governance model should manage conflicts of interest that could undermine record accuracy or conceal material AI usage.

Conflicts may arise when a team is responsible for delivering an AI initiative and also controls the only evidence used to confirm that the initiative complies with inventory requirements.

The organization should establish independent validation for material records and define escalation procedures when owners disagree with inventory findings.

Possible safeguards include:

- Independent review of high-risk system records.
- Separation of registration submission and validation.
- Reconciliation against external evidence.
- Escalation of unresolved ownership disputes.
- Independent verification of retirement.
- Review of material inventory exceptions.
- Audit sampling of self-certified records.

Independence should be proportionate to risk. Routine low-risk record updates may not require committee approval, but high-risk systems and significant omissions warrant stronger challenge.

The AI System Inventory must align with the organization's broader policy and standards framework.

Changes to supporting documents should be assessed for their impact on inventory fields, workflows, control requirements, and reporting. The inventory process should not continue using obsolete definitions after the governing policy has changed.

### 83. AI Inventory Regulatory and Compliance Governance

The organization must establish a process for identifying inventory requirements arising from applicable laws, regulations, contractual commitments, and internal governance obligations.

### 86. AI Inventory Classification Governance

### 87. AI Inventory Risk-Based Governance Tiers

The organization should establish risk-based governance tiers to determine the level of oversight required for different AI systems.

### 88. AI Inventory Exception Governance

The organization must govern exceptions to inventory requirements through a formal and traceable process.

The exception record should include:

- The affected system and requirement.
- The reason the requirement cannot be met.
- The resulting governance risk.
- The compensating measures.
- The approving authority.
- The effective and expiry dates.
- The remediation owner and target date.
- The conditions requiring early revocation.
- The required monitoring and review.

Exceptions involving unknown ownership, missing risk assessments, or unverified production authorization should receive particular scrutiny.

### 89. AI Inventory Dispute Resolution Governance

The organization must define how disputes about inventory ownership, classification, scope, and record accuracy are resolved.

Disputes may arise when business units disagree about who owns a system, whether an embedded feature qualifies for registration, which provider is responsible, or whether a system remains active.

The process should establish:

- The initial responsible decision-maker.
- Required evidence.
- The escalation path.
- The decision authority.
- Interim handling requirements.
- Documentation of the decision.
- Follow-up actions and deadlines.

The organization should distinguish factual disputes from policy interpretation and legal interpretation. Each may require a different authority or specialist review.

Where a dispute affects a high-risk system or prevents a reliable assessment, the organization should apply proportionate interim safeguards until the issue is resolved.

### 90. AI Inventory Governance for Embedded AI Capabilities

Embedded AI features in existing software require specific governance consideration because they may be introduced through product updates, licensing changes, or default settings rather than through a separate AI procurement project.

The organization should establish a process for identifying material AI capabilities embedded in enterprise software.

The review should determine:

- Whether the feature falls within inventory scope.
- Whether it is enabled or disabled.
- What data it can access.
- Whether information is transmitted to an external provider.
- Whether outputs influence business decisions.
- Whether users can invoke the feature without separate approval.
- Whether the provider changes the feature automatically.
- Whether additional assessments are required.

The organization should not assume that an existing software approval automatically covers a newly introduced AI capability.

Where embedded features are widespread, the inventory may record the parent application and link to individual AI capabilities when those capabilities have materially different data access, risk, or approval requirements.

### 91. AI Inventory Governance for Shadow AI

Shadow AI governance must define how unauthorized or unregistered AI usage is discovered, evaluated, and resolved.

The organization should establish a consistent response model that distinguishes between a legitimate use case requiring onboarding, an inappropriate use of an approved service, and activity that may involve a security or privacy incident.

The process should include:

- Discovery and initial triage.
- Identification of the user group and business purpose.
- Assessment of data exposure.
- Evaluation of supplier and service risks.
- Determination of registration requirements.
- Decision on approval, restriction, or prohibition.
- Incident escalation where necessary.
- Remediation and communication.
- Verification that the issue has been resolved.

The organization should provide a practical route for employees to request approved AI capabilities. If approved alternatives are difficult to obtain, unmanaged use may persist despite technical restrictions.

Governance must combine proportionate enforcement with usable approval pathways, clear communication, and appropriate monitoring.

### 92. AI Inventory Governance for Third-Party Services

The inventory governance model must account for AI services supplied by external providers and for AI capabilities embedded in supplier products.

Supplier onboarding and change processes should trigger inventory review where a service introduces a material AI capability.

The process should establish how provider information, contractual references, data processing roles, assurance records, and service dependencies are maintained.

Where suppliers provide incomplete information, the organization should document the limitation and determine whether it affects risk assessment or authorization.

The organization should also define how provider incidents, service changes, model replacements, and contract termination update the inventory.

Third-party service availability does not automatically establish that the service has been approved for a particular business purpose. Inventory governance must remain linked to the organization's procurement, risk assessment, and production authorization processes.

### 93. AI Inventory Governance for Multi-Model Systems

Systems that use multiple models require an inventory structure capable of representing the relationship between the overall application, its use cases, and its constituent models.

The organization should determine whether model-level records are necessary based on the models' distinct roles, providers, versions, risk characteristics, and lifecycle requirements.

For example, an application may use one model for embeddings, another for generation, and a third for classification. Recording only the primary generation model may conceal material dependencies.

The inventory should establish:

- The application or system identifier.
- The related model identifiers.
- Each model's function.
- Provider and version information.
- Relevant data flows.
- Material dependencies.
- Validation and assessment references.
- Change and replacement history.

A model replacement should trigger an evaluation of whether the overall system's risk assessment and approvals remain valid.

The organization should avoid excessive record duplication while preserving enough granularity to support meaningful risk analysis.

### 94. AI Inventory Governance for Agentic Systems

AI agents may combine models, memory, planning, tools, external APIs, and multi-step execution. Their inventory records must capture these capabilities at a level sufficient to support governance.

The organization should document the agent's intended purpose, permitted actions, connected tools, execution identity, degree of autonomy, human approval requirements, and material dependencies.

Where an agent can create or modify business records, communicate externally, or execute transactions, the inventory should link to the relevant risk and architecture assessments.

The governance process should require review when:

- New tools are added.
- Permissions are expanded.
- Persistent memory is introduced.
- Approval gates are removed or changed.
- New external systems become accessible.
- The agent's intended purpose changes.
- Its degree of autonomy increases.
- Its execution identity changes.

An agent should not be treated as a low-risk conversational tool merely because users interact with it through a chat interface.

### 95. AI Inventory Governance for Model and Provider Changes

The organization must establish how material changes to models and providers are identified and reflected in inventory governance.

Changes may occur through explicit deployment updates, supplier releases, service migrations, or provider-managed changes that are not initiated by the organization.

The process should define how the organization obtains change information, determines materiality, updates records, and initiates reassessment.

Relevant changes include:

- Model deprecation or replacement.
- Changes to model routing.
- New provider processing arrangements.
- Changes in data retention or training practices.
- New model capabilities.
- Material performance changes.
- Changes to hosting or deployment regions.
- Changes in security assurance.
- Changes in contractual terms.

Where the organization cannot reliably determine whether a provider has changed the service, the inventory should record the limitation and apply appropriate monitoring or contractual controls.

### 96. AI Inventory Governance for Retired and Dormant Systems

The organization must distinguish active systems from dormant, suspended, and retired systems.

A dormant system may no longer be used regularly but could retain active credentials, data, integrations, or externally accessible endpoints. It should not be assumed to have no residual risk.

The governance process should define:

- How inactivity is detected.
- When a system becomes subject to dormant-status review.
- Who confirms continued business need.
- Which access and data controls remain required.
- Whether reactivation requires renewed approval.
- When decommissioning must begin.
- How residual obligations are closed.

Reactivation of a dormant system should trigger validation that its model, configuration, provider, data flows, and approvals remain appropriate.

The inventory should preserve historical records for retired systems according to retention requirements and avoid reusing their identifiers for unrelated systems.

### 97. AI Inventory Governance Reporting to the Board

Board-level reporting should provide visibility into the organization's ability to identify and govern its AI estate.

Reporting should focus on material gaps and their implications for risk oversight rather than presenting only inventory totals.

Depending on the organization's exposure, reports may include:

- Total registered AI systems and use cases.
- Known or estimated unregistered AI usage.
- Coverage by risk tier.
- High-risk systems without confirmed ownership.
- Systems missing required assessments or approvals.
- Material discrepancies between inventory and technical discovery.
- Significant third-party dependencies.
- Overdue inventory remediation.
- Critical retired systems with unresolved access or data issues.
- Trends in inventory quality and certification.

Reports should distinguish verified counts from estimates and disclose important limitations in discovery coverage.

Board-level reporting should identify decisions or resources required to address material weaknesses and track whether management actions improve inventory reliability.

### 98. AI Inventory Governance Management Reporting

Management reporting should provide the detail needed to maintain inventory accuracy and resolve operational issues.

Reports may identify missing mandatory fields, unassigned owners, overdue reviews, unresolved duplicates, unregistered discoveries, inconsistent lifecycle statuses, and failed integrations.

Each material finding should have an accountable owner, required action, target date, and escalation route.

Management should review trends rather than focusing only on the number of open issues. Repeated failures within the same business unit or system category may indicate a structural problem in registration, procurement, ownership, or change management.

The organization should also assess whether reporting is timely enough to support decisions. A quarterly report may be insufficient for rapidly changing high-risk systems, while lower-risk records may require less frequent oversight.

### 99. AI Inventory Governance Incident Management Integration

Inventory governance must be integrated with incident management so that incidents can trigger corrections to inventory records and broader discovery activities.

An incident may reveal that the inventory omitted a system, misidentified a provider, failed to capture a sensitive data flow, or recorded incorrect permissions.

The incident process should determine whether:

- Inventory records require immediate correction.
- Other systems may share the same deficiency.
- Additional discovery is necessary.
- Risk classifications and approvals remain valid.
- A systemic inventory control failure has occurred.
- Management reporting or regulatory records require updating.
- Corrective actions must be assigned and verified.

The organization should retain links between the incident record, the affected inventory entries, and the resulting remediation.

Incident closure should not automatically close the underlying inventory governance issue. The organization must verify that the relevant record and control deficiencies have been corrected.

### 100. AI Inventory Governance Change Management Integration

Inventory governance must be integrated with the organization's change management process.

Changes to AI applications, models, providers, integrations, data access, and operating environments may affect inventory attributes and trigger new governance requirements.

The change process should identify which modifications require:

- Inventory updates.
- Reclassification.
- Risk reassessment.
- Security or privacy review.
- Architecture review.
- Vendor assessment.
- Renewed production authorization.
- Updated dependency records.
- Additional monitoring.
- Revised recovery procedures.

The organization should define which changes can be processed through standard workflows and which require enhanced review.

Change management should not assume that a technical change is low risk merely because it does not alter the application name or business owner. Changes in data access or autonomy can materially affect exposure without changing the visible product.

### 101. AI Inventory Governance for Acquisitions and Divestitures

Mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, and major organizational restructuring can create significant inventory coverage and ownership challenges.

The organization should establish a process for identifying AI systems inherited through acquisitions, transferred between entities, or affected by divestiture.

The process should assess:

- Existing AI system inventories.
- Ownership and accountability.
- Model and provider dependencies.
- Data processing arrangements.
- Contractual and licensing restrictions.
- Risk and security assessments.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Integration and access dependencies.
- Unsupported or unauthorized systems.
- Retirement or migration requirements.

Inherited records should not automatically be treated as compliant with the receiving organization's governance requirements. The organization should reconcile them against its own inventory schema, control requirements, and approval processes.

Where immediate complete validation is impractical, the organization should prioritize systems according to risk and document interim restrictions and remediation plans.

### 102. AI Inventory Governance Assurance and Audit

The organization must establish assurance activities to evaluate whether inventory governance controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

Assurance should examine both the quality of recorded data and the completeness of the inventory population.

Possible procedures include:

- Sampling registered systems and verifying attributes.
- Comparing inventory records with independent discovery sources.
- Testing ownership certification.
- Reviewing lifecycle transition evidence.
- Testing duplicate detection.
- Verifying assessment and approval links.
- Reviewing exceptions and expiry handling.
- Confirming retirement and decommissioning.
- Evaluating access and change controls.
- Testing the reliability of management reporting.

Assurance findings should be prioritized according to their consequences for risk management and compliance.

A failure to identify an entire category of AI usage may be more significant than isolated missing fields because it undermines the reliability of the inventory as a whole.

Findings must be tracked to closure, with independent verification where warranted.

### 103. AI Inventory Governance Continuous Improvement

The organization should continuously improve its inventory governance process using evidence from operational experience, control testing, incidents, audit findings, and changes in the AI environment.

Improvement priorities may include:

- Increasing discovery coverage.
- Simplifying registration workflows.
- Improving data quality validation.
- Strengthening ownership accountability.
- Automating material change detection.
- Improving reconciliation with enterprise systems.
- Enhancing risk-based review frequencies.
- Reducing recurring exceptions.
- Improving reporting and escalation.
- Strengthening retirement verification.

The organization should measure whether improvements produce meaningful results, such as fewer unregistered systems, better ownership coverage, faster updates, and more reliable assessment linkage.

Automation should be evaluated for accuracy and unintended effects. Increased workflow completion does not necessarily demonstrate improved inventory quality.

### 104. AI Inventory Governance Maturity Assessment

The organization should periodically assess the maturity of its inventory governance capabilities.

An illustrative maturity scale is:

| Level          | Description                                                                                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1 — Ad hoc     | AI systems are identified inconsistently and records depend on individual knowledge              |
| 2 — Developing | Registration processes exist, but coverage and data quality remain uneven                        |
| 3 — Defined    | Standardized inventory requirements, ownership, and workflows are established                    |
| 4 — Managed    | Coverage, quality, and control performance are measured and regularly reviewed                   |
| 5 — Optimized  | Discovery, reconciliation, and lifecycle governance improve continuously using reliable evidence |

The assessment should examine actual operating evidence rather than the existence of policies or tools alone.

The organization should prioritize maturity improvements according to the risk of inventory failure, the importance of affected systems, and the feasibility of remediation.

The objective is not to achieve a maturity label for its own sake. It is to ensure that the organization can reliably identify and govern the AI systems for which it is accountable.

### 105. AI Inventory Governance Final Principle

AI inventory governance establishes the accountability, decision rights, standards, oversight, and assurance mechanisms needed to maintain a trustworthy view of the organization's AI estate.

An effective governance model ensures that records are created and maintained by accountable owners, independently challenged where warranted, reconciled with authoritative sources, and connected to the risk and approval processes that govern system operation.

The organization must distinguish between **inventory administration and system ownership**, **self-certification and independent assurance**, **a documented system and a discovered system**, and **a governance record and an enforceable operating authorization**.

**Core Principle:** AI inventory governance is effective when the organization has clear accountability for its AI estate, reliable mechanisms to discover and validate systems, traceable decisions, and sufficient oversight to ensure that inventory information remains trustworthy throughout the AI lifecycle.

### 107. AI Inventory System of Record Design

### 109. AI System, Model, Use Case, and Deployment Separation

The inventory must distinguish the AI system, its underlying models, its approved use cases, and its deployed instances.

These concepts are related but are not interchangeable.

| Entity     | Meaning                                            | Example                                      |
| ---------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| AI system  | Governed solution delivering a business capability | Customer support assistant                   |
| Model      | Model component used by the solution               | Hosted language model                        |
| Use case   | Specific approved purpose                          | Answering product questions                  |
| Deployment | Operational instance of the solution               | Production deployment in a cloud environment |
| Provider   | Organization supplying a model or service          | External AI service provider                 |
| Component  | Supporting capability used by the solution         | Retrieval service or orchestration engine    |

This separation enables the organization to identify changes that affect only one component or use case and those that materially change the entire system.

For example, adding a model to an existing application may require a component-level update and a risk reassessment without necessarily creating an entirely new system record. Conversely, using the same model for a substantially different purpose may require a distinct use-case record and new approval.

The organization should define when a change requires a new inventory record, a linked child record, or an update to an existing record.

### 112. AI Inventory Discovery Architecture

AI inventory discovery should combine complementary sources to identify systems that may not be registered through formal governance workflows.

Discovery sources may include:

- Cloud resource inventories.
- Enterprise software catalogs.
- Approved AI platform APIs.
- SaaS discovery services.
- Procurement and contract records.
- Source-code and dependency analysis.
- Endpoint and network telemetry.
- Identity and access records.
- Model registries and deployment pipelines.
- Data processing inventories.
- Business-owner attestations.
- Security monitoring and incident findings.

Each source has limitations. Network telemetry may identify connections to an AI provider without proving which business application initiated the connection. Procurement records may identify a purchased service without proving that the AI capability is enabled. Source-code analysis may identify a model library without proving that a model is deployed in production.

The discovery process should therefore separate detection from confirmation.

A typical workflow is:

```
Discovery Signal
      |
      v
Normalize and Enrich
      |
      v
Duplicate and Match Analysis
      |
      v
Determine Inventory Scope
      |
      v

      |
      v
Validate System and Purpose
      |
      v
Register or Link to Existing Record
      |
      v
Trigger Required Assessments
      |
      v
Track Resolution and Evidence
```

Unconfirmed findings should remain visible in a discovery queue until resolved. The organization should not silently discard a finding solely because it cannot immediately identify an owner.

### 113. AI Inventory Reconciliation Engine

The reconciliation engine compares authoritative inventory records with independent discovery sources to identify omissions, inconsistencies, and stale information.

Reconciliation rules should identify conditions such as:

- Discovered AI services with no matching inventory record.
- Inventory records with no recent evidence of existence.
- Provider mismatches.
- Conflicting lifecycle statuses.
- Model version discrepancies.
- Missing or inconsistent ownership.
- Unregistered integrations.
- Decommissioned assets still receiving traffic.
- Duplicate system records.
- Changes to deployment environments not reflected in the inventory.

Matching should use reliable identifiers wherever possible. Name-based matching alone can produce false positives and false negatives.

The reconciliation process should assign each discrepancy a severity, responsible owner, target resolution date, and escalation path.

High-risk discrepancies may require immediate investigation. Lower-risk data quality issues may be handled through routine remediation workflows.

The organization should retain reconciliation results and evidence of resolution so that it can demonstrate not only that checks were performed, but also that material discrepancies were addressed.

### 114. AI Inventory Identity and Access Architecture

The inventory platform must enforce access controls that protect sensitive system information and preserve the integrity of governance records.

The access model should be based on role-based access control (RBAC), attribute-based access control (ABAC), or a suitable combination.

Representative roles may include:

- Inventory administrator.
- Business system owner.
- Technical system owner.
- AI governance reviewer.
- Security assessor.
- Privacy reviewer.
- Procurement or vendor risk reviewer.
- Internal auditor.
- Executive reporting user.
- Integration service identity.

Permissions should distinguish viewing, submitting, editing, validating, approving, exporting, and administering records.

High-risk fields, approval decisions, exceptions, and audit evidence may require stronger access restrictions than ordinary descriptive metadata.

The platform should maintain an audit trail for material record changes and privileged actions.

Where sensitive technical information is stored, the organization should evaluate whether access to the inventory itself could create an attacker's roadmap to critical AI systems and dependencies.

### 117. AI Inventory Data Lineage and Provenance

The organization should maintain sufficient lineage information to explain where inventory data originated and how it was transformed.

For material attributes, the platform should record the source system, synchronization or collection time, validation status, and relevant transformation or mapping rules.

Where a field is manually entered, the organization should identify the responsible person or process and retain the relevant change history.

Data lineage supports:

- Investigation of conflicting records.
- Verification of provider information.
- Analysis of model and deployment changes.
- Evidence collection for audits.
- Assessment of stale information.
- Reconciliation of discovery signals.
- Accountability for record corrections.

Provenance information is particularly important when the inventory combines data from automated discovery, vendor documentation, technical owners, and governance assessments.

The organization should not present inferred attributes as verified facts. The record should distinguish confirmed information, estimates, assumptions, and unresolved questions.

### 118. AI Inventory Versioning and Historical Traceability

The inventory should preserve enough history to reconstruct material changes to a system's identity, purpose, dependencies, risk classification, and authorization.

Historical traceability should support questions such as:

- What information was recorded when the system was approved?
- Which model version was in use at a particular time?
- Who changed the intended purpose?
- When did a provider or deployment change?
- Which risk assessment supported the authorization?
- What restrictions or exceptions were active?
- When was the system suspended or retired?

The organization should implement versioning and retention controls that preserve the integrity of historical records.

Where fields are synchronized from another authoritative source, the inventory should retain relevant change events or references to the source history when needed for governance.

Historical traceability does not mean retaining every technical artifact indefinitely. Retention should follow applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, security, and records management requirements.

### 119. AI Inventory Evidence Repository Integration

The inventory should link to a governed evidence repository for supporting documents and records.

### 120. AI Inventory Risk and Assessment Integration

The inventory must connect system records to the risk management and assessment processes governing AI deployment and operation.

### 121. AI Inventory Monitoring and Observability Integration

The inventory should integrate with relevant operational monitoring capabilities to help identify discrepancies between approved and actual system behavior.

Monitoring signals may include model deployments, API activity, changes to access permissions, new external connections, unusual data transfers, and provider service changes.

- Unapproved AI services.

- Deviations from approved architecture.

Monitoring must be designed with appropriate privacy, security, and proportionality safeguards.

The inventory should also make clear which monitoring capabilities are available and where material coverage limitations remain.

### 122. AI Inventory Cloud and Multi-Cloud Architecture

Organizations operating across multiple cloud environments should establish a consistent inventory model while preserving provider-specific technical attributes.

The architecture should support common fields for system identity, ownership, risk, lifecycle, and authorization, alongside environment-specific information such as resource identifiers, subscriptions, projects, accounts, regions, and deployment services.

Integration may involve cloud asset inventories, AI platform APIs, container registries, model endpoints, and cloud security management tools.

The design should account for:

- Multiple cloud providers.
- Hybrid and on-premises deployments.
- Regional data processing.
- Cross-cloud dependencies.
- Provider-managed AI services.
- Ephemeral infrastructure.
- Resource replacement and redeployment.
- Different identity and access models.

Cloud resource identifiers should not be used as the sole enterprise AI system identifier because resources may be recreated or moved while the governed system remains the same.

The organization should map cloud resources to stable inventory records and preserve the relationships needed to understand the deployment's security and operational context.

### 123. AI Inventory Architecture for SaaS and External AI Services

The inventory architecture must support AI services that are accessed through SaaS applications, external APIs, and provider-managed platforms.

The organization may have limited direct visibility into the provider's internal models or infrastructure. In such cases, it should record the information available, identify material uncertainties, and establish appropriate assurance mechanisms.

The record should distinguish between:

- The external AI service.
- The organization's application or business use case.
- The provider's underlying model, where known.
- The data transmitted to the provider.
- The contractual and assurance relationship.
- The organization's configuration and controls.

The architecture should support updates when providers disclose material changes to models, data handling, hosting, or service functionality.

Where the provider does not disclose model-level details, the organization should not invent them. The inventory should explicitly indicate that the information is unavailable or not verified and assess whether the limitation is acceptable for the intended use.

### 124. AI Inventory Scalability and Performance

The inventory architecture should support the expected growth in AI systems, model components, deployments, discovery events, assessment records, and historical data.

Scalability considerations include:

- Record volume.
- Relationship complexity.
- Discovery-event throughput.
- API request rates.
- Search and reporting performance.
- Concurrent workflow activity.
- Historical retention.
- Integration failure recovery.
- Regional deployment requirements.
- Availability and recovery objectives.

The organization should avoid designing exclusively for the current number of registered systems when AI adoption is expected to expand significantly.

Performance requirements should be based on operational needs. For example, a high-risk discovery event may require rapid triage, while a routine metadata synchronization can be processed through a scheduled batch.

Scalability must not undermine data quality, access control, or auditability. Increased throughput is not valuable if records become duplicated, inconsistent, or impossible to trace.

### 125. AI Inventory Resilience, Backup, and Recovery

The inventory is a governance-critical repository and should be protected against data loss, unauthorized modification, and prolonged service disruption.

The organization should define appropriate backup, recovery, availability, and continuity requirements according to the inventory's role in enterprise risk management and operational authorization.

Controls may include:

- Regular backups.
- Protected backup credentials.
- Recovery testing.
- Versioned records.
- Integrity verification.
- Replication where justified.
- Documented recovery procedures.
- Alternative access to critical governance records.
- Monitoring for unauthorized changes.
- Defined recovery objectives.

The organization should determine how critical inventory functions will continue during an outage.

For example, if the inventory platform is unavailable, the organization may need a controlled process for recording urgent changes and reconciling them after service restoration.

Recovery testing should verify the integrity of the records, relationships, approvals, and audit history—not merely the ability to restart the application.

### 126. AI Inventory Security Threat Model

The inventory platform itself requires a security threat model because it may contain sensitive information about business-critical AI systems, providers, integrations, data flows, and control weaknesses.

Potential threats include:

- Unauthorized access to inventory records.
- Modification of risk or approval status.
- Deletion of evidence or audit history.
- Manipulation of discovery results.
- Compromise of integration identities.
- Malicious insertion of false records.
- Abuse of administrative privileges.
- Exposure of sensitive architecture information.
- Denial of service.
- Exploitation of vulnerable integrations.

The threat model should consider confidentiality, integrity, and availability, with particular emphasis on record integrity where inventory information informs production authorization and risk decisions.

Mitigations may include least-privilege access, strong authentication, segregation of duties, input validation, protected audit logging, integrity checks, secure APIs, and independent reconciliation.

The organization should also evaluate whether an attacker could manipulate discovery sources to make an unauthorized system appear approved or to conceal an existing system.

### 128. AI Inventory Secrets and Credential Management

Integration identities used by the inventory must be managed through the organization's approved secrets and credential management processes.

Credentials may be required to access cloud asset inventories, AI platforms, SaaS discovery tools, procurement systems, and governance repositories.

The architecture should enforce:

- Dedicated service identities.
- Least-privilege access.
- Secure secret storage.
- Rotation or renewal according to policy.
- Credential revocation when integrations are retired.
- Monitoring of privileged and service-account activity.
- Separation between development, test, and production credentials.
- Prohibition of hard-coded secrets.

The inventory should record integration ownership and status without storing plaintext credentials or unnecessary secret material.

A compromised discovery identity may allow an attacker to suppress or manipulate inventory data. Integration security is therefore part of inventory integrity, not merely an infrastructure concern.

### 129. AI Inventory Privacy and Data Minimization

The inventory should contain only the personal or sensitive information necessary for its governance purpose.

Although the inventory primarily describes systems, it may include owner contact details, user populations, data categories, affected stakeholder groups, and references to sensitive assessments.

The organization should establish:

- A defined purpose for collected fields.
- Appropriate access restrictions.
- Data minimization requirements.
- Retention and deletion rules.
- Separation of inventory metadata from detailed personal data.
- Controls over exports and reporting.
- Privacy review for new fields or integrations.

The inventory should generally describe categories of personal data and processing activities rather than copying actual personal records into the inventory.

Where a field is unnecessary for governance decisions, it should not be collected simply because the platform supports it.

### 130. AI Inventory Data Retention and Archival Architecture

The organization should establish retention rules for active records, retired systems, discovery findings, audit history, and supporting evidence.

Retention requirements may differ by record type and applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal obligations.

The architecture should support:

- Defined retention periods.
- Legal holds where applicable.
- Controlled archival.
- Preservation of material historical decisions.
- Secure deletion when authorized.
- Retention of audit evidence.
- Traceability between archived systems and related assessments.
- Verification of deletion from relevant repositories where required.

Retirement of an AI system should not automatically trigger immediate deletion of its inventory record. Historical records may be necessary to explain prior decisions, investigate incidents, or demonstrate compliance.

Conversely, retaining all information indefinitely may create unnecessary privacy, security, and storage risks.

### 131. AI Inventory Test and Quality Assurance Architecture

The inventory solution should be subject to structured testing before deployment and after material changes.

Testing should evaluate functional correctness, data integrity, security, integration reliability, workflow behavior, and reporting accuracy.

Representative test scenarios include:

- Duplicate registration attempts.
- Missing mandatory fields.
- Unauthorized status changes.
- Conflicting source-system attributes.
- Failed synchronization.
- Expired assessment references.
- Incorrect owner assignments.
- Model version changes.
- Retirement of a system with active dependencies.
- Restoration from backup.
- Reconciliation of a discovered but unregistered system.

Testing should verify both expected behavior and failure handling.

For example, if a provider integration becomes unavailable, the inventory should expose the synchronization failure rather than continuing to report the affected data as freshly verified.

Test evidence should be retained according to the organization's change management and assurance requirements.

### 132. AI Inventory DevSecOps and Release Management

### 133. AI Inventory Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity Integration

The inventory recovery model should be integrated with enterprise disaster recovery and business continuity planning.

The organization should identify which inventory capabilities are essential during a disruption, such as identifying critical AI dependencies, confirming approved production systems, or locating responsible owners.

Continuity arrangements should define:

- Critical inventory services.
- Recovery time and recovery point objectives.
- Backup and restoration procedures.
- Alternative access to critical information.
- Manual processing procedures.
- Communication and escalation.
- Reconciliation after recovery.
- Validation of restored approvals and relationships.

Recovery priorities should reflect business impact. A disruption affecting inventory reporting may have different consequences from a disruption that prevents verification of production authorization for a critical AI system.

Exercises should include realistic scenarios such as a compromised inventory administrator account, corrupted records, failed integrations, or loss of the primary inventory platform.

### 134. AI Inventory Technical Debt and Architecture Governance

The organization should monitor technical debt that weakens the reliability or maintainability of the inventory architecture.

Examples include duplicate repositories, undocumented integrations, unsupported connectors, inconsistent identifiers, custom fields without owners, manual synchronization, and obsolete classification values.

Architecture governance should evaluate whether the inventory remains aligned with enterprise standards and whether the current design can support emerging AI deployment patterns.

Technical debt should be prioritized according to its consequences for:

- Inventory completeness.
- Data quality.
- Security.
- Operational resilience.
- Auditability.
- Integration reliability.
- Cost of maintenance.
- Ability to support new AI capabilities.

A technical limitation that prevents the organization from discovering critical AI deployments should be treated as a governance risk rather than merely an IT modernization issue.

High-risk AI systems should receive priority during initial registration and validation.

The roadmap should include clear ownership, resource requirements, dependencies, delivery milestones, and acceptance criteria. A large inventory population should not be treated as complete merely because records have been imported from existing systems.

### 137. AI Inventory Operating Cost and Resource Model

The organization should estimate the resources required to establish and maintain the inventory.

Cost categories may include:

- Platform licensing or development.
- Integration implementation.
- Discovery and monitoring tools.
- Data stewardship.
- Business and technical owner participation.
- Governance and risk review.
- Security engineering.
- Assurance and audit.
- Training and communications.
- Ongoing maintenance and support.

The operating model should account for recurring activities, including certification, reconciliation, exception review, data correction, and lifecycle updates.

A low-cost initial implementation may be appropriate when the AI estate is small, but the organization should avoid creating an unsustainable manual process that cannot keep pace with adoption.

Resource planning should reflect the complexity and risk of the estate rather than using the number of registered systems as the sole cost driver.

### 138. AI Inventory Key Performance and Risk Indicators

The organization should define metrics that measure inventory completeness, accuracy, timeliness, and governance effectiveness.

Representative indicators include:

| Metric                            | What it measures                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Registration coverage             | Proportion of identified in-scope AI systems with validated inventory records |
| Ownership coverage                | Proportion of records with confirmed accountable owners                       |
| Mandatory-field completeness      | Proportion of records meeting required data quality rules                     |
| Certification completion          | Proportion of records certified within the required period                    |
| Reconciliation discrepancy rate   | Frequency of material inconsistencies between inventory and discovery sources |
| Mean time to register             | Time between confirmed discovery and completed registration                   |
| Overdue remediation rate          | Proportion of inventory findings past their target date                       |
| Assessment linkage coverage       | Proportion of applicable systems linked to required assessments               |
| Unauthorized discovery resolution | Proportion of identified unauthorized systems with a documented disposition   |
| Retirement verification rate      | Proportion of retired systems with required closure evidence                  |

Metrics must have defined calculation rules, data sources, reporting frequency, and accountable owners.

The organization should distinguish key performance indicators (KPIs), which measure operational performance, from key risk indicators (KRIs), which signal potential exposure.

A high registration count is not proof of completeness. Metrics should be interpreted alongside discovery coverage, confidence in source data, and the materiality of unresolved discrepancies.

### 140. AI Inventory Architecture Review Checklist

Before approving the inventory architecture, the organization should verify that the design addresses the following areas.

- The authoritative system of record is identified.
- The data model distinguishes systems, models, use cases, and deployments where necessary.
- Unique identifiers and relationship rules are defined.
- Mandatory metadata and validation rules are documented.
- Discovery sources and their limitations are understood.
- Reconciliation and discrepancy management are implemented.
- Risk assessments and approvals can be traced to inventory records.
- Access control and segregation of duties are defined.
- Integrations use secure authentication and least privilege.
- Material changes are captured through controlled workflows.
- Historical records and audit trails are preserved appropriately.
- Backup, recovery, and continuity requirements are tested.
- Privacy, retention, and evidence-management requirements are addressed.
- Reporting metrics have documented calculation rules.
- Architecture changes are subject to governance and assurance.
- The operating model has sufficient resources and accountable owners.

The checklist should be adapted to the organization's risk profile and implementation scope. Completion of the checklist is evidence of review, not automatic proof that the architecture is effective.

### 141. AI Inventory Control Validation and Acceptance Testing

The organization should define acceptance tests that demonstrate whether the implemented inventory supports the required governance outcomes.

Examples include:

| Test scenario                                           | Expected result                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A new in-scope AI service is discovered                 | A finding is created and routed for validation                           |
| A record lacks a mandatory field                        | The validation process identifies and prevents inappropriate completion  |
| A model version changes                                 | The record is updated or a discrepancy is raised                         |
| An owner fails to certify a high-risk system            | The overdue certification is escalated                                   |
| An assessment expires                                   | The relevant governance workflow is triggered                            |
| An unauthorized user attempts to change approval status | The action is denied and logged                                          |
| A production system is retired                          | Closure requirements and dependency checks are performed                 |
| An integration fails                                    | The failure is visible and stale data is not presented as newly verified |
| An inventory record conflicts with a discovery source   | A discrepancy is created for resolution                                  |
| Historical evidence is requested                        | The relevant record history and linked evidence can be retrieved         |

Acceptance criteria should be measurable and tied to defined requirements.

The organization should retain test results, identified defects, remediation evidence, and approval decisions. Critical failures should be resolved before the inventory is relied upon for material governance decisions.

### 142. AI Inventory Architecture Exception and Technical Limitation Register

The organization should maintain a register of material limitations affecting the inventory architecture.

Examples include unavailable provider metadata, incomplete cloud discovery coverage, unsupported SaaS integrations, delayed synchronization, unresolved identifier conflicts, and insufficient historical records.

Each limitation should include:

- Description and affected capability.
- Systems or business units impacted.
- Risk and operational consequences.
- Reason the limitation exists.
- Compensating measures.
- Responsible owner.
- Remediation plan.
- Target date.
- Approval or exception reference.
- Review and closure evidence.

Limitations should not be hidden within architecture documentation or treated as resolved merely because the platform has been deployed.

Where a limitation affects high-risk systems, the organization should determine whether additional controls, restrictions, or escalation are necessary until the limitation is resolved.

### 144. AI System Inventory Architecture and Governance Acceptance Criteria

The inventory should be accepted as operational only when the organization can demonstrate that it supports the required governance outcomes.

Acceptance criteria should include:

- A formally defined scope and accountable owner.
- A documented and implemented metadata schema.
- A validated initial population of in-scope systems.
- A controlled process for new registrations.
- Traceability to relevant models, use cases, providers, and deployments.
- Defined risk and assessment linkages.
- Effective access and change controls.
- Operational discovery and reconciliation procedures appropriate to the environment.
- Documented exception and discrepancy handling.
- Reliable lifecycle and retirement management.
- Tested reporting and metrics.
- Appropriate historical traceability and evidence retention.
- Completed acceptance testing.
- A defined operating model and improvement backlog.

The organization should distinguish between technical deployment completion and governance readiness.

A repository may be technically available while remaining unsuitable for enterprise use if ownership is unresolved, records are unvalidated, discovery coverage is unknown, or material control failures remain open.

### 146. AI Inventory Architecture Continual Improvement

The architecture should evolve as the organization's AI estate, technology platforms, threat environment, and regulatory obligations change.

The organization should periodically review whether its discovery mechanisms, data model, integrations, controls, and reporting remain fit for purpose.

Improvement may be required when:

- New AI deployment patterns emerge.
- Agentic systems introduce new dependencies.
- Existing discovery tools lose coverage.
- Model providers change their service interfaces.
- New regulatory requirements apply.
- Material audit findings recur.
- Data quality deteriorates.
- Inventory scale exceeds existing capacity.
- New enterprise platforms become authoritative sources.
- Business restructuring changes system ownership.

Each material architecture improvement should have a defined rationale, accountable owner, implementation plan, and validation criteria.

The objective is to maintain an inventory that remains useful for real governance decisions rather than allowing it to become a static catalogue that reflects only the organization's historical AI estate.

### 147. Core AI System Inventory Architecture Principle

The AI System Inventory architecture must connect authoritative records, discovery capabilities, risk and assessment workflows, security controls, lifecycle management, and governance reporting into a coherent operating capability.

Its effectiveness depends on accurate relationships between systems, models, use cases, providers, deployments, and dependencies. It also depends on secure integrations, traceable changes, reliable reconciliation, and a defined operating model.

The organization must distinguish between **a repository and an inventory capability**, **discovery signals and validated records**, **registration and production authorization**, **technical deployment and governance readiness**, and **dashboard metrics and verified control effectiveness**.

**Core Principle:** An AI System Inventory is operationally effective when it provides a secure, scalable, traceable, and continuously maintained representation of the organization's AI estate, enabling accountable owners and governance functions to make reliable decisions throughout the AI lifecycle.
