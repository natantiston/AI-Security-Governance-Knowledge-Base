# 20.18 — AI Risk Acceptance and Audit Checklists

## Part 1 — AI Risk Acceptance Governance, Decision Frameworks, and Accountability

### 1. Purpose and Scope of AI Risk Acceptance

AI risk acceptance is the formal governance process through which an authorized individual or body decides that a defined level of residual risk associated with an AI system is tolerable under specified conditions.

Risk acceptance does not mean that a risk has been eliminated, that a control deficiency is acceptable in every context, or that the organization is relieved of its legal and regulatory obligations. It means that an authorized risk owner has reviewed the available evidence, considered the potential consequences, evaluated treatment alternatives, and made a documented decision within the organization's risk appetite and delegated authority.

AI systems introduce risk characteristics that make disciplined risk acceptance particularly important. These include probabilistic outputs, model uncertainty, changing behavior across versions, dependence on external providers, prompt injection, data leakage, autonomous tool execution, and the potential for outputs to influence consequential decisions.

An organization therefore needs a repeatable framework for deciding:
- Which AI risks may be accepted.
- Which risks require additional treatment.
- Who has authority to accept particular categories of risk.
- What evidence must support the decision.
- Which conditions must be satisfied before acceptance becomes effective.
- How long an acceptance remains valid.
- What changes require reassessment.
- How accepted risks are monitored, reviewed, and eventually closed.

This lesson establishes practical templates and checklists for governing these decisions.

#### 1.1 Scope of the Risk Acceptance Framework

The framework should apply to AI systems throughout their lifecycle, including:

| Lifecycle stage | Risk acceptance application |
|---|---|
| Use-case initiation | Evaluate whether the proposed business purpose and intended use are acceptable |
| Design and architecture | Accept or reject residual risks associated with the proposed architecture |
| Development and integration | Evaluate risks arising from data pipelines, model development, APIs, tools, and application integrations |
| Testing and validation | Decide whether unresolved findings permit progression to the next lifecycle stage |
| Deployment | Make a documented release decision based on residual risk and required safeguards |
| Production operation | Accept defined operational risks under established monitoring and control conditions |
| Material change | Reassess risk following changes to models, data, integrations, autonomy, or intended use |
| Incident response | Decide whether a system can safely resume operation after an incident |
| Decommissioning | Evaluate residual risks involving retained data, model artifacts, credentials, dependencies, and records |

Risk acceptance should be proportionate to the system's criticality, the sensitivity of the information processed, the consequences of incorrect outputs, the degree of autonomy, and the extent of external exposure.

#### 1.2 Risk Acceptance Versus Risk Treatment

Risk acceptance is only one possible risk-treatment decision.

| Risk response | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Do not initiate or continue the activity creating the risk | Reject an AI use case that cannot be operated within acceptable safety or legal boundaries |
| Mitigate | Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact | Introduce authorization controls for AI agent tool execution |
| Transfer or share | Allocate specified financial or contractual consequences to another party where appropriate | Obtain contractual commitments, insurance, or defined provider responsibilities |
| Accept | Formally retain the residual risk within authorized limits | Accept a bounded, documented limitation after evaluating alternatives |
| Defer a decision | Postpone a final decision while additional evidence is collected, with interim restrictions where necessary | Keep a pilot restricted until a security test is completed |

Transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability for its own obligations, and deferring a decision should not become an informal means of operating indefinitely without authorization.

#### 1.3 Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Risk acceptance must distinguish inherent risk from residual risk.

**Inherent risk** is the risk before considering the relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after considering the design and operating effectiveness of the controls that are actually implemented.

For example, an AI agent with access to a customer database and the ability to modify records may have significant inherent risk because an incorrect or manipulated action could affect customer information.

If the agent is restricted by least-privilege authorization, transaction limits, independent approval for sensitive actions, and monitoring, the residual risk may be lower. However, the organization must validate the controls rather than assume that their presence guarantees effectiveness.

A risk should not be classified as acceptable merely because the residual-risk rating falls below a numerical threshold. The organization must also consider mandatory requirements, potentially severe consequences, uncertainty, and the quality of the supporting evidence.

#### 1.4 When Formal Risk Acceptance Is Required

Formal risk acceptance should be required when:
- A material security control cannot be implemented before deployment.
- A significant vulnerability remains unresolved.
- An AI system has a documented limitation that creates meaningful exposure.
- A third-party AI provider cannot meet a required control.
- A proposed deployment exceeds a standard operating baseline.
- A business owner requests continued operation despite an identified risk.
- A security assessment identifies unresolved high-risk findings.
- A system change introduces additional autonomy or access privileges.
- A remediation deadline cannot be met.
- A risk remains after an incident or a major control failure.

The organization should define thresholds that trigger formal approval. Minor operational risks may be handled through routine procedures, while high-impact risks may require senior executive or committee-level review.

#### 1.5 Risk Acceptance Is Not a Substitute for Mandatory Compliance

A risk acceptance decision must not be used to override an obligation that the organization is not legally or contractually permitted to waive.

For example, if an applicable legal requirement mandates a specific action, a business owner cannot make that obligation disappear by accepting the associated risk.

The organization should distinguish between:
- Discretionary internal controls that may permit a formally authorized exception.
- Contractual obligations that require assessment by the appropriate legal or commercial authority.
- Legal or regulatory obligations that cannot be waived through ordinary internal approval.
- Safety requirements for which the organization's governance framework prohibits acceptance beyond defined limits.
- Security requirements designated as non-waivable by policy.

Where the permissibility of a proposed decision is uncertain, the relevant legal, compliance, privacy, or safety function should assess the matter before approval.

**Core Principle:** AI risk acceptance is a documented decision to retain defined residual risk within authorized boundaries; it is not risk elimination, blanket permission, or a waiver of mandatory obligations.

### 2. AI Risk Acceptance Principles and Governance Rules

A reliable AI risk acceptance process should follow consistent principles across business units, technical teams, and AI use cases.

Without common principles, different teams may accept similar risks under inconsistent conditions. This can lead to fragmented accountability, weak control enforcement, and difficulty demonstrating that the organization's AI risk decisions are defensible.

The principles below establish a minimum governance baseline.

#### 2.1 Accountability

Every accepted risk must have an identifiable risk owner.

The risk owner is accountable for understanding the business implications of the risk, ensuring that the decision is made at the appropriate authority level, and monitoring whether the assumptions supporting acceptance remain valid.

The risk owner does not necessarily implement the technical controls. That responsibility may belong to engineering, IT operations, cybersecurity, a model owner, or a third-party provider.

**Governance requirement:** No material AI risk should be accepted without a named risk owner and documented approval authority.

#### 2.2 Evidence-Based Decisions

Risk acceptance must be based on documented evidence rather than assumptions or informal assurances.

Relevant evidence may include:
- AI system inventory records.
- Threat models and risk assessments.
- Security architecture reviews.
- Model and application testing.
- Penetration testing and adversarial testing.
- Privacy and data protection assessments.
- Third-party assurance reports.
- Control test results.
- Incident history.
- Business impact assessments.
- Known limitations and unresolved findings.

Where evidence is incomplete, the decision record should explicitly describe the uncertainty and explain how it affects the assessment.

#### 2.3 Proportionality

The depth of review should be proportionate to the risk.

A restricted internal assistant that uses approved, non-sensitive information may require a simpler acceptance process than an autonomous system that can execute financial transactions or influence consequential customer decisions.

Proportionality must not be used to bypass mandatory requirements. It determines the level of assessment, review, and oversight appropriate to the situation.

#### 2.4 Explicit Residual-Risk Ownership

The risk owner must understand what remains exposed after controls have been applied.

A statement such as "security has reviewed the system" is not sufficient.

The decision record should identify:
- The risk scenario.
- The affected assets and business processes.
- The expected consequences.
- The controls currently operating.
- The limitations of those controls.
- The residual-risk rating and rationale.
- The conditions under which the risk was accepted.

#### 2.5 Time-Bound Decisions

Risk acceptance should have a defined review date and, where appropriate, an expiration date.

This is especially important when acceptance is based on a temporary control gap, an unresolved vulnerability, a third-party limitation, or an interim architecture.

Permanent acceptance may be appropriate for certain residual risks inherent in an approved system design, but it still requires periodic review and reassessment following material changes.

#### 2.6 Independent Challenge

The person requesting risk acceptance should not automatically be the sole person assessing and approving it.

Independent review is particularly important when:
- The risk is high or critical.
- The decision affects regulated or sensitive data.
- The system has consequential or autonomous capabilities.
- The proposed acceptance concerns a mandatory internal control.
- The same control weakness has caused repeated incidents.
- The requester has a strong incentive to proceed despite unresolved risk.

The required degree of independence should be defined in the governance framework.

#### 2.7 Traceability

Each material decision should be traceable to:
- The relevant AI system.
- The identified risk.
- The applicable control requirements.
- The assessment evidence.
- The authorized decision-maker.
- The approval conditions.
- The review and expiration dates.
- The subsequent treatment or closure outcome.

Traceability supports management oversight, internal audit, external assurance, and consistent risk reporting.

#### 2.8 Change Sensitivity

Risk acceptance is valid only while its material assumptions remain sufficiently accurate.

Reassessment should be triggered by relevant changes, including:
- A new model or model version.
- A material change to training, evaluation, or operational data.
- New retrieval sources or integrations.
- Expanded tool permissions.
- Increased autonomy.
- A change in intended use.
- A significant security incident.
- A failed control.
- A material change in the provider or its service.
- New legal, regulatory, or contractual requirements.

#### 2.9 Risk Acceptance Governance Checklist

| Governance principle | Verification question | Expected evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Accountability | Is a named risk owner accountable for the decision? | Risk record and ownership assignment |
| Authority | Is the decision within the approver's delegated authority? | Approval matrix and decision record |
| Evidence | Are relevant assessment results available? | Risk assessment and supporting evidence |
| Proportionality | Is the review depth appropriate to the risk? | Documented assessment rationale |
| Residual risk | Is the remaining exposure explicitly described? | Residual-risk assessment |
| Compliance | Have non-waivable requirements been evaluated? | Relevant specialist assessment |
| Independence | Has appropriate independent review occurred? | Review and challenge record |
| Time limitation | Is a review date or expiry defined? | Review schedule |
| Change sensitivity | Are reassessment triggers documented? | Risk acceptance conditions |
| Traceability | Can the decision be reconstructed? | Linked records and audit trail |

**Core Principle:** Consistent risk acceptance requires accountable ownership, evidence-based assessment, independent challenge, and ongoing validity checks.

### 3. AI Risk Acceptance Versus Security Exceptions, Risk Waivers, and Risk Treatment

Organizations often use terms such as risk acceptance, security exception, waiver, and risk treatment interchangeably. This creates ambiguity about what was approved, who owns the risk, and which requirements remain applicable.

A well-designed GRC framework defines these concepts separately while allowing their records and workflows to be connected.

#### 3.1 Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is a decision to retain identified residual risk.

Example:

An organization assesses the risk associated with a customer-service AI assistant. After implementing approved access controls, monitoring, and human review for consequential actions, the authorized risk owner determines that the remaining risk is within the organization's approved risk appetite.

The decision should record the residual risk, the controls supporting the assessment, the owner's rationale, and the conditions for reassessment.

Risk acceptance does not necessarily mean that a control is missing. Residual risk remains even when controls operate as intended.

#### 3.2 Security Exception

A security exception authorizes a defined deviation from a specified internal control, policy, standard, or baseline for a defined period or under defined conditions.

Example:

An AI application cannot immediately implement a required logging capability. The organization evaluates alternatives, establishes compensating controls, and authorizes a time-limited deviation subject to monitoring and remediation.

An exception generally requires a risk assessment because the deviation may introduce or increase risk.

#### 3.3 Risk Waiver

A risk waiver is an organization's formal term for an authorized waiver of a particular internal requirement. Its exact meaning depends on the organization's policy and delegated authority framework.

Some organizations use "waiver" and "exception" synonymously. Others distinguish a waiver from a temporary exception.

The organization should define these terms explicitly rather than assuming that all stakeholders interpret them identically.

A waiver of an internal requirement does not automatically waive a legal, regulatory, or contractual obligation.

#### 3.4 Risk Treatment

Risk treatment describes the action taken in response to risk.

Possible treatments include:
- Avoiding the activity.
- Implementing additional controls.
- Redesigning the system.
- Restricting functionality.
- Sharing specified consequences through contractual arrangements or insurance.
- Accepting residual risk.

Risk treatment is the broader process; risk acceptance is one possible decision within it.

#### 3.5 Comparison Matrix

| Dimension | Risk acceptance | Security exception | Risk waiver | Risk treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Retain defined residual risk | Authorize a deviation from a requirement | Waive a specified internal requirement under defined authority | Decide how to respond to risk |
| Requires identified risk | Yes, for a meaningful acceptance decision | Normally, because the deviation may introduce risk | Normally, where the waived requirement affects risk | Yes |
| Requires a control deviation | Not necessarily | Yes | Usually, depending on policy definition | Not necessarily |
| Time limitation | Depends on the risk and governance policy; review is required | Commonly time-bound or condition-bound | Depends on the organization's waiver policy | Depends on the selected treatment |
| Typical approver | Authorized risk owner | Authorized exception approver and risk owner, as defined by policy | Authority designated by the waiver policy | Relevant treatment owner and decision authority |
| Example | Accepting limited residual risk after controls are implemented | Temporarily deviating from a logging standard | Formally waiving an internal control requirement | Restricting an agent's permissions to reduce risk |

#### 3.7 Example: AI Agent with an Unresolved Authorization Control

A business unit proposes an AI agent that can retrieve information from an enterprise CRM platform. A security review finds that the integration cannot yet enforce the required fine-grained authorization policy.

The organization should not treat this as a single administrative decision.

It should separately establish:

1. **Risk identification:** The agent may access or manipulate information beyond its intended scope.
2. **Risk assessment:** Evaluate the likelihood, impact, attack paths, and affected data.
3. **Treatment analysis:** Consider redesign, reduced permissions, a different integration, or delayed deployment.
4. **Exception decision:** Determine whether a deviation from the internal authorization requirement is permissible.
5. **Risk acceptance decision:** Determine whether the remaining risk can be accepted by the authorized risk owner.
6. **Operating conditions:** Define permitted functionality, monitoring, review dates, and restrictions.
7. **Reassessment:** Review the decision when the authorization design changes or evidence indicates a control failure.

If the organization cannot establish effective safeguards or the risk exceeds its acceptable boundaries, the correct decision may be to restrict or reject the deployment.

**Core Principle:** Risk acceptance, security exceptions, waivers, and risk treatment are related but distinct governance concepts; their decisions and records must be clearly defined and traceable.

### 4. AI Risk Acceptance Roles, Responsibilities, and Decision Authority

Risk acceptance requires clear separation between those who identify risk, those who implement controls, those who own business outcomes, and those authorized to approve residual risk.

Without defined decision authority, teams may assume that a technical review constitutes business approval or that a project sponsor can accept any risk associated with a delivery deadline.

A mature governance framework specifies which roles can approve which risks, under what conditions, and when escalation is mandatory.

#### 4.1 Principal Roles

| Role | Risk acceptance responsibilities |
|---|---|
| Business owner | Explains the business objective, operational consequences, and business impact of the risk |
| AI system owner | Maintains system accountability, confirms intended use, and coordinates lifecycle risk management |
| Risk owner | Accountable for the risk and its treatment; accepts residual risk within delegated authority |
| Cybersecurity | Assesses security threats, control effectiveness, vulnerabilities, and mitigation options |
| AI governance function | Ensures that AI-specific governance, oversight, and lifecycle requirements are considered |
| Security architect | Evaluates architecture, trust boundaries, dependencies, and alternative designs |
| Data owner | Confirms data classification, permitted use, access requirements, and sensitivity |
| Privacy function | Assesses relevant personal-data processing and privacy risks |
| Legal and compliance | Evaluates applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual constraints |
| Model owner | Provides information about model behavior, limitations, evaluation results, and model changes |
| Engineering or operations | Implements controls, maintains operational safeguards, and supplies technical evidence |
| Third-party risk management | Evaluates external providers and relevant contractual or assurance evidence |
| Internal audit | Provides independent assurance over governance and control processes; ordinarily does not own management's risk decision |
| Executive management or risk committee | Decides matters escalated beyond delegated operational authority |

The exact role names may vary. Organizations should assign these responsibilities according to their existing governance structure.

#### 4.2 Risk Owner Versus Control Owner

These roles serve different purposes.

**Risk owner:** Accountable for the business or operational risk and its treatment decision.

**Control owner:** Accountable for the design, implementation, operation, or monitoring of a control.

For example, a business owner may accept a defined residual risk associated with an AI use case, while the cybersecurity team owns the implementation of a monitoring control.

The control owner should provide evidence of control effectiveness but should not automatically become the risk owner simply because the issue is technical.

#### 4.3 Illustrative Risk Acceptance Authority Matrix

The following matrix is illustrative and must be adapted to the organization's risk appetite, policies, and delegated authority framework.

| Risk category | Typical review participants | Potential decision authority |
|---|---|---|
| Low-risk operational limitation | AI system owner, relevant control owner | Designated operational risk owner |
| Moderate security risk | Business owner, cybersecurity, system owner | Authorized business risk owner |
| High security risk | Security leadership, business owner, relevant specialist functions | Senior risk owner or delegated executive |
| High privacy or compliance risk | Privacy, legal, compliance, security, business owner | Authority designated by the applicable governance framework |
| Critical enterprise risk | Executive leadership, security leadership, relevant governance committee | Executive or committee-level authority, where acceptance is permitted |
| Potentially prohibited activity | Legal, compliance, safety, and relevant accountable functions | Must be resolved under applicable mandatory requirements; ordinary risk acceptance may not be available |

The table does not establish universal approval rights. The organization must define actual decision rights, escalation thresholds, and prohibited acceptance categories.

**RACI definitions:**
- **R — Responsible:** Performs the work.
- **A — Accountable:** Owns the outcome or decision.
- **C — Consulted:** Provides required input.
- **I — Informed:** Receives updates.

The organization should adjust the matrix to avoid assigning accountability ambiguously. In particular, the risk owner and the formal approver may be different roles when policy requires independent authorization.

#### 4.5 Delegation of Authority Requirements

The governance framework should define:
- Risk categories each role may accept.
- Maximum residual-risk levels within each authority.
- Whether financial or operational impact thresholds apply.
- Which security findings require mandatory escalation.
- Which risks cannot be accepted.
- When independent review is mandatory.
- Whether approval must be recorded in a GRC platform.
- How temporary or emergency decisions are handled.
- Who may approve renewals.
- What happens when the designated risk owner is unavailable.

Delegated authority should be documented and periodically reviewed. A person should not approve a risk solely because they hold a senior title if the decision falls outside their formal authority.

#### 4.6 Risk Acceptance Approval Checklist

Before approval, confirm that:

- [ ] The affected AI system and business process are identified.
- [ ] The risk scenario is clearly described.
- [ ] Inherent and residual risk have been assessed.
- [ ] Relevant security controls have been evaluated.
- [ ] Privacy, legal, compliance, and safety considerations have been addressed where applicable.
- [ ] Alternative treatment options have been considered.
- [ ] The decision is consistent with risk appetite.
- [ ] Mandatory requirements have not been improperly waived.
- [ ] The risk owner and approver have appropriate authority.
- [ ] Approval conditions and restrictions are explicit.
- [ ] A review date and reassessment triggers are defined.
- [ ] Supporting evidence is retained.
- [ ] Required stakeholders have been consulted.
- [ ] Related risk, control, system, and exception records are linked.

**Core Principle:** Risk acceptance authority must be explicit, proportionate to the risk, and supported by clear accountability, appropriate specialist review, and a traceable decision record.

### 5. AI Risk Acceptance Decision Framework

A formal decision framework helps the organization distinguish risks that can be accepted from those requiring further treatment, escalation, or rejection.

The framework should not rely solely on a numerical score. AI risk decisions may involve uncertainty, potentially severe consequences, limitations in evidence, or obligations that cannot be waived through ordinary business approval.

#### 5.1 Decision Inputs

Before deciding whether to accept residual risk, reviewers should evaluate:

1. **Business necessity:** Is the AI capability needed for a legitimate and defined business objective?
2. **Risk exposure:** What threats, vulnerabilities, and failure scenarios remain?
3. **Potential impact:** What could happen to individuals, customers, operations, data, or the organization?
4. **Control effectiveness:** Are relevant safeguards implemented and tested?
5. **Uncertainty:** Which material assumptions remain unverified?
6. **Alternatives:** Can the risk be reduced through redesign, restriction, or a different solution?
7. **Risk appetite:** Is the remaining exposure within approved organizational boundaries?
8. **Compliance:** Are applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and policy requirements satisfied?
9. **Authority:** Is the proposed decision within the approver's delegated authority?
10. **Monitoring:** Can the risk be detected, reviewed, and reassessed during operation?

#### 5.2 Decision Outcomes

| Outcome | When appropriate | Required action |
|---|---|---|
| Accept | Residual risk is within approved boundaries and evidence is sufficient | Record the decision and establish monitoring |
| Accept with conditions | Risk is acceptable only if specified safeguards or restrictions are implemented | Define measurable conditions and verify them before activation |
| Mitigate further | Additional controls can materially reduce risk | Assign actions, owners, and target dates |
| Restrict | Risk can be reduced by limiting users, data, functions, or autonomy | Implement and validate restrictions |
| Defer | Important evidence is missing and a decision cannot yet be justified | Keep deployment or operation restricted as appropriate |
| Escalate | Risk exceeds delegated authority or requires specialist judgment | Refer the decision to the authorized body |
| Reject or avoid | Risk is unacceptable or the activity cannot be conducted within applicable boundaries | Do not deploy or discontinue the affected activity |

The decision framework should allow escalation where uncertainty or potential impact warrants additional review, even when a preliminary risk score appears acceptable.

#### 5.4 Decision Rationale Requirements

The approval record should explain:
- Why the risk exists.
- Why the selected treatment is appropriate.
- Which alternatives were considered.
- Why the residual risk is acceptable or unacceptable.
- Which controls support the decision.
- Which limitations remain.
- What conditions must be maintained.
- Who is accountable for monitoring.
- What changes trigger reassessment.
- When the decision will be reviewed.

A defensible decision is one that can be reconstructed from the evidence available at the time, not one that merely contains an approval signature.

#### 5.5 Example: Acceptance Decision for an Internal AI Assistant

**Use case:** An internal AI assistant summarizes approved company documentation for employees.

**Identified risk:** The assistant may retrieve information from documents that the requesting employee is not authorized to access.

**Inherent risk:** Moderate to high, depending on the sensitivity and breadth of the accessible document repository.

**Existing controls:**
- Enterprise authentication.
- Document-level access restrictions.
- Retrieval authorization.
- Monitoring of relevant access and retrieval activity.
- Security testing against attempts to retrieve restricted information.

**Remaining uncertainty:** The organization has not yet completed adversarial testing of all document connectors.

**Treatment options:**
1. Delay deployment until all testing is complete.
2. Restrict the assistant to a smaller, approved document corpus.
3. Deploy to a limited pilot group with verified access restrictions.
4. Deploy broadly without additional restrictions.

The fourth option should not be considered acceptable merely because it accelerates deployment.

**Potential decision:** Permit a limited pilot if the access controls have been validated for the pilot scope, the remaining uncertainty is documented, and the authorized risk owner determines that the residual risk is acceptable. Broader deployment remains subject to the required testing and approval.

**Approval conditions:**
- No unassessed document repositories may be connected.
- Access restrictions must be enforced independently of model instructions.
- Relevant access events must be logged.
- Testing must be completed before the next deployment stage.
- Any confirmed unauthorized retrieval triggers reassessment and appropriate response.

This example demonstrates conditional risk acceptance without treating a pilot approval as authorization for unrestricted production deployment.

**Core Principle:** A risk acceptance decision must follow a structured evaluation of permissibility, evidence, control effectiveness, residual risk, alternatives, and delegated authority.

It does not authorize use beyond the documented scope, waive applicable mandatory obligations, or remove the requirement to reassess the risk when material changes occur.

This statement should be adapted to the organization's policies and actual decision. It must not be used as boilerplate to imply acceptance where the required assessment or approval has not occurred.

#### 6.3 Decision Quality Checklist

Before finalizing the record, verify:

- [ ] The risk statement identifies a meaningful event and consequence.
- [ ] The AI system and business context are sufficiently described.
- [ ] Inherent and residual risk are distinguished.
- [ ] Control effectiveness is supported by evidence.
- [ ] Material uncertainty is documented.
- [ ] Alternatives have been considered.
- [ ] Applicable obligations have been assessed.
- [ ] The decision is consistent with approved risk appetite.
- [ ] The risk owner and approver have appropriate authority.
- [ ] Conditions are measurable and enforceable.
- [ ] Monitoring and review responsibilities are assigned.
- [ ] Reassessment and revocation triggers are defined.
- [ ] Related GRC records are linked.
- [ ] The final decision and its rationale are retained.

**Core Principle:** A complete AI risk acceptance record explains the risk, the evidence, the alternatives, the authorized decision, the conditions, and the mechanism for future reassessment.

### 7. AI Risk Acceptance and Residual-Risk Rating

Residual-risk rating provides a structured way to compare assessed exposure with the organization's approved risk appetite.

However, numerical ratings are decision-support mechanisms, not substitutes for professional judgment. AI systems can produce low-frequency but high-impact outcomes, and uncertainty may be substantial where evidence is incomplete or system behavior is difficult to predict.

The organization should use a consistent methodology while preserving the ability to escalate risks that require qualitative judgment.

#### 7.1 Rating Components

A typical methodology considers:

- **Likelihood:** The probability or plausibility of a defined risk scenario within a specified period.
- **Impact:** The severity of the consequences if the scenario occurs.
- **Control effectiveness:** The extent to which implemented controls reduce likelihood or impact.
- **Exposure:** The number and sensitivity of affected assets, users, processes, and external parties.
- **Uncertainty:** The quality and completeness of available evidence.
- **Risk appetite:** The level and type of risk the organization is willing to retain.

Some organizations calculate risk using a likelihood-impact matrix. Others use quantitative estimates, scenario analysis, or multiple dimensions.

The selected methodology should be documented and consistently applied.

#### 7.2 Illustrative Likelihood Scale

| Rating | Description | Example interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Rare | Highly unusual under current conditions | No credible scenario identified under the assessed operating assumptions, though risk is not impossible |
| 2 — Unlikely | Plausible but not expected in normal operation | Requires a relatively uncommon combination of conditions |
| 3 — Possible | Credible under identifiable circumstances | A realistic scenario exists and relevant exposure is present |
| 4 — Likely | Expected to occur in relevant conditions or observed with meaningful frequency | Exposure and threat conditions make recurrence credible |
| 5 — Almost certain | Expected repeatedly or already occurring frequently | Strong evidence of recurring events or persistent exposure |

The organization must define the time horizon and supporting evidence for each rating.

#### 7.3 Illustrative Impact Scale

| Rating | Description | AI-related example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Negligible | Minimal operational or business effect | Minor incorrect output in a controlled, non-consequential test |
| 2 — Minor | Limited disruption or low-impact error | Localized issue affecting a small number of internal users |
| 3 — Moderate | Meaningful operational, financial, confidentiality, or compliance impact | Unauthorized access to a limited set of internal business information |
| 4 — Major | Significant business disruption, sensitive-data exposure, or material control failure | An agent performs unauthorized changes across an important business process |
| 5 — Severe | Potentially catastrophic, widespread, irreversible, or highly consequential harm | Major sensitive-data compromise or an AI-enabled action that creates severe safety or business consequences |

These descriptions are illustrative. Organizations should tailor them to their operations, applicable obligations, and impact assessment methodology.

#### 7.4 Illustrative Risk Matrix

The following matrix uses a simple likelihood × impact calculation for demonstration only.

| Likelihood \ Impact | 1 — Negligible | 2 — Minor | 3 — Moderate | 4 — Major | 5 — Severe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 — Rare | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| 2 — Unlikely | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| 3 — Possible | 3 | 6 | 9 | 12 | 15 |
| 4 — Likely | 4 | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 5 — Almost certain | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |

An organization might define rating bands such as low, moderate, high, and critical, but those thresholds must be approved internally. A simple multiplication score does not capture every dimension of AI risk and should not be used as the sole basis for accepting severe or uncertain scenarios.

#### 7.5 AI-Specific Rating Considerations

AI risk assessments should consider factors such as:

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Model uncertainty | Outputs may vary across inputs and operating conditions |
| Prompt injection | Untrusted instructions may influence retrieval or tool use |
| Agent autonomy | The system may execute actions without immediate human intervention |
| Tool privileges | Excessive permissions may magnify the consequences of an unsafe output |
| Data sensitivity | Exposure may create privacy, confidentiality, or contractual consequences |
| System connectivity | Downstream integrations may expand the attack surface and impact |
| Human oversight | Review may be ineffective if reviewers lack context, authority, or time |
| Provider dependency | Limited transparency or evidence access may constrain investigation |
| Model and configuration changes | Previous test results may no longer represent the current system |
| Scale of operation | A repeated failure may affect many users or records quickly |

#### 7.6 When Numerical Scores Are Insufficient

A numerical score should not override:
- A non-waivable legal or regulatory requirement.
- An unacceptable safety condition.
- A prohibition established by organizational policy.
- A material lack of evidence concerning a potentially severe outcome.
- A known failure of a critical preventive control.
- A risk that exceeds the approver's delegated authority.

For example, a low estimated likelihood should not automatically justify accepting a scenario that could produce severe, irreversible harm when the available evidence is insufficient to establish that the risk is controlled.

**Core Principle:** Residual-risk ratings support consistent decisions, but acceptance must also consider evidence quality, uncertainty, potential severity, mandatory requirements, and the organization's approved risk appetite.

### 8. AI Risk Acceptance Conditions, Monitoring, and Reassessment Triggers

A risk acceptance decision is not a permanent declaration that a system is safe. It is a decision based on defined conditions, evidence, and assumptions.

Those conditions must be translated into operational requirements so that the accepted risk remains visible and can be reassessed when circumstances change.

#### 8.1 Types of Acceptance Conditions

**Technical conditions**
- Restrict access to approved users and service identities.
- Enforce least privilege for AI tools and integrations.
- Disable unapproved capabilities.
- Maintain required logging and monitoring.
- Validate authorization at the appropriate enforcement points.
- Apply rate limits, transaction limits, or execution timeouts where justified.
- Maintain approved model and configuration baselines.

**Operational conditions**
- Limit deployment to an approved pilot group.
- Require human approval for defined consequential actions.
- Assign a monitoring owner.
- Establish an incident escalation procedure.
- Restrict the system to approved business processes.
- Maintain an approved fallback or shutdown procedure.

**Governance conditions**
- Review the risk at defined intervals.
- Reassess the system after material changes.
- Complete specified remediation actions.
- Retain evidence of control effectiveness.
- Obtain renewed approval when the original acceptance expires or its assumptions change.

#### 8.2 Measurable Versus Vague Conditions

| Vague condition | Improved condition |
|---|---|
| Monitor the AI system closely | Review defined security alerts daily and escalate specified high-impact events according to the incident response standard |
| Improve security later | Implement the approved authorization control by the documented target date and provide successful test evidence |
| Limit agent access | Restrict the agent to enumerated tools and approved operations, enforce authorization at the execution boundary, and test unauthorized invocation attempts |
| Review the risk periodically | Reassess the risk every defined interval and after specified material changes |
| Keep sensitive data secure | Prohibit defined data categories from entering the service unless an approved assessment and control design explicitly permit them |
| Obtain management approval | Record approval by the designated risk authority before the system enters the approved operating state |

Conditions should be testable, attributable to an owner, and linked to evidence.

#### 8.3 Monitoring Plan Template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Risk acceptance ID | Reference to the accepted risk |
| Risk scenario | Risk being monitored |
| Indicator | Observable signal or metric |
| Data source | System or record providing evidence |
| Threshold | Condition requiring investigation or escalation |
| Monitoring frequency | How often monitoring occurs |
| Monitoring owner | Responsible role |
| Escalation path | Who must be notified |
| Required action | Response to a threshold breach |
| Evidence retention | How monitoring evidence is preserved |
| Review date | Date of the next formal review |

#### 8.4 Reassessment Triggers

A formal reassessment should be considered when:
- The AI model is replaced or materially updated.
- The system's intended purpose changes.
- New data sources are connected.
- The sensitivity of processed data increases.
- The system gains additional tool permissions.
- Autonomy or decision-making authority expands.
- A new vulnerability or attack technique becomes relevant.
- A compensating control fails.
- An incident or near miss challenges the original risk assumptions.
- The provider changes material service capabilities or data-handling practices.
- A material legal, regulatory, or contractual requirement changes.
- A risk owner or accountable business unit changes.
- Monitoring indicates that the risk exceeds its approved threshold.

The organization should distinguish between a routine review and a trigger-based reassessment. A material event may require immediate review rather than waiting for the next scheduled date.

#### 8.5 Risk Acceptance Suspension Criteria

The organization should define conditions under which the system must be restricted or the acceptance suspended.

Potential criteria include:
- A critical safeguard becomes unavailable.
- Unauthorized consequential actions are detected.
- A sensitive-data exposure exceeds the approved scope.
- The system operates outside its authorized use case.
- Required monitoring becomes unavailable for a period that exceeds the approved tolerance.
- A material assumption underlying the acceptance is invalidated.
- The risk owner withdraws acceptance.
- The organization determines that continued operation would violate an applicable requirement.

Suspension should follow approved operational procedures and account for safety and continuity considerations. Where immediate shutdown could itself create unacceptable consequences, the organization should use an approved safe-state procedure.

#### 8.6 Periodic Review Checklist

- [ ] The AI system remains within its approved intended use.
- [ ] The risk scenario and affected assets remain accurately described.
- [ ] Relevant controls remain implemented.
- [ ] Control effectiveness is supported by current evidence.
- [ ] Residual risk remains within approved boundaries.
- [ ] No material incident has invalidated the original assessment.
- [ ] Required conditions have been met.
- [ ] Outstanding remediation remains on track.
- [ ] Monitoring thresholds remain appropriate.
- [ ] Relevant provider and dependency assumptions remain valid.
- [ ] Applicable requirements have been reassessed where necessary.
- [ ] The risk owner remains accountable and authorized.
- [ ] The decision remains valid for the current system configuration.
- [ ] The next review date and any required actions are recorded.

**Core Principle:** Risk acceptance remains valid only while its assumptions, safeguards, scope, and approval conditions remain sufficiently accurate and effective.

This lifecycle ensures that accepted risks remain visible and that approval does not become an indefinite authorization to operate with unresolved security weaknesses.

The lifecycle should be integrated with the organization's AI governance framework, enterprise risk management process, security exception management, change management, and system development lifecycle.

### 12. AI Risk Acceptance Control Validation and Assurance

Risk acceptance depends on understanding whether the controls supporting the decision are genuinely effective.

A control listed in a policy, architecture diagram, or implementation plan is not necessarily an operating control. The organization must distinguish between control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness.

This distinction is especially important for AI systems because security behavior can depend on model configuration, orchestration logic, retrieval permissions, external tools, provider settings, and changing data or usage patterns.

#### 12.1 Three Levels of Control Evaluation

| Evaluation level | Key question | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Design effectiveness | Is the control appropriately designed to address the identified risk? | Architecture review, control design, threat model |
| Implementation effectiveness | Has the control been deployed as intended? | Configuration records, deployment evidence, access policies |
| Operating effectiveness | Does the control work consistently under relevant operating conditions? | Test results, monitoring records, sampled transactions, incident analysis |

A risk acceptance decision should not claim that a control reduces risk if the control has only been proposed but not implemented.

Where implementation is incomplete, the record should clearly distinguish the current residual risk from the expected risk after planned remediation.

#### 12.2 AI-Specific Control Validation Areas

**Identity and access management**
- Verify that user and service identities are authenticated.
- Confirm that permissions follow least privilege.
- Test whether access restrictions are enforced at the data or tool execution boundary.
- Check whether service credentials are appropriately stored, rotated, and restricted.

**Prompt and instruction security**
- Test whether untrusted inputs can influence privileged actions.
- Assess whether the system distinguishes trusted instructions from retrieved or user-supplied content.
- Verify that security controls do not depend solely on the model voluntarily following instructions.

**Retrieval and data security**
- Confirm that retrieval respects source-level permissions.
- Test whether unauthorized documents can be retrieved through indirect queries.
- Evaluate whether sensitive information can be exposed through outputs, logs, or debugging records.

**Tool and action execution**
- Verify that tools have narrowly defined permissions.
- Confirm that sensitive actions require appropriate authorization.
- Test input validation, parameter restrictions, and transaction limits.
- Evaluate whether the system can perform prohibited actions through alternative tool paths.

**Monitoring and incident response**
- Confirm that relevant events are logged.
- Verify that alerts reach the responsible operational teams.
- Test whether incident responders can reconstruct relevant AI activity.
- Confirm that emergency restrictions and safe-state procedures work as intended.

**Third-party dependencies**
- Verify that contractual and technical assurances support the actual deployment.
- Confirm that provider limitations are documented.
- Evaluate whether external dependencies can be monitored sufficiently to support the risk decision.

#### 12.3 Evidence Quality Assessment

| Evidence characteristic | Strong evidence | Weak evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Specificity | Directly relates to the accepted risk and control | General assurance with no risk-specific detail |
| Currency | Reflects the current system version and configuration | Based on an older deployment |
| Authenticity | Produced by a traceable system, reviewer, or provider | Informal statement with unclear origin |
| Completeness | Covers the relevant scope and important failure paths | Tests only a narrow or unrepresentative scenario |
| Reproducibility | Results can be repeated or independently verified | Results cannot be reconstructed |
| Independence | Reviewed by an appropriately independent party where required | Relies entirely on the requester's unsupported assertion |
| Traceability | Linked to the risk, control, test, and decision | Stored without a reliable relationship to the approval |

Evidence should be retained in accordance with the organization's security, privacy, and records-management requirements.

| Field | Required information |

| Validation ID | Unique identifier |
| Risk acceptance ID | Related acceptance |
| Control ID | Control being validated |
| Control objective | Risk reduction intended by the control |
| Test scope | System, configuration, environment, and components tested |
| Test method | Inspection, interview, configuration review, sampling, or technical test |
| Test date | Date performed |
| Tester | Individual or team performing the test |
| Expected result | Required control behavior |

#### 12.5 Failed Control Validation

When a control fails, the organization should determine whether the failure invalidates the acceptance decision.

The response should consider:
1. Whether the failed control was essential to the original risk assessment.
2. Whether other controls provide sufficient independent protection.
3. Whether the failure expands the risk beyond the approved scope.
4. Whether the system should be restricted or placed into a safe state.
5. Whether incident response is required.
6. Whether the risk owner and approver must be notified.
7. Whether the acceptance must be suspended or revoked.
8. Whether similar systems may have the same weakness.

A failed test should not automatically be treated as a minor remediation item if the original approval depended on the control functioning correctly.

**Core Principle:** Control effectiveness must be demonstrated through current, relevant evidence; an accepted risk cannot remain justified when a critical supporting control has failed without reassessment.

### 13. AI Risk Acceptance Audit Checklist

An AI risk acceptance audit evaluates whether the organization has authorized, documented, monitored, and reviewed accepted risks according to its governance requirements.

The objective is not simply to confirm that approval records exist. The audit should determine whether the process is reliable, the decisions are defensible, and the organization can demonstrate that accepted risks remain within approved boundaries.

Internal audit should preserve its independence and should not become the operational owner of risk acceptance decisions it later needs to assess.

#### 13.1 Audit Objectives

An audit may seek to determine whether:

- Risk acceptance policies and procedures are defined.
- Delegated approval authority is documented.
- Risk assessments are complete and consistent.
- Decisions are supported by evidence.
- Mandatory obligations are appropriately considered.
- Residual risks are within approved boundaries.
- Acceptance conditions are implemented and monitored.
- Expired or invalid acceptances are identified.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Records are complete, traceable, and protected.
- Management reporting supports appropriate oversight.

#### 13.2 Governance and Policy Audit

| Audit ID | Audit question | Expected evidence | Example finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| RA-GOV-01 | Is there an approved AI risk acceptance policy or procedure? | Approved policy and version history | Process is informal or undocumented |
| RA-GOV-02 | Are risk acceptance roles and responsibilities defined? | Governance charter, RACI, role descriptions | Ownership is unclear |
| RA-GOV-03 | Is delegated approval authority documented? | Approval matrix and delegation records | Decisions are approved by unauthorized roles |
| RA-GOV-04 | Are non-waivable requirements identified? | Policy provisions and compliance guidance | Internal approval is incorrectly treated as a legal waiver |
| RA-GOV-05 | Are escalation criteria established? | Risk thresholds and escalation procedures | High-risk decisions remain at an inappropriate level |
| RA-GOV-06 | Are independence requirements defined? | Review procedures and evidence of challenge | Requester effectively approves their own request without required review |
| RA-GOV-07 | Are periodic reviews required? | Policy requirements and review schedules | Acceptances remain open indefinitely without reassessment |

#### 13.3 Risk Assessment Audit

| Audit ID | Audit question | Expected evidence | Example finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| RA-RSK-01 | Is the risk scenario clearly described? | Risk statement and supporting analysis | Record states only "AI security risk" |
| RA-RSK-02 | Are inherent and residual risk distinguished? | Risk assessment and methodology | Rating does not account for controls |
| RA-RSK-03 | Are controls evaluated for effectiveness? | Control tests and assessment results | Controls are assumed effective without evidence |
| RA-RSK-04 | Are material AI-specific threats considered? | Threat model and relevant test results | Agent permissions and prompt injection are omitted where relevant |
| RA-RSK-05 | Is uncertainty documented? | Assumptions, evidence gaps, and limitations | Decision implies confidence unsupported by evidence |
| RA-RSK-06 | Are alternative treatments evaluated? | Treatment analysis | Acceptance is selected without considering feasible mitigations |
| RA-RSK-07 | Is the decision consistent with risk appetite? | Approved risk criteria and decision rationale | High residual risk is accepted without authorized escalation |

#### 13.4 Approval and Lifecycle Audit

| Audit ID | Audit question | Expected evidence | Example finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| RA-LIF-01 | Is the approval made by an authorized person? | Approval record and authority matrix | Approval exceeds delegated authority |
| RA-LIF-02 | Are approval conditions explicit? | Decision record | Conditions are vague or unenforceable |
| RA-LIF-03 | Are preconditions verified before activation? | Test evidence and release authorization | System goes live before mandatory safeguards are implemented |
| RA-LIF-04 | Are review and expiration dates recorded? | Acceptance register | Acceptance has no defined review point |
| RA-LIF-05 | Are material changes reassessed? | Change records and reassessments | Model or integration changes without risk review |
| RA-LIF-06 | Are failed controls escalated? | Incident records, test results, and decisions | Failed safeguards do not trigger reassessment |
| RA-LIF-07 | Are revoked or expired acceptances enforced? | Access restrictions, status records, and operational evidence | System continues operating under an expired decision |

#### 13.5 Monitoring and Reporting Audit

| Audit ID | Audit question | Expected evidence | Example finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| RA-MON-01 | Is a monitoring owner assigned? | Ownership records | No role is accountable for ongoing monitoring |
| RA-MON-02 | Are monitoring requirements defined? | Monitoring plans and operational procedures | Acceptance conditions are not monitored |
| RA-MON-03 | Are exceptions and overdue reviews reported? | GRC dashboard and reports | Management cannot identify expired acceptances |
| RA-MON-04 | Are recurring risk patterns analyzed? | Trend analysis and remediation plans | Similar risks are repeatedly accepted without systemic action |
| RA-MON-05 | Are incidents linked to prior acceptance decisions? | Incident and risk records | The organization cannot determine whether accepted risks contributed to an incident |
| RA-MON-06 | Are records retained and protected? | Access logs, retention settings, and audit trail | Decision evidence can be changed without traceability |

#### 13.6 Audit Sampling Strategy

A risk-based sample may include:
- All critical or highest-severity acceptances within the audit scope.
- A representative sample of high-risk acceptances.
- Acceptances that have expired or passed their review dates.
- Acceptances with overdue remediation.
- Acceptances involving sensitive data or consequential AI actions.
- Acceptances involving third-party models or external services.
- Acceptances renewed multiple times.
- Acceptances associated with incidents or failed control tests.
- A sample of lower-risk decisions to assess consistency and completeness.

The auditor should define the population, sample selection method, testing period, and any limitations. Sampling does not provide evidence about records that were not examined unless the methodology supports that inference.

#### 13.7 Audit Conclusion Categories

The organization may classify results as:
- Effective.
- Effective with improvement opportunities.
- Partially effective.
- Ineffective.
- Unable to conclude because of insufficient evidence.

The exact terminology should follow the internal audit methodology.

**Core Principle:** An effective AI risk acceptance audit verifies both the integrity of the approval process and the continued validity of the risk decisions made through it.

### 14. AI Risk Acceptance Audit Evidence and Documentation Requirements

Auditability depends on the organization's ability to reconstruct the risk acceptance decision and demonstrate that its conditions were followed.

A record containing only a risk description and an approval signature is usually insufficient for material risks. The organization should retain the evidence necessary to understand the assessment, challenge the decision, validate the controls, and establish what happened after approval.

#### 14.1 Core Evidence Categories

| Risk evidence | Risk assessment, threat model, impact analysis | Explains the identified exposure |
| Control evidence | Configuration reviews, test results, monitoring records | Supports claims about control effectiveness |
| Treatment evidence | Alternatives analysis, remediation plan, restriction proposal | Demonstrates that reasonable options were evaluated |
| Approval evidence | Signed decision, workflow history, approval timestamps | Establishes who approved the risk and when |
| Condition evidence | Control implementation results, deployment gates, restriction records | Demonstrates that approval conditions were satisfied |

The evidence management process should address:
- Access control and authorization.
- Protection against unauthorized modification.
- Version history.
- Timestamps and decision chronology.
- Identification of evidence creators and reviewers.
- Retention and disposal requirements.
- Secure handling of sensitive information.
- Traceability between evidence and the relevant risk.
- Reproducibility of material testing where feasible.
- Preservation of relevant evidence during incidents or investigations.

Evidence retention must be proportionate and consistent with applicable privacy, confidentiality, contractual, and records-management obligations. Retaining more data indefinitely is not automatically better governance.

#### 14.4 Evidence Handling for AI Systems

AI assessments may involve prompts, model outputs, retrieved documents, tool calls, system logs, model configurations, and security test artifacts.

These records can themselves contain sensitive data, credentials, personal information, or proprietary content.

The organization should therefore define:
- Which artifacts must be retained.
- Which artifacts must be redacted or pseudonymized.
- Who can access the records.
- Whether full prompt and output retention is necessary.
- How provider-generated evidence is obtained and verified.
- How evidence is preserved when an incident is suspected.
- How long each evidence category must be retained.
- How deletion and legal holds are managed.

The evidence strategy should provide enough information to investigate material events without collecting or exposing unnecessary sensitive content.

7. Security testing results.

12. Proof that mandatory preconditions were satisfied.
13. Monitoring records.
14. Periodic review results.
15. Relevant change records.
16. Incident or exception records, if applicable.
17. Remediation and corrective action records.
18. Renewal, suspension, revocation, or closure evidence.

The requested evidence should be limited to what is relevant to the audit objective and authorized scope.

#### 14.6 Evidence Deficiency Assessment

When evidence is missing, the auditor should distinguish between:
- A control that was not implemented.
- A control that was implemented but not documented.
- A control that was documented but not tested.
- A test that was performed but is no longer current.
- Evidence that exists but cannot be accessed.
- Evidence that cannot reliably support the assertion being made.

These situations have different implications and should not be collapsed into a single conclusion.

A missing document does not always prove that a control failed, but it may prevent the organization from demonstrating that the control operated effectively.

**Core Principle:** AI risk acceptance must be supported by an evidence trail that is relevant, current, protected, traceable, and sufficient to reconstruct the decision and its subsequent management.

### 15. AI Risk Acceptance Findings, Remediation, and Corrective Action Management

Audit findings should lead to corrective action that addresses the underlying governance or control weakness. Merely updating documentation or obtaining retrospective approval may not resolve the exposure.

The organization should distinguish between a documentation deficiency, a process failure, a control design weakness, a control operating failure, and a decision that exceeded authorized risk boundaries.

#### 15.1 Common Findings

| Finding category | Example | Potential consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Governance deficiency | No approved risk acceptance procedure exists | Decisions vary across business units |
| Authority deficiency | A project manager approves a risk outside delegated authority | The decision may be invalid under internal governance |
| Assessment deficiency | Residual risk is recorded without evaluating control effectiveness | Exposure may be understated |
| Evidence deficiency | Approval exists but the supporting assessment cannot be located | Decision cannot be adequately reconstructed |
| Condition deficiency | Approval conditions are vague or not assigned to owners | Conditions may not be implemented |
| Monitoring deficiency | Accepted risks are not periodically reviewed | Material changes may go undetected |
| Lifecycle deficiency | Expired acceptances remain active | Systems may operate without current authorization |
| Change-management deficiency | Model updates do not trigger reassessment | Previous evidence may no longer support the decision |
| Reporting deficiency | Management cannot identify high-risk acceptances | Oversight and resource allocation are weakened |
| Systemic control deficiency | Similar risks are repeatedly accepted | Underlying architecture or governance problems persist |

#### 15.2 Finding Severity

Severity should be determined using the organization's approved audit methodology. Relevant considerations include:
- Actual and potential impact.
- Likelihood of recurrence.
- Number and criticality of affected systems.
- Exposure of sensitive information.
- Duration of the weakness.
- Whether the issue affects a mandatory requirement.
- Whether compensating controls exist and are effective.
- Whether management knew about the weakness.
- Whether the issue has caused incidents or repeated findings.

A missing approval record for a low-risk internal tool may have a different significance from an unauthorized acceptance of a critical control failure affecting a high-impact AI system.

#### 15.3 Corrective Action Plan Template

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | Unique identifier |
| Related risk acceptance ID | Affected acceptance record |
| Audit observation | What was identified |
| Criteria | Requirement or control expected |
| Root cause | Why the issue occurred |
| Risk and impact | Potential consequences |
| Immediate containment | Short-term action to reduce exposure |
| Corrective action | Action addressing the identified deficiency |
| Preventive action | Action intended to reduce recurrence |
| Action owner | Accountable person |
| Supporting teams | Teams responsible for implementation |
| Target date | Planned completion |
| Interim safeguards | Controls maintained until completion |
| Success criteria | Measurable conditions for closure |
| Validation method | How completion and effectiveness will be tested |
| Evidence reference | Supporting implementation and validation records |
| Closure approver | Authorized person confirming closure |
| Status | Current action state |

#### 15.4 Root Cause Analysis

A useful root cause analysis should go beyond describing the immediate failure.

For example:

**Observation:** Multiple AI risk acceptances have expired without review.

**Weak root cause:** The risk owners forgot to review them.

**Stronger investigation:**
- The GRC platform does not generate reliable reminders.
- No role is accountable for reviewing the overdue-acceptance report.
- Business units have inconsistent review procedures.
- Expiration does not trigger an operational restriction or escalation.
- Management reporting does not identify overdue high-risk decisions.

The corrective actions may therefore include workflow changes, clear ownership, automated reminders, escalation rules, and enforcement of expiration controls.

The investigation should verify which causes are supported by evidence rather than assuming that a plausible explanation is necessarily the root cause.

#### 15.5 Corrective Action Validation

Before closing a finding, verify that:
- The action has been implemented.
- The relevant control or process operates as intended.
- The original failure scenario has been addressed.
- Interim safeguards remain effective until permanent remediation is validated.
- Similar systems or business units have been assessed for the same weakness.
- The relevant policy, workflow, or technical configuration has been updated.
- Required owners have accepted their responsibilities.
- Supporting evidence is retained.
- Residual risk has been reassessed.
- The finding's closure is approved by the appropriate authority.

#### 15.6 Escalation of Overdue Actions

Overdue corrective actions should be escalated according to their risk and impact.

| Condition | Potential response |
|---|---|
| Low-impact action slightly overdue | Notify the action owner and update the target date through the approved process |
| Moderate action overdue | Escalate to the responsible manager and reassess interim safeguards |
| High-risk control weakness remains unresolved | Notify the risk owner and relevant security or GRC leadership |
| Critical control failure or unacceptable exposure | Consider immediate restriction, suspension, or other approved containment measures |
| Repeated missed deadlines | Escalate the systemic issue to senior management or the relevant governance committee |

An extension should not be treated as an administrative update only. Where the extension changes the period of exposure, the organization should reassess the risk and obtain the required approval.

#### 15.7 Corrective Action Closure Checklist

- [ ] The finding has a documented root cause.
- [ ] Immediate containment has been evaluated.
- [ ] Corrective and preventive actions have named owners.
- [ ] Target dates and interim safeguards are recorded.
- [ ] Completion criteria are measurable.
- [ ] Implementation evidence is available.
- [ ] Control effectiveness has been validated.
- [ ] Similar systems have been assessed where appropriate.
- [ ] Residual risk has been reassessed.
- [ ] Risk acceptance records have been updated.
- [ ] Required escalation and approval have occurred.
- [ ] Closure evidence is retained.

**Core Principle:** Corrective action is complete only when the underlying weakness has been addressed, effectiveness has been validated, and the organization has evaluated whether the same deficiency exists elsewhere.

### Part 2 Core Principle

**AI risk acceptance must be governed throughout its lifecycle, supported by validated controls and traceable evidence, and subject to independent audit and effective corrective action.**

A formal approval is only one component of defensible risk governance. The organization must also prove that the decision was authorized, its conditions were implemented, the supporting controls remain effective, and any material change or control failure triggers appropriate reassessment.

## Part 3 — AI Audit Execution, Compliance Testing, and Governance Oversight

### 16. AI Risk Acceptance Audit Planning and Scope Definition

An AI risk acceptance audit evaluates whether an organization identifies, assesses, approves, documents, monitors, and periodically reassesses risks associated with AI systems in accordance with its governance framework.

The objective is not simply to verify that risk acceptance forms have been completed. Auditors must establish whether acceptance decisions are authorized, supported by defensible evidence, consistent with risk appetite, and enforced throughout the period in which the accepted risk remains active.

An effective audit examines both the decision-making process and the operational controls that make the decision enforceable.

#### 16.1 Audit Objectives

The audit should establish whether:

1. Every applicable AI risk acceptance is recorded in an authoritative register.
2. The accepted risk has been assessed using an approved methodology.
3. The residual risk is within the organization's authorized acceptance limits.
4. The decision was made by an appropriately authorized risk owner or approval authority.
5. Required compensating controls are implemented and operating effectively.
6. Acceptance conditions, expiration dates, and review requirements are enforced.
7. Material changes and security incidents trigger reassessment when required.
8. Risk acceptance decisions are traceable to the relevant AI system, use case, risk assessment, and control evidence.
9. Expired, revoked, or rejected acceptances cannot be used to justify continued unauthorized operation.
10. Management receives accurate reporting on outstanding, overdue, and high-risk acceptances.

#### 16.2 Audit Scope

The scope should be based on the organization's AI risk exposure, governance obligations, and the maturity of its risk acceptance process.

| Scope dimension | Audit considerations | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| AI system coverage | Which AI systems and use cases are subject to risk acceptance? | AI inventory, system classification records |
| Risk coverage | Which inherent and residual risks have been accepted? | AI risk register, risk assessment reports |
| Governance | Who can approve acceptance, and under what conditions? | Risk policies, delegation matrix |
| Technical controls | Are the required security controls implemented? | Configuration records, test results |
| Data protection | Have privacy and data protection risks been evaluated? | DPIAs where required, data-flow diagrams |
| Third-party dependencies | Are supplier and model-provider risks included? | Vendor assessments, contractual commitments |
| Lifecycle management | Are acceptances reviewed after material changes? | Change records, review logs |
| Compliance | Are legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements satisfied? | Compliance assessments, control mappings |
| Monitoring | Are acceptance conditions monitored over time? | Dashboards, alerts, monitoring reports |
| Closure | Are resolved or expired acceptances formally closed? | Closure approvals, remediation evidence |

#### 16.3 Risk-Based Audit Prioritization

Not all accepted risks require the same level of audit scrutiny. The audit team should prioritize cases with potentially severe consequences, weak governance, significant exposure, or a history of control failure.

| Priority | Characteristics | Recommended audit treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Potential severe harm, major regulatory exposure, highly privileged AI agents, or material security weaknesses | Immediate or specially scoped review; verify whether operation must be suspended |
| High | Sensitive data, externally accessible AI, significant third-party dependencies, or major residual risk | Detailed control testing and evidence validation |
| Medium | Moderate business impact, established controls, and manageable residual risk | Targeted testing and sample-based evidence review |
| Low | Limited impact, constrained exposure, and demonstrably effective controls | Periodic review using proportionate procedures |

Priority classifications must follow the organization's approved risk methodology. Auditors should not independently redefine risk appetite simply to make an acceptance appear reasonable.

#### 16.4 Audit Planning Deliverables

Before fieldwork begins, the audit team should prepare:

- Audit objectives and scope statement.
- Applicable policies, standards, and control criteria.
- AI system and risk acceptance populations.
- Risk-based sampling strategy.
- Stakeholder interview plan.
- Evidence request list.
- Control testing procedures.
- Audit timetable and reporting arrangements.
- Escalation criteria for potentially unacceptable risk.

The audit plan should identify any independence limitations, incomplete records, system-access restrictions, and dependencies on specialist reviewers.

### 17. Audit Criteria and Control Mapping

Audit criteria define the requirements against which evidence is evaluated. Without explicit criteria, an audit may produce inconsistent conclusions or rely on an auditor's personal expectations rather than established organizational requirements.

Criteria should be derived from applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, approved policies, governance standards, and documented control requirements.

#### 17.1 Sources of Audit Criteria

| Criteria source | What the auditor evaluates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| AI governance policy | Required governance approvals and accountability | A high-risk acceptance requires designated executive approval |
| AI risk management policy | Risk assessment and treatment requirements | Residual risk must be calculated using the approved methodology |
| Information security policy | Minimum security requirements | Encryption and access control requirements remain mandatory |
| Privacy requirements | Applicable personal-data obligations | Required privacy assessment and lawful processing arrangements |
| Supplier governance | Third-party assurance and accountability | Material model-provider risks are assessed before approval |
| Change management | Reassessment requirements | Material model or data-flow changes trigger a risk review |
| Internal audit requirements | Evidence retention and auditability | Approval and control-testing records are retained |
| Regulatory obligations | Applicable mandatory requirements | The organization demonstrates compliance with relevant legal duties |
| Contractual commitments | Customer, partner, and supplier obligations | Agreed security commitments are not contradicted by an exception |
| Risk appetite statement | Authorized exposure limits | Risks exceeding delegated thresholds require escalation |

An auditor must distinguish between mandatory obligations and internal controls that may be subject to an approved exception. A management risk acceptance cannot automatically waive a legal requirement, regulatory obligation, contractual commitment, or other non-waivable duty.

#### 17.2 Mapping Risk Acceptance to Control Requirements

Each acceptance should be traceable to the controls that prevent, detect, or reduce the associated risk.

For example, if an organization accepts a residual risk involving an AI assistant that can retrieve confidential documents, the auditor should examine the controls supporting that decision.

| Risk scenario | Relevant control objective | Example audit test |
|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized retrieval of confidential documents | Enforce least-privilege access | Verify retrieval permissions and test unauthorized access scenarios |
| Prompt injection causes unsafe tool use | Restrict and validate tool execution | Review tool permissions, input handling, and execution logs |
| Sensitive data appears in model output | Reduce unauthorized disclosure | Inspect output filtering, data-loss prevention, and testing evidence |
| Supplier changes model behavior | Manage third-party and model-change risk | Review supplier notifications and reassessment records |
| Monitoring fails to detect misuse | Detect security-relevant events | Verify logging coverage, alert configuration, and incident escalation |
| Acceptance expires without reassessment | Enforce time-bound authorization | Inspect expiration controls and test overdue-acceptance reporting |

The auditor should not conclude that a control is effective merely because a policy describes it. Operating effectiveness requires evidence that the control was implemented and performed as intended during the relevant period.

### 18. Audit Testing Procedures for AI Risk Acceptance

Audit testing determines whether documented governance requirements operate effectively in practice. Testing should address the completeness of records, authorization, risk evaluation, control implementation, ongoing monitoring, and lifecycle enforcement.

#### 18.1 Population Completeness Testing

The auditor should establish whether the population of accepted AI risks is complete and reliable.

Potential procedures include:

1. Obtain the authoritative AI risk acceptance register.
2. Reconcile acceptance records against the AI risk register.
3. Compare high-risk findings from AI security assessments with acceptance records.
4. Review security exceptions and identify those that effectively permit AI risk acceptance.
5. Examine incident records for unresolved risks that may have been accepted informally.
6. Compare deployment approvals with documented risk decisions.
7. Investigate duplicate, missing, inconsistent, or unreferenced records.

The objective is to detect undocumented acceptance decisions, incomplete records, and cases where an AI system operates under an assumed approval that cannot be demonstrated.

#### 18.2 Approval Authorization Testing

For each selected acceptance, verify:

- The approver held the required authority on the approval date.
- The approval complied with the delegation-of-authority matrix.
- The approver was not approving a decision outside their delegated risk threshold.
- Required stakeholders completed their reviews.
- Conflicts of interest were identified and managed where relevant.
- Approval occurred before the activity requiring authorization, unless an approved emergency procedure applied.
- The decision and its rationale are recorded.
- Any required conditions were communicated to the operational owner.

**Important:** An approval recorded in a workflow system is not sufficient by itself. The auditor must validate the approver's authority and the decision's substantive compliance with the applicable rules.

#### 18.3 Residual Risk Assessment Testing

The auditor should reconstruct or independently verify selected risk assessments.

| Test | Procedure | Expected result |
|---|---|---|
| Inherent risk | Review the scenario, threat, vulnerability, likelihood, and impact | The risk assessment is complete and consistent with the approved methodology |
| Control effectiveness | Inspect the evidence supporting relevant controls | Control effectiveness ratings are evidence-based |
| Residual risk | Recalculate the residual risk where possible | The result agrees with the documented methodology |
| Risk appetite | Compare residual risk with authorized thresholds | Acceptance is within the approver's authority and organizational limits |
| Assumptions | Inspect dependencies and assumptions | Material assumptions are explicit, justified, and monitored |
| Uncertainty | Review material limitations in evidence or model behavior | Uncertainty is documented and reflected in the decision |
| Impact | Examine relevant business, security, privacy, and safety consequences | Material impact dimensions have not been omitted |

Where risk scoring is qualitative, the auditor should verify consistent application of the approved scoring criteria rather than impose an unrelated numerical model.

#### 18.4 Compensating Control Testing

If acceptance depends on compensating controls, the auditor must establish that those controls are implemented, effective, and sustainable.

For example, a business may temporarily accept a residual risk because a full technical remediation cannot be deployed immediately. The approval may require enhanced monitoring, restricted access, manual review, or a deployment limitation.

The audit should verify:

- Each compensating control has an accountable owner.
- The control is technically or procedurally implemented.
- Evidence demonstrates that the control operates as intended.
- Required monitoring or review frequency is met.
- Control failures are escalated.
- The acceptance is reconsidered if a compensating control becomes ineffective.
- The arrangement has a defined review or expiration date where appropriate.

A compensating control should not be treated as effective solely because its owner confirms that it exists. Where feasible, the auditor should inspect configurations, observe procedures, examine logs, or independently test the control.

#### 18.5 Acceptance Lifecycle Testing

Test whether the acceptance remains valid throughout its lifecycle.

| Lifecycle event | Audit test |
|---|---|
| Initial approval | Confirm required reviews and approvals were completed |
| Implementation | Verify acceptance conditions were implemented before the relevant activity |
| Periodic review | Confirm reviews occurred at the required frequency |
| Material change | Verify reassessment was triggered when required |
| Security incident | Determine whether the incident required reconsideration of accepted risk |
| Expiration | Verify the acceptance expired or was formally renewed |
| Revocation | Confirm revoked acceptance no longer authorizes the activity |
| Remediation | Verify the risk was reassessed after remediation |
| Closure | Confirm closure was supported by evidence and authorized appropriately |

The auditor should distinguish between a documented review and a meaningful reassessment. A review that merely changes the review date without evaluating whether the risk remains acceptable may not satisfy the underlying control objective.

### 19. Risk-Based Sampling and Population Completeness

Sampling enables auditors to test a manageable number of risk acceptance records while obtaining evidence about the operation of governance controls. Sampling does not remove the need to establish that the underlying population is sufficiently complete and accurate.

#### 19.1 Build the Audit Population

The population should include all records relevant to the audit objective, which may include:

- Active AI risk acceptances.
- Expired or revoked acceptances.
- Emergency approvals.
- Accepted security assessment findings.
- AI-related security exceptions.
- Temporary compensating-control arrangements.
- High-risk AI deployment approvals.
- Risks associated with third-party AI services.
- Material AI changes that required reassessment.

The exact population depends on the scope. If several registers or workflow systems are used, the auditor should reconcile them and document how duplicates and missing references are handled.

#### 19.2 Sample Selection Strategy

A combined approach is often appropriate.

| Sampling method | Application | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Risk-based selection | Select records with the greatest potential impact | Does not independently support statistical conclusions |
| Judgmental sampling | Select unusual, complex, or previously problematic cases | Depends on documented professional judgment |
| Random sampling | Select records without deliberate preference | May miss rare but high-impact cases |
| Stratified sampling | Divide the population into risk or system categories | Requires reliable classification |
| Targeted exception sampling | Examine expired, overdue, emergency, or high-risk acceptances | Focuses on known risk indicators rather than the whole population |
| Full-population analytics | Analyze all records using data queries or workflow reports | Depends on data quality, query completeness, and system access |

A robust audit may combine full-population analytics with detailed testing of a risk-based sample.

#### 19.3 Practical Sampling Framework

The following is an illustrative planning framework, not a universal statistical sampling standard.

| Population characteristics | Illustrative approach |
|---|---|
| Small population with high impact | Consider testing all records |
| Large population with critical-risk acceptances | Test all critical cases where practicable, plus targeted high-risk cases |
| Large population with mixed risk levels | Stratify by severity, system, business unit, and approval type |
| Population containing expired or overdue records | Target these records and investigate whether they indicate a systemic control failure |
| New or substantially changed process | Increase detailed testing of implementation and authorization |
| Repeated historical failures | Expand testing to determine the extent and cause of the weakness |
| Reliable structured data | Use population analytics to identify anomalies before selecting samples |

Sample size should reflect audit objectives, control frequency, population size, risk, expected deviation rate, and the level of assurance required. Statistical sampling, where used, should follow the organization's approved audit methodology.

#### 19.4 Example: Sampling an AI Risk Acceptance Register

Assume an organization has 120 recorded AI risk acceptances:

- 8 classified as critical.
- 27 classified as high.
- 55 classified as medium.
- 30 classified as low.

An illustrative audit strategy could be:

1. Test all 8 critical-risk acceptances.
2. Select a risk-based sample from the 27 high-risk acceptances.
3. Select samples from the medium- and low-risk populations.
4. Include expired, renewed, emergency, and recently changed records.
5. Perform full-population analytics for missing owners, absent expiration dates, overdue reviews, and invalid status combinations.
6. Expand testing if exceptions suggest a systemic weakness.

The actual sample sizes should be determined by the audit methodology and evidence needs. The example is not a statistical assurance calculation.

#### 19.5 Sampling Exceptions and Audit Conclusions

A single exception may have different implications depending on its nature.

For example:

- A missing administrative field may indicate a documentation weakness.
- An approval by an unauthorized person may invalidate the governance decision.
- An expired acceptance supporting continued production use may indicate a potentially serious control failure.
- A missing compensating control may invalidate a key assumption underlying the accepted residual risk.

The auditor should evaluate the severity, cause, recurrence, and potential population-wide impact of each exception. Where appropriate, the auditor should expand the sample, perform additional testing, or recommend a full-population review.

### 20. Testing Approval Authority and Segregation of Duties

Risk acceptance creates a governance decision with potentially significant business and compliance consequences. Approval authority must therefore be explicit, proportionate to risk, and protected against conflicts of interest.

#### 20.1 Authority Verification

The audit should verify that the organization has documented:

- Which roles may accept specific levels of residual risk.
- Whether authority varies by AI risk classification.
- Whether certain risk categories require additional approval.
- How delegated authority is assigned and withdrawn.
- Whether emergency approvals follow a controlled procedure.
- How approval conflicts are identified.
- Whether approval evidence is retained.

The authority model should distinguish between the person who owns the business risk, the person who assesses the risk, the person who implements controls, and the person authorized to approve acceptance.

#### 20.2 Illustrative Approval Matrix

| Risk category | Business risk owner | Security or risk function | Additional approval |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Designated business owner | Review according to policy | As specified by delegation rules |
| Medium | Business owner with appropriate authority | Documented risk review | Additional approval if required by policy |
| High | Senior business risk owner | Formal security and risk review | Designated senior executive or committee, as required |
| Critical | Appropriate executive risk owner | Formal multidisciplinary assessment | Executive or risk committee decision, where permitted |

This matrix is illustrative. Each organization must define its own approval structure, thresholds, and non-delegable responsibilities. No approval level should be interpreted as permission to disregard mandatory legal or regulatory obligations.

#### 20.3 Segregation-of-Duties Testing

The auditor should determine whether the same person can create a risk assessment, approve acceptance, implement the relevant controls, and certify that those controls are effective without independent challenge.

Potential audit procedures include:

1. Compare the risk owner's identity with the approver's identity.
2. Compare the approver with the control implementer and tester.
3. Inspect workflow permissions and role assignments.
4. Examine evidence of independent review for higher-risk decisions.
5. Investigate cases in which the same individual performed multiple incompatible roles.
6. Verify that any permitted exception to segregation of duties has a documented justification and compensating review.

Small organizations may have limited staffing and may not be able to segregate every activity. In such cases, independent oversight, second-person review, or another documented compensating measure may be necessary.

#### 20.4 Governance Red Flags

The following conditions warrant investigation:

- The business sponsor approves their own acceptance despite a policy requiring independent authorization.
- A system owner approves risks beyond their delegated threshold.
- A risk assessment is completed after the production deployment without an authorized emergency justification.
- An approver has no evidence of reviewing the underlying assessment.
- Acceptance records use generic justifications such as "business requirement" without a documented analysis.
- The person responsible for remediation also independently certifies remediation without required verification.
- The same acceptance is repeatedly renewed without evidence of reassessment.
- A risk committee receives only aggregate metrics and cannot obtain underlying decision records.

These conditions do not automatically prove misconduct or control failure. They are indicators that should be investigated against the applicable criteria and available evidence.

**Core Principle**

AI risk acceptance audits must verify the integrity of the complete decision lifecycle: population completeness, appropriate criteria, valid authorization, defensible residual-risk assessment, effective compensating controls, and enforceable lifecycle conditions.

An acceptance record is not sufficient evidence of effective governance. Auditors must establish that the decision was valid when made and remained valid as the AI system, its operating environment, and its risk exposure evolved.
