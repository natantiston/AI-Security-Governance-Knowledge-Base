# 20.12 — AI Governance RACI and Governance Charter

## Part 1 — Governance Foundations, Organizational Structure, Roles, and Accountability

### 1. Introduction to AI Governance RACI and Governance Charter

AI governance requires a clearly defined organizational structure that establishes who makes decisions, who implements controls, who evaluates risks, and who remains accountable for the outcomes of AI systems.

Organizations frequently adopt AI policies, risk assessment procedures, security controls, and compliance requirements without adequately defining the responsibilities of the people expected to implement them. This creates accountability gaps, duplicated activities, conflicting decisions, and situations in which critical risks remain unresolved because no individual or function has clear decision-making authority.

Two complementary governance instruments address these problems:

**AI Governance Charter:** A formal document that establishes the mandate, authority, objectives, organizational structure, decision rights, responsibilities, and operating principles of the organization's AI governance function.

**AI Governance RACI Matrix:** A responsibility assignment tool that identifies who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed for specific AI governance activities, decisions, and deliverables.

These instruments serve different purposes but must operate together.

The charter establishes the governance framework. The RACI matrix translates that framework into operational responsibilities.

An effective AI governance model must answer the following questions:

- Who authorizes the development, procurement, and deployment of an AI system?
- Who determines the AI system's risk classification?
- Who owns AI-related business risks?
- Who defines AI security and privacy requirements?
- Who independently challenges risk assessments and control effectiveness?
- Who approves exceptions to established governance requirements?
- Who monitors AI systems after deployment?
- Who can suspend or restrict an AI system when unacceptable risks emerge?
- Who is responsible for regulatory compliance and audit evidence?
- Who resolves disagreements between business, technical, security, and compliance stakeholders?

Without explicit answers, governance becomes dependent on informal relationships rather than enforceable organizational responsibilities.

The purpose of this lesson is to establish a practical governance structure that can be implemented within an enterprise, incorporated into an AI management system, and adapted to different organizational sizes and risk profiles.

### 2. Objectives of the AI Governance Charter

The AI Governance Charter defines the organization's formal approach to governing AI activities across their lifecycle.

It establishes the governance mandate and creates a common understanding of the authority and responsibilities of the functions involved.

A well-designed charter should achieve the following objectives.

| Objective | Description | Expected Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Establish governance authority | Define the organizational mandate for AI governance | Formal recognition of governance responsibilities |
| Define decision rights | Identify who can approve, restrict, or reject AI activities | Consistent and authorized decisions |
| Establish accountability | Assign ownership for AI risks, controls, and outcomes | Clear responsibility for unresolved issues |
| Define governance scope | Identify the systems, services, business units, and activities covered | Consistent application of governance requirements |
| Integrate risk management | Connect AI governance with enterprise risk management | AI risks incorporated into organizational risk decisions |
| Integrate security and privacy | Establish relationships with cybersecurity, privacy, and data governance | Coordinated risk assessment and control implementation |
| Support regulatory compliance | Assign responsibility for identifying and managing applicable obligations | Documented compliance ownership |
| Establish escalation procedures | Define how unresolved risks and governance disputes are escalated | Timely resolution of material issues |
| Enable lifecycle oversight | Establish governance checkpoints from initiation through retirement | Continued oversight of AI systems |
| Support assurance | Define reporting, review, monitoring, and audit responsibilities | Evidence of governance effectiveness |
| Enable continuous improvement | Establish periodic charter review and governance maturity assessment | Governance evolves with technology and organizational risk |

The charter should not be treated as a general statement of organizational commitment.

It must establish practical authority, identify accountable roles, and define how governance decisions are made and enforced.

For example, stating that the organization is committed to responsible AI does not explain who can stop a deployment when a critical security weakness remains unresolved.

The charter should identify the authority responsible for that decision, the conditions that trigger escalation, and the process for recording the outcome.

### 3. Objectives of the AI Governance RACI Matrix

The RACI matrix translates governance responsibilities into an operational assignment model.

RACI is an acronym for four responsibility categories.

| RACI Category | Meaning | Primary Function |
|---|---|---|
| R — Responsible | The role that performs the work or produces the deliverable | Execution |
| A — Accountable | The role ultimately answerable for the activity or decision | Ownership and decision accountability |
| C — Consulted | Roles whose expertise or input is required before a decision or action is finalized | Consultation |
| I — Informed | Roles that must receive information about the activity, decision, or outcome | Communication |

The distinction between Responsible and Accountable is particularly important.

A security engineer may be Responsible for conducting an AI security assessment, while the designated security authority is Accountable for the formal security decision within its delegated mandate.

Similarly, a technical team may implement a remediation action, while the AI system owner remains Accountable for ensuring that the system's outstanding risks are managed.

The RACI matrix should prevent the following situations:

- Multiple functions assuming that another team owns a critical decision.
- Several individuals believing that they are independently accountable for the same decision.
- Risk assessments being completed without a designated decision-maker.
- Technical teams accepting business risks without appropriate authority.
- Business owners making security decisions without required specialist input.
- Governance committees receiving information without having a clearly defined decision mandate.
- Auditors discovering that no one is accountable for maintaining required evidence.

A RACI matrix should therefore be designed around actual governance activities and decisions, rather than merely listing departments and their general responsibilities.

### 4. Relationship Between the AI Governance Charter and RACI Matrix

The charter and RACI matrix are complementary governance artifacts.

The charter establishes the governance model, while the RACI matrix assigns responsibility for implementing that model.

| Governance Dimension | AI Governance Charter | AI Governance RACI Matrix |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Establishes governance mandate and authority | Assigns responsibilities for specific activities |
| Scope | Defines organizational and operational coverage | Identifies which roles participate in each activity |
| Decision rights | Defines delegated decision-making authority | Identifies the Accountable role for each activity |
| Committees | Establishes committee purpose and authority | Assigns committee participation and responsibilities |
| Risk management | Establishes principles for risk ownership and escalation | Assigns risk assessment, treatment, approval, and monitoring activities |
| Security | Establishes security governance expectations | Assigns responsibility for security assessment and control implementation |
| Privacy | Establishes privacy governance relationships | Assigns privacy review and related decision responsibilities |
| Compliance | Establishes compliance oversight requirements | Assigns control mapping, compliance review, and evidence responsibilities |
| Reporting | Defines governance reporting obligations | Assigns responsibility for producing and reviewing reports |
| Exceptions | Defines exception approval principles | Assigns responsibility for requesting, reviewing, approving, and tracking exceptions |
| Review | Defines charter review and amendment requirements | Defines responsibility for maintaining activity-level assignments |

Consider an organization that has formally established an AI Governance Committee.

The charter may specify that the committee reviews high-risk AI proposals and resolves escalated governance issues.

The RACI matrix then identifies the committee's role in specific activities, such as approving governance standards, reviewing material risk exceptions, or recommending restrictions on an AI system.

However, the committee's presence in a RACI matrix does not automatically grant it legal, regulatory, financial, or operational authority. Its actual powers must be established by the charter and any relevant organizational delegations.

**Governance rule:** The RACI matrix must reflect the authority established by the charter; it must not create decision-making powers that the organization has not formally delegated.

### 5. Scope of the AI Governance Charter

The charter should explicitly define the activities, systems, and organizational entities covered by AI governance.

A vague scope creates opportunities for AI systems to operate outside established oversight.

The scope should consider the following dimensions.

| Scope Dimension | Questions to Address |
|---|---|
| AI technologies | Which AI technologies and capabilities are covered? |
| Business functions | Which departments and business units must comply? |
| Lifecycle stages | Does governance cover ideation, procurement, development, testing, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement? |
| Deployment models | Are internally hosted, externally hosted, embedded, and hybrid AI systems included? |
| External providers | Does governance cover third-party AI services, models, and AI-enabled products? |
| Data processing | Which data categories and processing activities are covered? |
| Users | Are employees, contractors, business partners, and external users included where relevant? |
| Autonomous capabilities | Are AI agents and systems capable of initiating actions included? |
| Regulatory exposure | Which legal and regulatory jurisdictions may affect governance obligations? |
| Business criticality | Are additional requirements established for systems supporting critical operations? |
| Geographic coverage | Which countries, regions, subsidiaries, and legal entities fall within the governance model? |
| Exceptions | Who can authorize exclusions, and under what conditions? |

#### 5.1 Example of an enterprise AI governance scope

An international enterprise may establish a charter covering:

- AI systems developed internally.
- Generative AI applications used by employees.
- AI capabilities embedded in enterprise software.
- AI services procured from external providers.
- AI systems processing confidential, personal, or regulated information.
- AI agents capable of accessing enterprise applications or executing business actions.
- AI-supported decisions affecting customers, employees, suppliers, or other stakeholders.
- AI systems used in cybersecurity, fraud detection, customer service, marketing, and business operations.
- AI models and supporting components maintained by internal engineering teams.
- AI systems deployed in public cloud, private cloud, on-premises infrastructure, or hybrid environments.

The organization may establish different governance requirements according to system risk, but it should not assume that a third-party AI service is outside its governance responsibilities simply because the underlying model is hosted by a supplier.

The charter should also explain how the organization handles AI capabilities that are embedded within products or services purchased by individual business units.

#### 5.2 Scope exclusions

Exclusions should be narrow, documented, and authorized.

For example, an organization may distinguish personal experimentation using an approved low-risk tool from the deployment of an AI system that processes confidential corporate data or performs customer-facing business functions.

However, an exclusion should not automatically exempt an activity from applicable information security, privacy, acceptable-use, or legal requirements.

Where a system is excluded from the full governance process, the organization should identify which alternative controls remain applicable and who is accountable for enforcing them.

### 6. Core Principles of AI Governance Accountability

The governance charter should establish principles that guide responsibility assignment and decision-making across the organization.

#### 6.1 Clear ownership

Every in-scope AI system must have an identifiable owner or accountable organizational function.

Ownership must include responsibility for ensuring that the system is governed, assessed, monitored, and managed throughout its lifecycle.

An AI system without an accountable owner presents a governance failure because no one can reliably authorize its use, approve its risk treatment, or ensure that its obligations remain fulfilled.

#### 6.2 Separation of duties

Where appropriate, the organization should separate control implementation, independent assessment, and risk acceptance.

For example, a development team may implement an AI security control, while a security assurance function evaluates whether the control meets the required standard.

The business risk owner may then make a risk decision within delegated authority.

This separation reduces conflicts of interest and prevents the team responsible for delivering a system from being the sole authority determining whether its risks are acceptable.

The degree of separation should be proportionate to the organization's size, the system's risk, and the consequences of control failure.

#### 6.3 Proportionate governance

Governance requirements should reflect the system's risk profile, intended purpose, capabilities, data, deployment context, and potential impact.

A low-risk internal productivity tool should not necessarily require the same approval process as an AI agent capable of modifying financial records or a high-impact system used in a regulated business process.

However, proportionate governance must not be interpreted as permission to disregard mandatory legal, contractual, or security requirements.

#### 6.4 Independent challenge

Material AI risks should receive appropriate challenge from qualified personnel who are not solely responsible for delivering the system.

Independent challenge may be provided by cybersecurity, enterprise risk, privacy, compliance, legal, model validation, internal audit, or other specialist functions, depending on the nature of the risk.

The purpose is to test assumptions, validate evidence, challenge unsupported conclusions, and identify risks that may be overlooked by delivery teams.

#### 6.5 Traceable decisions

Material governance decisions must be documented with sufficient detail to reconstruct what was decided, by whom, on what evidence, and under which authority.

This includes approvals, risk acceptances, exceptions, deployment restrictions, and decisions to discontinue a system.

#### 6.6 Escalation of unacceptable risk

The organization must define what happens when a system presents risks that exceed its approved thresholds.

Escalation should identify the appropriate decision-maker and establish possible outcomes, including remediation, additional safeguards, restricted deployment, delayed launch, suspension, or rejection.

#### 6.7 Lifecycle accountability

Accountability does not end when an AI system is approved for production.

System owners and designated control owners must continue to manage changes, incidents, vulnerabilities, performance deterioration, supplier changes, and other conditions that may affect the original risk decision.

#### 6.8 No delegation without authority

A role may be assigned responsibility for performing an activity without receiving authority to make the associated risk decision.

For example, an AI Security Lead may be responsible for coordinating risk assessments but may not have authority to accept a material business risk.

The charter must distinguish the authority to perform work from the authority to approve its outcome.

### 7. Organizational Model for AI Governance

An enterprise AI governance model should establish how strategic oversight, business ownership, operational implementation, specialist assurance, and independent review interact.

The precise structure depends on the organization's size, industry, regulatory exposure, and existing governance arrangements.

A practical enterprise model may include the following levels.

| Governance Level | Primary Participants | Principal Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| Board or Board-Delegated Committee | Board members or formally delegated directors | Strategic oversight, material risk oversight, and governance accountability within delegated responsibilities |
| Executive Leadership | CEO, COO, CIO, CTO, CISO, and other relevant executives | Enterprise priorities, resources, and executive-level risk decisions |
| AI Governance Committee | Representatives from business, technology, security, privacy, risk, legal, and compliance | Cross-functional governance decisions and escalated matters within delegated authority |
| AI Governance Office or Function | AI governance lead, program manager, governance specialists | Governance framework, standards, coordination, reporting, and process administration |
| Enterprise Risk and Compliance | Risk managers, compliance officers, control assurance specialists | Risk integration, compliance oversight, control mapping, and assurance coordination |
| Cybersecurity and AI Security | CISO organization, security architects, AI security specialists | Security requirements, technical assessments, security monitoring, and security risk recommendations |
| Privacy and Data Governance | Privacy officers, data protection specialists, data owners | Personal-data protection, data governance, and applicable privacy obligations |
| Business and Product Ownership | Business owners, product owners, process owners | Business justification, intended purpose, operational ownership, and business risk management |
| AI Engineering and Technology | AI engineers, platform engineers, application owners, IT operations | Secure implementation, testing, deployment, operation, and remediation |
| Procurement and Supplier Management | Procurement, vendor risk, contract management | Supplier due diligence, contractual controls, and supplier lifecycle oversight |
| Internal Audit | Independent assurance personnel | Independent evaluation of governance design and operating effectiveness |

This structure should be integrated with existing organizational committees and management systems wherever possible.

Creating a separate AI governance committee does not automatically improve governance. The committee must have a clear mandate, defined membership, appropriate expertise, access to decision-makers, and a documented escalation route.

The organization should also avoid confusing the AI Governance Office with the business risk owner. The governance function may coordinate assessments and maintain standards, but the business function responsible for the use case generally remains accountable for the business consequences of using the AI system, subject to the organization's formal risk ownership model.

#### 8.1 Governance information flow

The reporting model should support two-way information flow.

**Upward reporting includes:**

- AI risk exposure and material risk trends.
- High-risk system approvals and exceptions.
- Significant security findings and incidents.
- Overdue remediation and unresolved governance disputes.
- Regulatory and contractual compliance concerns.
- Resource constraints and control deficiencies.
- Changes in the AI system portfolio.

**Downward communication includes:**

- Approved AI governance policies and standards.
- Risk appetite and escalation thresholds.
- Mandatory assessment requirements.
- Approval conditions and restrictions.
- Remediation priorities.
- Changes in regulatory or internal requirements.
- Decisions affecting business units and AI system owners.

A governance structure is effective only when decisions reach the people responsible for implementing them and material issues reach the authorities responsible for resolving them.

### 9. AI Governance Committee: Mandate and Responsibilities

The AI Governance Committee provides a coordinated mechanism for addressing cross-functional AI governance issues.

Its mandate should define the decisions it can make, the matters it reviews, the matters it escalates, and the responsibilities that remain with individual executives or business owners.

#### 9.1 Core responsibilities

| Responsibility | Description |
|---|---|
| Governance framework oversight | Review and recommend or approve governance standards within delegated authority |
| AI portfolio oversight | Review the organization's AI inventory, risk classifications, and material portfolio exposures |
| Risk escalation | Review material risks referred by system owners or specialist functions |
| Governance approval | Make decisions within its formally delegated authority |
| Exception oversight | Review or approve material exceptions where authorized |
| Regulatory coordination | Review significant changes in applicable AI obligations |
| Security and privacy oversight | Ensure relevant specialist risks are considered in material decisions |
| Performance monitoring | Review governance metrics, overdue actions, and recurring deficiencies |
| Resource prioritization | Recommend investment in risk reduction, control improvement, and governance capability |
| Incident oversight | Review significant AI incidents and lessons learned within its mandate |
| Policy and standard review | Sponsor periodic reviews of AI governance requirements |
| Dispute resolution | Resolve cross-functional issues or escalate matters beyond its authority |

The committee should not automatically assume the operational responsibilities of the CISO, business risk owners, privacy officers, engineering teams, or legal counsel.

Its function is to make authorized decisions, provide coordinated oversight, and ensure that material issues are addressed.

#### 9.2 Committee decision boundaries

The charter should explicitly distinguish among matters that the committee may approve, matters it may recommend, and matters that must be referred to another authority.

| Decision Type | Possible Committee Role | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Governance framework | Approve or recommend | Subject to the organization's delegated authority |
| Standard AI risk methodology | Approve or recommend | Consistent with enterprise risk management |
| High-risk AI proposal | Review, approve, or recommend | Depends on formal delegation and applicable requirements |
| Material security exception | Review or approve | Cannot override non-waivable obligations |
| Enterprise risk appetite | Recommend | Normally established by the appropriate executive or board authority |
| Material legal interpretation | Obtain and consider legal advice | Committee members do not replace qualified legal counsel |
| Security control implementation | Monitor and challenge | Technical implementation remains with designated owners |
| Business risk acceptance | Decide only if explicitly authorized | Otherwise refer to the authorized business risk owner |
| Suspension of an AI service | Exercise authority only where delegated | Emergency powers and operational procedures must be defined |
| Independent audit conclusion | Receive and consider findings | Must not interfere with Internal Audit independence |

#### 9.3 Committee operating requirements

The charter should establish:

- Committee chair and deputy, where applicable.
- Required membership and specialist representation.
- Meeting frequency.
- Quorum requirements.
- Decision-making method.
- Rules for conflicts of interest.
- Required documentation of decisions.
- Escalation and emergency decision procedures.
- Reporting obligations.
- Record retention.
- Periodic review of the committee's effectiveness.

For urgent AI security matters, the charter should define an expedited decision process rather than requiring every decision to wait for the next scheduled committee meeting.

### 10. Key Roles in AI Governance

An effective governance model requires a clear distinction between executive accountability, business ownership, governance coordination, technical implementation, independent assurance, and risk acceptance.

The roles below are representative. A smaller organization may combine some roles, while a regulated or multinational enterprise may require more specialized responsibilities.

#### 10.1 Board or delegated board committee

The board or its delegated committee provides strategic oversight according to its legal responsibilities and governance mandate.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Overseeing material enterprise risks associated with AI.
- Reviewing significant governance deficiencies.
- Ensuring that management establishes appropriate governance arrangements.
- Receiving reports on material incidents and risk exposure.
- Challenging whether management has adequate resources and controls.

The board should not ordinarily be expected to review individual technical questionnaire responses or manage routine AI security remediation.

#### 10.2 Executive sponsor

The executive sponsor supports the governance program, secures organizational commitment, and resolves issues requiring executive intervention.

Responsibilities may include:

- Sponsoring the AI governance operating model.
- Ensuring appropriate funding and resources.
- Resolving conflicts between business priorities and governance requirements.
- Escalating material risk decisions to the appropriate authority.
- Ensuring that governance obligations are incorporated into business operations.

The executive sponsor's authority should be explicitly defined rather than inferred from seniority alone.

#### 10.3 AI Governance Lead

The AI Governance Lead coordinates the framework, processes, and reporting mechanisms required to operate AI governance.

Responsibilities may include:

- Maintaining the AI governance framework.
- Coordinating policy and standard development.
- Maintaining governance procedures and templates.
- Coordinating the AI system inventory and assessment processes.
- Monitoring governance metrics.
- Preparing committee reporting.
- Tracking governance actions and escalations.
- Coordinating stakeholder engagement and training.
- Maintaining the governance RACI matrix.
- Supporting the review of governance effectiveness.

The AI Governance Lead should not automatically be assigned ownership of every AI business risk. Risk ownership should remain with the designated organizational roles.

#### 10.4 Chief Information Security Officer

The CISO is responsible for the cybersecurity function within the organization's established mandate.

Relevant AI governance responsibilities may include:

- Establishing security policies and requirements.
- Defining cybersecurity assessment expectations.
- Overseeing security risk assessment and monitoring capabilities.
- Ensuring that AI security risks are integrated into enterprise cybersecurity processes.
- Escalating material security weaknesses.
- Advising on security-related deployment restrictions.
- Coordinating incident response for relevant AI security incidents.

The CISO's precise authority over AI systems must be defined in the charter and aligned with the organization's risk and approval model.

#### 10.5 AI Security Lead

The AI Security Lead focuses on the technical and operational security of AI systems.

Responsibilities may include:

- Developing AI-specific security standards.
- Conducting or coordinating AI threat modeling.
- Reviewing AI architecture and trust boundaries.
- Assessing LLM, RAG, and AI-agent security risks where relevant.
- Evaluating model and application security controls.
- Coordinating AI security testing and red-team activities.
- Reviewing security evidence.
- Recommending risk treatment and remediation.
- Monitoring emerging AI security threats.
- Advising on technical safeguards and deployment conditions.

The AI Security Lead may recommend that a system not proceed to production, but the authority to approve, reject, or suspend the system must be established by organizational policy and delegated authority.

#### 10.6 Business or AI System Owner

The AI System Owner is the designated organizational role accountable for the system's lifecycle management and the responsibilities assigned by the governance framework.

Responsibilities may include:

- Maintaining an accurate system inventory entry.
- Ensuring that the intended purpose and approved use are documented.
- Coordinating required assessments.
- Ensuring that appropriate resources are available for remediation.
- Monitoring system performance and relevant risks.
- Ensuring that material changes are reviewed.
- Maintaining required operational documentation.
- Escalating incidents and material control weaknesses.
- Ensuring that approval conditions are implemented.

The AI System Owner and Business Risk Owner may be the same person in some organizations, but they are not automatically interchangeable roles.

#### 10.7 Business Risk Owner

The Business Risk Owner is the designated individual accountable for managing a defined business risk within the organization's risk framework.

Responsibilities may include:

- Understanding the business consequences of the AI use case.
- Reviewing the relevant risk assessment.
- Ensuring that risk treatment decisions are appropriate.
- Allocating resources for corrective actions.
- Accepting residual risk only within delegated authority.
- Escalating risks that exceed authorized thresholds.
- Monitoring the continuing acceptability of the risk.

A technical team may provide risk analysis, but it should not accept business risk merely because it has implemented the system.

#### 10.8 AI Engineering and Platform Teams

These teams implement, operate, and maintain AI systems and their supporting infrastructure.

Responsibilities may include:

- Secure system design and implementation.
- Model and application integration.
- Infrastructure and API security.
- Identity and access control implementation.
- Secure development and testing.
- Logging and monitoring integration.
- Vulnerability remediation.
- Configuration and change management.
- Technical evidence collection.
- Operational resilience and recovery.

They should provide accurate evidence of implementation and disclose material technical limitations.

They should not independently determine that a business risk is acceptable unless the relevant authority has been explicitly delegated to them.

### 11. Specialist Governance Roles

AI governance requires input from functions responsible for privacy, legal obligations, data management, procurement, enterprise risk, and independent assurance.

Their involvement should be based on defined responsibilities rather than informal consultation.

| Role | Primary Governance Contribution | Typical Involvement |
|---|---|---|
| Privacy Officer or DPO, where applicable | Privacy risk, data protection obligations, and relevant assessments | AI systems processing personal data |
| Legal Counsel | Legal interpretation, contracts, liability, and regulatory obligations | Material legal questions and contractual commitments |
| Compliance Officer | Compliance obligations, control mapping, and compliance monitoring | Systems subject to applicable regulatory or internal requirements |
| Enterprise Risk Management | Risk methodology, risk aggregation, and risk reporting | Material AI risks and enterprise risk decisions |
| Data Protection and Data Governance Teams | Data classification, quality, provenance, retention, and access | AI systems processing organizational or personal data |
| Procurement | Supplier onboarding, commercial controls, and sourcing procedures | Third-party AI products and services |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Supplier due diligence and ongoing assurance | Material external AI dependencies |
| Model Risk Management, where applicable | Model validation, performance risk, and model governance | Models subject to the organization's model risk framework |
| IT Operations | Operational reliability, change management, recovery, and monitoring | Production AI services and supporting infrastructure |
| Human Resources | Workforce-related governance, training, and employment implications | AI affecting employees or workforce processes |
| Internal Audit | Independent assessment of governance and control effectiveness | Risk-based audit engagements |
| Business Continuity Management | Continuity planning and recovery coordination | AI supporting critical business services |

The charter should define when these functions must participate, what deliverables they are expected to provide, and how disagreements are resolved.

For example, an AI system that processes employee information may require privacy, legal, security, HR, and business-owner involvement. However, not every participant should have an identical approval role.

Consultation should not be confused with formal approval authority.

### 12. Role Separation: AI Governance, AI Security, and AI Risk Management

Organizations often use these terms interchangeably, but they represent different responsibilities.

**AI Governance** establishes the policies, decision structures, accountability mechanisms, and oversight processes through which AI activities are managed.

**AI Security** focuses on protecting AI systems, their data, models, interfaces, infrastructure, and operational capabilities against security threats.

**AI Risk Management** identifies, evaluates, treats, monitors, and reports risks associated with AI systems and their use.

These functions overlap but should not be treated as identical.

| Dimension | AI Governance | AI Security | AI Risk Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary objective | Establish accountable oversight and decision-making | Protect AI systems and supporting assets | Manage uncertainty and potential adverse outcomes |
| Main concern | Authority, accountability, policies, and oversight | Threats, vulnerabilities, security controls, and attack paths | Risk identification, analysis, treatment, and monitoring |
| Typical deliverables | Charter, RACI, policies, governance decisions | Security architecture, threat models, test reports, control assessments | Risk registers, risk assessments, treatment plans |
| Primary stakeholders | Governance leaders, executives, committees | CISO, security architects, engineers, security operations | Risk owners, enterprise risk, governance, business leadership |
| Decision contribution | Determines how decisions are governed | Provides security analysis and recommendations | Supports risk treatment and acceptance decisions |
| Typical failure | Unclear authority and accountability | Inadequate security controls | Risks are unidentified, misrated, or unmanaged |

A mature operating model integrates these functions while preserving their distinct responsibilities.

For example, the AI Security Lead may identify a prompt-injection weakness, the AI Risk Manager may assess its likelihood and impact, the Business Risk Owner may determine the appropriate business risk treatment within delegated authority, and the governance function may ensure that the decision follows the approved process.

This arrangement creates a traceable chain from technical observation to formal governance outcome.

### 13. Decision Rights and Delegated Authority

Decision rights define who is authorized to make specific decisions and under what conditions.

They are a foundational component of the governance charter because assigning a person responsibility for an activity does not necessarily give that person authority to approve its outcome.

The charter should define decision rights for at least the following activities:

- Approval of the AI governance framework.
- Classification of AI system risk.
- Approval of AI use cases.
- Approval of AI architecture and security controls.
- Acceptance of residual business risk.
- Approval of security exceptions.
- Approval of supplier AI services.
- Authorization to deploy into production.
- Suspension or restriction of an AI system.
- Acceptance of remediation delays.
- Approval of material changes.
- Closure of material security findings.
- Retirement or decommissioning of AI systems.

#### Example decision-rights model

| Decision | Proposed Decision Authority | Required Input |
|---|---|---|
| Approve enterprise AI governance charter | Authorized executive or governance authority | Legal, risk, security, privacy, compliance |
| Approve standard assessment methodology | Designated governance authority | AI security, risk, engineering, compliance |
| Classify AI system risk | Designated risk-classification authority under policy | Business owner, governance, security, privacy as applicable |
| Approve routine security remediation | Authorized technical or service owner | Security control owner |
| Accept moderate residual business risk | Authorized Business Risk Owner within delegated limits | Security and enterprise risk |
| Accept high residual risk | Senior authority specified in the risk delegation framework | Business, security, enterprise risk, relevant specialists |
| Approve a security exception | Authorized exception approver | Control owner, security, risk owner |
| Approve production deployment | Designated release or system authorization authority | System owner, security, operations, relevant governance functions |
| Suspend a system during a serious incident | Incident authority or delegated operational/security authority | Incident response and relevant system owner |
| Approve system retirement | Designated Business or System Owner | Security, operations, data governance, legal where applicable |

This is an illustrative model, not a universal delegation of authority.

The organization must establish thresholds and decision rights in accordance with its existing management structure, risk appetite, legal obligations, and operational requirements.

### 14. Accountability for AI Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is one of the most important areas to address in the AI Governance Charter.

The organization must distinguish between identifying a risk, recommending a treatment, implementing a control, and formally accepting the remaining risk.

These activities may involve different roles.

For example, an AI Security Engineer may identify that an AI agent has excessive permissions. The engineering team may propose permission restrictions, and the security function may evaluate the proposed mitigation. The Business Risk Owner may then decide whether the remaining risk is acceptable within delegated authority.

If the residual risk exceeds that authority, the decision must be escalated.

#### Risk acceptance responsibilities

| Activity | Responsible Role | Accountability Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Identify the risk | Security, engineering, risk, or business function | The finding must be documented accurately |
| Assess likelihood and impact | Designated assessor or risk function | The methodology and assumptions must be recorded |
| Recommend treatment | Control owner and relevant specialists | Recommendations must address the risk scenario |
| Implement treatment | Designated technical or business owner | Implementation must be verified |
| Evaluate residual risk | Risk function and relevant specialists | Remaining exposure must be explicit |
| Accept residual business risk | Authorized Business Risk Owner or delegated authority | Acceptance must remain within approved limits |
| Approve security exception | Designated exception authority | Exception conditions and expiry must be documented |
| Monitor accepted risk | Assigned risk owner and control owners | Material changes require review |
| Escalate exceeded thresholds | Designated risk and governance functions | Risk cannot be accepted beyond delegated authority |

Risk acceptance must not be used as a substitute for implementing mandatory controls.

Where an obligation cannot lawfully be waived or the system violates a non-negotiable organizational requirement, the organization must pursue an appropriate alternative, restrict the system, redesign the solution, or reject the use case.

### 16. Governance Escalation and Conflict Resolution

AI governance requires a defined process for resolving disagreements between functions with different objectives.

For example:

- A business owner wants to launch an AI feature before a planned commercial deadline.
- The security team identifies an unresolved vulnerability.
- The privacy function raises concerns about personal-data processing.
- Legal counsel identifies contractual or regulatory restrictions.
- The engineering team states that remediation requires additional development time.

Without a formal escalation model, the decision may be made informally by the stakeholder with the greatest influence rather than by the authority designated to manage the risk.

#### Example escalation model

```text id="aigov_escalation_01"
Issue Identified
      |
      v
Responsible Team Documents Issue
      |
      v
Relevant Control / Risk Owner Reviews
      |
      v
Can the Issue Be Resolved Within
Existing Delegated Authority?
      |
   +--+--+
   |     |
  Yes    No
   |     |
   v     v
Resolve  Escalate to Designated
and      Risk / Security / Governance
Document Authority
         |
         v
    Formal Decision
         |
         v
 Record Rationale, Conditions,
 Actions, and Accountable Owner
         |
         v
 Monitor Resolution and Compliance
```

#### Escalation principles

1. **Escalate based on defined thresholds.** Material security, privacy, legal, safety, and operational risks should trigger escalation according to documented criteria.

2. **Preserve specialist independence.** A business deadline should not automatically override a required security or legal review.

3. **Document disagreements.** Material differences in assessment conclusions should be recorded rather than silently removed from the final report.

4. **Use the appropriate decision authority.** An issue should be escalated to the role authorized to resolve it, not merely to the most senior person available.

5. **Define emergency procedures.** Incidents and imminent threats may require immediate containment or restriction before a routine governance meeting can occur.

6. **Track decisions to completion.** An escalation is not resolved until the decision is documented and required actions have been assigned.

The charter should establish how unresolved disputes are handled, including the authority to impose temporary restrictions when an AI system presents a serious and immediate risk.

### 19. Governance RACI Design Principles

The RACI matrix should be designed to support real operational decisions rather than merely documenting the names of participating departments.

The following principles should guide its development.

**Principle 1 — One clearly designated Accountable role per activity.**

For each activity in a conventional RACI matrix, identify one role that is ultimately accountable for the outcome. If multiple approvals are required, distinguish the separate decisions rather than assigning several Accountable roles to one undifferentiated activity.

**Principle 2 — Responsible roles must have the capability and resources to perform the work.**

Assigning responsibility without providing access, expertise, time, or authority to execute the activity creates a nominal rather than effective assignment.

**Principle 3 — Consultation must be purposeful.**

Consulted roles should provide input that materially informs the activity or decision. Excessive consultation can delay governance without improving the outcome.

**Principle 4 — Informed roles must receive relevant information.**

Information-sharing obligations should specify the decisions, reports, incidents, or changes that must be communicated.

**Principle 5 — Decision authority must be consistent with the charter.**

A RACI assignment must not grant a role authority beyond its formal organizational mandate.

**Principle 6 — RACI assignments must be activity-specific.**

A department may be Accountable for one activity, Responsible for another, and Consulted on a third. Responsibilities should be assigned to specific activities rather than generalized across entire departments.

**Principle 7 — Independence requirements must be preserved.**

Where independent assurance is required, the RACI must not assign the independent reviewer sole responsibility for implementing the control being reviewed.

**Principle 8 — RACI must be maintained.**

Organizational changes, new AI technologies, revised approval thresholds, and audit findings may require updates to the matrix.

A RACI matrix that no longer reflects actual decision-making can create a false impression of accountability.

### 21. Common RACI Design Errors

A RACI matrix can appear complete while failing to establish effective accountability.

Common design errors include:

| Error | Why It Is a Problem | Corrective Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple Accountable roles for one activity | Creates uncertainty about who makes the final decision | Designate one Accountable role or split the activity into distinct decisions |
| No Accountable role | Leaves the outcome without a clear owner | Assign an authorized accountable role |
| No Responsible role | No one is explicitly assigned to perform the work | Assign the execution responsibility |
| Every stakeholder marked Consulted | Creates unnecessary approval-like bottlenecks | Limit consultation to roles whose input is materially required |
| Every stakeholder marked Informed | Produces excessive communication and weakens important notifications | Define relevant recipients and communication triggers |
| RACI conflicts with formal delegation | Assigns decisions to roles without authority | Reconcile the matrix with the charter and delegation framework |
| Technical ownership confused with business risk ownership | Technical delivery teams may be pressured to accept business risk | Separate implementation accountability from risk acceptance |
| Independent assurance assigned to control implementers | Undermines independent challenge | Preserve appropriate separation of duties |
| Generic department-level assignments | Conceals the actual role responsible for delivery | Identify specific organizational roles or positions |
| RACI not updated after organizational change | Creates obsolete responsibility assignments | Establish ownership and periodic review |
| RACI treated as a substitute for procedures | Does not explain how the work must be performed | Link the RACI to documented workflows and standards |
| RACI used without escalation rules | Unresolved disputes remain unresolved | Define decision authority and escalation routes |

The RACI should be reviewed with the people assigned to its roles to confirm that the responsibilities are understood and operationally achievable.

### 23. Core Principle — Authority, Accountability, and Execution Must Be Explicit

An effective AI Governance Charter establishes the formal mandate under which AI systems are governed, while the RACI matrix translates that mandate into clear operational responsibilities.

The charter must identify who has authority to make decisions, which obligations apply, how risks are escalated, and how governance oversight is maintained. The RACI must identify who performs the work, who is accountable for the outcome, whose expertise must be consulted, and who must be informed.

Neither instrument is sufficient in isolation.

A charter without an operational RACI may establish principles without ensuring that responsibilities are implemented. A RACI without a formal charter may assign activities without establishing legitimate decision authority.

The organization must therefore maintain alignment between governance authority, risk ownership, technical implementation, independent assurance, and formal approval.

The effectiveness of the model should be evaluated by whether responsibilities are understood, decisions are made by authorized roles, material risks receive appropriate treatment, and governance obligations can be demonstrated through reliable records.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Governance charter vs. governance policy | The charter establishes the mandate and operating authority; policies establish requirements and rules |
| Charter vs. RACI | The charter defines governance authority; the RACI assigns activity-level responsibilities |
| Responsible vs. Accountable | Responsible performs the work; Accountable is answerable for the outcome |
| Consultation vs. approval | Consultation provides input; approval exercises formal decision authority |
| Business ownership vs. technical ownership | Business ownership addresses intended purpose and business outcomes; technical ownership addresses implementation and operation |
| Risk assessment vs. risk acceptance | Assessment analyzes exposure; acceptance is an authorized decision concerning residual risk |
| Governance oversight vs. operational execution | Oversight evaluates and directs governance; execution implements required activities and controls |
| Independent assurance vs. control implementation | Assurance evaluates the adequacy of controls; implementation establishes and operates them |
| Delegation vs. abdication | Delegation assigns authorized responsibilities; it does not eliminate the delegating authority's remaining obligations |
| Documented responsibility vs. effective accountability | A written assignment defines expectations; effective accountability requires authority, capability, action, and evidence |

**Final governance principle:** AI governance is effective only when every material activity has a clear owner, every decision is made within legitimate authority, every material risk has an accountable decision-maker, and the organization can demonstrate that its governance responsibilities are operating in practice.

### 26. RACI Assignment Rules and Governance Controls

The RACI framework should follow consistent assignment rules.

These rules should be documented in the methodology used to create and maintain the matrix.

#### 26.1 Responsible assignment

The Responsible role performs the activity or produces the required deliverable.

There may be multiple Responsible roles when several teams must perform distinct parts of an activity. However, the division of work must be clear.

For example, an AI security assessment may require:

- The AI Security Lead to evaluate AI-specific threats.
- The Infrastructure Lead to provide infrastructure security evidence.
- The Engineering Lead to provide application and model implementation evidence.
- The Data Owner to confirm relevant data classifications and permitted uses.

The assessment process must identify which role coordinates the final assessment record.

#### 26.2 Accountable assignment

The Accountable role is ultimately answerable for the activity's outcome.

For a conventional RACI activity, the organization should assign one clearly designated Accountable role.

Where multiple formal approvals are required, the activity should be divided into distinct decisions rather than assigning several Accountable roles to an ambiguous combined activity.

For example:

| Activity | Accountable Role |
|---|---|
| Approve the business justification | Business Owner |
| Validate security assessment conclusions | Designated Security Authority |
| Accept residual business risk | Authorized Business Risk Owner |
| Authorize production release | Designated Release Authority |

These are separate decisions and may legitimately involve different authorities.

#### 26.3 Consulted assignment

Consulted roles provide information, specialist advice, or challenge before an activity or decision is finalized.

Consultation should be mandatory when required by policy, regulation, contract, or the organization's governance framework.

For example, a system processing personal data may require privacy review, while a system capable of executing privileged actions may require a detailed security architecture review.

The organization should define which consultation requirements are mandatory and which are discretionary.

#### 26.4 Informed assignment

Informed roles receive information about relevant activities, decisions, incidents, or outcomes.

Being Informed does not automatically grant approval authority or the right to veto a decision.

The matrix should identify when notification is required and the form of communication expected.

For example, a material AI security incident may require notification to security leadership, legal counsel, privacy specialists, executive management, and other parties according to the incident classification and applicable obligations.

#### 26.5 RACI validation rules

Before approving the matrix, verify that:

- Each activity has a defined outcome.
- Each activity has at least one Responsible role.
- Each conventional RACI activity has one clearly designated Accountable role.
- Consultation requirements are proportionate.
- Required notifications are explicit.
- All assignments align with formal authority.
- Conflicts of interest are addressed.
- Independent assurance remains appropriately independent.
- No assignment implies that an individual can waive a mandatory requirement.
- The role catalogue is consistent with organizational terminology.

These rules reduce ambiguity and improve the reliability of governance execution.

The organization should never treat a technical assessment score as automatic authorization to accept a risk.

### 36. Managing RACI Conflicts and Overlapping Responsibilities

AI governance activities frequently cross organizational boundaries.

A security assessment may require input from AI engineering, cybersecurity, privacy, data governance, legal, procurement, and enterprise risk. Without explicit decision boundaries, these functions may disagree about who owns the final outcome.

Common conflicts include:

- The business owner wants to launch a system, while security identifies unresolved risks.
- Engineering believes a control is implemented, while security considers the evidence insufficient.
- Procurement considers supplier due diligence complete, while security identifies unresolved technical gaps.
- The AI Governance Committee believes an issue is within its authority, while executive management retains the relevant approval right.
- A risk owner accepts residual risk, but a mandatory control requirement remains unmet.
- Internal Audit identifies a weakness that management has already marked as remediated.

The RACI should not attempt to resolve every possible dispute through additional letters.

Instead, the organization should establish an explicit conflict-resolution mechanism.

#### Recommended resolution sequence

| Step | Required Action | Expected Record |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define the disputed activity or decision | Issue statement |
| 2 | Identify the relevant charter provision and RACI assignment | Governance reference |
| 3 | Determine whether the disagreement concerns facts, control effectiveness, risk, or authority | Dispute classification |
| 4 | Obtain the evidence and specialist input needed to resolve the disagreement | Assessment record |
| 5 | Identify the authorized decision-maker | Decision authority record |
| 6 | Escalate if the issue exceeds delegated authority | Escalation record |
| 7 | Document the decision and its rationale | Decision log |
| 8 | Assign actions and deadlines | Remediation or action register |
| 9 | Update the RACI or supporting procedure if the dispute reveals a structural ambiguity | Controlled change record |

A disagreement should not be considered resolved merely because the stakeholders agree to proceed.

The organization must determine whether the decision is authorized, whether the relevant risk is acceptable, and whether any mandatory requirements remain unmet.

---

### 37. Core Principle — RACI Must Reflect Real Decision Authority

The value of an AI Governance RACI matrix depends on whether it accurately represents how work is performed, how risks are assessed, and how decisions are authorized.

A matrix that assigns letters without defining deliverables, decision rights, evidence requirements, and escalation paths can create the appearance of accountability without producing reliable governance.

A mature RACI framework must connect operational execution to formal governance authority, preserve the distinction between technical implementation and business risk acceptance, and ensure that independent assurance remains appropriately independent.

The matrix should be reviewed with the people assigned to its roles, tested against real governance scenarios, and maintained whenever organizational responsibilities or decision authorities change.

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Distinction | Correct Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Role catalogue vs. RACI matrix | The catalogue defines roles; the matrix assigns them to specific activities |
| Responsibility vs. authority | Responsibility concerns performing or owning work; authority concerns the right to make a decision |
| Activity accountability vs. risk ownership | Accountability for completing an activity does not automatically confer authority to accept its associated business risk |
| Control implementation vs. control validation | Implementers establish controls; designated assurance roles evaluate whether they meet requirements |
| Consultation vs. consensus | Consultation gathers necessary input; it does not require every stakeholder to agree |
| Committee oversight vs. operational ownership | A committee provides oversight or makes delegated decisions; operational teams remain responsible for their assigned work |
| Incident response vs. incident governance | Response manages containment and recovery; governance establishes authority, escalation, and accountability |
| Risk acceptance vs. mandatory compliance | Authorized risk acceptance cannot waive non-waivable legal or regulatory obligations |
| RACI documentation vs. effective governance | A completed matrix records assignments; effective governance requires those assignments to be understood, resourced, and followed |

**Final governance principle:** Every AI governance activity must have a clear execution owner, an accountable decision-maker, appropriate specialist involvement, and an escalation route consistent with the organization's formal authority structure.

# 20.12 — AI Governance RACI and Governance Charter

## Part 3 — Governance Charter Design, Decision Rights, Escalation Mechanisms, and Enterprise Operating Model

### 38. Establishing the AI Governance Charter as the Enterprise Authority Framework

The AI Governance Charter establishes the formal mandate, authority, scope, responsibilities, and operating principles of the organization's AI governance function. It defines how AI-related decisions are made, who is authorized to make them, which risks require escalation, and how governance activities are integrated into enterprise operations.

The charter is more than a statement of intent. It is an organizational control document that translates executive direction into enforceable governance arrangements.

A well-designed charter should answer the following questions:

- Why does the organization require AI governance?
- Which AI systems, services, use cases, and business activities fall within its scope?
- Which organizational bodies oversee AI governance?
- Who owns business, technical, security, privacy, and compliance risks?
- Which decisions can be made by operational teams?
- Which decisions require independent review or formal approval?
- What happens when a proposed AI deployment fails a mandatory security requirement?
- How are unresolved disagreements escalated?
- How are governance performance and residual risk reported to executive management?
- How is the charter maintained as the organization's AI capabilities evolve?

The charter should establish the governance structure, while supporting policies, standards, procedures, and templates define how individual requirements are implemented.

| Governance Instrument | Primary Purpose | Typical Contents |
|---|---|---|
| AI Governance Charter | Establishes the governance mandate and authority | Scope, objectives, governance bodies, decision rights, escalation |
| AI Governance Policy | Defines mandatory organizational governance requirements | Principles, responsibilities, oversight obligations |
| AI Security Policy | Establishes security requirements for AI systems | Security controls, risk requirements, minimum safeguards |
| AI Risk Management Standard | Defines the risk assessment and treatment methodology | Risk criteria, scoring, treatment, acceptance |
| AI Governance Procedures | Describe operational workflows | Intake, assessment, approval, monitoring |
| RACI Matrix | Assigns responsibilities to activities | Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed |
| AI Governance Committee Terms of Reference | Defines the committee's operation | Membership, quorum, agenda, decision rules |
| AI Decision Register | Records significant governance decisions | Decision, authority, rationale, conditions, owner |
| AI Risk Register | Records and tracks AI-related risks | Risk statements, ratings, controls, treatment plans |
| AI Control Matrix | Connects obligations to controls and evidence | Requirements, control owners, testing, evidence |

The organization should maintain consistency between these documents. A charter that delegates authority to a committee must not be contradicted by a procedure that gives the same decision exclusively to an operational team.

**Implementation requirement:** Each governance document should have an owner, an approval authority, a version, an effective date, a review frequency, and a controlled distribution method.

### 40. Defining the Scope of the AI Governance Charter

The scope of the charter determines which AI-related activities are subject to organizational governance.

A scope limited to internally developed machine-learning models is usually insufficient for an enterprise that also uses third-party AI services, embedded AI features, generative AI applications, autonomous agents, and AI-enabled business platforms.

The scope should consider AI systems according to their capabilities, deployment arrangements, intended purposes, and potential impact.

#### 40.1 AI system categories

| Category | Examples | Governance Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Internally developed AI | Predictive models, classification systems, internal language models | Development controls, training data, model validation |
| Third-party AI services | Hosted language models, external inference APIs | Supplier assurance, data exposure, contractual obligations |
| AI-enabled enterprise applications | CRM, ERP, productivity, customer-service platforms | Configuration, integration, identity, data access |
| Generative AI applications | Content generation, summarization, code generation | Prompt injection, data leakage, output reliability |
| AI agents | Systems that plan and execute actions using tools | Permission boundaries, action authorization, execution monitoring |
| Embedded AI features | AI capabilities integrated into existing software | Vendor dependencies, configuration governance, feature activation |
| AI-assisted decision systems | Systems supporting recruitment, credit, customer eligibility, or operational decisions | Human oversight, impact assessment, accountability |
| Fine-tuned or customized models | Models adapted to organizational data or workflows | Training-data governance, evaluation, change management |
| AI development and evaluation tools | Model-training platforms, testing environments, evaluation pipelines | Access control, supply-chain risk, artifact integrity |
| Shadow AI | AI tools used outside approved procurement or governance processes | Discovery, exposure assessment, remediation |

These categories should be treated as inventory and governance dimensions rather than mutually exclusive classifications. One AI service may belong to several categories.

The charter should specify which entities and functions are covered, including relevant subsidiaries, business units, technology teams, contractors, and outsourced service providers.

### 41. Establishing the AI Governance Committee Mandate

The AI Governance Committee provides cross-functional oversight and makes decisions within its formally delegated authority.

Its purpose is to resolve governance issues that cannot be managed effectively by a single operational function, review significant AI risks, monitor governance performance, and ensure that material decisions are made by the appropriate authority.

The committee should not become an approval bottleneck for every AI activity. Routine decisions should remain with designated operational owners when they fall within established policy and delegated authority.

#### 41.1 Recommended committee responsibilities

6. Monitoring overdue high-risk remediation actions.
7. Reviewing material incidents and lessons learned.
8. Monitoring exceptions and accepted residual risks.
9. Reviewing significant changes in AI technology or operating models.
10. Recommending changes to governance policy and standards.
11. Monitoring governance metrics and maturity.
12. Escalating matters that exceed its delegated authority.

### 42. Establishing Governance Decision Rights

Decision rights specify who may approve, reject, conditionally approve, suspend, or escalate an AI-related activity.

They must be defined separately from general participation in governance processes.

For example, an AI Security Lead may provide a technical recommendation without having authority to accept the associated business risk. Similarly, the AI Governance Committee may review a high-risk use case without possessing the legal authority to authorize a regulated activity.

A decision-rights framework should identify both the decision and the conditions under which the authority may be exercised.

#### 42.1 Decision categories

| Decision Category | Example Decision | Typical Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Business sponsorship | Whether the use case meets a legitimate business need | Business Owner |
| Governance classification | Which assessment and oversight requirements apply | Designated Governance Authority |
| Technical security approval | Whether defined security requirements are satisfied | Designated Security Authority |
| Privacy determination | Whether required privacy review or safeguards are adequate | Authorized privacy function, within its mandate |
| Supplier selection | Whether a supplier meets procurement and assurance requirements | Authorized Procurement or Business Authority |
| Residual risk acceptance | Whether remaining risk is acceptable within delegated limits | Authorized Risk Owner |
| Production release | Whether deployment may proceed under release governance | Release Authority |
| Exception approval | Whether a permitted deviation may be authorized | Designated Exception Authority |
| Emergency containment | Whether an affected system must be restricted or isolated | Authorized Incident or Operational Authority |
| Regulatory notification | Whether and how an external notification must be made | Authorized function under applicable law and procedure |
| Retirement | Whether a system may be decommissioned | AI System Owner and relevant operational authorities |

These authorities may be distributed across several roles. The charter must identify the actual decision owner rather than relying on generic titles.

#### 42.2 Decision conditions

Every significant approval should identify:

1. The system or use case being approved.
2. The scope of the approval.
3. The evidence reviewed.
4. Material findings and unresolved issues.
5. The residual risk rating.
6. The approving authority.
7. Conditions or limitations attached to the decision.
8. Required monitoring or remediation.
9. Expiry or reassessment triggers, where applicable.
10. The decision date and record identifier.

An approval should not be interpreted as permanent authorization if the system's intended purpose, data, model, permissions, supplier, or operating environment changes materially.

### 43. Designing a Delegated Authority Matrix

A delegated authority matrix translates governance principles into practical approval thresholds.

The thresholds should be established using the organization's approved risk methodology and risk appetite. They should not be invented independently by the AI Governance Committee or assigned solely according to a technical score.

A sample matrix is shown below.

| Risk or Decision Condition | Operational Owner | Specialist Review | Higher-Level Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low-risk use case with no material control gaps | May progress within delegated authority | As required by policy | Not normally required |
| Moderate-risk use case | Business and system owners coordinate assessment | Security, privacy, and other applicable specialists | Escalate if thresholds or policy require |
| High-risk use case | Cannot rely solely on routine operational approval | Formal cross-functional assessment | Designated senior authority or committee |
| Critical or unacceptable risk | Do not proceed under normal approval | Immediate specialist and risk review | Executive escalation and formal decision |
| Unresolved mandatory security requirement | Remediation or approved control-compliant alternative required | Designated security authority | Escalation if unresolved |
| Residual risk exceeding the owner's delegated limit | Owner documents and escalates the risk | Enterprise Risk Management and relevant specialists | Higher authority with sufficient delegated power |
| Temporary security exception | Requesting owner documents justification and compensating controls | Security and relevant control owners | Designated exception authority |
| Material incident affecting an active AI system | Immediate containment under incident procedures | Security, operations, legal, privacy as applicable | Incident authority and executive escalation according to severity |
| Material system or supplier change | Change assessment before implementation, unless emergency procedures apply | Security, risk, privacy, and other relevant specialists | Reapproval according to the change's risk and impact |

The matrix should specify whether an approval is mandatory, advisory, or conditional.

It should also identify decisions that cannot be delegated, such as decisions reserved to a statutory officer, board, executive authority, or other legally designated decision-maker.

#### 43.1 Separation between risk acceptance and control compliance

The delegated authority matrix must distinguish two different questions:

- Is the residual risk within the organization's approved risk appetite and accepted by an authorized owner?
- Have mandatory legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal control requirements been satisfied?

An authorized person may be able to accept certain residual business risks. That does not automatically authorize the organization to disregard a mandatory requirement.

Where a mandatory requirement is not satisfied, the system must follow the applicable remediation, alternative-control, exception, or escalation procedure. If no permitted path exists, deployment must not proceed.

### 45. Governance Escalation Framework

Escalation provides a formal mechanism for addressing decisions, risks, incidents, or disagreements that exceed an individual's authority or cannot be resolved at the operational level.

Without escalation rules, unresolved issues may remain in project registers, be informally accepted, or be bypassed to meet delivery deadlines.

An escalation framework should define the trigger, receiving authority, required evidence, response expectation, and interim restrictions.

#### 45.1 Escalation triggers

Common triggers include:

- Residual risk exceeding delegated acceptance limits.
- Failure to satisfy a mandatory security requirement.
- Disagreement over risk classification or control effectiveness.
- A material privacy or regulatory concern.
- A critical supplier assurance gap.
- A high-severity AI security incident.
- An overdue remediation action involving material risk.
- A proposed change that materially expands system permissions or intended purpose.
- A request to use data outside the approved scope.
- A suspected violation of AI governance policy.
- A conflict of interest affecting a governance decision.
- A decision that requires authority reserved to executive management or another designated body.

#### 45.2 Escalation path


The process should distinguish routine governance escalation from urgent incident escalation. A critical security event may require immediate containment before a full governance review is possible.

### 46. Governance Charter Review and Change Control

An AI Governance Charter must evolve as the organization introduces new technologies, enters new markets, changes its operating model, or becomes subject to additional obligations.

However, the charter should not be changed informally whenever an individual project encounters a difficulty.

Changes must follow a controlled process that preserves accountability and prevents unauthorized weakening of governance requirements.

#### 46.1 Review triggers

The charter should be reviewed when:

- The organization introduces a materially different category of AI capability.
- AI agents receive new forms of tool access or execution authority.
- A significant AI security or governance incident occurs.
- The organization acquires another business or AI provider.
- The enterprise risk appetite or delegated authority model changes.
- Applicable laws or regulatory requirements change.
- Audit findings identify a structural governance weakness.
- The AI Governance Committee's mandate changes.
- Roles or organizational reporting lines change materially.
- Governance metrics reveal recurring accountability failures.
- A major transformation changes the AI operating model.

A scheduled review should also occur at a defined interval, even if no material event has occurred.

#### 46.2 Change-control workflow

| Stage | Activity | Required Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Initiation | Identify the need for a charter amendment | Change request |
| 2. Impact analysis | Evaluate implications for roles, authority, policies, and procedures | Impact assessment |
| 3. Consultation | Obtain input from affected functions | Consultation record |
| 4. Risk and compliance review | Evaluate governance, security, legal, and compliance consequences | Review findings |
| 5. Draft amendment | Prepare revised charter language | Draft revision |
| 6. Approval | Obtain approval from the designated authority | Approval record |
| 7. Publication | Publish the controlled version | Updated document record |
| 8. Communication | Inform affected stakeholders | Communication evidence |
| 9. Implementation | Update related RACI matrices, procedures, and templates | Implementation records |
| 10. Verification | Confirm that changes have been incorporated | Closure record |

A charter amendment should trigger a review of related governance artifacts. Updating the charter without updating its dependent RACI matrices and procedures can create contradictory authority assignments.

### 47. Core Principle — The Charter Must Translate Governance Intent into Enforceable Authority

An AI Governance Charter is effective only when its mandate, scope, decision rights, delegated authority, escalation mechanisms, and records are reflected in actual organizational behavior.

A charter should not create authority that conflicts with the organization's legal structure, executive delegations, statutory responsibilities, or established risk management framework.

The organization must distinguish governance oversight from operational execution, technical assurance from business risk acceptance, and conditional approval from unrestricted authorization.

**The central governance principle is that every material AI decision must be made by an authorized role, supported by proportionate evidence, recorded with sufficient context, and subject to the conditions and escalation requirements established by the organization.**

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Charter vs. policy | The charter establishes governance authority and operating arrangements; policies establish mandatory requirements |
| Governance committee vs. executive authority | A committee exercises only the authority formally delegated to it |
| Risk acceptance vs. deployment approval | Accepting residual risk does not automatically authorize production deployment |
| Conditional approval vs. unrestricted approval | Conditional approval permits progression only when its conditions are satisfied |
| Operational escalation vs. incident escalation | Routine governance escalation addresses unresolved decisions; incident escalation supports urgent response and containment |
| Decision record vs. meeting minutes | A decision record captures the formal decision and its conditions; minutes capture the relevant proceedings |
| Charter amendment vs. operational exception | An amendment changes the governance framework through formal approval; an exception authorizes a permitted deviation within established rules |
| Governance documentation vs. governance effectiveness | Documents establish the intended model; effectiveness depends on whether the model is implemented, followed, monitored, and enforced |

### 48. Implementing the AI Governance Charter Across the Enterprise

An AI Governance Charter becomes operational when its authority structure is embedded into enterprise processes, technology delivery, procurement, risk management, security operations, and executive reporting.

Organizations frequently establish governance committees and publish policies without integrating them into the workflows where AI-related decisions actually occur. This creates a gap between documented governance and operational practice.

The implementation objective is to make governance a normal part of AI-related business activity rather than a separate administrative process performed after technical decisions have already been made.

#### 48.1 Implementation principles

The implementation should follow six principles:

1. **Authority before activity:** Define who can approve, reject, or escalate a decision before launching the associated workflow.
2. **Risk-proportionate governance:** Apply controls and oversight according to the potential impact and risk of the AI use case.
3. **Lifecycle integration:** Embed governance requirements throughout planning, procurement, development, deployment, monitoring, and retirement.
4. **Evidence-based decisions:** Require documented evidence for material approvals and risk acceptance.
5. **Separation of duties:** Avoid assigning incompatible responsibilities to the same individual where independent review is necessary.
6. **Continuous improvement:** Use incidents, assessments, audit findings, and performance metrics to improve the governance model.

#### 48.2 Implementation workstreams

| Workstream | Key Activities | Primary Owner | Expected Deliverables |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governance authority | Approve charter, committee mandate, and delegated authority | Executive Sponsor | Approved charter and authority matrix |
| Organizational accountability | Confirm role appointments and responsibilities | AI Governance Lead | Role register and RACI |
| AI inventory | Identify AI systems, applications, models, and agents | AI System Owners and IT | AI inventory |
| Risk management | Establish assessment and risk acceptance processes | Enterprise Risk Management | AI risk methodology and risk register |
| Security integration | Embed security requirements into the AI lifecycle | CISO and AI Security Lead | Security standards and assessment workflows |
| Privacy and data governance | Define data-use review and privacy escalation | Privacy and Data Governance functions | Review procedures and evidence requirements |
| Procurement | Integrate AI governance into supplier onboarding | Procurement and Third-Party Risk | Supplier assessment requirements |
| Technology delivery | Integrate governance gates into delivery and release processes | Technology and Engineering Leads | Delivery gates and release criteria |
| Reporting | Define governance metrics and reporting cadence | AI Governance Lead | Dashboard and management reports |
| Assurance | Establish control testing and independent review | Control Owners and Internal Audit | Testing plans and assurance reports |
| Training | Educate stakeholders on authority and responsibilities | AI Governance Lead and HR | Training materials and completion records |

The workstreams should be coordinated through a documented implementation plan with named owners, milestones, dependencies, and acceptance criteria.

### 50. Integrating Governance into the AI Lifecycle

The governance framework should be integrated into existing enterprise workflows, including project management, procurement, software development, change management, and incident response.

The objective is to establish control gates at the points where decisions create meaningful changes in risk.

#### 50.1 Governance stage-gate model

| Gate | Lifecycle Stage | Required Activities | Exit Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| G0 | Idea initiation | Identify business need, proposed AI capability, and business sponsor | Use case registered |
| G1 | Initial screening | Classify purpose, data, affected parties, autonomy, and potential impact | Initial classification recorded |
| G2 | Detailed assessment | Perform applicable AI risk, security, privacy, legal, and supplier assessments | Required assessments completed |
| G3 | Design approval | Review architecture, threat model, data flows, access controls, and safeguards | Design approval obtained |
| G4 | Development or configuration | Implement required controls and document material design decisions | Implementation evidence available |
| G5 | Testing and validation | Test security, functionality, reliability, and relevant impact mitigations | Acceptance criteria satisfied |
| G6 | Production authorization | Review unresolved findings, residual risks, monitoring, and operational readiness | Authorized release decision recorded |
| G7 | Operational monitoring | Monitor performance, security, misuse, incidents, and control effectiveness | Monitoring operating as required |
| G8 | Material change | Reassess material changes to purpose, data, model, supplier, or permissions | Change approval recorded |
| G9 | Retirement | Revoke access, manage data and artifacts, preserve required records, and close dependencies | Decommissioning verified |

Not every AI system requires identical assessment depth. Governance intensity should be proportionate to risk, but mandatory legal, contractual, and organizational requirements must still be satisfied.

#### 50.2 Example: AI-powered customer-service assistant

Consider a telecommunications company introducing an AI-powered customer-service assistant integrated with Salesforce, a customer-facing application, and internal knowledge repositories.

The governance workflow should address:

- Whether the proposed use case is approved for customer-facing deployment.
- Whether customer data may be sent to the selected model provider.
- Whether the model can retrieve information across customer accounts.
- Whether the assistant can initiate account changes or financial transactions.
- Whether tool access is restricted to explicitly authorized actions.
- Whether human escalation is available for sensitive cases.
- Whether outputs are logged appropriately without unnecessarily retaining sensitive information.
- Whether prompt injection or manipulated retrieved content could influence actions.
- Whether the supplier's contractual and security commitments are adequate.
- Whether monitoring can detect misuse, unauthorized access, or abnormal behavior.

A system that only drafts responses for an employee may require a different control profile from an autonomous agent that can modify customer accounts.

The governance decision should therefore be based on the actual capabilities and impact of the proposed deployment, not merely the label *AI assistant*.

### 51. AI Governance Metrics, KPIs, and KRIs

Governance reporting provides management with evidence that the framework is being implemented and that material risks are being addressed.

Metrics should measure not only administrative activity but also control effectiveness, risk exposure, accountability, and decision quality.

A high number of completed assessments does not necessarily indicate effective governance if assessments are superficial, high-risk findings remain unresolved, or unapproved AI systems continue operating.

#### 51.1 Governance performance indicators

| Metric ID | Metric | Calculation or Measurement | Reporting Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOV-KPI-01 | AI inventory coverage | Registered AI systems divided by identified AI systems within the measurement scope | Measure inventory completeness |
| GOV-KPI-02 | Assessment completion rate | Systems with required completed assessments divided by systems requiring assessment | Measure assessment coverage |
| GOV-KPI-03 | Approval compliance | Deployments with required documented approvals divided by deployments reviewed | Measure compliance with approval gates |
| GOV-KPI-04 | RACI assignment coverage | Material governance activities with designated accountable owners divided by material activities reviewed | Identify accountability gaps |
| GOV-KPI-05 | Governance training completion | Personnel completing required training divided by personnel assigned training | Measure training coverage |
| GOV-KPI-06 | Decision turnaround time | Median or percentile time between a complete submission and a recorded decision | Identify governance bottlenecks |
| GOV-KPI-07 | Remediation closure rate | Findings closed within the required period divided by findings due for closure | Measure remediation execution |
| GOV-KPI-08 | Evidence completeness | Sampled governance decisions with all required evidence divided by decisions sampled | Assess record quality |
| GOV-KPI-09 | Reassessment timeliness | Systems reassessed within the required interval divided by systems due for reassessment | Measure ongoing oversight |
| GOV-KPI-10 | Governance exception review compliance | Exceptions reviewed by their required review dates divided by exceptions due for review | Monitor exception governance |

#### 51.2 Key risk indicators

| Metric ID | Risk Indicator | Potential Warning Signal | Possible Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOV-KRI-01 | Unregistered AI usage | Increase in discovered systems outside the inventory | Strengthen discovery and intake controls |
| GOV-KRI-02 | Overdue critical findings | Material security findings exceed remediation deadlines | Escalate and consider restricting deployment |
| GOV-KRI-03 | Unauthorized deployment | Systems enter production without required authorization | Investigate process failure and apply corrective controls |
| GOV-KRI-04 | Expired exceptions | Exceptions remain active beyond their approved validity | Reassess, renew through authorized process, or remove |
| GOV-KRI-05 | Repeated control failures | Similar findings recur across multiple systems | Address systemic control weaknesses |
| GOV-KRI-06 | Unapproved model or supplier changes | Material changes occur without required reassessment | Improve change detection and approval integration |
| GOV-KRI-07 | Unresolved ownership gaps | High-risk systems lack confirmed accountable owners | Escalate ownership assignment |
| GOV-KRI-08 | Material incidents | Increase in AI-related security or governance incidents | Review threat model, safeguards, and operational controls |
| GOV-KRI-09 | Excessive privileged AI actions | Agent or service identities exercise unexpected privileges | Investigate authorization and monitoring |
| GOV-KRI-10 | Persistent governance disputes | Repeated unresolved decisions or unclear authority | Review charter, delegated authority, and RACI |

Metrics should be defined consistently, with documented calculation methods, data sources, owners, reporting frequencies, thresholds, and escalation rules.

#### 51.3 Avoiding misleading metrics

Governance metrics should be interpreted in context.

For example:

- A rising number of identified AI systems may indicate improving discovery rather than deteriorating governance.
- A low incident count may reflect effective prevention or inadequate detection.
- A high assessment completion rate may conceal poor assessment quality.
- A short approval time may indicate efficient processes or insufficient review.
- A low exception count may indicate strong controls or poor exception reporting.

The organization should combine quantitative indicators with qualitative review, sampling, trend analysis, and control testing.

| Approval Authority | Authorized exception decision-maker |

### 54. Governance Assurance and Control Effectiveness

AI governance assurance evaluates whether the framework is properly designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

A policy may be approved and a RACI matrix may be complete, yet governance may still fail if decision-makers lack authority, risk assessments are bypassed, remediation is not enforced, or evidence cannot be produced.

Assurance should therefore test both documentary compliance and operational behavior.

#### 54.1 Assurance dimensions

| Assurance Dimension | Example Test | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Governance design | Are roles, decision rights, and escalation routes clearly defined? | Charter, policies, RACI, authority matrix |
| Implementation | Have designated owners accepted their responsibilities? | Role assignments, communications, training |
| Operating effectiveness | Are required reviews performed before deployment? | Assessment records, release records, approvals |
| Decision authority | Were decisions made by authorized roles? | Decision register, delegation records |
| Risk treatment | Are material findings assigned and remediated? | Risk register, action tracking, validation |
| Exception management | Are exceptions approved, time-limited, and monitored? | Exception register and review records |
| Evidence integrity | Can significant decisions be reconstructed? | Controlled records and audit trails |
| Change governance | Do material changes trigger reassessment? | Change records, reassessments, approvals |
| Monitoring | Are indicators reviewed and acted upon? | Dashboards, minutes, corrective actions |
| Independence | Is assurance sufficiently independent of the activities assessed? | Assurance charter, reporting arrangements |

#### 54.2 Sample assurance tests

**Test A — Governance authorization**

Select a sample of AI systems deployed during the review period. Confirm that each system completed the required governance assessments and received approval from the correct authority before deployment.

**Test B — Risk acceptance**

Select a sample of high-risk findings marked as accepted. Confirm that the risk owner had sufficient delegated authority, the residual risk was documented, and any required escalation occurred.

**Test C — Exception validity**

Select active exceptions and verify their approval, scope, expiry date, compensating controls, monitoring arrangements, and remediation plans.

**Test D — Material change**

Select AI systems that experienced material changes. Determine whether the changes triggered reassessment and reapproval when required.

**Test E — Accountability**

Select material governance activities and verify that accountable owners were assigned, actions were completed, and overdue issues were escalated.

**Test F — Decision traceability**

Select material governance decisions and determine whether an independent reviewer can reconstruct the decision from its recorded rationale, supporting evidence, authority reference, and follow-up actions.

Findings should be classified according to the organization's assurance methodology, assigned to accountable owners, and tracked to verified closure.

### 55. AI Governance Maturity Assessment

An AI governance maturity assessment evaluates how consistently and effectively the organization performs its governance activities.

Maturity should not be measured solely by the number of policies or committees established. It should reflect the quality of implementation, decision-making, monitoring, and continuous improvement.

The following five-level model can be adapted for internal benchmarking.

| Level | Maturity | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial | Governance is informal, inconsistent, and heavily dependent on individuals |
| 2 | Developing | Basic policies and responsibilities exist, but implementation varies across teams |
| 3 | Defined | A documented framework, RACI, risk methodology, and formal approval workflows are established |
| 4 | Managed | Governance performance is measured, control operation is monitored, and accountability is enforced |
| 5 | Optimized | Governance is continuously improved using assurance results, incident learning, metrics, and changing risk conditions |

#### 55.1 Maturity assessment domains

| Domain | Assessment Question |
|---|---|
| Charter and authority | Is the governance mandate approved and understood? |
| Roles and accountability | Are material activities assigned to accountable owners? |
| Decision rights | Are approvals and risk acceptance governed by delegated authority? |
| AI inventory | Is the inventory sufficiently complete and maintained? |
| Risk management | Are AI risks assessed and treated consistently? |
| Security and privacy integration | Are relevant specialist reviews embedded in the lifecycle? |
| Supplier governance | Are third-party AI dependencies assessed and monitored? |
| Lifecycle controls | Are governance gates integrated into delivery and change processes? |
| Exceptions | Are deviations controlled, reviewed, and closed? |
| Monitoring and reporting | Are metrics meaningful and linked to management action? |
| Assurance | Is governance independently evaluated where appropriate? |
| Continuous improvement | Are lessons learned incorporated into policies and controls? |

#### 55.2 Maturity scoring precautions

Maturity scores should be supported by evidence rather than self-assessment alone.

A high score in documentation does not compensate for ineffective operational controls. Organizations should therefore evaluate design, implementation, and operating effectiveness separately.

The assessment should also distinguish between:

- A control that does not exist.
- A control that exists but is not consistently implemented.
- A control that operates but lacks reliable evidence.
- A control that is implemented and evidenced but not independently tested.
- A control whose effectiveness has been tested and improved over time.

The objective is to identify prioritized improvements, not to produce an impressive maturity score without meaningful operational change.

### 57. Governance Implementation Roadmap

A phased implementation approach allows the organization to establish authority and accountability first, then progressively integrate governance into operational processes.

The roadmap below is illustrative. Actual duration depends on organizational complexity, the existing control environment, the number of AI systems, and available resources.

| Phase | Indicative Period | Priority Activities | Exit Criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Establish Authority | Weeks 1–4 | Approve charter, appoint sponsor, identify governance lead, define initial authority | Governance mandate approved |
| Phase 2 — Define Accountability | Weeks 3–6 | Confirm roles, establish RACI, define escalation and decision rights | Accountabilities and authorities documented |
| Phase 3 — Establish Baseline | Weeks 4–8 | Identify AI systems, inventory current governance processes, identify major gaps | Initial AI inventory and gap assessment available |
| Phase 4 — Implement Core Controls | Weeks 6–12 | Introduce risk assessment, security review, approval, and exception workflows | Core governance workflows operational |
| Phase 5 — Integrate Delivery | Weeks 9–16 | Embed gates into procurement, project delivery, architecture, and change management | Governance integrated into priority workflows |

Phases may overlap. High-risk AI systems and material control gaps should receive priority rather than waiting for completion of the entire roadmap.

#### 57.1 First 30-day priorities

During the first month, the organization should concentrate on establishing a credible minimum governance structure.

Recommended priorities:

1. Appoint an executive sponsor and AI Governance Lead.
2. Identify the current and anticipated AI use cases.
3. Define the charter's initial scope.
4. Establish the governance committee or an appropriate equivalent.
5. Approve an initial role catalogue and RACI.

9. Identify existing policies and procedures that require alignment.

The first 30 days should establish ownership and decision authority, not attempt to perfect every technical control.

### 58. Practical Scenario — Resolving a Governance Dispute

**Scenario:** A business unit wants to deploy an AI agent that can retrieve customer information from a CRM platform and execute selected account-management actions.

The business owner believes the project is ready. The AI Engineering Lead confirms that functional testing has passed. However, the AI Security Lead identifies an unresolved weakness in authorization boundaries that could allow the agent to access records beyond the approved customer context.

The project team argues that the business deadline is important and proposes deploying the system while the weakness is investigated.

#### 58.1 Governance analysis

The disagreement involves several distinct decisions:

- Whether the business use case remains justified.
- Whether the identified weakness is material.
- Whether the proposed deployment meets mandatory security requirements.
- Whether compensating controls are sufficient.
- Who has authority to accept the residual risk.
- Whether the deployment can be approved while remediation remains outstanding.

The Business Owner's sponsorship does not establish authority to override a security requirement.

Similarly, the AI Security Lead's technical finding does not necessarily confer authority to accept business risk or make every deployment decision. The actual decision rights must follow the charter and delegated authority matrix.

#### 58.2 Required workflow

| Step | Action | Accountable Function |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Record the finding and its potential impact | AI Security Lead or designated finding owner |
| 2 | Assess the exposure and determine whether immediate restrictions are needed | Designated Security and Operational Authorities |
| 3 | Document affected permissions, data, tools, and execution paths | AI Engineering and Security Architecture |
| 4 | Determine whether the weakness violates a mandatory requirement | Relevant Control Owner and appropriate Compliance or Legal function, where needed |
| 5 | Develop remediation or evaluate permitted compensating controls | AI Engineering and Security |
| 6 | Reassess residual risk and document the recommendation | Business Risk Owner with relevant specialists |
| 7 | Obtain the decision from the authority designated by the charter | Authorized Decision-Maker |
| 8 | Record the decision, conditions, and rationale | AI Governance Function or designated record owner |
| 9 | Verify remediation before any conditional release requirements are treated as satisfied | Designated Validation or Release Authority |
| 10 | Monitor the deployed system according to the approved conditions | AI System Owner and operational teams |

#### 58.3 Possible outcomes

**Outcome A — Deployment rejected pending remediation**

The authorization boundary is a mandatory control, and no permitted alternative can adequately satisfy the requirement. Deployment is blocked until remediation and validation are complete.

**Outcome B — Deployment conditionally authorized**

The charter and applicable requirements permit a defined alternative control. The authorized decision-maker approves deployment subject to documented restrictions, evidence, monitoring, and closure conditions.

**Outcome C — Escalation required**

The residual risk exceeds the decision-maker's authority or the functions disagree on the applicable requirements. The issue is escalated to the designated authority. The system remains subject to the restrictions required by the applicable release and security procedures while the decision is pending.

The governance process should produce a traceable decision rather than resolving the dispute through informal agreement.

### 60. Core Principle — Governance Must Be Authorized, Operational, Measurable, and Verifiable

An effective AI Governance Charter establishes more than a committee structure or a collection of responsibilities. It creates a functioning system of decision authority, accountability, risk management, operational controls, reporting, and independent assurance.

The governance framework must be embedded into the organization's actual decision processes. Its effectiveness should be demonstrated through authorized decisions, completed assessments, controlled exceptions, timely remediation, traceable evidence, and meaningful oversight.

**The central principle is that AI governance is effective only when authority is explicit, responsibilities are operational, decisions are evidence-based, risks are managed within approved limits, and governance performance can be independently evaluated.**

#### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Governance design vs. governance operation | Design defines the intended structure; operation demonstrates that it works in practice |
| Committee oversight vs. operational ownership | The committee oversees or decides within its mandate; operational owners remain responsible for assigned activities |
| KPI vs. KRI | KPIs measure performance against objectives; KRIs indicate changing or accumulating risk |
| Approval vs. assurance | Approval authorizes a decision; assurance evaluates the design or effectiveness of controls |
| Risk acceptance vs. mandatory compliance | Risk acceptance cannot automatically waive legal or other non-waivable obligations |
| Governance exception vs. control remediation | An exception permits a defined deviation where authorized; remediation restores compliance |
| Documentation vs. evidence | Documentation describes intended arrangements; evidence demonstrates what actually occurred |
| Maturity vs. effectiveness | Maturity describes the development of governance capability; effectiveness concerns whether controls achieve their intended outcomes |
| Governance coverage vs. governance quality | Coverage indicates how much activity is included; quality concerns whether governance is appropriate and effective |
| Accountability vs. participation | Participation contributes to a decision; accountability identifies who is answerable for the assigned outcome |

**Practical completion criterion:** The AI Governance RACI and Governance Charter package should be considered ready for organizational adoption only after the charter, committee mandate, RACI, delegated authority matrix, escalation process, decision register, reporting arrangements, and supporting procedures have been reviewed and approved by the appropriate organizational authorities.
