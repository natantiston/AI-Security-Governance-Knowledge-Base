# 20.17 — AI Incident Report and Security Exception Templates

## Part 1 — AI Security Incident Governance, Classification, and Incident Reporting Fundamentals

### 1. Introduction to AI Security Incident Reporting and Security Exceptions

AI systems introduce security risks that extend beyond conventional software vulnerabilities. An AI-enabled application may disclose sensitive information through generated responses, follow malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content, invoke unauthorized tools, misuse delegated privileges, or trigger unintended actions in connected enterprise systems.

When these events occur, organizations need a structured process to identify, report, investigate, contain, remediate, and learn from them.

An **AI security incident report** records a suspected or confirmed event involving an AI system that compromises, or threatens to compromise, confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, safety, regulatory compliance, or business operations.

A **security exception** is a formally authorized deviation from an established security requirement, policy, standard, or control baseline. It documents why the deviation is necessary, what risk it introduces, what compensating controls apply, and who is accountable for accepting the residual risk.

These artifacts serve different governance purposes.

| Dimension | AI Security Incident Report | AI Security Exception |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Document and manage a security event | Govern an approved deviation from a requirement |
| Trigger | Suspected or confirmed incident, near miss, or security event requiring investigation | Inability or justified decision not to satisfy an applicable requirement |
| Timing | Usually created when an event is detected or reported | Normally created before the deviation is implemented |
| Main concern | What happened, why it happened, and how to contain and recover | What requirement is not being met and why the remaining risk is acceptable |
| Typical owner | Incident manager or designated security incident lead | Control owner and designated risk owner |
| Main evidence | Alerts, logs, tool-call traces, system state, investigation records | Risk assessment, justification, compensating controls, approvals |
| Outcome | Containment, remediation, recovery, and lessons learned | Time-bounded authorization, remediation commitment, or rejection |
| Review trigger | New evidence, incident progression, recovery, or post-incident review | Expiry, material change, control failure, or changed risk |
| Governance relationship | Feeds risk registers and control improvements | Records a deliberate and authorized risk decision |

An incident should not be treated as an exception merely because an existing control failed. Similarly, an approved exception does not authorize an incident, conceal a control failure, or waive unrelated obligations.

#### 1.1 Why AI Requires Specialized Incident Reporting

Traditional incident management remains essential for AI systems, but the investigation may need to examine additional elements:

- Model behavior and model version.
- System instructions and policy configuration.
- User prompts and untrusted retrieved content.
- Context assembly and retrieval results.
- Agent memory and state.
- Tool calls and tool arguments.
- Identity and authorization decisions.
- Multi-agent delegation and execution chains.
- Model-provider and third-party service dependencies.
- Generated outputs and downstream actions.
- Human approval decisions.
- Safety and business-rule enforcement.
- Model, prompt, tool, and configuration changes.

For example, a conventional application incident might involve unauthorized access to a customer database. An AI-enabled incident could begin with malicious instructions embedded in a retrieved document, cause an agent to invoke a legitimate integration in an unauthorized way, and ultimately disclose customer information through an external channel.

The investigation must therefore reconstruct not only the technical event but also the sequence of instructions, decisions, permissions, and actions that produced the outcome.

#### 1.2 Incident Reporting and Exception Management as Governance Controls

Incident reporting and exception management are operational governance controls because they provide evidence that the organization can respond to failures and govern deviations.

A mature program should establish:

1. A defined reporting channel.
2. Clear incident classification criteria.
3. An accountable incident management function.
4. Procedures for containment and evidence preservation.
5. AI-specific investigation procedures.
6. Formal security exception criteria.
7. Risk acceptance and approval authority.
8. Remediation tracking.
9. Reporting and escalation requirements.
10. Lessons-learned and control-improvement processes.

The program should integrate with existing security operations, privacy, legal, compliance, business continuity, vendor management, and enterprise risk management functions.

**Core Principle:** AI security incidents require evidence-based investigation of the complete AI-enabled workflow, while security exceptions require explicit and accountable authorization of a defined deviation. Neither process should be used as a substitute for the other.

### 2. Scope and Applicability

The incident reporting and security exception processes should apply to AI systems throughout their lifecycle, including development, testing, deployment, operation, maintenance, and retirement.

The organization should define which systems and events are covered rather than assuming that all AI-related issues belong to the same incident process.

#### 2.1 AI Systems Within Scope

| AI system category | Example | Potential security concern |
|---|---|---|
| Generative AI applications | Enterprise writing assistant | Sensitive information disclosure |
| Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) | Internal knowledge assistant | Unauthorized retrieval or prompt injection |
| AI agents | Agent that executes enterprise workflows | Unauthorized tool execution |
| Multi-agent systems | Agents that delegate tasks to other agents | Privilege propagation and accountability gaps |
| AI-enabled CRM | Customer service assistant | Unauthorized access to customer records |
| AI-enabled e-commerce | Shopping or order-management assistant | Unauthorized transactions or disclosure |
| AI coding assistants | Development copilot | Exposure of source code or insecure generated code |
| AI security operations tools | Security alert investigation agent | Incorrect response actions or alert manipulation |
| Machine learning services | Fraud detection or classification model | Data poisoning or model manipulation |
| Third-party AI services | Externally hosted model API | Provider-side exposure or dependency failure |
| Embedded AI features | AI functionality inside business software | Unclear ownership or inherited configuration risk |
| Autonomous workflow systems | Agent that executes approved business processes | Unintended or excessive actions |

The process should cover systems developed internally, purchased from vendors, hosted by cloud providers, or accessed through external APIs.

#### 2.2 Events Within Scope

AI-related events should be evaluated under the organization's incident classification criteria when they involve actual or suspected security, privacy, safety, operational, or compliance consequences.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized disclosure of personal or confidential information.
- Prompt injection leading to an unauthorized action.
- Agent execution beyond approved permissions.
- Unauthorized changes to enterprise records.
- Compromise of an agent's service identity or credentials.
- Manipulation of agent memory or persistent state.
- Unauthorized retrieval from restricted information sources.
- Malicious or compromised AI components.
- Unintended execution of code or external commands.
- Security-control bypass in an AI-enabled workflow.
- Compromise of a model, tool, or integration.
- Loss of critical AI service availability.
- Abuse of the system to generate prohibited or harmful operational outputs.
- Failure of a mandatory human-approval control.
- A near miss that reveals a credible pathway to a significant incident.

Not every incorrect or low-quality model response constitutes a security incident. The organization should distinguish ordinary quality defects from events that threaten security, privacy, safety, or other defined incident criteria.

#### 2.3 Scope of Security Exceptions

Security exceptions may be required when an AI system cannot meet an established requirement, including:

- A mandatory logging control cannot be implemented in its standard form.
- A legacy integration does not support the required authentication mechanism.
- A vendor limitation prevents compliance with an internal technical standard.
- A temporary deployment requires a narrowly bounded alternative control.
- A particular monitoring capability is unavailable during a controlled migration.
- A documented business requirement conflicts with a non-mandatory internal security baseline.

These examples do not automatically justify approval. Each exception must be evaluated against the applicable policy, legal obligations, contractual requirements, risk tolerance, and available alternatives.

An exception should not be used where the organization has no authority to waive the requirement.

#### 2.4 Scope Boundaries and Related Processes

The incident report and exception record should connect to related organizational processes without replacing them.

| Related process | Relationship |
|---|---|
| Cybersecurity incident response | Coordinates triage, containment, investigation, eradication, and recovery |
| Privacy incident management | Evaluates personal-data breaches and related notification obligations |
| AI governance | Assesses AI-specific impacts, accountability, and lifecycle implications |
| Enterprise risk management | Records material risks, treatment decisions, and residual exposure |
| Vulnerability management | Tracks technical weaknesses identified during an incident or assessment |
| Change management | Governs approved changes resulting from remediation or exceptions |
| Third-party risk management | Coordinates incidents involving AI providers, model hosts, or integration vendors |
| Business continuity | Addresses disruption to critical AI-enabled processes |
| Legal and compliance | Evaluates legal, regulatory, contractual, and preservation requirements |
| Internal audit | Independently evaluates process design and operating effectiveness |

A single event may activate several of these processes simultaneously. The organization should define coordination and escalation rules to avoid gaps or contradictory decisions.

**Core Principle:** Scope should be based on the system's capabilities, dependencies, data, and potential impact—not merely on whether a product is marketed as an AI system.

### 3. AI Security Incident Classification and Severity

Incident classification determines how quickly an event must be escalated, which teams must participate, what containment measures may be required, and who can authorize recovery.

AI incidents should be classified using both conventional cybersecurity criteria and AI-specific considerations. The fact that a model produced an unexpected output does not, by itself, determine incident severity. The relevant question is what occurred, what could occur next, and what impact the event may have.

#### 3.1 Incident Classification Categories

| Category | Description | Illustrative example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality breach | Sensitive information is disclosed to an unauthorized party | An agent returns restricted customer records to an unauthorized user |
| Integrity compromise | Data, instructions, configurations, or business records are altered without authorization | An agent modifies account details without required approval |
| Availability incident | An AI service or critical dependency becomes unavailable | An agent service is disrupted by resource exhaustion |
| Identity and access incident | Credentials, identities, or permissions are abused | An attacker uses a compromised agent credential |
| Prompt or context manipulation | Malicious instructions influence the system's behavior | Retrieved content causes an agent to attempt an unauthorized action |
| Tool execution incident | A connected tool performs an unauthorized or unsafe operation | An agent issues an unapproved refund through a business API |
| Data governance incident | Data is used, retained, retrieved, or disclosed contrary to requirements | An agent retrieves data across a restricted access boundary |
| Model or component compromise | A model, dependency, or AI component is compromised or maliciously modified | A compromised package changes agent execution behavior |
| Safety incident | AI behavior contributes to a potential or actual safety impact | An agent controlling a physical process initiates an unsafe command |
| Governance or compliance incident | A mandatory control or obligation is breached | A system is operated without a required approval |
| Third-party AI incident | An external AI service or provider dependency experiences a security event | A provider reports compromise affecting an integrated service |
| Near miss | A credible pathway to harm is identified before material impact occurs | A blocked tool call reveals a prompt-injection attack path |

Classification categories may overlap. For example, an agent using a compromised service identity to export customer data may constitute an identity incident, a confidentiality incident, and a potential privacy breach.

#### 3.2 Severity Assessment Dimensions

Severity should consider several dimensions rather than relying on a single technical indicator.

| Dimension | Questions to evaluate |
|---|---|
| Business impact | Are critical business processes affected? |
| Data sensitivity | Does the event involve personal, confidential, regulated, or restricted information? |
| Authorization | Did the agent or user exceed approved permissions? |
| Action consequence | Were financial, legal, customer, or operational actions executed? |
| Scope | How many users, records, agents, systems, or business units may be affected? |
| Persistence | Can the event recur through memory, configuration, credentials, or scheduled execution? |
| Propagation | Can the event spread to other tools, agents, or connected systems? |
| Reversibility | Can the resulting actions be reversed safely and completely? |
| Detectability | Can the organization reliably identify the full scope of the event? |
| Regulatory exposure | Are notification, reporting, or other legal obligations potentially triggered? |
| Safety | Could the event cause physical harm or a material safety impact? |
| Control failure | Did a mandatory safeguard fail, or was it bypassed? |

The assessment should consider both confirmed impact and credible potential impact. Lack of evidence is not proof that no impact occurred.

#### 3.3 Illustrative Severity Levels

The following model is an example that should be adapted to the organization's incident management policy.

| Severity | General description | Example | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEV-1 — Critical | Severe or potentially catastrophic impact; active compromise of critical capabilities or sensitive information | An autonomous agent is performing unauthorized privileged actions across production systems | Immediate escalation, containment, and executive notification under policy |
| SEV-2 — High | Significant compromise or credible exposure with material business consequences | Restricted customer data is disclosed through an AI integration | Urgent investigation, containment, and relevant stakeholder escalation |
| SEV-3 — Moderate | Limited or contained impact requiring formal investigation and remediation | An agent attempts an unauthorized record modification that is blocked but reveals a control weakness | Timely investigation, corrective action, and documented follow-up |
| SEV-4 — Low | Limited impact with no established material compromise | A low-impact policy violation is detected and safely contained | Standard incident handling and control improvement |
| Observation / Near Miss | A suspicious event or control failure has not produced confirmed material impact | A prompt-injection test triggers an unauthorized tool request that is denied | Record, evaluate, and escalate according to risk and incident criteria |

These labels are illustrative. The organization must establish its own severity definitions, escalation timelines, and response commitments.

#### 3.4 AI-Specific Escalation Rules

The following conditions should prompt urgent evaluation and may justify immediate containment:

- An agent executes an unauthorized privileged action.
- Sensitive information is confirmed or credibly suspected to have reached an unauthorized recipient.
- A human-approval gate is bypassed.
- An agent's credentials or privileged identity are compromised.
- A tool can execute destructive or irreversible operations outside its approved scope.
- An agent appears to be propagating unauthorized instructions or actions across multiple systems.
- A safety-critical AI system behaves outside approved operating boundaries.
- A provider compromise may affect the confidentiality or integrity of the organization's AI environment.
- The organization cannot establish what actions an agent executed because critical logs are missing.

An initial severity rating is provisional. It should be updated as evidence emerges.

#### 3.5 Classification Decision Record

For every reportable event, capture:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Incident ID | Unique incident reference |
| Detection date and time | When the event was first detected |
| Reporting date and time | When the event was reported |
| Affected system | Agent, model, application, or service |
| Environment | Development, testing, staging, or production |
| Incident category | One or more applicable categories |
| Initial severity | Provisional rating |
| Confirmed impact | Established consequences |
| Potential impact | Credible consequences still under investigation |
| Scope | Known affected users, records, and systems |
| Containment status | Whether activity has been stopped or restricted |
| Escalation status | Stakeholders notified under the applicable procedure |
| Classification rationale | Evidence supporting the selected category and severity |
| Review owner | Person responsible for reassessment |

**Core Principle:** Incident severity should reflect actual and credible potential consequences, not simply the apparent sophistication of the attack or the unusualness of the model's behavior.

### 4. AI Security Incident Reporting Roles and Responsibilities

An incident reporting process requires clear accountability. AI incidents can span cybersecurity, AI governance, privacy, business operations, application engineering, and external providers. Without defined responsibilities, the investigation may become fragmented, particularly when the model, agent runtime, and downstream tools are operated by different teams.

The organization should establish a responsibility model that integrates with its existing incident response structure.

#### 4.1 Key Roles

| Role | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Reporter | Reports a suspected incident or near miss through an approved channel |
| AI system owner | Provides system context and coordinates system-specific decisions |
| Incident manager | Coordinates the response, timeline, priorities, and stakeholder communication |
| Security operations team | Detects, triages, investigates, and monitors security events |
| AI engineering team | Provides technical expertise on model integration, orchestration, prompts, memory, and tool execution |
| Application or platform owner | Investigates affected applications and downstream services |
| Security architect | Evaluates architecture, trust boundaries, and control weaknesses |
| Privacy or data protection function | Evaluates personal-data implications and applicable privacy obligations |
| Legal and compliance | Advises on legal, regulatory, contractual, and evidence-preservation requirements |
| Business owner | Assesses operational consequences and business priorities |
| Risk owner | Evaluates material residual risk and treatment options |
| Vendor manager | Coordinates with relevant third-party AI or technology providers |
| Communications function | Coordinates authorized external or internal communications where required |
| Internal audit | Provides independent assurance when appropriate; does not ordinarily direct the operational response |

One individual may hold multiple roles in a smaller organization, but the allocation of decision authority must remain explicit.

#### 4.2 Incident Response RACI

RACI identifies who is **Responsible**, **Accountable**, **Consulted**, and **Informed**.

| Activity | Incident manager | Security operations | AI engineering | Business owner | Privacy/legal | Risk owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial triage | A | R | C | I | C | I |
| Severity classification | A | R | C | C | C | I |
| Technical investigation | A | R | R | I | C | I |
| Agent containment | A | R | R | C | C | I |
| Business impact analysis | C | C | C | A/R | C | I |
| Privacy impact evaluation | C | C | C | I | A/R | I |
| Remediation planning | A | R | R | C | C | C |
| Recovery recommendation | A | R | R | C | C | I |
| Residual-risk acceptance | C | C | C | C | C | A/R |
| Post-incident review | A/R | R | R | C | C | C |

**Note:** The table is an illustrative allocation, not a universal assignment of authority. In particular, privacy notification decisions, operational shutdown authority, and residual-risk acceptance must follow the organization's formal governance model and applicable law.

#### 4.3 Reporting Channels

The organization should provide clear reporting channels for employees, contractors, service providers, and other authorized stakeholders.

Possible channels include:

- Security operations center (SOC).
- Corporate incident reporting portal.
- Dedicated security incident email address.
- IT service management platform.
- AI governance or responsible AI reporting channel.
- Emergency contact route for critical incidents.
- Third-party security notification channel.

Reports should be routed to an accountable team even when the reporter cannot determine whether the event is an AI incident, a privacy incident, or a conventional cybersecurity event.

#### 4.4 Minimum Reporting Expectations

A report should capture the information reasonably available at the time:

- What happened or was observed.
- When it occurred and when it was detected.
- Which AI system or agent was involved.
- Which users, business processes, or systems may be affected.
- What information or actions may be involved.
- Whether the activity is ongoing.
- What evidence is available.
- What containment actions have already been taken.
- Who reported the event and how they can be contacted.

Reporters should not be required to prove an incident before submitting a credible concern. Initial reporting exists to enable investigation, not to assign blame.

**Core Principle:** Incident response requires one clearly accountable coordination function, supported by the technical and governance specialists needed to investigate the complete AI-enabled workflow.

### 5. AI Security Incident Intake and Initial Triage Template

The incident intake record is the initial structured capture of a suspected event. It should be concise enough to complete quickly while preserving sufficient information for effective triage.

The intake process must distinguish facts from assumptions. In particular, it should record what has been observed, what remains unknown, and what requires verification.

#### 5.1 Incident Intake Form

| Field | Information to record |
|---|---|
| Incident ID | Unique identifier generated by the incident management process |
| Reported by | Name or authorized reporting identity |
| Reporter contact | Approved contact details |
| Date and time reported | Timestamp with time zone |
| Date and time detected | Timestamp with time zone, if known |
| Event start time | Estimated start, if known |
| AI system or agent | System name and inventory identifier |
| Model or provider | Model/service identifier, if known |
| Agent version | Application, prompt, configuration, or release version |
| Environment | Development, testing, staging, or production |
| Event category | Suspected incident type |
| Event description | Factual summary of what was observed |
| Affected data | Data categories potentially involved |
| Affected tools | APIs, connectors, applications, or services |
| Actions performed | Known tool calls or downstream actions |
| Current status | Ongoing, contained, or unknown |
| Initial severity | Provisional severity under organizational criteria |
| Immediate actions | Actions already taken |
| Evidence references | Log identifiers, alert IDs, timestamps, or approved evidence locations |
| Known impact | Confirmed consequences |
| Potential impact | Plausible consequences under investigation |
| Additional support | Teams or specialists required |
| Initial triage owner | Person or team responsible for follow-up |

#### 5.2 Example: Unauthorized Tool Execution Attempt

**Scenario:** An enterprise AI agent retrieves a document containing malicious instructions. The agent attempts to invoke a customer-management API to export records. The authorization gateway blocks the request, and the export does not occur.

| Intake field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Incident ID | AI-INC-2026-0042 |
| AI system | Customer Operations Agent |
| Environment | Production |
| Event category | Prompt/context manipulation and unauthorized tool execution attempt |
| Detection source | Security monitoring alert |
| Event description | Retrieved document contained instructions that led the agent to request an unapproved export action |
| Known action | Export tool invocation attempted |
| Control result | Authorization gateway denied the request |
| Confirmed impact | No successful export identified at initial triage |
| Potential impact | Unauthorized access or disclosure if the authorization control failed |
| Initial severity | Provisional; determined under the organization's severity criteria |
| Immediate action | Preserve relevant logs and review the agent's subsequent execution |
| Evidence | Agent execution trace, gateway denial event, retrieval record, and relevant timestamps |
| Initial owner | Security incident response |

This example should not be classified as harmless solely because the gateway blocked the first request. Investigators should determine whether the agent made additional attempts, invoked alternative tools, accessed restricted information through another route, or altered downstream state.

#### 5.3 Initial Triage Questions

The triage team should answer the following questions as early as possible:

**System identification**
- Which agent, model, application, and environment are involved?
- Is the affected system business-critical?
- Does the system have autonomous execution or delegation capabilities?

**Activity and impact**
- What happened?
- Is the activity still ongoing?
- Were unauthorized actions completed or merely attempted?
- Was sensitive information accessed, processed, or disclosed?
- Are downstream systems affected?

**Security boundaries**
- Did the event involve an identity or permission failure?
- Did a tool execute beyond its authorized scope?
- Did an approval control fail or get bypassed?
- Could the same attack path affect other agents or systems?

**Containment**
- Can the activity be stopped safely?
- Can affected tools be disabled without causing unacceptable business disruption?
- Are credentials or tokens potentially compromised?
- Is additional monitoring required?

**Governance**
- Does the event require privacy, legal, compliance, or vendor escalation?
- Are there applicable reporting deadlines?
- Must evidence be preserved for further investigation?
- Does the event indicate a control failure or a previously accepted risk?

#### 5.4 Triage Record and Handover

The triage owner should record:

1. The initial classification and its rationale.
2. The confirmed facts and unresolved questions.
3. The current scope of potentially affected systems and data.
4. The immediate containment decision.
5. The evidence that must be preserved.
6. The teams notified and the time of notification.
7. The next investigation actions and accountable owners.
8. The next required severity review.

If the event is outside the incident team's remit, the handover should identify the receiving team and confirm that responsibility has been accepted. A referral without an accountable recipient should not be treated as a completed handover.

**Core Principle:** The intake process must capture sufficient facts to initiate a controlled investigation without delaying reporting until the cause, impact, or responsible party is fully understood.

### 6. AI Incident Evidence Preservation and Chain of Custody

Evidence preservation is essential for understanding an AI incident, validating findings, supporting remediation, and satisfying applicable audit or legal requirements.

AI systems can generate large volumes of transient context and execution data. Relevant evidence may be distributed across model-provider logs, agent orchestration services, retrieval systems, tool gateways, identity platforms, databases, and downstream applications.

The organization must preserve enough evidence to reconstruct the event while protecting sensitive information and respecting applicable retention and privacy requirements.

#### 6.1 Evidence Categories

| Evidence category | Examples | Investigative purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Identity evidence | Authentication logs, token issuance, role assignments | Determine which identity acted and what authority it possessed |
| Prompt and instruction evidence | System instructions, user prompts, retrieved instructions | Reconstruct the instruction context, subject to appropriate data handling |
| Model execution evidence | Model version, request IDs, timestamps, response metadata | Correlate model interactions and identify configuration changes |
| Tool execution evidence | Tool names, arguments, authorization decisions, results | Establish which actions were requested, permitted, blocked, or completed |
| Data access evidence | Retrieval records, database queries, access-control decisions | Determine whether sensitive information was accessed |
| Memory and state evidence | Relevant state transitions, memory writes, session references | Investigate persistence and cross-session effects |
| Infrastructure evidence | Application logs, network telemetry, runtime events | Identify infrastructure-level compromise or abnormal activity |
| Configuration evidence | Prompt versions, tool definitions, policy rules, deployment configuration | Establish the system's configuration at the time |
| Change records | Release tickets, model changes, configuration changes | Determine whether a recent change contributed |
| Monitoring evidence | Alerts, detections, dashboards, incident timestamps | Reconstruct detection and escalation |
| Human decision evidence | Approval records, operator actions, override records | Determine whether required human controls operated |
| Downstream evidence | Transaction records, messages sent, data modifications | Establish actual business consequences |

The exact evidence set should be proportionate to incident severity, system architecture, and applicable obligations.

#### 6.2 Evidence Preservation Principles

**Preserve the original where feasible.** Avoid changing the original evidence while investigating it. If collection requires transformation, retain the original where lawful and practical and document the transformation.

**Record timestamps consistently.** Include time zones and account for clock differences between systems.

**Correlate events.** Use request IDs, trace IDs, session IDs, tool-call IDs, transaction IDs, and other available identifiers to connect evidence across services.

**Restrict access.** Incident evidence may contain credentials, personal data, confidential prompts, customer records, or proprietary information.

**Maintain integrity.** Use approved storage controls and integrity-verification methods appropriate to the evidence type.

**Document collection.** Record who collected the evidence, when it was collected, from which source, and how it was handled.

**Minimize unnecessary data.** Preserve information necessary for investigation while avoiding uncontrolled replication of sensitive information.

**Respect retention requirements.** Coordinate evidence retention with legal, privacy, security, and records-management requirements.

#### 6.3 Chain-of-Custody Record

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Evidence ID | Unique identifier |
| Incident ID | Related incident reference |
| Evidence description | What the item contains |
| Source system | System or service from which it was obtained |
| Collection date and time | Timestamp with time zone |
| Collector | Authorized individual or process |
| Collection method | Approved method used |
| Original location | Source location or record identifier |
| Storage location | Approved evidence repository |
| Integrity reference | Hash or other integrity-verification information, where appropriate |
| Access history | Relevant access or transfer records |
| Transformations | Redaction, normalization, export, or conversion performed |
| Transfer details | Sender, recipient, date, and purpose |
| Retention requirement | Applicable preservation period or hold |
| Disposal authorization | Required authorization when retention ends |

A hash can help detect changes to a collected file, but it does not independently prove that the file was authentic before collection or that the collection process was complete. Integrity measures must be combined with source validation and documented handling.

#### 6.4 AI-Specific Evidence Challenges

| Challenge | Risk | Recommended response |
|---|---|---|
| Provider logs are unavailable | Incomplete reconstruction of model interactions | Record provider limitations and preserve available local telemetry |
| Context is truncated | Important instructions or retrieved content may be missing | Preserve relevant context metadata and available source references |
| Model outputs are nondeterministic | The same input may not reproduce the original behavior | Preserve original execution evidence rather than relying solely on replay |
| Memory changes after the event | Evidence may be overwritten or altered | Preserve relevant state snapshots or approved records promptly |
| Tool calls execute asynchronously | The initial request may not show the final outcome | Correlate execution IDs and downstream transaction records |
| Logs contain sensitive information | Evidence collection may create additional exposure | Apply access controls, minimization, and approved redaction |
| Multiple agents are involved | Accountability and event order may be unclear | Correlate parent-child execution relationships and delegated identities |
| Provider retention is limited | Evidence may disappear before collection | Escalate promptly and use approved retention arrangements where available |

#### 6.5 Evidence Handling Restrictions

Investigators should not:
- Copy sensitive evidence into unapproved AI services.
- Share credentials or access tokens in incident tickets.
- Modify production data merely to reproduce an incident without authorization.
- Rely on a generated explanation as proof of what the system actually did.
- Assume that a model's account of its internal reasoning is a complete or authoritative execution record.
- Delete or overwrite potentially relevant logs without authorization.
- Preserve personal or confidential data indefinitely without a valid basis.

**Core Principle:** Incident conclusions must be supported by preserved, attributable, and appropriately protected evidence—not solely by model-generated explanations or retrospective assumptions.

### 7. AI Security Incident Containment and Immediate Response Decisions

Containment aims to prevent further harm while preserving the ability to investigate and recover. In AI systems, containment may require restricting a specific tool, revoking an identity, disabling a workflow, isolating an agent, or suspending the entire service.

The appropriate response depends on the active threat, the agent's capabilities, the potential consequences of continued operation, and the operational impact of containment.

#### 7.1 Containment Options

| Option | Appropriate use | Important consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Disable a specific tool | A particular integration is being abused | Verify that alternative tools cannot achieve the same prohibited action |
| Revoke or rotate credentials | Credentials may be compromised or overused | Identify dependent workflows and invalidate relevant sessions or tokens |
| Restrict permissions | Agent authority exceeds approved requirements | Confirm that the new permissions are enforced outside model instructions |
| Suspend autonomous execution | Continued execution may cause material harm | Determine whether queued or asynchronous tasks remain active |
| Require human approval | Consequential actions need additional oversight | Ensure approval cannot be bypassed through alternate execution paths |
| Isolate the runtime | The agent or its environment may be compromised | Preserve necessary telemetry and avoid unintended effects on dependent services |
| Disable retrieval or memory writes | Retrieved content or persistent state may be contributing to the event | Assess whether read-only operation remains safe |
| Block external network access | External communication is suspected to be involved | Identify legitimate dependencies and preserve evidence |
| Suspend the agent | Risk cannot be adequately contained through narrower restrictions | Coordinate business continuity and recovery arrangements |
| Disable a downstream transaction path | Unauthorized business actions are occurring | Verify whether already submitted transactions require separate cancellation or recovery |

Containment should target the smallest practical component that adequately controls the risk. However, minimizing disruption must not take precedence over preventing serious harm.

#### 7.2 Containment Decision Matrix

| Situation | Initial response consideration | Required verification |
|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized action blocked before execution | Restrict the affected action path if needed and investigate the attempted execution | Confirm no alternative tool or route completed the action |
| Unauthorized action completed | Stop further activity and assess reversal or recovery | Establish the downstream state and potential impact |
| Suspected credential compromise | Revoke or rotate affected credentials through approved procedures | Confirm old credentials and sessions are no longer usable |
| Sensitive information may have been disclosed | Prevent further disclosure and preserve relevant evidence | Determine what was exposed, to whom, and when |
| Prompt injection causes repeated attempts | Restrict affected content sources or tool access while investigating | Verify the agent cannot bypass restrictions through alternate paths |
| Agent behavior is unexplained and potentially dangerous | Suspend affected capabilities or the service according to severity | Establish a safe operating state before resumption |
| Third-party provider reports compromise | Apply provider-specific contingency and containment procedures | Determine whether the organization's data, credentials, or integration were affected |

These are decision aids, not automatic rules. Incident responders should follow approved emergency authority, severity thresholds, and safety requirements.

#### 7.3 Safe Containment of Autonomous and Asynchronous Workflows

Stopping the agent interface does not necessarily stop every action initiated by the agent.

The investigation should consider:
- Queued jobs.
- Scheduled tasks.
- Background workers.
- Long-running tool executions.
- Child agents.
- Webhooks and callbacks.
- Retry mechanisms.
- Previously issued credentials or tokens.
- Transactions submitted to downstream systems.
- External messages or requests already sent.

A robust containment plan should identify where execution can continue after the primary agent is disabled.

#### 7.4 Emergency Containment Checklist

- [ ] Incident severity and active threat have been evaluated.
- [ ] The authorized containment decision-maker has been identified.
- [ ] Relevant tool execution has been restricted or stopped.
- [ ] Affected credentials have been assessed for revocation or rotation.
- [ ] Queued and asynchronous actions have been identified.
- [ ] Downstream systems have been checked for completed actions.
- [ ] Relevant evidence has been preserved.
- [ ] Monitoring has been increased where appropriate.
- [ ] Business, privacy, legal, and vendor stakeholders have been notified as required.
- [ ] Containment actions and their timestamps have been documented.
- [ ] Criteria for safe recovery have been established.

**Core Principle:** Containment must stop the harmful execution path, including asynchronous and delegated activity, rather than merely disabling the visible interface through which the agent was accessed.

AI incidents may require coordination among security operations, AI engineering, business operations, privacy, legal, compliance, third-party providers, and executive leadership.

- Identify decisions that require authorization.

- Meet applicable contractual and legal communication obligations.

### 9. AI Security Exception Governance and Approval Fundamentals

An AI security exception documents a controlled departure from an established security requirement. It allows the organization to evaluate a specific deviation transparently rather than permitting informal, undocumented, or indefinite non-compliance.

Exceptions are not a mechanism for making an unsafe system acceptable by administrative approval alone. They require a justified business need, a documented risk assessment, appropriate compensating controls, an authorized decision, and ongoing oversight.

#### 9.1 Common AI Security Exception Scenarios

| Scenario | Potential exception request | Key considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Legacy integration | Temporary use of an approved alternative authentication pattern | Credential protection, scope restriction, migration plan |
| Logging limitation | Alternative telemetry where a standard logging mechanism is unavailable | Investigation capability, sensitive-data exposure, evidence retention |
| Provider limitation | Compensating controls for a documented provider capability gap | Contractual requirements, data exposure, provider assurance |
| Restricted deployment window | Temporary deviation from a standard rollout control | Deployment risk, rollback capability, monitoring |
| Unsupported technical control | Alternative control for a system that cannot implement the standard mechanism | Equivalent risk reduction, residual exposure |
| Temporary environment constraint | Time-bounded deviation during a controlled migration | Environment isolation, data restrictions, expiry conditions |
| Legacy model integration | Alternative validation or monitoring control | Model limitations, use-case risk, safe operating boundaries |

These examples are not pre-approved exceptions. Each request must be assessed against the specific requirement and the authority to grant a deviation.

#### 9.2 Exception Categories

Organizations may classify exceptions according to the type of requirement involved.

| Category | Scope |
|---|---|
| Identity and access | Authentication, authorization, privileged access, or credential management |
| Data protection | Classification, encryption, retention, masking, or access restrictions |
| Logging and monitoring | Telemetry, alerting, traceability, or evidence retention |
| Architecture | Network isolation, segmentation, approved deployment patterns |
| Model and application security | Validation, testing, configuration, or runtime controls |
| Third-party assurance | Provider requirements, contractual controls, or external dependencies |
| Change management | Testing, approval, release, or rollback requirements |
| Operational resilience | Recovery, redundancy, containment, or continuity controls |
| AI-specific governance | Agent autonomy, human oversight, model changes, or use-case restrictions |

#### 9.3 Exception Request Minimum Information

A formal exception request should include:

- Exception ID.
- Requesting business unit.
- AI system and version.
- Affected environment and capabilities.
- Exact requirement being waived or modified.
- Policy or standard reference.
- Business justification.
- Technical reason compliance is not currently feasible.
- Alternatives considered.
- Inherent and residual risk.
- Potential business and regulatory impact.
- Proposed compensating controls.
- Control owners.
- Remediation plan, if applicable.
- Requested start and end dates.
- Required approval authorities.
- Monitoring and review requirements.
- Conditions that invalidate the exception.

The request should be sufficiently specific that a reviewer can determine precisely what is being authorized.

An exception should be rejected when the deviation is not permitted, the risk exceeds the approver's authority, or compensating controls cannot adequately address the exposure.

#### 9.5 Exception Versus Risk Acceptance

These concepts are related but not identical.

| Concept | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Security exception | Authorizes a defined deviation from an applicable requirement, where such authorization is permitted |
| Risk acceptance | Records an authorized decision to retain a specific residual risk |
| Compensating control | Provides an alternative safeguard intended to reduce exposure |
| Remediation plan | Defines how the deficiency will be corrected |
| Policy waiver | Formally permits deviation from a policy requirement when the policy's governance process allows it |
| Regulatory exemption | A legal or regulatory mechanism whose availability and conditions depend on the applicable legal framework |

An exception often requires risk acceptance, but not every risk acceptance is an exception. A residual risk may exist even when the system complies with the applicable requirements.

Conversely, a security exception may be required to authorize a technical deviation even when alternative controls substantially reduce the associated risk.

#### 9.6 Non-Negotiable Boundaries

The organization should identify requirements that cannot be waived through its internal exception process. These may include legal prohibitions, mandatory regulatory obligations, contractual commitments, safety requirements, and controls explicitly designated as non-waivable by organizational policy.

A management approval cannot independently remove an obligation imposed by law or by a binding external requirement.

**Core Principle:** A security exception is a controlled, documented, and authorized deviation—not an informal permission to operate indefinitely with a known weakness.

1. The immediate threat has been contained or otherwise controlled.

## Part 2 — AI Security Incident Investigation, Root Cause Analysis, and Impact Assessment

### 11. AI Security Incident Investigation Methodology

AI security incident investigation is the structured process of determining what happened, how it happened, which systems and data were affected, whether the threat remains active, and what corrective actions are necessary.

Unlike conventional application investigations, AI investigations may need to reconstruct a sequence involving user instructions, retrieved content, model outputs, orchestration logic, tool authorization, agent memory, delegated tasks, and downstream transactions.

The investigator must distinguish between the model's generated explanations and independently verifiable evidence of system behavior. A model-generated explanation may help formulate a hypothesis, but it should not be treated as authoritative proof of why an event occurred.

#### 11.1 Investigation Objectives

Every investigation should address six primary objectives:

1. **Establish the facts:** Determine what happened, when it happened, and how it was detected.
2. **Determine the scope:** Identify affected agents, users, data, tools, identities, and downstream systems.
3. **Establish the execution sequence:** Reconstruct the relevant instructions, decisions, authorization checks, and actions.
4. **Determine the cause:** Identify technical, architectural, operational, or governance weaknesses that contributed to the event.
5. **Evaluate the impact:** Determine actual consequences and credible potential consequences.
6. **Define corrective action:** Establish containment, remediation, recovery, and longer-term control improvements.

The investigation should also determine whether the event represents an isolated failure or evidence of a systemic weakness affecting other AI systems.

#### 11.2 Investigation Phases

| Phase | Principal activities | Required output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Preparation | Confirm authorization, scope, severity, and investigation ownership | Investigation plan |
| 2. Evidence preservation | Secure relevant logs, traces, configurations, and downstream records | Evidence register |
| 3. Timeline reconstruction | Correlate timestamps, identities, prompts, tool calls, and transactions | Event timeline |
| 4. Scope analysis | Identify affected systems, records, users, and related execution paths | Impacted asset register |
| 5. Technical analysis | Examine the model integration, orchestration, controls, and execution behavior | Technical findings |
| 6. Root cause analysis | Identify the underlying causes and contributing factors | Root cause analysis |
| 7. Impact assessment | Evaluate confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, safety, and business effects | Impact assessment |
| 8. Remediation planning | Define corrective actions, owners, priorities, and deadlines | Remediation plan |
| 9. Validation | Test corrections and verify that the relevant attack path is controlled | Validation results |
| 10. Closure review | Confirm evidence, decisions, notifications, and follow-up actions | Investigation conclusion |

These phases can overlap during urgent incidents. Evidence preservation and containment should not be delayed while waiting for the investigation plan to be finalized.

#### 11.3 Investigation Questions by Technical Layer

| Layer | Investigation questions |
|---|---|
| User interface | What did the user submit, and what did the user see? |
| Prompt and instruction layer | Which instructions and policies were active? |
| Retrieval layer | What documents or external content entered the context? |
| Model layer | Which model and configuration were used, and what output was recorded? |
| Orchestration layer | How were decisions, tool calls, retries, and delegated tasks coordinated? |
| Identity layer | Which identities and permissions were used? |
| Tool layer | What actions were requested, authorized, denied, or completed? |
| Memory layer | Was information written to or retrieved from persistent state? |
| Data layer | Which records were accessed, modified, transmitted, or deleted? |
| Infrastructure layer | Were runtime, network, or platform controls involved? |
| Monitoring layer | Which events were detected, and which were not detected? |
| Governance layer | Were approval, change-management, and risk-management requirements followed? |

The investigation should follow the actual execution path rather than assume that the event originated in the model.

#### 11.4 Investigation Principles

- Preserve evidence before performing actions that may overwrite or destroy it, where operationally feasible.
- Separate confirmed facts from assumptions and hypotheses.
- Use timestamps and correlation identifiers to establish event sequence.
- Examine both successful and blocked actions.
- Investigate alternative execution paths and retries.
- Determine whether downstream systems completed actions after the agent was stopped.
- Record evidence gaps and explain their effect on the conclusions.
- Protect sensitive evidence and restrict access to authorized personnel.
- Avoid changing production conditions merely to reproduce the event without appropriate authorization.
- Escalate new evidence that materially changes the severity or scope.

**Core Principle:** An AI incident investigation must reconstruct the system's observable execution and control decisions using evidence from the complete workflow, rather than relying solely on the model's explanation of its own behavior.

### 12. AI Incident Timeline Reconstruction and Execution Tracing

Timeline reconstruction establishes the chronological sequence of events surrounding an incident.

For an AI agent, the timeline may need to include user input, retrieval operations, model requests, tool selection, authorization decisions, human approvals, asynchronous execution, and downstream effects.

A reliable timeline is essential for determining whether a control operated correctly, where the attack path crossed a security boundary, and whether additional unauthorized actions occurred.

#### 12.1 Timeline Data Sources

| Source | Relevant information |
|---|---|
| Application logs | Requests, responses, session identifiers, and application errors |
| Model gateway logs | Model requests, response metadata, provider identifiers, and timestamps |
| Agent orchestration traces | Task transitions, planning steps recorded by the application, retries, and delegation |
| Retrieval logs | Queries, document identifiers, access decisions, and retrieved source references |
| Tool gateway logs | Tool invocation, validated arguments, authorization outcome, and execution status |
| Identity provider logs | Authentication, token issuance, role assignment, and revocation |
| API gateway logs | Requests, responses, rate limits, and authorization failures |
| Database audit logs | Reads, writes, deletions, and affected records |
| Workflow engine logs | Queued jobs, scheduled tasks, retries, and completion status |
| Human approval records | Approval requests, approver identity, decisions, and timestamps |
| Security monitoring | Alerts, correlation rules, and detection timestamps |
| Change management | Model, prompt, configuration, permission, and deployment changes |

The availability and content of these records depend on the system design and provider capabilities. Missing evidence must be recorded as an investigation limitation.

#### 12.2 Example AI Agent Incident Timeline

**Scenario:** An AI agent attempts to export customer information after processing a malicious document.

| Time (UTC) | Event | Source | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 09:14:02 | User requests a summary of a customer case | Application log | Initial user activity |
| 09:14:03 | Agent retrieves a document containing malicious instructions | Retrieval log | Untrusted content enters the workflow |
| 09:14:05 | Model response results in a request to invoke an export tool | Orchestration trace | Potential instruction manipulation |
| 09:14:05 | Tool gateway evaluates the request | Tool gateway log | Authorization boundary reached |
| 09:14:05 | Export action is denied | Authorization log | Preventive control blocks execution |
| 09:14:06 | Agent attempts an alternative retrieval operation | Retrieval log | Possible continued attack path |
| 09:14:07 | Security monitoring generates an alert | SIEM event | Detection initiated |
| 09:15:10 | Incident responder disables the affected tool capability | Change or control log | Containment action |
| 09:16:00 | Investigators confirm no successful export in the reviewed logs | Database and tool logs | Preliminary impact conclusion |

This timeline is illustrative. Investigators must validate the event sequence against actual records and account for clock synchronization, delayed logging, and asynchronous processing.

#### 12.3 Correlation and Traceability

Where supported, the organization should use consistent identifiers to connect events across systems.

Recommended identifiers include:

- Incident ID.
- User session ID.
- Agent execution ID.
- Parent execution ID.
- Model request ID.
- Tool invocation ID.
- Authentication or token reference.
- Workflow or job ID.
- Transaction ID.
- Retrieved document or record ID.

Identifiers should be designed to support correlation without unnecessarily exposing personal information or credentials.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Sequence number | Chronological event number |
| Timestamp | Date and time, including time zone |
| Source system | System that recorded the event |
| Actor or identity | User, service identity, agent, or external actor |

#### 12.5 Evidence Gaps and Timeline Confidence

An incomplete timeline should not be presented as definitive.

For each material gap, record:

1. Which event cannot be established.
2. Which log or data source is unavailable.
3. Why the evidence is unavailable.
4. What alternative sources were examined.
5. How the gap affects the impact assessment.
6. Whether additional investigation is possible.
7. Whether the uncertainty changes the severity or containment decision.

For example, a missing tool execution log does not prove that the tool was never called. If downstream records cannot independently establish the outcome, the organization may need to maintain a higher level of uncertainty in its findings.

**Core Principle:** Timeline reconstruction must distinguish between an action that was requested, an action that was authorized, an action that was executed, and an outcome that was independently verified.

### 13. AI Incident Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis identifies the underlying conditions that allowed an incident to occur or increased its impact.

A superficial conclusion such as "the AI hallucinated" or "the model followed a malicious prompt" does not adequately explain a security incident. It describes an observed behavior but may not identify the control failure that made the behavior consequential.

An effective root cause analysis examines the interaction between model behavior, system architecture, access control, business logic, monitoring, human oversight, and governance.

#### 13.1 Root Cause Categories

| Category | Example root cause |
|---|---|
| Architecture | Model output can directly trigger a privileged operation without an independent policy enforcement layer |
| Identity and access | Agent service identity has excessive permissions |
| Tool security | Tool arguments are not independently validated |
| Prompt and context handling | Untrusted retrieved instructions are not safely isolated from privileged workflows |
| Data governance | Retrieval does not enforce the user's source-level access rights |
| Memory management | Persistent memory accepts unverified information that influences later actions |
| Orchestration | Delegated tasks inherit broader permissions than intended |
| Human oversight | High-impact actions can proceed without required approval |
| Monitoring | Tool calls are logged, but unauthorized patterns are not detected |
| Change management | A new model or tool configuration was deployed without security regression testing |
| Third-party dependency | A provider limitation or compromised component undermines a control |
| Incident response | The organization cannot revoke an agent's access quickly |
| Governance | No owner is accountable for the relevant control |
| Testing | Adversarial scenarios did not cover the attack path that occurred |

Multiple causes may contribute to one incident. The investigation should distinguish the initiating event from the conditions that enabled it and the weaknesses that increased its impact.

#### 13.2 Five Whys Analysis

The Five Whys technique can help trace an event from its immediate cause to a deeper control weakness. It should be used as a structured investigative aid, not as a requirement to stop after exactly five questions.

**Example incident:** An agent attempts to export restricted customer records.

| Question | Illustrative answer |
|---|---|
| Why did the agent attempt the export? | The execution flow selected an export tool after processing malicious retrieved content |
| Why could the request reach the export tool? | The agent was configured with access to the tool |
| Why was the request not blocked earlier? | The workflow did not apply a sufficiently restrictive action policy before tool invocation |
| Why was the action policy insufficient? | Tool access was defined broadly, without a sufficiently granular authorization model |
| Why was the weakness not identified before deployment? | Assessment testing did not cover this prompt-injection and unauthorized-action scenario |

The investigation may identify several contributing causes, including architecture, authorization design, and testing gaps. The objective is to determine which changes would prevent recurrence or materially reduce the impact.

#### 13.3 Root Cause Versus Contributing Factor

| Element | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Triggering event | Event that initiated the incident sequence | A malicious document entered the retrieval context |
| Exploited weakness | Deficiency used to advance the attack | Tool invocation lacked adequate action-level restrictions |
| Root cause | Underlying condition that allowed the weakness to exist or persist | The architecture relied on broad tool access rather than enforceable authorization |
| Contributing factor | Condition that increased likelihood or impact | Monitoring did not alert on the attempted export |
| Detection gap | Failure to identify the event promptly | Relevant tool-call telemetry was not integrated into security monitoring |
| Impact amplifier | Condition that increased the consequences | The agent could access a large volume of customer records |
| Governance failure | Deficiency in accountability, policy, or oversight | No control owner was assigned to validate agent permissions |

These categories are analytical distinctions. Organizations may use different terminology, provided the investigation produces a clear and actionable explanation.

**Incident reference:** [Incident ID]

**Incident summary:**  
[Describe the event and its verified outcome.]

**Triggering event:**  
[Identify the event that initiated the sequence.]

**Exploited weakness:**  
[Identify the technical or process weakness involved.]

[Explain the underlying condition that permitted the incident.]

#### 13.5 Avoiding Common Root Cause Errors

Investigators should avoid:
- Attributing the event exclusively to model unpredictability without examining system controls.
- Treating a malicious prompt as the complete root cause when authorization controls also failed.
- Assuming that user error explains the incident without evaluating the system's safeguards.
- Closing the investigation after identifying the first technical defect.
- Recommending training when an architectural control is required.
- Recommending more logging without defining how the events will be detected and acted upon.
- Declaring a root cause with high confidence when material evidence is missing.

**Core Principle:** Root cause analysis should identify the system and governance conditions that made the incident possible, enabling corrective actions that reduce recurrence and limit future impact.

### 14. AI Security Incident Business Impact and Data Exposure Assessment

The business impact assessment determines what the incident means for the organization, its customers, employees, partners, and other affected parties.

Technical severity alone is insufficient. A relatively simple tool invocation may cause substantial harm if it modifies a financial transaction, exposes highly sensitive information, or affects a critical business process.

The assessment must distinguish confirmed impact from potential impact and explicitly document uncertainty.

#### 14.1 Business Impact Dimensions

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Was sensitive information accessed or disclosed to an unauthorized party? |
| Integrity | Were records, transactions, decisions, or configurations altered without authorization? |
| Availability | Were critical services interrupted or degraded? |
| Financial | Did the incident cause financial loss, fraud, penalties, or recovery costs? |
| Operational | Were business workflows delayed, disrupted, or rendered unreliable? |
| Privacy | Was personal data processed, exposed, altered, or otherwise affected in a potentially reportable way? |
| Legal and regulatory | Could contractual, statutory, or regulatory requirements be implicated? |
| Safety | Could the incident cause physical harm or an unsafe operational condition? |
| Reputation | Could the event undermine customer, employee, or partner trust? |
| Intellectual property | Were source code, designs, proprietary models, or confidential business information exposed? |
| Third-party impact | Were vendors, customers, partners, or connected organizations affected? |
| Cascading impact | Could the incident propagate through other agents, tools, or dependent systems? |

The organization should use its established business impact and data classification methodologies where available.

#### 14.2 Data Exposure Assessment

A data exposure investigation should determine:

1. What data was involved?
2. How was the data classified?
3. Which identities or processes accessed it?
4. Was the access authorized?
5. Was the data merely retrieved internally, or was it transmitted externally?
6. Was it included in model context, tool arguments, outputs, logs, or memory?
7. Who or what could receive the information?
8. Was the data retained by a provider or downstream service?
9. Can the organization establish the extent of access or disclosure?
10. What containment, remediation, and notification evaluations are required?

The presence of data in a model context does not automatically establish external disclosure. Conversely, the absence of an obvious user-visible response does not prove that no data was transmitted to an external service or downstream tool.

#### 14.3 Data Exposure Matrix

| Data category | Sensitivity | Potential consequence | Evidence to examine |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public information | Low | Limited direct impact, subject to context | Output and transmission records |
| Internal business information | Moderate | Unauthorized internal disclosure or competitive disadvantage | Access logs and recipients |
| Confidential business data | High | Commercial, contractual, or reputational harm | Retrieval, output, and provider transmission logs |
| Personal data | Depends on context and applicable law | Privacy impact and possible notification obligations | Data categories, affected individuals, access and disclosure records |
| Authentication secrets | Critical | Unauthorized access to systems and accounts | Secret exposure, token use, identity logs |
| Financial or payment information | High to critical | Fraud, financial loss, or compliance exposure | Transaction records and access logs |
| Source code and intellectual property | High | Intellectual property loss or system compromise | Repository access, generated outputs, external transmission |
| Safety-critical operational data | Context-dependent | Unsafe decisions or physical harm | System commands, operational state, approval and execution records |

Classification should reflect the organization's information classification scheme and the sensitivity of the particular data set.

#### 14.4 Actual Versus Potential Impact

| Impact status | Meaning | Reporting treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed | Supported by evidence | Record as established impact |
| Probable | Strong evidence supports the conclusion, but a material uncertainty remains | Record the rationale and outstanding uncertainty |
| Possible | A credible pathway exists, but evidence is insufficient to determine whether the impact occurred | Record as potential impact |
| Not identified | No evidence of the impact has been found within the investigation performed | State the scope and limitations of the investigation |
| Unknown | Evidence is insufficient to make a reliable determination | Preserve uncertainty and consider further investigation |

The phrase "no impact identified" should not be used as a synonym for "no impact occurred."

### 15. AI Security Incident Remediation and Corrective Action Planning

Remediation converts investigation findings into actions that remove the underlying weakness, restore safe operation, and reduce the probability or impact of recurrence.

An effective remediation plan addresses the root cause and contributing factors rather than focusing exclusively on the event's visible symptom.

For AI systems, corrective action may require changes to tool permissions, authorization architecture, data access, orchestration logic, model configuration, monitoring, operational procedures, or governance controls.

#### 15.1 Types of Corrective Action

| Action category | Objective | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate containment | Stop ongoing harmful activity | Disable the affected tool capability |
| Technical remediation | Remove or reduce the identified weakness | Enforce action-level authorization at the tool gateway |
| Architectural correction | Address a systemic design deficiency | Separate model-generated proposals from privileged execution |
| Identity remediation | Correct excessive or compromised access | Revoke affected credentials and reduce agent permissions |
| Data protection | Prevent unauthorized access or disclosure | Enforce source-level access checks during retrieval |
| Monitoring improvement | Improve detection and investigation | Add alerts for repeated denied high-impact tool calls |
| Testing improvement | Prevent recurrence and detect regressions | Add adversarial and negative authorization tests |
| Process correction | Improve operational execution | Require documented approval before consequential actions |
| Governance improvement | Clarify accountability and decisions | Assign an accountable owner for agent access-control reviews |
| Third-party remediation | Address provider or dependency weaknesses | Restrict affected integration and obtain documented corrective evidence |

#### 15.2 Remediation Prioritization

Remediation priority should consider:

- Incident severity.
- The likelihood of recurrence.
- The impact of the underlying weakness.
- Whether exploitation is ongoing.
- The number and criticality of affected systems.
- Whether a mandatory control has failed.
- The effectiveness of available compensating controls.
- The availability of a safe workaround.
- The time needed to implement and validate a correction.
- Dependencies on vendors or external service providers.

A low-complexity correction may be prioritized immediately if it closes an active high-impact attack path. Conversely, a large architectural improvement may require phased implementation, provided interim controls adequately reduce exposure and the remaining risk is authorized.

| Root cause | Underlying condition addressed |

| Action type | Technical, architectural, operational, governance, or other |
| Priority | Risk-based remediation priority |
| Owner | Accountable implementation owner |

| Target date | Planned completion |

#### 15.4 Example Corrective Action Plan

**Incident:** AI agent attempted an unauthorized customer-data export after processing malicious retrieved content.

| Action | Type | Priority | Validation criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disable the affected export capability pending review | Containment | Immediate | Export attempts are denied and no alternate route remains available |
| Restrict the agent's service identity to approved operations | Identity and access | High | Access tests demonstrate that unauthorized resources and actions are denied |
| Introduce action-level authorization at the tool gateway | Architecture | High | Adversarial tests cannot bypass the authorization decision |
| Separate untrusted retrieved content from privileged execution decisions | Application security | High | Relevant prompt-injection scenarios cannot authorize prohibited actions |
| Add monitoring for repeated denied high-impact actions | Detection | High | Test events generate the expected alert and incident record |
| Expand adversarial regression testing | Testing | High | Test suite covers the identified attack path and relevant alternatives |
| Review other agents using the same integration | Governance | High | Related agents are identified and assessed for the same weakness |
| Update the security assessment and risk register | Governance | Medium | Records reflect the root cause, residual risk, and verified controls |

The priorities and deadlines should be assigned under the organization's approved incident and remediation procedures.

#### 15.5 Remediation Verification

A corrective action should not be considered effective merely because a configuration was changed or a development task was marked complete.

Verification should establish that:
1. The intended change was implemented in the correct environment.
2. The relevant weakness is no longer exploitable under the tested conditions.
3. Alternative attack paths have been considered.
4. The correction has not created an unacceptable new risk.
5. Relevant regression tests pass.
6. Monitoring and operational controls function as intended.
7. Supporting evidence is retained.
8. Residual risk is reassessed.

For high-risk corrections, independent verification should be considered.

#### 15.6 Systemic Remediation

A single incident may reveal a weakness in a shared architecture, common tool gateway, central identity configuration, or organization-wide development practice.

The organization should therefore ask whether corrective action is required beyond the affected agent.

For example, if one agent was able to invoke an unauthorized operation because the shared tool gateway trusted model-generated parameters, the remediation should evaluate all agents that rely on the same gateway.

**Core Principle:** Corrective action is complete only when the underlying weakness has been addressed, the effectiveness of the correction has been verified, and related systems have been evaluated for the same exposure.

## Part 3 — AI Security Exceptions, Risk Decisions, Compensating Controls, and Approval Governance

### 16. AI Security Exception Management Framework

An AI security exception is a formally documented, time-bound authorization to deviate from an established security policy, standard, control requirement, or technical baseline.

Exceptions may be necessary when an AI initiative has a legitimate business requirement that cannot immediately satisfy an existing control. However, an exception must not become an informal mechanism for bypassing security requirements, avoiding remediation, or deploying an inadequately assessed AI system.

AI security exceptions require particular attention because an AI system may introduce dynamic behavior, probabilistic outputs, external dependencies, delegated authority, and data-processing activities that complicate the assessment of residual risk.

An exception authorizes a defined deviation under specified conditions. It does not automatically eliminate the underlying risk, waive applicable legal obligations, or establish that the system is secure.

#### 16.1 Objectives of AI Security Exception Management

An enterprise AI security exception process should:

1. Establish a controlled mechanism for requesting deviations from security requirements.
2. Require a clear business justification for each deviation.
3. Identify the AI system, affected controls, and relevant business processes.
4. Evaluate the security, privacy, operational, regulatory, and safety implications.
5. Define compensating controls that reduce exposure.
6. Assign accountable risk owners and authorized approvers.
7. Establish an expiration date and review frequency.
8. Track remediation activities and outstanding dependencies.
9. Preserve evidence of the decision and its rationale.
10. Prevent expired or unauthorized exceptions from remaining active.

The exception process should integrate with the organization's broader GRC, change-management, risk-acceptance, and audit processes.

#### 16.2 When an AI Security Exception May Be Appropriate

Potential scenarios include:

| Scenario | Example | Required consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary control implementation gap | A new internal AI assistant is awaiting deployment of centralized security monitoring | Define interim monitoring, deployment restrictions, and a remediation deadline |
| Technical limitation | A third-party AI service does not support a required enterprise authentication feature | Assess alternative controls, integration restrictions, and provider limitations |
| Legacy integration dependency | An AI application depends on an existing system that cannot immediately support the approved authentication standard | Isolate the integration and establish a migration plan |
| Temporary logging limitation | A provider does not expose a particular diagnostic event | Identify alternative evidence sources and assess the impact on detection and investigation |
| Controlled pilot | A limited AI pilot needs a temporary deviation from a standard operational control | Restrict participants, data categories, functions, duration, and system connectivity |
| Model evaluation environment | A test environment requires controlled access to data or capabilities normally restricted in production | Enforce environment segregation and prevent unauthorized data movement |
| Time-bound migration | A business-critical AI workflow requires a phased transition to the target security architecture | Establish milestones, interim controls, and explicit risk approval |

These scenarios are candidates for formal assessment, not automatic grounds for granting an exception.

#### 16.3 Situations That Require Escalation or Prohibition

Some deviations cannot be approved through ordinary exception procedures.

Examples include:

- A proposed deviation that would violate applicable law or a binding regulatory obligation.
- A control deficiency that creates an unacceptable safety risk.
- An AI system that would process prohibited data or perform a prohibited activity.
- A request to bypass mandatory access controls without an authorized alternative.
- A request to disable a critical safeguard without effective containment.
- An exception that would expose credentials or secrets without adequate protection.
- A request that conflicts with a contractual obligation that the organization cannot lawfully or contractually waive.
- A deviation that exceeds the delegated risk-acceptance authority of the proposed approver.

The organization should define non-waivable requirements and escalation criteria explicitly. Legal, privacy, safety, and regulatory obligations must be evaluated by the relevant accountable functions rather than treated as ordinary discretionary security controls.

### 17. AI Security Exception Request and Business Justification Template

The exception request is the formal starting point for evaluating a proposed deviation. It should provide enough information for reviewers to understand why the deviation is necessary, what is affected, which alternatives exist, and what conditions would make the request acceptable.

A vague request such as "the AI project cannot meet the security standard" is insufficient. The requester should identify the precise control requirement, explain the technical or business constraint, and demonstrate that reasonable alternatives have been considered.

#### 17.1 Required Request Information

**A. Request identification**

- Exception ID:

- Requester:
- Department:

- Technical owner:
- AI system name:
- AI system inventory ID:

- Proposed effective date:

- Policy or standard:
- Control identifier:
- Required control:
- Current implementation:
- Requested deviation:
- Systems, integrations, and data affected:
- Business processes affected:
- Reason the requirement cannot currently be met:

**C. Business justification**

The requester should explain:

1. The business outcome the AI system supports.
2. Why the deviation is necessary.
3. The consequences of not granting the exception.
4. Whether an alternative implementation is feasible.
5. Whether the business requirement can be met with reduced functionality.
6. Whether deployment can be delayed until the required control is implemented.
7. Why the requested duration is appropriate.
8. Which parties or customers may be affected.

**D. Technical justification**

The technical owner should document:

- Available compensating controls.

#### 17.2 Alternatives Analysis

Before granting an exception, reviewers should evaluate whether the objective can be achieved without the deviation.

| Alternative | Example | Assessment question |
|---|---|---|
| Implement the required control | Add enterprise authentication before production deployment | Can the requirement be satisfied within the delivery timeline? |
| Redesign the workflow | Replace autonomous execution with a human-approved workflow | Can risk be reduced through architecture? |
| Restrict functionality | Disable external sharing or privileged tool calls | Can the business objective be achieved with a smaller capability set? |
| Restrict data | Use synthetic or de-identified data in a pilot | Can the system operate without the sensitive data? |
| Isolate the environment | Run the system in a restricted network segment | Does isolation adequately limit exposure? |
| Use an alternative provider | Select a service that supports the required control | Is switching technically and commercially feasible? |
| Delay deployment | Defer release until the control is implemented | Is the business impact of delay acceptable? |
| Apply compensating controls | Introduce an independent policy enforcement gateway | Do the alternative controls provide sufficient risk reduction? |

The analysis should identify why the selected option is preferable to the alternatives.

#### 17.3 Example: Temporary Logging Exception

**Scenario:** An enterprise AI application uses a third-party model service that does not expose a specific diagnostic event required by the organization's standard monitoring baseline.

**Requested deviation:** Temporary exception from the requirement to collect the specified provider-side event.

**Business justification:** The service supports a time-sensitive business workflow. The missing event is not available through the provider's current interface.

**Security concerns:**
- Reduced visibility into certain model-service operations.
- Potential delay in detecting misuse.
- Incomplete reconstruction of some provider-side execution events.
- Reduced confidence during incident investigations.

**Alternatives considered:**
- Select a provider with the required telemetry.
- Delay deployment until the logging requirement is met.
- Restrict the application to lower-risk use cases.
- Collect additional application-side and gateway telemetry.

**Proposed compensating controls:**
- Log application requests and responses according to approved data-handling requirements.
- Record model and provider identifiers where available.
- Capture tool invocation and authorization decisions independently of provider logs.
- Restrict sensitive data sent to the service.
- Alert on suspicious usage and unusual request volumes.
- Establish a documented provider escalation process.
- Review the exception at defined intervals.

**Conditions for approval:**
- The system must not process data categories excluded by the approved assessment.
- Compensating telemetry must be tested before activation.
- The business owner must accept the documented residual risk within delegated authority.
- The exception must expire on a defined date.
- The technical owner must review alternative solutions before renewal.

**Important limitation:** Application-side logs do not automatically replace missing provider-side evidence. The assessment must establish which events remain unobservable and how that limitation affects detection and investigation.

#### 17.4 Business Impact of Rejection

The requester must describe the consequences of rejecting the exception without exaggerating the business impact.

Consider:
- Delayed deployment or release.
- Loss of a planned business capability.
- Additional engineering or licensing costs.
- Contractual or operational dependencies.
- Availability of a safe workaround.
- Impact on customers or internal users.
- Whether the business objective can be achieved through a narrower implementation.

Business urgency is a relevant decision factor, but it does not independently establish that a security exception is acceptable.

#### 17.5 Requester Declaration

The requester should confirm that:

- The information supplied is accurate to the best of their knowledge.
- The requested deviation is clearly defined.
- Known risks and limitations have been disclosed.
- Reasonable alternatives have been considered.
- Proposed compensating controls can be implemented and maintained.
- The system will remain within the approved scope.
- Any material change will be reported.
- The exception will be reassessed before renewal or expiration.

**Core Principle:** A valid exception request demonstrates necessity, defines the deviation precisely, evaluates alternatives, and provides enough evidence for an informed risk decision.

### 18. AI Security Compensating Controls Assessment

Compensating controls are alternative safeguards used to reduce risk when a required control cannot be implemented as specified.

A compensating control is not automatically equivalent to the original control. Its effectiveness must be assessed against the threat, the control objective, the system architecture, and the remaining exposure.

For AI systems, a compensating control should be evaluated for its ability to operate independently of the behavior it is intended to constrain. For example, asking an AI model to follow a policy is not equivalent to enforcing that policy through a deterministic authorization mechanism.

#### 18.1 Compensating Control Assessment Criteria

| Criterion | Assessment question |
|---|---|
| Control objective | What security outcome must be preserved? |
| Threat coverage | Which threat scenarios does the control mitigate? |
| Independence | Does the control operate independently of the potentially compromised component? |
| Enforcement | Is the control mandatory, or can the AI system bypass it? |
| Coverage | Does the control cover all relevant execution paths? |
| Reliability | Does it operate consistently under expected and adverse conditions? |
| Detectability | Can control failure be detected? |
| Evidence | Can the organization demonstrate that the control operates as intended? |
| Operational ownership | Who maintains and monitors the control? |
| Failure behavior | Does the system fail safely when the control is unavailable? |
| Residual risk | What exposure remains after the control is implemented? |
| Sustainability | Can the control remain effective throughout the exception period? |

#### 18.2 AI-Specific Compensating Control Examples

| Original requirement | Possible compensating control | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-grained tool authorization | Restrict the agent to a small, explicitly approved set of low-impact tools | Restricted tool availability does not replace authorization for every sensitive operation |
| Complete provider telemetry | Collect application, gateway, identity, and downstream transaction logs | Provider-side events may remain unavailable |
| Human approval for consequential actions | Enforce an independent approval workflow before execution | Approval must be meaningful, attributable, and resistant to automated bypass |
| Source-level retrieval authorization | Restrict the agent to a segregated corpus containing only approved information | Segregation may become ineffective if the corpus is not maintained or access changes |
| Production secrets management | Remove privileged credentials and use a constrained, short-lived service identity | Limited credentials must still be protected and monitored |
| Autonomous execution limits | Enforce action quotas, transaction limits, and execution timeouts | Quotas reduce impact but may not prevent every harmful action |
| Network isolation | Restrict outbound connectivity to approved destinations | Allowed destinations may still expose sensitive data if their purpose and permissions are too broad |
| Prompt-injection resilience | Apply tool-side policy enforcement and validate untrusted inputs | Input filtering alone cannot guarantee protection against prompt injection |
| Independent security testing | Add continuous monitoring and recurring targeted adversarial tests | Testing and monitoring do not fully substitute for a missing preventive control |

The assessment must not assume that a collection of weaker controls automatically provides the same protection as the original requirement.

#### 18.3 Control Effectiveness Assessment

Each proposed compensating control should be evaluated using a structured method.

**Step 1 — Define the objective**

State the security outcome required by the original control.

**Step 2 — Identify the threat**

Determine the attack or failure scenario that the control is intended to prevent, detect, or mitigate.

**Step 3 — Map the alternative control**

Explain exactly where the compensating control operates in the architecture.

**Step 4 — Evaluate bypass paths**

Determine whether the agent, user, tool, service identity, or downstream integration can avoid the control.

**Step 5 — Test the control**

Use appropriate functional, security, and adversarial tests.

**Step 6 — Evaluate failure conditions**

Determine what happens when the control is unavailable, misconfigured, overloaded, or presented with unexpected inputs.

**Step 7 — Document residual risk**

Record threats that remain insufficiently controlled.

**Step 8 — Establish monitoring**

Define evidence sources, alert conditions, and review responsibilities.

#### 18.5 Example: Replacing Broad Agent Permissions with a Restricted Execution Design

**Control deficiency:** A required fine-grained permission model cannot be implemented immediately in an existing integration.

**Proposed alternative design:**
- Remove access to unnecessary tools.
- Expose a limited set of predefined operations.
- Validate every operation through a gateway.
- Enforce parameter and transaction limits.
- Require human approval for sensitive actions.
- Use a restricted service identity.
- Log authorization decisions and execution results.
- Monitor for repeated denied actions and unusual usage.

**Validation requirements:**
- Attempt to invoke an unapproved operation.
- Attempt to alter parameters to exceed authorized scope.
- Attempt to use the tool with another user's data.
- Attempt to bypass approval.
- Test repeated requests and retries.
- Simulate gateway unavailability.
- Verify that downstream services enforce their own authorization where required.

**Decision consideration:** The design may provide meaningful risk reduction, but the organization must determine whether the remaining limitations are acceptable for the specific system, data, and business process.

**Core Principle:** Compensating controls must be independently enforceable, tested against realistic threats, and evaluated for residual risk; their presence alone does not establish control equivalence.

### 19. AI Security Exception Approval and Risk Decision Governance

Exception approval is a risk-governance decision, not merely an administrative sign-off.

The decision-maker must have the authority to accept the relevant category and level of risk. Technical teams can recommend controls and explain feasibility, but implementation responsibility does not automatically confer authority to accept business risk.

The approval model should be proportionate to the system's criticality, data sensitivity, potential harm, regulatory context, and degree of autonomy.

#### 19.1 Roles and Responsibilities

| Role | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| Requester | Defines the deviation and provides the business and technical justification |
| Business owner | Explains the business need and consequences of rejection |
| AI system owner | Confirms system scope, functionality, and operational dependencies |
| Security architecture | Evaluates architecture, trust boundaries, and control alternatives |
| Cybersecurity or GRC | Coordinates risk assessment and validates governance requirements |
| Privacy officer or privacy function | Evaluates personal-data implications where applicable |
| Legal and compliance | Evaluates applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual constraints |
| Safety or specialist risk function | Assesses relevant safety, financial, or domain-specific consequences |
| Control owner | Explains the original control objective and evaluates proposed alternatives |
| Risk owner | Accepts or rejects residual risk within delegated authority |
| Approver | Records the authorized decision according to the approval framework |
| Internal audit | Provides independent assurance where appropriate; ordinarily does not own management's risk decision |

One person may hold multiple roles in a smaller organization, but incompatible responsibilities and conflicts of interest should be managed.

#### 19.2 Risk-Based Approval Matrix

The following matrix is illustrative and must be adapted to the organization's delegated authority framework.

| Risk classification | Example characteristics | Typical review | Decision authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | Limited functionality, non-sensitive data, minimal external exposure | System owner and security reviewer | Authorized operational risk owner |
| Moderate | Internal business data, bounded integrations, limited operational impact | Security, business, and control-owner review | Designated business risk owner |
| High | Sensitive data, consequential tool access, significant third-party dependencies | Cross-functional risk review, including relevant privacy or compliance functions | Senior risk owner or delegated executive |
| Critical | Potential severe harm, major business disruption, highly privileged actions, or material regulatory exposure | Formal multidisciplinary review and executive escalation | Executive or risk committee, where permitted by policy |

**Important:** A high or critical classification does not mean the exception must be approved by a particular role in every organization. The actual authority must be defined in the organization's governance framework. Some risks may be non-acceptable regardless of seniority.

#### 19.3 Approval Decision Options

| Decision | Meaning | Required documentation |
|---|---|---|
| Approve | Residual risk is accepted within delegated authority | Rationale, conditions, owner, expiry, and review schedule |
| Approve with conditions | Authorization depends on specific safeguards or milestones | Conditions, implementation evidence, responsible owners, and activation criteria |
| Reject | The deviation is not acceptable | Decision rationale and required alternative action |
| Return for revision | Information or controls are insufficient | Outstanding evidence, assessment gaps, or required changes |
| Escalate | The risk exceeds the current approver's authority or requires specialist review | Escalation rationale and receiving authority |
| Suspend | An existing exception cannot safely continue under current conditions | Immediate restrictions, reassessment requirements, and reinstatement criteria |
| Revoke | The authorization is withdrawn | Revocation reason, effective time, and containment or remediation requirements |

An approval should be explicit. Silence, meeting attendance, email visibility, or failure to object should not be treated as authorization unless an approved governance procedure expressly establishes a valid decision mechanism.

#### 19.4 Approval Conditions

Conditions may include:

- Limiting the exception to a particular environment.
- Restricting data classifications or user populations.
- Disabling specified agent capabilities.
- Requiring human approval for consequential actions.
- Implementing additional monitoring.
- Maintaining a documented manual review process.
- Completing a remediation milestone by a specified date.
- Reassessing the exception after a model, tool, or architecture change.
- Prohibiting use for specified high-impact decisions.
- Requiring periodic evidence of control effectiveness.

Conditions should be measurable and verifiable.

For example, "monitor the system closely" is insufficient. A better condition specifies which events must be monitored, who reviews them, how often review occurs, which thresholds trigger escalation, and what evidence demonstrates compliance.

#### 19.6 Separation of Duties and Independent Challenge

Where practical, the person requesting an exception should not be the sole person assessing and approving it.

Independent challenge is particularly important when:
- The system processes sensitive or regulated data.
- The exception affects a critical preventive control.
- The AI agent can perform consequential actions.
- The request involves a significant third-party dependency.
- The business owner has a strong incentive to bypass the requirement.
- Previous incidents or audit findings concern the same control.
- The requested deviation is being renewed repeatedly.

The degree of independence should be proportionate to the risk.

**Core Principle:** An AI security exception is valid only when its residual risk has been assessed, the decision is made by an authorized risk owner, and the approval conditions are explicit and enforceable.

### 20. Exception Monitoring, Expiration, Renewal, and Revocation

An approved exception creates an ongoing governance obligation. Risk can change after approval because of new attack techniques, changes in model behavior, new integrations, expanded data access, provider changes, or modifications to the business process.

An exception that was reasonable under one set of conditions may become unacceptable when those conditions change.

Exception management must therefore continue throughout the authorization period rather than end when the approval is recorded.

#### 20.1 Monitoring Requirements

The organization should monitor:

- Whether the exception remains within its approved scope.
- Whether compensating controls remain operational.
- Whether required remediation milestones are being met.
- Whether the system's risk classification has changed.
- Whether incidents or near misses reveal additional exposure.
- Whether new tools, models, data sources, or integrations have been introduced.
- Whether usage or autonomy has increased.
- Whether provider terms or capabilities have changed.
- Whether new legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal requirements apply.
- Whether the exception remains necessary.

Monitoring intensity should be proportionate to residual risk and the criticality of the affected system.

#### 20.2 Exception Review Triggers

| Trigger | Required response |
|---|---|
| Scheduled review date | Reassess necessity, controls, scope, and residual risk |
| New security incident | Reevaluate the exception and its compensating controls |
| Material model change | Determine whether prior testing and risk assumptions remain valid |
| New tool or integration | Assess expanded privileges and attack surface |
| Change in data classification | Reassess privacy, confidentiality, and regulatory exposure |
| Significant increase in autonomy | Reevaluate the consequences of agent actions |
| Change in provider terms or service architecture | Review dependencies, evidence access, and control capabilities |
| Failed compensating control | Suspend or restrict the exception and reassess risk |
| Missed remediation milestone | Escalate, reassess, and consider suspension |
| New regulatory or contractual requirement | Determine whether the deviation remains permissible |
| Change in business ownership | Confirm continued accountability and approval authority |
| Exception approaching expiration | Decide whether to remediate, renew, or terminate |

A review trigger does not automatically mean that an exception must be revoked. It means that the assumptions supporting the existing decision must be reassessed.

#### 20.3 Expiration Management

Every temporary exception should have an explicit expiration date.

A suitable expiration process includes:

1. Generating advance notifications before expiry.
2. Confirming the status of outstanding remediation.
3. Reassessing the control deficiency and compensating controls.
4. Obtaining updated evidence of effectiveness.
5. Recalculating residual risk where conditions have changed.
6. Obtaining a new authorized decision if renewal is justified.
7. Disabling or restricting the exception when it expires, where technically and operationally feasible.
8. Recording closure or any approved replacement authorization.

An exception should not be renewed automatically simply because the original implementation deadline was missed.

If the deviation cannot be removed before expiration, the organization should require a new assessment and explicit authorization. A new approval should not be assumed to inherit the original risk decision.

#### 20.4 Renewal Assessment

A renewal request should answer:

- Why does the deviation still exist?
- What progress has been made against the original remediation plan?
- Why was the original deadline not met?
- Are the original business assumptions still valid?
- Have threat conditions or the system's exposure changed?
- Have compensating controls remained effective?
- Have incidents, near misses, or audit findings occurred?
- Are additional controls required?
- Is a different technical or operational solution now available?
- What is the new target date for eliminating the deviation?
- Who will remain accountable for the residual risk?

Repeated renewal should be treated as a potential indicator of a systemic control implementation problem.

#### 20.5 Revocation and Emergency Suspension

An exception may need to be suspended or revoked if:
- A compensating control fails.
- The system exceeds its approved scope.
- An incident demonstrates that the residual risk is no longer acceptable.
- The exception was approved using materially inaccurate information.
- Required remediation milestones are missed without acceptable justification.
- The business owner or risk owner withdraws support.
- Applicable requirements change.
- New evidence demonstrates that the original risk assessment was materially incomplete.

Emergency suspension should follow the organization's incident and operational risk procedures. Where immediate suspension could itself cause significant harm, the organization should activate an approved safe-state or continuity process rather than simply leaving the system in an uncontrolled condition.

#### 20.6 Exception Monitoring Dashboard

| Metric | Definition | Management purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Active exceptions | Number of currently active exceptions | Measures outstanding deviations |
| High-risk active exceptions | Active exceptions classified as high or critical | Highlights concentrated exposure |
| Exceptions approaching expiry | Exceptions within the defined pre-expiry window | Supports timely review |
| Expired exceptions still active | Expired authorizations where the deviation remains operational | Identifies authorization failures |
| Overdue remediation actions | Corrective actions past their target dates | Measures remediation discipline |
| Repeated renewals | Exceptions renewed beyond a defined threshold | Identifies systemic implementation issues |
| Exceptions without current evidence | Active exceptions lacking required review or control evidence | Identifies assurance gaps |
| Compensating control failures | Controls found ineffective during the reporting period | Indicates immediate reassessment needs |
| Average exception duration | Average elapsed time for active or closed exceptions | Helps identify persistent deviations |
| Revocation rate | Proportion of exceptions revoked during a period | Supports analysis of changing risk conditions |
| Exceptions by business unit | Distribution across organizational areas | Identifies concentration and accountability patterns |
| Exceptions by control domain | Distribution by identity, data, monitoring, architecture, and other domains | Highlights recurring control weaknesses |

Metrics should be interpreted in context. A higher exception count does not necessarily mean governance is worse, and a lower count does not prove that the organization is managing AI risk effectively. The quality of assessments, timeliness of remediation, effectiveness of controls, and appropriateness of approvals also matter.

#### 20.7 Exception Closure Checklist

Before closing an exception, confirm:

- [ ] The original deviation has been eliminated or formally replaced by an authorized control design.
- [ ] Required implementation evidence has been collected.
- [ ] Relevant security tests have passed.
- [ ] Compensating controls have been removed only when they are no longer needed.
- [ ] Residual risk has been reassessed.
- [ ] Outstanding findings have been resolved or separately authorized.
- [ ] The AI system inventory and risk register have been updated.
- [ ] Relevant policies, standards, and architecture records have been updated.
- [ ] The control owner has confirmed the new operating state.
- [ ] The risk owner or authorized closure approver has confirmed closure.
- [ ] Audit evidence has been retained according to applicable retention requirements.
- [ ] Monitoring has been updated to reflect the permanent control design.

#### 20.8 Final Governance Principle

An AI security exception should remain visible, time-bound, reviewable, and accountable for its entire lifecycle. The organization should be able to determine at any point why the exception exists, which risks it creates, which controls reduce those risks, who authorized the decision, when it expires, and what must happen before it can be closed.

**Core Principle:** An AI security exception is governed throughout its lifecycle—from request and approval through monitoring, renewal, revocation, or closure—and must never become an undocumented or permanent substitute for effective security controls.

## 21. End-to-End AI Security Incident and Exception Management Workflow

An enterprise AI security governance program requires a coordinated process for handling security incidents, control failures, near misses, and approved deviations from security requirements.

AI incidents and security exceptions are related but distinct governance processes. An incident requires investigation, containment, impact assessment, and recovery. An exception is a formally authorized deviation from a requirement that may create residual risk. An incident can reveal an existing exception's inadequacy, while an exception can identify weaknesses that require additional monitoring and response preparation.

The organization should maintain separate workflows while integrating their records, ownership, and risk decisions.

### 21.2 Workflow Stages and Required Outputs

| Stage | Incident management activity | Exception management activity | Required evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification | Detect suspicious activity, control failure, or harmful AI behavior | Identify an unmet control requirement | Alert, report, assessment finding, or exception request |
| 2. Classification | Determine incident category and initial severity | Identify affected control and requested deviation | Classification record |
| 3. Ownership | Assign incident commander and investigation team | Assign requester, business owner, and risk owner | Named owners and responsibilities |
| 4. Assessment | Establish scope, affected systems, and potential impact | Assess inherent risk and available alternatives | Investigation or risk assessment |
| 5. Risk treatment | Contain, eradicate, recover, and mitigate exposure | Define compensating controls and approval conditions | Response plan or control plan |
| 6. Decision | Determine escalation and recovery authorization | Approve, reject, return, or escalate | Recorded decision |
| 7. Execution | Implement containment, recovery, and corrective actions | Implement approved controls and restrictions | Technical and operational evidence |
| 8. Validation | Verify containment and recovery effectiveness | Verify compensating control effectiveness | Test results and monitoring records |
| 9. Monitoring | Detect recurrence and outstanding exposure | Review exception scope, conditions, and expiry | Monitoring records |
| 10. Closure | Confirm recovery, ownership, and corrective actions | Confirm remediation, expiry, revocation, or closure | Closure approval and audit trail |

### 21.3 Integration Rules

The organization should establish explicit rules for connecting the two workflows.

- An incident involving a system with an active exception must reference that exception.
- An exception must be reassessed when an incident challenges the assumptions underlying its approval.
- A material control failure must be recorded as an incident, finding, or other governed event according to the organization's classification criteria.
- A recurring incident should trigger a review of related exceptions and control deficiencies.
- An exception should not be used to authorize an uncontrolled response to an active incident.
- Incident remediation actions should be tracked until verified, even when the incident itself is formally closed.
- A closed incident should not automatically close an associated exception.
- An exception's expiration should not be postponed merely because an incident investigation remains open.

### 21.4 Example: AI Agent Performs an Unauthorized Action

An internal AI agent has permission to retrieve customer information and initiate selected CRM operations. An incident investigation discovers that the agent executed an operation outside its intended business scope.

The organization should:

1. Contain the unauthorized capability or affected integration.
2. Preserve the relevant agent traces, identity records, tool-call logs, and downstream transaction evidence.
3. Determine whether customer data was accessed, changed, disclosed, or deleted.
4. Establish whether the activity involved prompt injection, authorization failure, excessive privileges, a tool defect, or another cause.
5. Identify affected customers, records, systems, and business processes.
6. Determine whether an existing security exception weakened an important control.
7. Implement corrective measures and validate them through testing.
8. Reassess the agent's permissions and related AI systems.
9. Evaluate applicable notification, contractual, legal, and regulatory obligations.
10. Obtain appropriate closure approval and document lessons learned.

The investigation should distinguish between the model producing an unsafe recommendation and the system actually executing an unauthorized action. The latter may involve a separate authorization, integration, or operational control failure.

**Core Principle:** AI incident and exception workflows should remain distinct but interconnected, ensuring that incidents produce corrective action and exceptions remain subject to ongoing risk governance.

## 22. AI Incident Severity Classification and Escalation Matrix

An AI security incident severity model establishes how rapidly incidents must be investigated, who must be notified, and which containment and recovery decisions require escalation.

Severity should reflect actual and reasonably anticipated impact rather than relying exclusively on the AI model's behavior or the apparent sophistication of an attack.

For example, a single unauthorized tool call may be critical if it transfers highly sensitive information or initiates an irreversible transaction. Conversely, an incorrect model response in an isolated test environment may be low severity if it has no meaningful downstream impact.

### 22.1 Severity Assessment Dimensions

Evaluate incidents against the following dimensions:

| Dimension | Assessment questions |
|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Was sensitive data accessed, exposed, inferred, or disclosed without authorization? |
| Integrity | Were model configurations, datasets, prompts, policies, records, or outputs altered improperly? |
| Availability | Is the AI system unavailable, degraded, or vulnerable to sustained denial of service? |
| Safety | Could the system cause physical, psychological, financial, or other consequential harm? |
| Autonomy and authority | Did an AI agent execute actions beyond its approved permissions? |
| Scope | How many users, systems, customers, or records may be affected? |
| Persistence | Can the attack or harmful behavior recur automatically? |
| Propagation | Could the issue affect connected systems, downstream workflows, or other AI deployments? |
| Regulatory impact | Could the event trigger legal, regulatory, contractual, or reporting obligations? |
| Recoverability | Can the system and affected data be restored reliably? |
| Confidence | How reliable is the available evidence about the incident's scope and impact? |

Uncertainty should not automatically result in a low severity classification. Where potentially severe harm is plausible, the organization should apply appropriate precautionary escalation until the evidence supports a revised assessment.

### 22.2 Illustrative Severity Matrix

The following model is a starting point. Organizations should define thresholds and response targets according to their risk appetite, operational requirements, and applicable obligations.

| Severity | Typical characteristics | Illustrative response expectations | Escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical | Active compromise of a high-impact AI system; major sensitive-data exposure; unauthorized consequential actions; credible risk of severe harm | Immediate response, containment, and executive escalation | Incident commander, CISO or equivalent, accountable executive, and relevant specialist functions |
| High | Significant unauthorized access, material control failure, substantial service disruption, or credible exposure of sensitive information | Urgent investigation and containment under the organization's high-severity SLA | Security leadership, system owner, business risk owner, and relevant privacy or compliance functions |
| Moderate | Bounded security event with limited confirmed impact but meaningful risk of escalation | Timely investigation, documented mitigation, and tracked corrective actions | Security operations, system owner, and relevant control owner |
| Low | Limited event with minimal impact and no evidence of broader compromise | Standard investigation and remediation workflow | Designated operational owner and security reviewer |

The organization must define exact response times in its incident response standard. The illustrative terms above are not universal service-level commitments.

### 22.3 Severity Escalation Triggers

Escalate an incident when:

- Evidence suggests that sensitive data has left an approved trust boundary.
- An AI agent has executed unauthorized financial, administrative, or operational actions.
- A system has bypassed a mandatory human approval mechanism.
- A compromise affects multiple AI systems or shared services.
- A third-party provider reports a potentially relevant security incident.
- A vulnerability is actively exploited.
- The incident may involve regulated personal data or protected business information.
- A safety-critical workflow produces potentially harmful actions.
- Evidence is insufficient to exclude a high-impact scenario.
- Containment is unsuccessful or the incident continues to expand.

### 22.4 Escalation Record

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Incident ID | Unique incident reference |
| Initial severity | Initial classification |
| Current severity | Latest assessed classification |
| Escalation reason | Trigger requiring additional attention |
| Potential impact | Known and suspected consequences |
| Decision required | Specific approval, resource, or intervention needed |
| Escalated to | Authorized role or response function |
| Escalation timestamp | Date and time |
| Response owner | Person coordinating the next actions |
| Decision and rationale | Recorded outcome |
| Next review | Required reassessment time |

### 22.5 Reclassification and De-escalation

Severity may change as evidence becomes available.

A lower severity classification should require documented justification showing that the relevant impact assumptions have changed. It should not be used simply because an incident has become operationally inconvenient or because containment has reduced visibility into the remaining exposure.

Similarly, a high-severity incident may remain high after containment if sensitive data has already been disclosed or the organization still faces substantial legal, contractual, or recovery obligations.

**Core Principle:** Incident severity must reflect plausible impact, system authority, propagation potential, and uncertainty—not merely the apparent complexity of the technical event.

## 23. AI Incident and Exception Metrics, KPIs, and KRIs

Metrics provide evidence that AI security governance processes are operating effectively. They help management identify recurring failures, assess response capability, monitor accepted deviations, and prioritize investments.

However, activity metrics alone are insufficient. The number of incident reports completed or exceptions reviewed does not prove that risk has been reduced.

An effective measurement program combines process performance indicators, risk indicators, control-effectiveness measures, and outcome measures.

### 23.1 AI Incident Management KPIs

| Metric | Definition | Governance purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Mean time to detect | Average elapsed time between incident occurrence and detection, where timestamps are sufficiently reliable | Measures detection performance |
| Mean time to triage | Average elapsed time from reporting to initial classification | Measures initial response efficiency |
| Mean time to contain | Average elapsed time from incident identification to effective containment | Measures containment capability |
| Mean time to recover | Average elapsed time required to restore an acceptable operational state | Measures recovery effectiveness |
| Repeat incident rate | Proportion of incidents recurring from previously identified causes | Indicates the effectiveness of corrective actions |
| Root cause completion rate | Proportion of incidents requiring root cause analysis for which an approved analysis has been completed | Measures investigation discipline |
| Corrective action closure rate | Proportion of corrective actions completed by their approved due dates | Measures remediation execution |
| Validation pass rate | Proportion of corrective actions that pass required effectiveness testing | Measures remediation quality |
| Evidence completeness | Proportion of incidents with required investigation and approval evidence | Measures auditability |
| Incident escalation compliance | Proportion of incidents escalated within defined response requirements | Measures adherence to escalation procedures |

Metric definitions should specify the applicable population, timestamp rules, exclusions, calculation period, and data source.

For example, mean time to contain should not be calculated using inconsistent start and end points across different incident categories.

### 23.2 AI Security Exception KRIs

| Metric | Definition | Risk signal |
|---|---|---|
| Active high-risk exceptions | Number of active high-risk or critical exceptions | Concentration of accepted exposure |
| Expired active exceptions | Number of exceptions that have expired while the deviation remains active | Governance and authorization failure |
| Overdue exception remediation | Number of remediation actions past their approved deadline | Persistent control deficiencies |
| Repeated exception renewals | Number of exceptions renewed beyond the organization's defined threshold | Potential systemic implementation problems |
| Compensating control failure rate | Proportion of tested compensating controls that fail required criteria | Weakness in alternative safeguards |
| Exceptions without accountable owners | Number of active exceptions lacking a current risk owner | Accountability failure |
| Exceptions with incomplete evidence | Number of active exceptions missing mandatory assessment, approval, or review evidence | Assurance deficiency |
| Average exception age | Average elapsed time since exception activation | Long-lived deviations |
| Exception concentration by control domain | Distribution of exceptions across security requirements | Recurrent control implementation weaknesses |
| Exceptions linked to incidents | Number of exceptions associated with incidents during the reporting period | Potential inadequacy of accepted controls |

A high number of exceptions linked to incidents does not by itself prove that the exceptions caused those incidents. The organization should investigate causal relationships rather than infer causation from correlation.

### 23.3 AI-Specific Operational Indicators

Organizations may also measure:

- Unauthorized AI tool invocation attempts.
- Policy enforcement failures involving AI-generated actions.
- Prompt-injection detections and confirmed exploitation.
- Sensitive-data exposure events involving AI inputs or outputs.
- Unapproved model or agent configuration changes.
- AI-related identity and privilege violations.
- Failed human-approval enforcement.
- Abnormal agent execution loops or excessive action sequences.
- Model or provider changes that trigger reassessment.
- Security findings identified during AI red-team exercises.
- Incidents associated with retrieval systems, embeddings, vector stores, or knowledge bases.
- Failures in AI-specific logging, traceability, and investigation evidence.

These indicators require reliable telemetry and consistent classification. High detection counts may indicate improved visibility rather than deteriorating security, while low counts may reflect inadequate monitoring.

### 23.4 Metric Interpretation and Thresholds

Thresholds should be defined using the organization's risk appetite, system criticality, historical baseline, and operational requirements.

| Condition | Possible management response |
|---|---|
| Increasing high-severity incidents | Review threat exposure, architecture, and preventive controls |
| Repeated incidents with the same root cause | Escalate systemic remediation and verify corrective actions |
| Rising expired-exception count | Review expiry automation, ownership, and enforcement |
| High renewal frequency | Investigate recurring technical debt and funding constraints |
| Increasing compensating-control failures | Reassess the acceptability of related exceptions |
| Poor evidence completeness | Improve logging, procedures, record retention, and staff training |
| Slow containment for high-impact incidents | Review response authority, automation, and playbooks |
| Large differences between business units | Investigate inconsistent governance implementation |

### 23.5 Management Dashboard Specification

An executive dashboard should include:

**Risk posture**
- Active incidents by severity.
- Active high-risk exceptions.
- Incidents involving sensitive data or consequential actions.
- AI systems affected by unresolved control deficiencies.

**Operational performance**
- Detection, triage, containment, and recovery times.
- Corrective actions overdue.
- Incident escalation compliance.
- Evidence completeness.

**Exception governance**
- Exceptions approaching expiry.
- Expired exceptions still active.
- Exceptions with repeated renewals.
- Compensating controls that failed testing.

**Assurance and improvement**
- Repeat incident rate.
- Corrective action validation results.
- Outstanding audit findings.
- Trends in AI-specific attack scenarios.

Each dashboard metric should have a defined owner, source system, reporting frequency, calculation method, and escalation threshold.

**Core Principle:** AI incident and exception metrics must measure control effectiveness and residual risk, not merely the volume of governance activities performed.

## 24. Lessons Learned and Post-Incident Review

A post-incident review determines what happened, why it happened, how the organization responded, and which changes are necessary to reduce the likelihood or impact of recurrence.

The purpose is not merely to document an incident or assign blame. It is to convert operational evidence into measurable improvements in architecture, controls, governance, and response capability.

AI incidents may involve several interacting causes, including model behavior, untrusted inputs, retrieval permissions, tool authorization, data governance, third-party dependencies, human oversight, and inadequate monitoring.

The review should therefore examine the complete system rather than assuming the model alone was responsible.

### 24.1 When a Post-Incident Review Is Required

A formal review should be required for incidents meeting criteria such as:

- Critical or high severity.
- Confirmed exposure of sensitive information.
- Unauthorized consequential AI actions.
- Material service disruption.
- Repeated incidents involving the same control.
- Failure of a compensating control supporting an active exception.
- Significant audit, contractual, regulatory, or customer impact.
- An incident that reveals a systemic governance weakness.
- A near miss that could plausibly have produced severe consequences.

Lower-severity incidents may be reviewed through a streamlined process, depending on organizational policy.

### 24.3 AI-Specific Lessons Learned

| Finding | Potential lesson | Example improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt injection led to unauthorized tool use | Model-level instructions were not sufficient enforcement | Implement independent tool authorization and least privilege |
| Sensitive content appeared in an AI response | Data access or output handling was inadequate | Strengthen retrieval authorization and data-loss controls |
| An agent repeated actions unexpectedly | Execution boundaries were insufficient | Introduce action limits, timeouts, loop detection, and safe termination |
| Incident reconstruction was incomplete | Critical execution events were not captured | Improve traceability across identity, gateway, model, and downstream systems |
| A third-party service delayed investigation | Provider evidence and escalation arrangements were inadequate | Establish contractual incident cooperation and evidence requirements |
| A compensating control failed | Approval assumptions were not supported by sufficient validation | Reassess the exception and strengthen independent control testing |
| Remediation did not prevent recurrence | The action addressed symptoms rather than the root cause | Conduct deeper causal analysis and verify corrective effectiveness |

### 24.4 Blameless Review and Accountability

A blameless review encourages accurate reporting and learning, but it does not eliminate accountability.

The review should distinguish between:
- Reasonable decisions made using the information available at the time.
- Systemic weaknesses in architecture, procedures, or governance.
- Ambiguous responsibilities or conflicting incentives.
- Deliberate violations of approved security requirements.
- Negligent or unauthorized actions requiring separate management review.

The review should focus on evidence and system conditions. Personnel matters, where necessary, should be handled through appropriate organizational processes rather than distorting the technical investigation.

## 25. Management Reporting and AI Governance Oversight

### 25.5 Governance Committee Review Questions

The AI governance committee should consider:

1. Are incidents increasing in frequency, severity, or business impact?
2. Are the same root causes appearing across multiple AI systems?
3. Are exceptions concentrated in specific control domains or business units?
4. Are exceptions being renewed because of genuine technical constraints or persistent underinvestment?
5. Are compensating controls independently tested?
6. Are high-risk incidents being escalated within the required timeframes?
7. Are remediation actions reducing repeat incidents?
8. Are changes to AI autonomy, integrations, and data access reflected in risk assessments?
9. Are third-party dependencies creating evidence or response limitations?
10. Are residual risks within the organization's approved risk appetite?
11. Do any decisions exceed the authority of the current risk owner?
12. Are additional resources or policy changes required?

**Core Principle:** Management reporting must translate AI incident and exception records into clear risk decisions, accountable actions, and evidence of governance effectiveness.

- Unique identifiers for AI systems, risks, controls, incidents, exceptions, findings, and actions.

- Defined record-retention requirements.
- Change history and audit trails.
- Validation rules to identify missing owners or broken relationships.
- Procedures for updating related records after material incidents.

Where a GRC platform supports automated relationships, those relationships should be tested to ensure that records remain accurate and accessible only to authorized users.

**Core Principle:** Integrated records provide traceability between AI systems, risks, controls, incidents, exceptions, and remediation, enabling enterprise-wide analysis rather than isolated case management.

## 27. Regulatory, Privacy, Contractual, and Third-Party Coordination

AI security incidents may create obligations beyond cybersecurity operations. Depending on the facts, an incident may require privacy assessment, legal review, contractual escalation, regulatory notification, customer communication, or coordination with an external AI provider.

The organization should establish procedures for identifying and evaluating these obligations without assuming that every AI incident is automatically a reportable regulatory event.

### 27.1 Coordination Requirements

| Function | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Cybersecurity | Investigates technical causes, coordinates containment, and preserves security evidence |
| Privacy | Assesses whether personal data was affected and evaluates applicable privacy obligations |
| Legal | Interprets legal requirements, contractual obligations, and relevant legal exposure |
| Compliance | Evaluates regulatory requirements and internal compliance implications |
| Procurement and vendor management | Coordinates provider notifications, contractual remedies, and vendor risk reviews |
| Business owner | Assesses operational and customer impact |
| Communications | Coordinates approved internal and external messaging |
| AI governance | Evaluates AI-specific governance, safety, oversight, and accountability implications |
| Records management | Supports evidence preservation and retention requirements |

### 27.2 Personal Data Incident Assessment

Where personal data may be involved, the organization should establish:

- What categories of personal data were affected.
- Whether the data was accessed, disclosed, altered, lost, or made unavailable.
- The categories of individuals potentially affected.
- The number of records or individuals involved, where reasonably ascertainable.
- The sensitivity of the information.
- The likelihood and severity of harm.
- Whether the data was encrypted or otherwise protected.
- Whether the event resulted from unauthorized AI retrieval, disclosure, training, inference, or downstream processing.
- Which controllers, processors, and providers have relevant responsibilities.
- Whether notification or documentation obligations apply.

The organization should involve its privacy and legal functions promptly. Applicable notification deadlines depend on the relevant law, the organization's role, the nature of the event, and the facts established during the assessment.

### 27.3 Third-Party AI Provider Coordination

When an incident involves an external model or AI service, the response plan should identify:
- The provider's security incident contact.
- Contractual notification and cooperation requirements.
- Available provider-side logs and investigation evidence.
- Data processing and retention arrangements.
- Model and service version information.
- Relevant service changes or known vulnerabilities.
- Support escalation and evidence-preservation procedures.
- Requirements for assessing downstream customer or business impact.

Provider statements should be treated as evidence to evaluate, not as a substitute for the organization's own risk assessment.

### 27.4 Contractual and Regulatory Assessment Record

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Incident ID | Internal reference |
| Potential obligation | Legal, regulatory, contractual, or policy requirement |
| Relevant jurisdiction | Applicable jurisdiction or contractual scope |
| Affected data or service | Relevant information or business capability |
| Responsible function | Legal, privacy, compliance, or other accountable team |
| Assessment status | Not started, in progress, completed, or escalated |
| Decision | Required action and rationale |
| Notification requirement | Whether notification is required, not required, or unresolved |
| Deadline | Applicable deadline, if established |
| Approval authority | Authorized decision-maker |
| Evidence | Supporting analysis and correspondence |

### 27.5 AI-Specific Governance Considerations

The assessment may also need to consider:
- Whether the system's intended purpose remains accurate.
- Whether the incident changes its risk classification.
- Whether human oversight mechanisms functioned as intended.
- Whether a model or agent performed actions beyond its documented scope.
- Whether training, evaluation, or operational data was affected.
- Whether a high-impact use case requires additional safety assessment.
- Whether the incident reveals deficiencies in the AI provider's controls.
- Whether deployment restrictions or suspension are necessary.

Applicable frameworks and regulations should be assessed according to scope, jurisdiction, system role, and effective requirements. A generic incident template cannot determine legal obligations without those facts.

**Core Principle:** AI incident response must coordinate cybersecurity investigation with privacy, legal, compliance, contractual, and AI governance assessments whenever the facts make those functions relevant.

## 28. AI Incident Tabletop Exercises and Response Readiness Testing

An incident response plan should be tested before a serious event occurs. Tabletop exercises allow teams to evaluate whether responsibilities, escalation paths, evidence sources, and decision procedures work under realistic conditions.

AI security exercises should include scenarios involving model behavior, retrieval systems, external tools, delegated authority, provider dependencies, and failures of human oversight.

The objective is to validate the organization's ability to make timely, evidence-based decisions—not to prove that a written plan exists.

### 28.1 Exercise Objectives

A tabletop exercise should test whether the organization can:

1. Recognize an AI-related security incident.
2. Classify the event and assign a response owner.
3. Determine which systems, users, and data may be affected.
4. Contain harmful activity without causing unnecessary disruption.
5. Preserve evidence needed for investigation.
6. Identify the limits of available telemetry.
7. Coordinate cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and business stakeholders.
8. Evaluate whether existing exceptions contributed to the incident.
9. Make authorized decisions about system suspension or recovery.
10. Track corrective actions and validate improvements.

### 28.2 Scenario A — Prompt Injection and Unauthorized Data Retrieval

**Scenario:** An AI assistant retrieves internal knowledge-base content. A malicious document contains instructions intended to manipulate the assistant into disclosing information outside the user's authorization.

**Exercise injects:**
- The assistant generates an unexpected response.
- Logs show a retrieval operation involving a restricted document.
- The team cannot immediately determine whether the content was disclosed externally.
- A business user reports that the response contained confidential material.

**Discussion questions:**
- What triggers incident declaration?
- Who can disable retrieval or restrict the application?
- Can the team identify the exact document and user context?
- Are retrieval permissions enforced independently of model instructions?
- What evidence is needed to determine whether disclosure occurred?
- Does an active exception affect logging or authorization?
- Who assesses potential privacy or contractual obligations?
- What criteria must be met before the service is restored?

**Expected evidence:** Incident record, containment decision, retrieval traces, access-control evidence, impact assessment, and corrective action plan.

### 28.3 Scenario B — AI Agent Performs Unauthorized CRM Actions

**Scenario:** An AI agent has permission to retrieve customer records and perform selected CRM updates. Following manipulated instructions, it initiates unauthorized changes.

**Exercise injects:**
- Several customer records are modified.
- The agent's activity log shows repeated tool calls.
- The downstream application accepts the operations.
- The team discovers that approval was enforced through the application interface but not through the underlying API.

**Discussion questions:**
- Can the team stop the agent without disabling the entire CRM platform?
- Does the downstream system independently validate authorization?
- Which credentials or tokens must be revoked?
- How are affected records identified and restored?
- Does the event reveal an architectural trust-boundary failure?
- Which other agents use the same integration pattern?
- How is remediation tested against direct API invocation?
- Who authorizes recovery?

**Expected evidence:** Tool-call trace, identity records, downstream transaction logs, affected-record inventory, root cause analysis, and validated authorization remediation.

### 28.4 Scenario C — Third-Party AI Provider Incident

**Scenario:** An external AI provider reports a security incident affecting a service used by the organization.

**Exercise injects:**
- The provider initially supplies limited technical details.
- The organization cannot determine whether its data was involved.
- The service supports a business-critical workflow.
- Contractual requirements specify incident cooperation and notification.

**Discussion questions:**
- Who contacts the provider?
- Which internal services depend on the affected provider?
- Can the organization restrict or suspend data transmission?
- What alternative service or manual workflow is available?
- Which contractual and regulatory assessments are required?
- How will the organization handle uncertainty about data exposure?
- What evidence is required before resuming normal use?

**Expected evidence:** Provider incident record, dependency assessment, business continuity decision, data exposure assessment, and recovery approval.

### 28.5 Scenario D — Compensating Control Failure

**Scenario:** An AI agent operates under an approved security exception. The exception relies on a gateway that limits the agent to approved tools. A configuration change permits an unauthorized operation.

**Exercise injects:**
- The gateway's policy configuration has changed.
- The exception is still active.
- The monitoring alert is delayed.
- The remediation deadline is approaching.

**Discussion questions:**
- Does the control failure trigger immediate suspension?
- Who owns the risk decision?
- Does the incident require escalation beyond the original approver?
- What evidence demonstrates the extent of the unauthorized access?
- Should the exception be revoked or reassessed?
- Which other systems use the same gateway configuration?
- How should the organization test the corrected enforcement mechanism?

**Expected evidence:** Exception review, control failure record, incident investigation, suspension decision, remediation plan, and retest results.

### 28.6 Exercise Evaluation Matrix

| Capability | Evaluation question | Evidence of effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Detection | Was the event recognized using available telemetry? | Alert or reporting record |
| Classification | Was severity assigned consistently? | Documented classification rationale |
| Escalation | Were the correct decision-makers engaged? | Escalation log |
| Containment | Were harmful actions restricted promptly? | Containment evidence |
| Investigation | Could the team reconstruct relevant events? | Timeline and evidence records |
| Data assessment | Could the team identify affected information? | Data exposure assessment |
| Exception governance | Were related exceptions identified and reviewed? | Linked exception record |
| Coordination | Were specialist functions engaged when required? | Decision and communication records |
| Recovery | Were restoration criteria established and validated? | Recovery approval and test results |
| Improvement | Were gaps assigned to accountable owners? | Corrective action register |

### 28.7 Exercise Follow-Up

Within the organization's defined post-exercise period, the facilitator should produce:
- A summary of objectives and participants.
- Decisions made and assumptions used.
- Gaps in roles, procedures, telemetry, and authority.
- Required corrective actions.
- Owners and target dates.
- Priorities based on risk.
- Retesting criteria.
- Approval of the final exercise report.

Exercises should be repeated when material architecture changes, significant incidents occur, or important response capabilities are introduced.

**Core Principle:** Incident readiness is demonstrated through tested decisions, reliable evidence, effective containment, and completed corrective actions—not through documentation alone.

## 30. Final Governance Principle — Incident and Exception Management Must Produce Verifiable Risk Reduction

AI security incident management and exception governance are effective only when they change the organization's risk posture in a demonstrable way.

A completed report, an approved exception, or a closed ticket is not sufficient evidence of effective governance. The organization must establish whether the underlying exposure has been contained, whether corrective actions address the actual cause, whether compensating controls work as intended, and whether the remaining risk is explicitly owned.

### 30.1 Enterprise Effectiveness Criteria

An organization should be able to demonstrate that:

- AI security incidents are identified, classified, and escalated consistently.
- Investigation procedures preserve reliable evidence and distinguish facts from assumptions.
- AI-specific risks involving model behavior, retrieval, tools, identities, and downstream actions are evaluated.
- Containment and recovery decisions are made by authorized personnel.
- Root cause analyses identify technical, process, and governance contributors.
- Corrective actions have accountable owners and measurable validation criteria.
- Security exceptions are documented, time-bound, and approved within delegated authority.
- Compensating controls are independently assessed and tested.
- Expired exceptions do not remain silently authorized.
- Incidents and exceptions are linked to the relevant AI systems, risks, controls, and findings.
- Privacy, legal, compliance, and contractual obligations are evaluated when relevant.
- Management reporting identifies systemic weaknesses and decisions requiring intervention.
- Lessons learned result in verifiable improvements.
- Governance records are retained and protected according to approved requirements.

### 30.2 Final Assurance Checklist

| Assurance question | Expected evidence |
|---|---|
| Can the organization identify the affected AI system and owner? | AI system inventory record |
| Can investigators reconstruct relevant activity? | Logs, traces, timestamps, and evidence records |
| Was the incident classified and escalated appropriately? | Incident classification and escalation records |
| Were affected data and business processes assessed? | Impact assessment |
| Was the root cause established or uncertainty documented? | Investigation report |
| Were corrective actions assigned and validated? | Action register and test results |
| Was any related exception reviewed? | Linked exception assessment and decision |
| Are active exceptions within their authorized scope and duration? | Exception register and monitoring evidence |
| Are compensating controls effective? | Test results and ongoing monitoring |
| Are legal, privacy, and contractual questions addressed where applicable? | Specialist assessments and recorded decisions |
| Are recurring weaknesses identified across systems? | Trend analysis and cross-register relationships |
| Has closure been authorized? | Closure decision and supporting evidence |
| Has the organization demonstrated risk reduction? | Verified control improvements and updated residual-risk assessment |

### 30.3 Final Core Principle

**AI incident and exception governance must produce verifiable risk reduction through evidence-based investigation, authorized risk decisions, effective compensating controls, accountable remediation, and independent validation.**

The ultimate measure of success is not how many incidents were reported or exceptions were approved. It is whether the organization can detect and contain AI-related threats, make defensible decisions under uncertainty, prevent recurring failures, and demonstrate that its AI systems operate within approved security and risk boundaries.
