# 19.06 — NIST AI RMF Assessment

## Part 1 — Foundations, Assessment Methodology, and the GOVERN Function

### 1. Introduction to NIST AI RMF Assessment

The NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides a voluntary, structured approach for organizations to manage risks associated with the design, development, deployment, evaluation, and use of artificial intelligence systems.

An assessment based on the NIST AI RMF evaluates how effectively an organization identifies, understands, measures, manages, and governs AI-related risks. It considers organizational governance, technical characteristics, intended use, affected stakeholders, and the context in which an AI system operates.

Unlike an assessment focused exclusively on cybersecurity vulnerabilities, an AI RMF assessment can examine a broader range of risks, including validity, reliability, transparency, explainability, privacy, fairness, security, and accountability.

The framework is intended to support risk-informed decisions rather than provide a universal pass-or-fail certification test.

### 2. Purpose of an AI RMF Assessment

The purpose of an AI RMF assessment is to determine whether the organization's AI risk management practices are appropriate for its AI systems, intended uses, and risk exposure.

An assessment helps identify governance weaknesses, incomplete risk processes, insufficient technical testing, unclear accountability, and ineffective risk treatments.

It also helps management understand whether existing practices provide sufficient evidence that AI risks are being addressed throughout the system lifecycle.

Assessment results can inform investment decisions, control improvements, supplier oversight, deployment approvals, and executive reporting.

The assessment should produce actionable findings rather than merely confirming that framework terminology appears in organizational policies.

### 3. Understanding the NIST AI RMF Structure

The NIST AI RMF 1.0, published in January 2023, organizes its core activities into four functions:

- **GOVERN:** Establishes policies, responsibilities, organizational culture, and processes for AI risk management.
- **MAP:** Establishes context and identifies the risks, impacts, stakeholders, and circumstances associated with an AI system.
- **MEASURE:** Uses appropriate qualitative and quantitative methods to analyze, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risks.
- **MANAGE:** Prioritizes risks and implements responses, treatments, and ongoing monitoring.

These functions are interconnected rather than strictly sequential. Governance supports all the other functions, while information gathered through mapping, measurement, and management can inform subsequent governance decisions.

An assessment should evaluate how effectively the functions work together in practice.

### 4. Understanding the AI RMF Core and Playbook

The AI RMF Core organizes activities into functions, categories, and subcategories. These elements describe outcomes and activities that organizations can use to structure their AI risk management practices.

The AI RMF Playbook provides suggested actions and implementation considerations that can help organizations apply the framework.

The Core provides the organizing structure for an assessment, while the Playbook offers practical guidance.

The Playbook should not automatically be treated as a mandatory checklist. Assessors should distinguish the framework's intended outcomes from illustrative implementation suggestions and determine which practices are appropriate for the organization.

### 5. Defining the Assessment Objective

An assessment objective describes what the organization wants to establish about its AI risk management capability.

Possible objectives include determining whether governance responsibilities are effective, evaluating the completeness of AI risk assessments, reviewing measurement practices, assessing risk treatment, or identifying gaps before deploying a new AI system.

The objective should be specific enough to guide evidence collection and support a defensible conclusion.

For example, an objective may be to determine whether a customer-facing generative AI assistant has an established process for identifying, evaluating, treating, and monitoring material security and privacy risks.

The objective should not promise to prove that an AI system is entirely safe or free from risk.

### 6. Defining the Assessment Scope

The assessment scope establishes the organizational boundaries, AI systems, business processes, lifecycle stages, and risk management activities included in the review.

The scope may cover an entire enterprise AI governance program, a specific AI product, a business unit, an AI supplier relationship, or a particular use case.

The assessor should identify relevant systems, datasets, infrastructure, external providers, human operators, and downstream integrations.

Scope exclusions should be documented and justified, especially where excluded components may affect the risks of the system being assessed.

A narrow scope can be appropriate, but the resulting conclusions must not imply that unassessed systems or processes have also been evaluated.

### 7. Identifying the AI Systems Under Assessment

The assessor should establish which AI systems, applications, models, and AI-enabled processes fall within the assessment scope.

An AI system may include more than the model itself. Relevant components may include data pipelines, retrieval systems, application interfaces, external APIs, orchestration tools, human review workflows, and monitoring services.

The assessment should distinguish between a standalone model, an integrated AI application, and the broader business process in which the application operates.

This distinction is important because material risks may originate in surrounding components rather than in the model alone.

### 8. Establishing the Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria define the outcomes and requirements against which evidence will be evaluated.

For a NIST AI RMF assessment, criteria may be derived from the framework's Core, applicable organizational policies, risk acceptance criteria, contractual commitments, and relevant legal or regulatory obligations.

The assessor should identify which criteria apply and explain how they will be interpreted.

Where an organization adopts only selected parts of the framework, the assessment should document that decision and avoid implying complete framework coverage.

Criteria should be sufficiently clear that another qualified assessor can understand how the conclusion was reached.

### 9. Distinguishing Assessment From Certification

The NIST AI RMF is a risk management framework, not a universal certification scheme with a single mandatory pass-or-fail test.

An organization may conduct an internal assessment, engage an independent assessor, or use the framework to support a broader assurance program.

The result is an evidence-based evaluation of the organization's practices against the defined criteria and scope.

A statement such as “assessed against the NIST AI RMF” should identify the framework version, assessment scope, methodology, and limitations.

The organization should not imply that NIST has certified its AI system merely because it conducted an AI RMF assessment.

### 10. Understanding the Difference Between AI RMF and AI Auditing

An AI RMF assessment is an evaluation structured around the framework's risk management outcomes. An AI audit is a defined assurance activity that may use the AI RMF as one set of criteria.

An organization can use the framework for self-assessment, gap analysis, risk program development, or independent audit planning.

An audit may additionally evaluate compliance with internal requirements, contractual obligations, or other standards.

The assessor should clearly identify whether the engagement is advisory, self-assessment, internal audit, independent assurance, or another type of review.

The distinction affects the expected independence, reporting language, evidence requirements, and confidence that stakeholders should place in the conclusions.

### 11. Identifying Assessment Stakeholders

AI RMF assessments may involve AI developers, system owners, information security teams, privacy specialists, legal and compliance personnel, business owners, procurement teams, risk managers, and executive leadership.

External stakeholders may include suppliers, customers, affected individuals, regulators, and other parties relevant to the system's intended use.

The assessor should identify who has responsibility for providing evidence, explaining processes, approving risk decisions, and implementing remediation.

Stakeholder participation should reflect the system's actual context and potential impacts.

An assessment based only on the development team may overlook business risks, privacy obligations, operational dependencies, or the experiences of affected individuals.

### 12. Establishing Assessment Independence and Objectivity

Assessment objectivity requires conclusions to be based on evidence and established criteria rather than personal preference or pressure from stakeholders.

Where possible, assessors should avoid evaluating their own work without appropriate safeguards.

The required degree of independence depends on the assessment's purpose, organizational arrangements, and applicable audit requirements.

An internal advisory review may use a different independence model from an independent assurance engagement.

Potential conflicts of interest should be identified and managed, and material limitations should be disclosed in the assessment report.

### 13. Understanding the AI System Lifecycle

AI risk management extends across the lifecycle of a system, including planning, design, data preparation, development, testing, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement.

Different risks can emerge at different stages. Data preparation may introduce quality or privacy issues, deployment may create access-control weaknesses, and operational changes may affect model behavior or reliability.

The assessor should determine which lifecycle stages are relevant to the scope and whether risk management responsibilities continue after deployment.

A one-time assessment may not provide sufficient assurance for systems that change frequently or operate in dynamic environments.

### 14. Understanding the Socio-Technical Nature of AI Risk

AI systems operate within technical, organizational, and human environments.

Risk can result from model behavior, data characteristics, application design, user expectations, business incentives, operational procedures, or interactions among these factors.

For example, a model may generate a technically plausible response that becomes harmful when used by an employee who assumes the response is always correct.

The assessor should therefore examine both technical controls and the surrounding organizational processes.

An assessment focused exclusively on model performance may miss risks arising from human reliance, unclear accountability, or inappropriate deployment context.

### 15. Establishing the Organizational Context

The assessor should understand the organization's objectives, operating environment, AI strategy, risk appetite, stakeholder expectations, and existing governance arrangements.

Relevant context includes the organization's industry, business model, geographic reach, critical processes, regulatory environment, and dependence on third-party AI services.

Context determines which AI risks matter most and what safeguards are appropriate.

For example, an AI tool used to summarize public information may require a different assessment approach from an AI system that influences access to financial services or supports a critical operational process.

### 16. Identifying Intended Use

Intended use describes the purpose for which an AI system is designed, approved, and deployed.

The assessor should determine whether the intended use is documented clearly and whether actual usage remains within the approved boundaries.

Relevant considerations include users, decisions supported, data inputs, outputs, permitted actions, operating conditions, and foreseeable misuse.

An AI system may be technically capable of performing tasks that the organization has not approved.

Assessment should determine whether organizational controls, technical restrictions, and user guidance help maintain the intended-use boundaries.

### 17. Identifying Foreseeable Misuse

Foreseeable misuse includes reasonably predictable uses or behaviors that could create risks even when they fall outside the formally approved purpose.

Examples include entering confidential information into an unapproved service, treating generated answers as verified facts, bypassing human review, or using an AI assistant to perform unauthorized actions.

The assessor should consider how misuse could occur and whether appropriate preventive, detective, or corrective controls exist.

The analysis should be proportionate to the system's capabilities, accessibility, context, and potential impact.

Foreseeable misuse should not be confused with every theoretically imaginable failure scenario.

### 18. Establishing AI System Ownership

Every material AI system should have clearly defined ownership and accountability arrangements appropriate to the organization's governance model.

Ownership may be divided among the business owner, technical owner, data owner, risk owner, supplier manager, and control operators.

The assessor should determine who is accountable for approving the use case, managing risk, authorizing changes, reviewing performance, and deciding whether deployment should continue.

Where responsibilities are shared, the allocation should be documented.

Unclear ownership can lead to unresolved risks, incomplete evidence, and delayed responses when a system's behavior or operating environment changes.

### 19. Identifying Affected Stakeholders

Affected stakeholders are individuals or groups who may experience benefits, harms, or other consequences from an AI system.

They may include direct users, employees, customers, applicants, suppliers, communities, and people whose information is processed by the system.

The assessor should determine whether relevant stakeholder groups have been identified and whether their interests have been considered in risk management decisions.

The analysis should be appropriate to the system's intended use and potential impacts.

Stakeholder identification should not be reduced to listing the system's direct users when other groups may be materially affected.

### 20. Understanding Organizational Risk Appetite

Risk appetite expresses the types and amounts of risk an organization is prepared to pursue or retain in support of its objectives.

An AI RMF assessment should examine how the organization translates its risk appetite into practical AI decisions.

Relevant evidence may include risk policies, approval thresholds, escalation rules, deployment criteria, and documented risk acceptance.

Risk appetite may vary by use case and impact. An organization may accept limited operational uncertainty in an internal productivity tool while applying stricter criteria to an AI system supporting critical decisions.

The assessor should evaluate whether actual decisions are consistent with approved risk criteria and appropriate authority.

### 21. Understanding AI Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance establishes acceptable boundaries or variation around defined risk objectives or thresholds.

For example, an organization may define a threshold for unresolved critical vulnerabilities, unacceptable data exposure, or degraded performance in a monitored AI application.

The assessor should determine whether relevant thresholds are defined, monitored, and linked to appropriate action.

Thresholds should be justified by the system's context and the organization's risk criteria.

A threshold is useful only when the organization knows what happens when it is exceeded and has a process for making the required decision.

### 22. Understanding the Difference Between Risk and Uncertainty

Risk assessment involves evaluating possible adverse outcomes, their likelihood or plausibility, and their consequences. Uncertainty concerns limitations in knowledge about system behavior, operating conditions, data, or future events.

AI systems can introduce uncertainty because behavior may depend on probabilistic generation, changing data, external services, or complex interactions.

The assessor should identify material uncertainties and determine whether the organization has suitable methods for investigating, monitoring, or limiting them.

Where reliable quantitative estimates are unavailable, qualitative assessments may be appropriate if the methodology and limitations are documented.

False precision should be avoided when evidence cannot support numerical estimates.

### 23. Establishing an AI Risk Taxonomy

An AI risk taxonomy provides a consistent way to categorize and communicate risks.

Possible categories include security, privacy, safety, reliability, validity, fairness, transparency, explainability, accountability, operational resilience, legal compliance, and third-party dependency.

An organization may use a tailored taxonomy that reflects its business context and risk methodology.

The assessor should evaluate whether categories are sufficiently comprehensive for the systems under review and whether definitions are applied consistently.

A taxonomy helps organize analysis, but it should not become a substitute for describing specific risk scenarios and their consequences.

### 24. Identifying AI-Specific Risk Scenarios

A risk scenario describes how a threat, condition, or failure could lead to an adverse outcome in a particular context.

For example, an AI assistant with excessive access to enterprise documents may disclose confidential information through a generated response.

Another scenario may involve an AI-enabled workflow executing an unauthorized transaction because its tool permissions are too broad.

The assessor should identify relevant scenarios based on system architecture, data, intended use, threat exposure, and operational context.

Each material scenario should be sufficiently specific to support assessment, treatment, and monitoring.

### 25. Distinguishing AI Risks From Conventional IT Risks

AI systems share many risks with conventional information technology, including unauthorized access, data loss, service disruption, insecure configuration, and supplier dependency.

However, AI may introduce additional considerations such as prompt injection, model manipulation, data poisoning, unreliable generated content, model extraction, and unexpected interactions with tools.

The assessor should identify which conventional controls remain applicable and which AI-specific risks require additional analysis.

The presence of an AI component does not make every risk unique to AI.

A sound assessment integrates established information security practices with additional measures justified by the AI system's characteristics.

### 26. Establishing the Assessment Methodology

The methodology defines how the assessor will gather evidence, evaluate practices, test controls, classify gaps, and formulate conclusions.

It may combine document review, interviews, observation, technical testing, sampling, configuration analysis, and examination of operational records.

The methodology should reflect the assessment objective, system complexity, risk exposure, and available evidence.

The assessor should document significant assumptions, limitations, and departures from the planned approach.

Consistent methodology supports repeatability and helps management understand the basis for the assessment results.

### 27. Selecting Appropriate Evidence Sources

Evidence sources may include policies, procedures, AI inventories, architecture diagrams, risk registers, test results, system logs, supplier assessments, meeting records, and deployment approvals.

Interviews can explain how processes operate, but interview statements alone may not demonstrate that controls operate effectively.

The assessor should seek corroboration through records, observation, configuration inspection, or testing where appropriate.

Evidence should be relevant to the criteria, sufficiently reliable, and representative of the process being assessed.

The assessment report should identify important evidence gaps that affect the strength of its conclusions.

### 28. Evaluating Documentary Evidence

Documentary evidence can demonstrate that governance arrangements and procedures have been defined.

Examples include AI policies, risk assessment procedures, model documentation requirements, incident response plans, and supplier review standards.

However, the existence of a document does not prove that the process is followed.

The assessor should compare documented requirements with operational evidence to determine whether the intended practices are implemented.

Documents should also be checked for scope, approval, ownership, currency, and consistency with actual system configurations.

### 29. Conducting Stakeholder Interviews

Interviews help the assessor understand responsibilities, operating practices, risk decisions, and process limitations.

Questions should be tailored to the interviewee's role and the assessment criteria.

For example, a business owner may explain intended use and risk acceptance, while an engineer may explain data flows, model deployment, and monitoring.

The assessor should distinguish personal understanding from formally approved organizational processes.

Important interview statements should be corroborated where possible, particularly when they support conclusions about high-impact controls.

### 30. Conducting Technical Assessment Activities

Technical assessment activities may include configuration reviews, access-control tests, vulnerability assessments, adversarial testing, data-flow validation, and examination of model evaluation results.

The assessment should use methods appropriate to the system and the risks under investigation.

Testing should be authorized, controlled, and designed to avoid unnecessary disruption or exposure of sensitive data.

The assessor should document the test conditions, tools or methods used, limitations, and observed results.

Technical tests provide evidence about specific conditions; they do not establish that every possible failure mode has been eliminated.

### 31. Evaluating Evidence Quality

Evidence quality depends on factors such as relevance, reliability, completeness, currency, traceability, and the method through which it was obtained.

For example, a current production configuration may provide stronger evidence of implemented access restrictions than an outdated design document.

A supplier's general statement may require corroboration before it can support a conclusion about a specific service.

The assessor should determine whether evidence actually supports the claim being evaluated.

Where evidence is incomplete or contradictory, the uncertainty should be investigated or documented as a limitation.

### 32. Establishing Assessment Sampling

Sampling allows the assessor to examine a subset of systems, records, controls, or events when exhaustive testing is impractical.

The sampling approach should reflect the assessment objective, population size, risk, diversity of systems, and known weaknesses.

The assessor should document the population, selection method, sample rationale, and limitations.

High-risk systems or previously problematic controls may warrant targeted testing in addition to representative sampling.

Conclusions should not be generalized beyond what the sampling method and evidence reasonably support.

### 33. Evaluating AI RMF Implementation Coverage

Implementation coverage describes which relevant framework outcomes and activities have been addressed within the defined assessment scope.

The assessor should map applicable Core elements to organizational processes and evidence.

The mapping should distinguish activities that are implemented, partially implemented, planned, not implemented, or not applicable under the documented methodology.

A broad framework mapping can help identify omissions, but a completed mapping exercise does not itself demonstrate effectiveness.

The assessor should validate material implementation claims through appropriate evidence.

### 34. Developing an AI RMF Assessment Matrix

An assessment matrix links framework outcomes to organizational practices and evidence.

Useful fields may include the function, category, subcategory, applicability, implementation status, evidence reference, identified gap, risk implication, action owner, and remediation status.

The matrix should use consistent definitions and provide traceability between criteria, evidence, and conclusions.

Organizations may tailor the matrix to the system's risk profile and assessment purpose.

The matrix is a working assessment tool rather than a substitute for professional judgment or a complete written report.

### 35. Understanding Implementation Status Categories

Organizations often use status categories to communicate assessment results.

Possible categories include implemented, partially implemented, planned, not implemented, and not applicable.

The organization should define the evidence required for each category.

For example, a planned control should not be classified as implemented simply because funding has been approved.

Similarly, a control may exist but be only partially implemented if it covers some relevant systems but not others.

Status categories should not be presented as official NIST certification ratings unless a separately defined and valid assessment scheme establishes them.

### 36. Distinguishing Design Adequacy From Operating Effectiveness

Design adequacy asks whether a process or control is appropriately designed to achieve its intended outcome.

Operating effectiveness asks whether it is actually implemented and performs as intended under relevant conditions.

An organization may have a well-designed AI risk assessment procedure that is not used for new deployments.

Conversely, teams may perform useful informal reviews without a documented process that provides consistent coverage.

A comprehensive assessment should consider both design and operation where relevant.

The distinction helps identify whether remediation requires better procedures, stronger implementation, improved monitoring, or all three.

### 37. Establishing Risk-Based Assessment Priorities

Not every AI system requires the same level of assessment effort.

Priorities may be influenced by business criticality, data sensitivity, autonomy, user population, external exposure, potential impact, regulatory context, and prior incidents.

The assessor should document how priorities are established and whether the chosen approach is consistent with the organization's risk criteria.

Higher-priority systems may require more detailed testing, broader stakeholder engagement, or more frequent reassessment.

Lower-priority classifications should remain subject to review when system capabilities, usage, or context change.

### 38. Understanding Proportionality in AI Risk Management

Proportionality means that the depth and formality of risk management should be appropriate to the system's context and potential consequences.

A low-impact internal summarization tool may need a different assessment depth from an AI system supporting critical infrastructure or consequential decisions about individuals.

Proportionality should not be used to dismiss risks without analysis.

The assessor should examine whether the organization has a defensible rationale for the measures selected and whether essential safeguards remain in place.

The framework supports contextual risk management rather than a single uniform control set for every AI application.

### 39. Evaluating Risk Management Throughout the Lifecycle

The assessor should determine whether risk management activities continue as the AI system evolves.

Relevant lifecycle events may include model updates, new data sources, changes to access permissions, provider changes, new user groups, and expansion into additional business processes.

Evidence may include change requests, reassessments, release approvals, monitoring records, and incident reviews.

An initial assessment may become outdated when the system's capabilities or operating context change materially.

The organization should define reassessment triggers and establish responsibility for initiating them.

### 40. Evaluating the Connection Between Governance and Technical Controls

AI governance establishes objectives, responsibilities, decision rights, and oversight. Technical controls implement or enforce relevant requirements within systems and infrastructure.

The assessor should determine whether governance commitments are translated into technical and operational practices.

For example, a policy requiring approved data sources should be supported by access controls, data validation, or other appropriate enforcement mechanisms.

A technical safeguard may also require governance processes to assign ownership, review exceptions, and manage changes.

Effective risk management requires these layers to work together.

### 41. Introduction to the GOVERN Function

The GOVERN function establishes the organizational structures and practices necessary to manage AI risks.

It supports the other AI RMF functions by defining accountability, policies, risk culture, workforce competence, third-party oversight, and processes for addressing emerging issues.

Governance should influence AI decisions throughout the lifecycle rather than operate as a separate compliance activity.

An assessment of GOVERN should examine whether the organization has established appropriate arrangements and whether those arrangements operate in practice.

### 42. Understanding GOVERN Categories

In NIST AI RMF 1.0, the GOVERN function includes categories covering policies, accountability structures, workforce diversity and competence, organizational culture, stakeholder engagement, and third-party considerations.

These categories address different but connected aspects of organizational readiness.

The assessor should evaluate the outcomes relevant to the system and assessment scope rather than assuming every organization must implement identical procedures.

Governance evidence should demonstrate how responsibilities and expectations are translated into decisions and operational practices.

### 43. Assessing AI Risk Management Policies

AI risk management policies establish the organization's expectations for identifying, evaluating, treating, and monitoring AI risks.

The assessor should examine whether the policies define scope, responsibilities, approval requirements, escalation procedures, and relationships with existing risk management processes.

Policies should be approved by appropriate authority and communicated to relevant personnel.

The assessor should also evaluate whether actual practices align with the documented expectations.

A policy that is disconnected from procurement, development, deployment, and operational decisions provides limited evidence of effective governance.

### 44. Assessing AI Policy Scope and Applicability

AI policies should explain which systems, organizational units, personnel, suppliers, and use cases are covered.

The scope should account for relevant AI use, including embedded AI capabilities and externally provided services where appropriate.

The assessor should examine whether applicability decisions are documented and whether exclusions are justified.

Policies that cover only formally developed models may overlook third-party applications or AI capabilities embedded in existing software.

The organization should have a practical way to determine whether a new use case falls within the policy's requirements.

### 45. Assessing AI Policy Approval and Ownership

The assessor should determine who owns the AI risk management policy and who has authority to approve it.

Evidence may include approval records, assigned responsibilities, review schedules, and governance committee decisions.

Policy ownership should include responsibility for maintaining the policy when the organization's technology, risks, or obligations change.

The assessor should verify that approval authority is appropriate to the policy's organizational significance.

Unclear ownership can result in conflicting requirements, outdated guidance, and inconsistent implementation.

### 46. Assessing AI Policy Review and Maintenance

AI policies should be reviewed when material changes occur and at intervals appropriate to the organization's governance arrangements.

Triggers may include significant incidents, new system capabilities, changes in supplier arrangements, new business uses, or changes in applicable obligations.

The assessor should review whether policy updates are evaluated, approved, communicated, and reflected in relevant procedures.

Evidence may include revision histories, meeting records, change assessments, and communication records.

A periodic review date alone does not demonstrate that the policy remains suitable for current risks.

### 47. Assessing Integration With Enterprise Risk Management

AI risk management should connect with the organization's broader enterprise risk management approach.

The assessor should determine whether AI risks can be escalated to appropriate decision-makers and whether they are evaluated consistently with other material business risks.

Relevant evidence may include risk registers, governance reporting, escalation procedures, and risk acceptance records.

Integration helps management consider AI risks alongside financial, operational, strategic, legal, and reputational exposure.

The assessment should identify whether AI risks are isolated within technical teams without appropriate enterprise oversight.

### 48. Assessing Integration With Information Security Governance

AI risk governance should connect with information security policies, the information security management system, and established security risk treatment processes.

Relevant activities may include identity management, data protection, secure development, incident response, supplier security, and operational resilience.

The assessor should identify overlaps and gaps between AI risk management and existing security governance.

The objective is not necessarily to create a separate process for every AI risk, but to ensure that existing processes address AI-specific characteristics where needed.

### 49. Assessing Integration With Privacy Governance

AI systems may process personal information during training, inference, retrieval, evaluation, or monitoring.

The assessor should determine whether privacy responsibilities and assessment processes are integrated into AI governance.

Relevant evidence may include privacy assessments, data inventories, approved processing purposes, retention rules, and access controls.

The assessment should consider whether privacy risks are identified early enough to influence design and deployment decisions.

The existence of a privacy program does not automatically demonstrate that every AI use case has been evaluated appropriately.

### 50. Assessing Integration With Legal and Compliance Functions

AI risk decisions may depend on applicable laws, contractual requirements, industry obligations, and organizational commitments.

The assessor should determine whether the organization identifies relevant requirements and assigns responsibility for interpreting and monitoring them.

Evidence may include obligations registers, legal reviews, compliance assessments, and documented approval decisions.

The organization should distinguish between requirements that are legally mandatory and practices adopted voluntarily as risk management measures.

The assessment should not assume that use of the NIST AI RMF alone establishes legal compliance.

### 51. Assessing AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities

Effective governance requires clear responsibilities for decisions and operational activities.

Roles may include executive sponsor, AI system owner, business owner, data owner, risk owner, security lead, privacy lead, model developer, deployment owner, and independent reviewer.

The assessor should determine whether responsibilities are documented, understood, and consistent with actual decision-making authority.

Where responsibilities overlap, the organization should establish how conflicts and unresolved issues are escalated.

Role descriptions should be evaluated against operational evidence rather than accepted solely because they exist in an organizational chart.

### 52. Assessing Executive Accountability

Executive accountability concerns whether senior leadership provides direction and oversight for material AI risks.

Evidence may include approved policies, resource decisions, risk acceptance approvals, governance meeting records, and responses to significant findings.

The assessor should determine whether executives receive information appropriate to their responsibilities and whether material issues receive timely attention.

Executive accountability does not require senior leaders to make every technical decision.

It requires appropriate oversight, clear authority, and accountability for organizational risk decisions.

### 53. Assessing Board-Level AI Oversight

Where board-level oversight is part of the organization's governance model, the assessor should determine whether AI-related risks are reported at an appropriate level.

Relevant evidence may include board or committee agendas, risk reports, decision records, and follow-up actions.

The assessment should examine whether reporting focuses on material exposure, significant changes, and decisions requiring oversight.

A board presentation alone does not prove effective governance if key risks are omitted or required actions are not followed through.

### 54. Assessing AI Governance Committees

An organization may use an AI governance committee or integrate AI risk oversight into existing governance forums.

The assessor should examine the committee's mandate, membership, authority, meeting cadence, escalation arrangements, and decision records.

Membership should provide access to the expertise needed for the organization's AI risk profile.

The committee should be able to resolve issues or escalate decisions that exceed its authority.

The existence of a committee should not be treated as sufficient evidence that governance is effective.

### 55. Assessing AI Risk Decision Rights

Decision rights define who may approve, reject, restrict, or suspend AI use under specified circumstances.

The assessor should examine whether decision rights are clear for system deployment, material changes, risk acceptance, supplier selection, and incident response.

Evidence may include approval matrices, workflow configurations, governance records, and exception approvals.

Decision rights should align with the significance of the decision and the accountability of the responsible role.

Ambiguous decision rights can result in unauthorized deployment or unresolved disputes over risk ownership.

### 56. Assessing AI Risk Escalation Procedures

Escalation procedures define how significant risks and unresolved issues are brought to appropriate decision-makers.

The assessor should determine whether escalation triggers, reporting channels, response expectations, and authority levels are documented.

Evidence may include incident escalations, risk committee records, exception workflows, and management decisions.

The assessment should consider whether personnel can escalate concerns without inappropriate obstruction.

A procedure should be evaluated using actual cases where possible, rather than solely through document review.

### 57. Assessing AI Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance should be performed by an authorized decision-maker with sufficient understanding of the risk and its implications.

The assessor should examine whether the organization defines acceptable risk thresholds, required approvals, documentation, and review conditions.

Acceptance records should identify the risk, rationale, residual exposure, relevant safeguards, and any expiry or reassessment conditions.

Technical teams should not automatically be assumed to have authority to accept business risks.

The assessor should identify cases where risk has effectively been accepted through inaction without the required authorization.

### 58. Assessing AI Governance Operating Models

An AI governance operating model describes how responsibilities and processes are organized across the enterprise.

Possible models include centralized governance, federated governance, and hybrid arrangements.

A centralized model may improve consistency, while a federated model may allow business units to respond to local context. A hybrid model may combine enterprise standards with distributed implementation.

The assessor should determine whether the chosen model is appropriate for the organization's size, complexity, and risk profile.

The label assigned to the model is less important than whether accountability, oversight, and implementation work effectively.

### 59. Assessing Three Lines and Assurance Responsibilities

Some organizations use a three-lines model or a similar structure to distinguish management responsibilities, risk and compliance oversight, and independent assurance.

The assessor should understand how the organization allocates AI risk management and assurance responsibilities.

The structure should support effective challenge and avoid inappropriate conflicts of interest.

The precise organizational model may vary, but important control owners, oversight functions, and independent reviewers should be identifiable.

The assessment should focus on actual responsibilities and independence rather than assuming a particular organizational chart is universally required by the NIST AI RMF.

### 60. Assessing AI Governance Across Business Units

AI governance may be distributed across multiple business units, each with different systems, data, and operating needs.

The assessor should determine whether enterprise requirements are communicated consistently and whether local implementation is monitored.

Evidence may include business-unit inventories, risk assessments, approval records, training, and governance reporting.

The organization should identify inconsistent practices and clarify where local decisions require enterprise approval.

Distributed responsibility should not result in material AI systems escaping inventory, risk assessment, or oversight.

### 61. Assessing AI Governance for Shadow AI

Shadow AI refers to AI tools or services used without appropriate organizational approval or oversight.

Such use may expose confidential information, create unmanaged supplier dependencies, or introduce unreliable outputs into business processes.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has processes to identify, evaluate, and appropriately manage unauthorized or previously unknown AI usage.

Possible evidence includes approved-service inventories, procurement records, endpoint or network monitoring, employee guidance, and incident reports.

The assessment should consider both technical restrictions and whether employees have practical access to approved alternatives.

### 62. Assessing AI Governance for Embedded AI Capabilities

AI capabilities may be incorporated into existing enterprise applications without being procured as standalone AI products.

Examples include AI features in collaboration platforms, customer relationship management systems, productivity software, and security tools.

The assessor should determine whether the organization can identify relevant embedded capabilities and assess their data handling, permissions, and intended use.

Procurement and change management processes should help identify when existing software introduces new AI-related risks.

The organization should not assume that an application previously approved for conventional use remains unchanged from a risk perspective after significant AI features are enabled.

### 63. Assessing AI Inventory Governance

An AI inventory provides visibility into the systems and use cases subject to governance.

The assessor should examine how systems are registered, updated, assigned owners, classified, and retired.

Relevant inventory fields may include purpose, provider, model, data types, deployment location, users, dependencies, risk classification, and approval status.

The inventory should be sufficiently complete to support risk-based oversight.

A list created only for a one-time assessment may provide limited assurance if it is not maintained as systems and uses change.

### 64. Assessing AI Intake and Approval Processes

An AI intake process allows the organization to evaluate proposed use cases before they are procured, developed, or deployed.

The assessor should determine whether the process identifies the intended purpose, affected stakeholders, data sensitivity, supplier dependencies, and potential consequences.

Approval criteria should determine whether a use case can proceed, requires additional assessment, or should be rejected.

Evidence may include intake forms, review records, approval workflows, and documented conditions.

The assessor should verify that teams do not bypass intake through informal purchasing, trial accounts, or unreviewed software features.

### 65. Assessing AI Procurement Governance

Procurement processes can help establish security, privacy, transparency, and assurance requirements before an AI service is acquired.

The assessor should determine whether procurement identifies AI-specific risks and involves the relevant stakeholders.

Evidence may include supplier due diligence, contractual reviews, security questionnaires, data-processing terms, and approval records.

The organization should assess whether requirements are appropriate to the service's capabilities, access, data processing, and business criticality.

Procurement approval alone does not establish that the service is secure or suitable for every intended use.

### 66. Assessing Third-Party AI Governance

Third-party AI governance addresses risks arising from providers, integrators, data suppliers, model developers, and other external dependencies.

The assessor should determine whether the organization identifies relevant suppliers, evaluates their risk, defines responsibilities, and monitors material changes.

Evidence may include supplier inventories, risk assessments, contractual clauses, assurance reports, and ongoing reviews.

The organization should consider the service actually used, not merely the supplier's overall reputation.

Third-party governance should also address what happens when a provider changes its service, limits transparency, or experiences a significant security incident.

### 67. Assessing AI Supplier Selection Criteria

Supplier selection criteria should consider whether the proposed service is appropriate for the intended use and risk profile.

Criteria may include security capabilities, data handling, reliability, transparency, contractual commitments, support arrangements, and available assurance evidence.

The assessor should determine whether the criteria are documented and applied consistently.

Where the organization selects a supplier despite identified limitations, the decision should be supported by appropriate risk evaluation and approval.

Commercial suitability alone does not establish that a supplier is acceptable from an AI risk management perspective.

### 68. Assessing AI Supplier Due Diligence

Due diligence provides information about a supplier's capabilities, controls, dependencies, and ability to meet relevant requirements.

The assessment may cover security certifications, independent reports, incident history, data-processing practices, resilience, and subcontractor arrangements.

The assessor should evaluate whether the due diligence is proportionate to supplier criticality and the sensitivity of the service.

Evidence should be current enough to support the decision and relevant to the specific service being acquired.

A generic questionnaire may be insufficient when the supplier has access to highly sensitive data or supports a critical business process.

### 69. Assessing AI Contractual Governance

Contracts may define data use, confidentiality, security requirements, incident notification, service availability, audit rights, and termination arrangements.

The assessor should determine whether relevant requirements are addressed and whether responsibilities are clearly allocated.

The organization should examine whether the contract permits the intended use and provides adequate safeguards for the data and service involved.

Where contract terms cannot meet internal requirements, the organization should assess the gap and determine whether additional controls or a different supplier are necessary.

Contractual language should be supported by operational oversight and available assurance evidence.

### 70. Assessing AI Supplier Change Management

AI suppliers may change models, service features, hosting arrangements, subprocessors, or data handling practices.

The assessor should determine whether the organization receives appropriate change information and evaluates material changes.

Relevant evidence may include provider notifications, contract provisions, internal change assessments, regression tests, and risk reassessments.

The organization should identify changes that may affect confidentiality, integrity, availability, performance, or approved use.

Where notification is limited, the organization should consider the implications for monitoring and assurance.

### 71. Assessing AI Supplier Exit and Portability

Supplier exit planning addresses how the organization will discontinue a service, migrate to an alternative, retrieve necessary information, and remove access.

The assessor should determine whether exit responsibilities and relevant dependencies are documented.

Evidence may include exit plans, data export arrangements, termination clauses, credential revocation procedures, and deletion confirmations.

The organization should consider whether switching providers would affect data availability, model behavior, business continuity, or audit evidence.

Exit planning should be proportionate to the supplier's criticality and the organization's dependency on the service.

### 72. Assessing Workforce Competence for AI Risk Management

AI risk management requires appropriate competence across governance, technical, business, and oversight roles.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified the knowledge and skills needed for relevant responsibilities.

Evidence may include role profiles, competence assessments, training plans, qualifications, and records of completed learning.

Competence requirements should reflect the decisions personnel are expected to make.

The organization should not assume that general AI awareness is sufficient for personnel responsible for security architecture, risk acceptance, or specialized testing.

### 73. Assessing AI Risk Awareness Training

AI risk awareness training helps personnel understand relevant risks, policies, reporting channels, and approved practices.

Topics may include confidential data handling, appropriate tool usage, output verification, prompt injection awareness, and reporting suspected incidents.

The assessor should determine whether training is relevant to employee roles and whether completion is tracked where required.

Training effectiveness may be evaluated through assessments, observed behavior, incident trends, or targeted reviews.

Attendance records alone do not demonstrate that employees understand or follow the required practices.

### 74. Assessing Role-Specific AI Training

Different roles require different levels of AI risk knowledge.

Developers may need training on secure AI development and testing, while business owners may need guidance on intended use, limitations, and risk acceptance.

Procurement personnel may need supplier assessment guidance, and executives may need to understand governance responsibilities and material risk indicators.

The assessor should determine whether training is aligned with role-specific duties.

The organization should reassess training needs when technology, responsibilities, or the risk environment changes materially.

### 75. Assessing AI Risk Culture

Risk culture concerns the behaviors, incentives, and shared expectations that influence how people identify and respond to risk.

The assessor should consider whether personnel are encouraged to raise concerns, challenge unsupported assumptions, report incidents, and follow approved processes.

Evidence may include governance decisions, issue escalation records, staff feedback, incident handling, and management responses.

A formally documented policy may have limited practical effect if staff are rewarded for bypassing controls or discouraged from reporting problems.

The assessment should consider how organizational behavior affects AI risk management outcomes.

### 76. Assessing Incentives and Performance Measures

Organizational incentives can influence whether teams prioritize responsible risk management or focus narrowly on delivery speed and adoption.

The assessor should examine whether performance measures encourage appropriate review, testing, documentation, and remediation.

For example, a team measured only on rapid AI deployment may have incentives to bypass approval or minimize reported issues.

The organization should consider whether relevant risk responsibilities are incorporated into performance expectations.

The assessment should focus on demonstrated incentive structures and their effects rather than assuming that every commercial objective creates a governance problem.

### 77. Assessing Stakeholder Engagement Processes

Stakeholder engagement helps the organization understand concerns, expectations, and potential impacts that may not be visible from internal technical analysis.

The assessor should determine whether relevant stakeholders are identified and engaged at appropriate lifecycle stages.

Evidence may include consultation records, feedback mechanisms, impact assessments, and documented responses to concerns.

Engagement should be proportionate to the system's context and potential consequences.

The organization should be able to explain how material feedback influenced risk decisions or why certain concerns were not adopted.

### 78. Assessing External Feedback and Complaint Handling

Feedback and complaints may reveal issues that internal testing has not identified.

The assessor should determine whether the organization provides appropriate channels for reporting problems and has a process for investigating and responding to them.

Relevant evidence may include complaint registers, incident tickets, response records, and trend analysis.

The organization should consider whether reports indicate recurring issues, unexpected behavior, or risks affecting particular stakeholder groups.

Feedback mechanisms should connect to risk management and improvement processes rather than operate as isolated customer service functions.

### 79. Assessing AI Risk Communication

Risk communication ensures that relevant information reaches the people who need it to make decisions or operate the system safely.

The assessor should determine whether limitations, known risks, approved uses, escalation procedures, and responsibilities are communicated appropriately.

Different audiences may require different levels of detail.

For example, end users may need clear operating instructions, while technical owners may require detailed architecture and testing evidence.

Communication should be accurate and understandable, and it should not overstate model reliability or the certainty of assessment results.

### 80. Assessing Transparency and Documentation Responsibilities

Governance arrangements should define responsibility for producing and maintaining documentation needed to understand and manage AI risks.

Relevant documentation may include system descriptions, intended-use statements, risk assessments, evaluation results, known limitations, and change histories.

The assessor should determine whether responsibilities are assigned and whether required records are available to relevant stakeholders.

Transparency does not necessarily mean disclosing every proprietary technical detail to every audience.

The organization should determine what information is necessary for each stakeholder to understand the relevant risks and make informed decisions.

### 81. Assessing AI Risk Documentation Standards

Documentation standards establish what information must be recorded, how records are structured, and how they are maintained.

The assessor should examine whether the standards cover relevant lifecycle stages and risk management decisions.

They may define minimum requirements for system descriptions, data sources, evaluation evidence, risk treatment, approval, and monitoring.

The assessment should determine whether standards are practical enough to be followed and sufficiently detailed to support traceability.

Inconsistent documentation can make it difficult to compare systems or demonstrate how material risks were addressed.

### 82. Assessing AI Governance Exceptions

Governance exceptions permit deviations from established requirements under defined conditions.

The assessor should determine whether exceptions require documented justification, risk assessment, appropriate approval, and periodic review.

Evidence may include exception registers, approval records, compensating controls, and expiry dates.

Repeated exceptions may indicate that the underlying policy, process, or technical architecture needs improvement.

Exceptions should not become an informal route for bypassing governance.

### 83. Assessing AI Governance Metrics

Governance metrics help management understand whether the AI risk management program is being implemented and whether key activities are completed.

Examples include inventory coverage, risk assessment completion, training completion, overdue remediation, and the percentage of systems with assigned owners.

The assessor should examine metric definitions, data sources, reporting frequency, ownership, and thresholds.

Metrics should be interpreted in context and should support decisions.

A high completion rate does not necessarily indicate that the underlying activities are thorough or effective.

### 84. Assessing AI Governance Reporting

Governance reporting communicates material AI risks, control performance, unresolved issues, and required decisions.

The assessor should determine whether reporting is timely, accurate, and appropriate to the recipient's responsibilities.

Evidence may include dashboards, risk committee papers, executive reports, and documented actions.

Reports should distinguish current risk exposure from planned remediation and should identify significant uncertainties.

The organization should demonstrate that material information reaches decision-makers who have the authority to act.

### 85. Assessing AI Governance Meeting Effectiveness

Governance meetings should support meaningful review, decision-making, and follow-through.

The assessor should examine agendas, attendance, decision records, action assignments, and completion tracking.

The quality of governance is demonstrated by the substance of decisions and actions rather than meeting frequency alone.

Recurring discussion of the same unresolved issue may indicate inadequate authority, resources, or accountability.

The organization should determine whether meeting arrangements remain suitable for the complexity and pace of its AI activities.

### 86. Assessing AI Risk Ownership Across the Lifecycle

Risk ownership may change as a system moves from development to deployment and operation.

The assessor should determine whether ownership transfers are explicit and whether incoming owners receive sufficient documentation and authority.

Evidence may include release approvals, operational handover records, service ownership assignments, and updated risk registers.

The organization should avoid gaps in accountability when projects transition into production or when a supplier assumes operational responsibilities.

Material risks should remain assigned even when responsibilities are distributed across several teams.

### 87. Assessing Governance for AI Development Teams

Development teams should understand the risk management requirements relevant to the systems they build.

The assessor should examine whether development processes incorporate risk assessment, secure design, testing, documentation, and approval requirements.

Evidence may include development standards, design reviews, test records, issue tracking, and release gates.

The organization should determine whether developers can escalate unresolved risks and whether release decisions are made by authorized personnel.

Development speed should not silently override established risk acceptance and approval requirements.

### 88. Assessing Governance for AI Operations Teams

Operations teams may be responsible for deployment, monitoring, access administration, incident handling, and service continuity.

The assessor should determine whether operational responsibilities are documented and supported by suitable procedures and resources.

Evidence may include runbooks, monitoring records, change approvals, incident tickets, and service reviews.

The organization should assess whether operational teams receive sufficient information about system limitations and known risks.

Governance should continue after deployment rather than ending when a project receives initial approval.

### 89. Assessing Governance for Business Owners

Business owners are often responsible for defining intended use, evaluating business impact, and ensuring that the system supports approved objectives.

The assessor should determine whether business owners understand their responsibilities and have access to the information needed for risk decisions.

Evidence may include use-case approvals, business impact assessments, risk acceptance records, and periodic reviews.

Business ownership should include responsibility for reassessing the use case when business processes or user expectations change.

Technical approval alone does not establish that a system remains suitable for its intended business purpose.

### 90. Assessing Governance for Data Owners

Data owners help establish the appropriate use, classification, access, quality, and retention of information used by AI systems.

The assessor should determine whether relevant datasets have assigned owners and whether data use is approved for the intended purpose.

Evidence may include data inventories, classification records, access approvals, data quality procedures, and retention requirements.

The assessment should consider whether data governance responsibilities extend to copies, indexes, derived datasets, and other relevant representations.

Unclear data ownership can prevent effective decisions about access, reuse, correction, and deletion.

### 91. Assessing Governance for Model Owners

Model owners may be responsible for maintaining model documentation, evaluation results, approved configurations, and lifecycle decisions.

The assessor should determine whether model ownership is defined and whether relevant responsibilities are carried out.

Evidence may include model inventories, version records, evaluation reports, change approvals, and monitoring reviews.

For externally hosted models, the organization should clarify which responsibilities remain with the provider and which remain with the deploying organization.

Model ownership should not be confused with ownership of every application, dataset, or business process that uses the model.

### 92. Assessing Governance for AI Security Owners

AI security owners coordinate security requirements and ensure that relevant controls are considered throughout the lifecycle.

Their responsibilities may include threat assessment, access control, secure integration, vulnerability management, security testing, and incident coordination.

The assessor should determine whether security ownership is clear and whether it has appropriate authority and resources.

Evidence may include security reviews, architecture approvals, testing records, and risk treatment plans.

Security ownership should integrate with AI governance and existing information security processes rather than operate independently of them.

### 93. Assessing Governance for Privacy Owners

Privacy owners help identify and manage risks associated with personal information and applicable privacy requirements.

The assessor should determine whether privacy responsibilities are included in relevant AI approval and change processes.

Evidence may include privacy assessments, processing records, retention decisions, and documented consultations.

Privacy review should occur early enough to influence system design and intended use where necessary.

The assessment should distinguish privacy governance responsibilities from broader AI risk management responsibilities while evaluating how they interact.

### 94. Assessing Governance for Legal and Compliance Owners

Legal and compliance personnel may help interpret obligations, assess contractual commitments, and advise on relevant regulatory requirements.

The assessor should determine whether these responsibilities are clearly assigned and whether material issues receive appropriate review.

Evidence may include legal assessments, obligations registers, contract reviews, and compliance approvals.

The organization should identify how conflicting interpretations or uncertain requirements are escalated.

Legal review should inform risk decisions without replacing technical assessment or operational control verification.

### 95. Assessing Governance for Internal Audit

Internal audit can provide independent assurance over AI governance and risk management, subject to its mandate and independence arrangements.

The assessor should determine whether the audit function has suitable access, competence, and authority to evaluate relevant AI risks.

Evidence may include audit plans, completed reports, testing records, and corrective action follow-up.

Internal audit should avoid taking responsibility for management decisions that it may later need to assess independently.

The organization should define how AI risks are incorporated into the risk-based audit plan.

### 96. Assessing Governance for Risk and Compliance Functions

Risk and compliance functions may provide oversight, challenge, reporting, and coordination across AI-related activities.

The assessor should examine their mandate, access to information, authority, and relationship with operational control owners.

Evidence may include risk reviews, compliance monitoring, governance reports, and escalation records.

Effective oversight requires the ability to identify gaps and challenge decisions where necessary.

The assessment should verify whether oversight findings lead to appropriate action or remain unresolved without adequate justification.

### 97. Assessing Governance for AI Incidents

AI incident governance establishes responsibility for identifying, reporting, classifying, investigating, escalating, and learning from AI-related incidents.

The assessor should determine whether incident processes address relevant AI-specific scenarios as well as conventional security incidents.

Examples may include unauthorized data disclosure, compromised model artifacts, misuse of connected tools, and failures in AI-enabled business workflows.

Evidence may include incident procedures, tabletop exercises, incident records, and lessons-learned actions.

The organization should ensure that material incidents inform risk reassessment and governance decisions.

### 98. Assessing Governance for AI System Suspension and Withdrawal

Governance arrangements should establish who can restrict, suspend, or withdraw an AI system when risks become unacceptable or required conditions are no longer satisfied.

The assessor should examine decision authority, triggering conditions, communication arrangements, and recovery or replacement procedures.

Evidence may include deployment approval criteria, incident procedures, shutdown mechanisms, and contingency plans.

The ability to stop a system may be particularly important when it has access to sensitive information or can execute consequential actions.

The organization should not rely on informal agreement alone where timely suspension is necessary.

### 99. Assessing GOVERN Function Effectiveness

The assessor should evaluate whether governance policies, roles, culture, stakeholder engagement, and third-party oversight operate together to support AI risk management.

Evidence should demonstrate more than the existence of formal committees and documents.

The assessment should consider whether responsibilities are understood, risks are escalated, decisions are authorized, resources are adequate, and identified weaknesses lead to corrective action.

Governance effectiveness should be evaluated in the context of the organization's AI portfolio and risk exposure.

The resulting findings should distinguish structural gaps from failures in implementation or operation.

### 100. Core Principles of NIST AI RMF Assessment

A NIST AI RMF assessment should begin with a clearly defined objective, scope, criteria, and methodology. It should use reliable evidence to evaluate the organization's AI risk management practices and communicate conclusions within the limits of that evidence.

The framework's GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE functions should be understood as interconnected activities. Governance establishes the responsibilities and conditions that support risk identification, measurement, treatment, and continual reassessment.

The GOVERN function is particularly important because effective AI risk management depends on clear accountability, suitable policies, competent personnel, appropriate stakeholder engagement, and oversight of external dependencies.

**The central principle of NIST AI RMF assessment is to evaluate whether AI risks are governed and managed through demonstrable organizational practices—not merely whether the organization has adopted the framework's terminology or completed a checklist.**

## Part 2 — MAP Function Assessment, Context Analysis, and AI Risk Identification

### 101. Introduction to the MAP Function

The MAP function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) establishes the context in which an AI system operates and identifies the risks, potential impacts, stakeholders, and circumstances relevant to its use.

MAP provides the contextual foundation for effective AI risk management. Before an organization can measure or manage risks, it must understand what the system does, why it is being used, how it interacts with its environment, and who may be affected.

An assessment of MAP evaluates whether the organization has systematically identified relevant contextual information and translated that information into usable risk management inputs.

The objective is not merely to describe the AI system but to establish a defensible understanding of its intended use, limitations, dependencies, and potential consequences.

### 102. Understanding MAP Function Categories

In NIST AI RMF 1.0, the MAP function includes categories addressing the context of the AI system, categorization of AI systems, capabilities and limitations, risks and impacts, and stakeholder considerations.

These categories help organizations develop a structured understanding of the system before making risk decisions.

The assessor should identify the applicable categories and subcategories within the assessment scope and determine what evidence demonstrates their implementation.

The evaluation should consider whether contextual information is sufficiently complete, accurate, and current to support subsequent risk analysis.

### 103. Assessing AI System Context

AI system context includes the business purpose, operating environment, user population, technical architecture, data flows, external dependencies, and consequences associated with the system.

The assessor should determine whether this information is documented and whether it reflects actual operating conditions.

Relevant evidence may include system descriptions, architecture diagrams, business process documentation, stakeholder analyses, and approved use-case records.

The assessment should identify gaps that could affect risk identification or cause the organization to misunderstand the system's exposure.

### 104. Assessing Business Purpose and Objectives

The business purpose explains why the organization is developing, acquiring, or deploying an AI system.

The assessor should determine whether objectives are documented, measurable where appropriate, and consistent with the approved use case.

Relevant questions include whether the system solves a defined business problem, whether AI is appropriate for the intended task, and whether the expected benefits justify the associated risks.

Evidence may include business cases, project approvals, requirements documents, and performance objectives.

An unclear business purpose can lead to uncontrolled scope expansion and difficulty determining whether the system remains suitable for its intended use.

### 105. Assessing Intended Users and Operating Conditions

The assessor should identify the intended users, their responsibilities, their level of expertise, and the conditions under which the AI system is expected to operate.

Users may include employees, customers, contractors, administrators, or automated services.

Operating conditions may include geographic location, language, data availability, workload, connectivity, and integration with other systems.

The assessment should determine whether the system has been evaluated under conditions representative of actual use.

A system tested only in a controlled development environment may behave differently when exposed to real-world inputs, diverse users, or operational constraints.

### 106. Assessing the Decision Context

The decision context describes how AI outputs influence business processes and decisions.

An AI system may provide information, recommend an action, rank alternatives, generate content, or execute an action automatically.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the degree of influence the AI system has over the final outcome.

Relevant factors include human review, decision reversibility, consequences of error, and the availability of alternative information.

A recommendation used as one input to a low-impact decision presents a different risk context from an automated decision that directly affects an individual's employment, finances, or access to services.

### 107. Assessing the AI System's Role in a Larger Process

An AI system should be assessed in the context of the business process in which it operates.

The assessor should identify upstream inputs, downstream consumers, human decision points, connected applications, and operational dependencies.

For example, an AI-generated classification may trigger a workflow that changes access permissions or initiates a financial transaction.

The risk assessment should examine the complete chain from input to consequential outcome rather than focusing exclusively on the model output.

This helps identify risks that arise from system integration, process design, or inappropriate reliance on AI-generated information.

### 108. Assessing AI System Boundaries

System boundaries define the components, services, data flows, and processes included in the assessment.

The assessor should determine whether boundaries are clearly documented and whether important dependencies outside those boundaries have been identified.

Relevant components may include models, APIs, retrieval systems, databases, identity services, monitoring platforms, and third-party providers.

Boundary definitions should reflect actual information flows and control relationships.

If an external service materially influences the system's behavior or risk exposure, the organization should not ignore it simply because it is outside the immediate technical environment.

### 109. Assessing AI Architecture Documentation

Architecture documentation helps explain how AI components interact with the surrounding environment.

The assessor should examine whether diagrams identify important components, interfaces, trust boundaries, data flows, and external dependencies.

The documentation should be sufficiently detailed to support risk analysis and technical review.

Where the architecture has changed, the assessor should determine whether the documentation has been updated and whether the changes were reviewed.

Outdated architecture diagrams can conceal new exposure paths, excessive permissions, or dependencies that were not considered during the original assessment.

### 110. Assessing AI Data Flows

Data-flow analysis identifies how information enters, moves through, and leaves an AI system.

The assessment should consider data collection, preprocessing, storage, training, retrieval, inference, logging, output generation, and deletion where relevant.

The assessor should identify sensitive information, external transfers, trust boundaries, and points where data may be transformed or retained.

Evidence may include data-flow diagrams, application configurations, API documentation, and data-processing records.

An incomplete data-flow analysis may cause the organization to overlook unauthorized disclosure, inappropriate reuse, or unmonitored transfers to external services.

### 111. Assessing AI Deployment Environments

AI systems may operate in public cloud environments, private infrastructure, on-premises environments, edge devices, or hybrid architectures.

The assessor should determine whether the deployment environment is documented and whether relevant environmental risks have been considered.

Factors may include physical security, network exposure, identity controls, resource constraints, regional data handling, and operational resilience.

The assessment should examine whether the selected deployment model is appropriate for the system's intended use and data sensitivity.

Deployment context can materially affect the risks and controls required even when the underlying model is unchanged.

### 112. Assessing AI Integration Dependencies

AI applications often depend on external APIs, enterprise applications, data platforms, identity services, and orchestration components.

The assessor should identify dependencies that could affect system behavior, availability, confidentiality, or integrity.

Relevant evidence may include dependency inventories, integration diagrams, API permissions, service agreements, and change records.

The assessment should determine whether dependencies are monitored and whether failures or changes could create unexpected outcomes.

An AI application may remain operational at the model level while becoming unsafe or unreliable because a connected service behaves differently.

### 113. Assessing Organizational Context Changes

AI system context can change as the organization introduces new users, changes business processes, adopts new models, or expands into new markets.

The assessor should determine whether the organization monitors contextual changes and evaluates their significance.

Evidence may include change requests, periodic reviews, updated impact assessments, and governance approvals.

The organization should define triggers for reassessment when a change affects the system's intended use, data, operating conditions, or potential consequences.

A previously acceptable risk assessment may no longer be valid when the context changes materially.

### 114. Assessing AI System Categorization

AI system categorization organizes systems according to relevant characteristics, intended uses, capabilities, and potential consequences.

An organization may classify systems by business criticality, autonomy, data sensitivity, impact severity, external exposure, or regulatory relevance.

The assessor should determine whether the categorization method is documented and applied consistently.

Categories should support practical decisions about assessment depth, approval requirements, monitoring, and assurance.

The organization should avoid treating a simple classification label as a substitute for understanding the specific risk scenarios associated with a system.

### 115. Assessing AI System Criticality

AI system criticality reflects the importance of the system to business operations and the consequences of its failure.

A system supporting a critical operational process may require stronger continuity arrangements and more extensive assurance than a nonessential productivity tool.

The assessor should determine whether criticality is based on documented criteria and whether classifications reflect actual dependencies.

Relevant evidence may include business impact assessments, continuity plans, system inventories, and service dependency maps.

Criticality classifications should be reviewed when the system's business role or operating environment changes.

### 116. Assessing AI System Autonomy

Autonomy describes the degree to which an AI system can operate or take actions without direct human intervention.

The assessor should determine whether autonomy is accurately documented and whether the organization understands the consequences of automated actions.

Relevant factors include decision authority, tool access, transaction permissions, execution limits, and human approval requirements.

Higher levels of autonomy may increase the consequences of incorrect outputs, compromised components, or inappropriate instructions.

The assessment should verify that the safeguards and oversight arrangements are appropriate to the system's actual level of autonomy.

### 117. Assessing AI System Reach and Scale

System reach describes the population, business processes, or environments affected by an AI system.

Scale may influence the number of users exposed to an error, the amount of information processed, and the speed at which adverse effects propagate.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has considered these factors when evaluating potential impact.

Relevant evidence may include deployment records, user counts, transaction volumes, integration maps, and business impact analyses.

A low-probability failure may still require significant attention when the system operates at a large scale or affects many stakeholders.

### 118. Assessing AI System Adaptability

Some AI systems change behavior through retraining, fine-tuning, configuration updates, retrieval changes, or adaptation to new inputs and environments.

The assessor should determine whether these mechanisms are documented and whether the organization understands how they affect system behavior.

Evidence may include model lifecycle records, change management procedures, evaluation results, and monitoring configurations.

The organization should identify which changes require reassessment or renewed approval.

An AI system should not be treated as static when its model, data, prompts, tools, or surrounding environment can materially alter its behavior.

### 119. Assessing AI System Interconnectivity

Interconnectivity refers to the relationships between an AI system and other systems, services, users, or automated processes.

The assessor should determine whether these connections create additional exposure or increase the potential consequences of failure.

Relevant factors include shared identities, API access, data exchange, downstream automation, and inherited dependencies.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, interface inventories, access reviews, and integration testing.

Interconnected systems may create cascading risks when an error or compromise in one component affects other components.

### 120. Assessing AI System Intended Performance

The organization should define what acceptable system performance means for the intended use.

Depending on the application, performance objectives may include accuracy, relevance, response time, reliability, robustness, or successful task completion.

The assessor should determine whether performance expectations are documented and supported by appropriate evaluation methods.

Performance criteria should reflect the real operational context rather than only development benchmarks.

A model can achieve strong benchmark results while remaining unsuitable for a particular use case because the benchmark does not reflect actual conditions.

### 121. Assessing AI Capabilities and Limitations

The MAP function includes consideration of the capabilities and limitations of the AI system.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified what the system can reasonably do, what it cannot reliably do, and under which conditions its performance may deteriorate.

Relevant evidence may include technical documentation, model evaluations, known limitations, user guidance, and operational testing.

The assessment should determine whether these limitations are communicated to people responsible for using or overseeing the system.

Unsupported assumptions about capability can lead to inappropriate deployment, excessive reliance, and avoidable harm.

### 122. Assessing Model and System Limitations

Model limitations may include restricted domain knowledge, sensitivity to input variation, weak performance on uncommon cases, or unreliable generation of factual information.

System-level limitations may arise from retrieval quality, application logic, integrations, permissions, or operational constraints.

The assessor should distinguish model limitations from limitations introduced by the surrounding application.

Evidence may include test results, error analyses, incident records, and system documentation.

The organization should establish whether identified limitations affect the approved use case and whether additional controls are necessary.

### 123. Assessing Generalization Limitations

Generalization concerns whether a model performs appropriately on inputs or conditions that differ from those represented in its training or evaluation data.

The assessor should determine whether evaluation methods consider the relevant operating population and expected variation.

Evidence may include test-set design, performance analysis, out-of-distribution testing, and documented assumptions.

The assessment should consider whether model behavior may deteriorate when users, data sources, languages, or operating conditions differ from those used during development.

Strong performance on familiar examples does not guarantee reliable behavior in unfamiliar contexts.

### 124. Assessing Performance Under Distribution Shift

Distribution shift occurs when the characteristics of operational data differ from those represented during development or evaluation.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified plausible sources of change and established appropriate monitoring or reassessment methods.

Potential sources include changing user behavior, new data sources, market conditions, seasonal patterns, and changes in upstream systems.

Evidence may include monitoring plans, baseline measurements, drift indicators, and reassessment records.

The organization should determine how significant performance deterioration is detected and what actions follow.

### 125. Assessing Uncertainty in AI Outputs

AI outputs may contain uncertainty because of incomplete information, ambiguous inputs, probabilistic generation, or limitations in model knowledge.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the forms of uncertainty relevant to the use case.

Where appropriate, evaluation should examine confidence estimates, calibration, abstention behavior, or other indicators that help identify unreliable outputs.

The assessment should not assume that a model's expressed confidence accurately reflects correctness.

When uncertainty cannot be reliably quantified, the organization should document the limitation and establish suitable safeguards.

### 126. Assessing Reliability and Repeatability

Reliability concerns whether the system performs consistently enough for its intended use. Repeatability concerns whether comparable conditions produce sufficiently consistent results where consistency is required.

Generative systems may produce different outputs for similar inputs, and this variability may be acceptable in some use cases but problematic in others.

The assessor should determine whether expected variability is documented and evaluated.

Evidence may include repeated-run tests, regression results, operational metrics, and acceptance criteria.

The assessment should distinguish acceptable variation from instability that could affect safety, compliance, or business outcomes.

### 127. Assessing Robustness

Robustness concerns the ability of an AI system to maintain acceptable performance under relevant variations, disturbances, or challenging conditions.

The assessor should determine whether robustness requirements reflect the intended operating environment.

Testing may include malformed inputs, noisy data, unusual requests, unexpected context, or relevant adversarial conditions.

Evidence should document the conditions tested, observed behavior, and limitations of the testing.

Robustness claims should be limited to the conditions supported by evidence rather than generalized to every possible input.

### 128. Assessing Explainability Requirements

Explainability concerns whether relevant people can understand how or why an AI system produced a result at a level appropriate to the use case.

The assessor should determine whether explainability requirements have been identified for developers, operators, reviewers, affected individuals, or other stakeholders.

The required level of explanation may depend on the system's impact and the decisions it supports.

Evidence may include model documentation, explanation methods, user interfaces, and validation results.

The assessment should distinguish between a useful explanation of system behavior and a plausible-sounding narrative that has not been validated.

### 129. Assessing Transparency Requirements

Transparency concerns the availability of relevant information about an AI system, including its purpose, capabilities, limitations, and operating conditions.

The assessor should determine which stakeholders need which information and whether the organization provides it appropriately.

Evidence may include system documentation, notices, user guidance, model documentation, and supplier disclosures.

Transparency requirements may be constrained by security, privacy, intellectual property, or contractual considerations, but those constraints should be assessed rather than assumed.

The objective is to provide information necessary for appropriate use, oversight, and risk management.

### 130. Assessing Human Oversight Requirements

Human oversight arrangements define how people supervise, review, intervene in, or override AI-supported processes.

The assessor should determine whether oversight is appropriate to the system's autonomy, risk, and potential consequences.

Relevant factors include reviewer competence, access to supporting information, time available for review, escalation authority, and the ability to reject outputs.

Evidence may include operating procedures, workflow configurations, training records, and observations of actual practice.

A nominal human approval step may provide little protection if reviewers routinely accept outputs without meaningful evaluation.

### 131. Assessing Human-AI Interaction Risks

Human-AI interaction can introduce risks when users overtrust, misunderstand, misuse, or become excessively dependent on AI outputs.

The assessor should examine whether the organization has considered how users interpret outputs and make decisions based on them.

Relevant evidence may include usability testing, user feedback, training materials, incident analysis, and interface design reviews.

The assessment should consider whether limitations and uncertainty are communicated clearly enough to support appropriate reliance.

Controls should address actual interaction patterns rather than assume that written disclaimers alone will prevent misuse.

### 132. Assessing the Need for Human Intervention

The organization should identify situations in which human intervention is necessary to prevent or limit harm.

Intervention requirements may include approval before consequential actions, escalation of uncertain cases, review of unusual outputs, or manual fallback during system failure.

The assessor should determine whether these requirements are documented and technically or procedurally supported.

Evidence may include workflow rules, approval logs, override records, and incident procedures.

The assessment should verify that intervention remains practical under real operating conditions, including high workload and time pressure.

### 133. Assessing AI System Failure Modes

A failure mode describes a way in which a system or component may fail to perform as intended.

AI-related failure modes may include incorrect predictions, fabricated information, data leakage, unauthorized tool use, degraded performance, or failure to recognize an out-of-scope request.

The assessor should determine whether relevant failure modes have been identified systematically.

Methods may include structured brainstorming, failure mode and effects analysis, threat modeling, scenario analysis, and incident review.

The analysis should link failure modes to potential consequences and identify where additional testing or controls are required.

### 134. Assessing Failure Consequences

Failure consequence analysis evaluates what could happen if a system behaves incorrectly or fails to operate.

Consequences may include financial loss, operational disruption, privacy harm, security compromise, legal exposure, or harm to individuals.

The assessor should determine whether consequences are evaluated in the context of actual system use.

Relevant factors include scale, reversibility, exposure duration, affected population, and the availability of fallback processes.

The analysis should not rely solely on the technical severity of the model error when the downstream business consequence may be substantially different.

### 135. Assessing AI Risk Prioritization Inputs

Risk prioritization depends on a sufficiently complete understanding of system context, potential impacts, existing controls, and relevant uncertainty.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has collected the information needed to compare risks and establish priorities.

Inputs may include impact severity, likelihood or plausibility, exposure, detectability, reversibility, and existing safeguards.

The organization should document its prioritization methodology and any limitations in the available information.

A risk rating should be traceable to the scenario and assumptions that support it.

### 136. Assessing AI Impact Categories

AI impacts may involve individuals, organizations, communities, or broader systems.

Relevant categories may include health and safety, privacy, civil rights, fairness, economic outcomes, information security, operational continuity, and environmental considerations where applicable.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has considered impact categories relevant to the use case.

The analysis should include indirect and downstream consequences where reasonably foreseeable.

A narrow focus on financial or operational impact may overlook material effects on people or other stakeholders.

### 137. Assessing Potential Harm to Individuals

AI systems may affect individuals through decisions, recommendations, profiling, generated content, or processing of personal information.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified plausible harms and the circumstances under which they could occur.

Examples may include inaccurate eligibility recommendations, disclosure of personal information, misleading guidance, or unfair treatment.

Evidence may include impact assessments, scenario analysis, testing results, and stakeholder feedback.

The assessment should consider the severity and distribution of potential harms rather than relying only on average system performance.

### 138. Assessing Group-Level and Societal Impacts

Some AI systems may create effects that extend beyond individual users or transactions.

Potential impacts include unequal access to services, amplification of harmful information, changes in workforce practices, or disproportionate effects on particular communities.

The assessor should determine whether broader impacts are relevant to the system and whether they have been evaluated.

Evidence may include impact assessments, consultation records, distributional analysis, and documented risk decisions.

The assessment should be proportionate to the system's reach and consequences, while avoiding unsupported claims about impacts that have not been evaluated.

### 139. Assessing Reversibility of AI Outcomes

Reversibility concerns whether an adverse outcome can be corrected and whether the consequences can be meaningfully mitigated.

Some outputs can be corrected immediately, while others may influence decisions or actions that are difficult to undo.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has evaluated reversibility and incorporated it into risk prioritization.

Relevant evidence may include rollback procedures, appeal mechanisms, correction workflows, and recovery plans.

Low reversibility may justify stronger preventive controls, more extensive testing, or additional human review.

### 140. Assessing the Duration of AI Impacts

The duration of an AI-related impact can influence its severity and the response required.

An inaccurate internal summary may be corrected quickly, while a persistent disclosure of confidential information or an incorrect consequential decision may have longer-lasting effects.

The assessor should determine whether impact duration has been considered where relevant.

Evidence may include incident scenarios, retention analysis, remediation procedures, and impact assessments.

Risk analysis should consider not only whether harm occurs but also how long it may persist and whether affected parties can obtain meaningful remediation.

### 141. Assessing Risk to Organizational Operations

AI systems may influence operational processes through automation, decision support, data processing, and integration with enterprise applications.

The assessor should determine whether operational risks have been identified, including service disruption, workflow errors, unreliable outputs, and dependency failures.

Evidence may include business impact assessments, process maps, continuity plans, and operational testing.

The analysis should consider how AI failure affects downstream processes and whether manual alternatives are available.

Operational risk should be evaluated according to business context rather than model performance alone.

### 142. Assessing AI-Related Reputational Risk

Reputational risk may arise when an AI system produces harmful outputs, mishandles data, behaves inconsistently with organizational commitments, or contributes to an adverse outcome.

The assessor should determine whether plausible reputational consequences have been considered in risk analysis.

Evidence may include stakeholder analysis, incident scenarios, communication plans, and risk registers.

The assessment should avoid speculative conclusions and should connect reputational exposure to specific scenarios and credible consequences.

Where reputational risk is material, the organization should establish appropriate escalation and response arrangements.

### 143. Assessing AI Risk Interdependencies

AI risks can interact and amplify one another.

For example, weak access controls may enable prompt injection to retrieve sensitive data, while inadequate monitoring delays detection and increases the resulting impact.

The assessor should determine whether the organization considers relationships among risks rather than evaluating each risk in isolation.

Methods may include attack-path analysis, dependency mapping, scenario analysis, and review of connected controls.

Risk prioritization should account for significant combinations of weaknesses that could create a greater overall exposure.

### 144. Assessing Cascading AI Failures

A cascading failure occurs when a problem in one component or process propagates to other components or business activities.

AI-enabled workflows may transmit incorrect classifications, recommendations, or generated information to multiple downstream systems.

The assessor should determine whether relevant propagation paths have been identified and whether safeguards limit the consequences.

Evidence may include architecture analysis, integration testing, dependency maps, and incident exercises.

Controls may include transaction limits, validation gates, isolation, approval steps, and mechanisms to halt downstream processing.

### 145. Assessing Common-Mode AI Failures

Common-mode failures occur when multiple systems fail for the same underlying reason.

Examples include several applications relying on the same compromised model provider, common training data with the same defect, or a shared identity configuration that grants excessive access.

The assessor should determine whether shared dependencies and common control weaknesses have been identified.

Evidence may include supplier inventories, architecture maps, shared-service assessments, and concentration risk analysis.

The organization should consider whether apparent redundancy provides genuine resilience when systems depend on the same underlying component.

### 146. Assessing AI Concentration Risk

AI concentration risk arises when the organization relies heavily on a limited number of models, suppliers, infrastructure providers, datasets, or technical platforms.

The assessor should determine whether significant concentration is understood and whether the resulting exposure is acceptable.

Relevant considerations include provider outages, pricing changes, service withdrawal, contractual restrictions, and common vulnerabilities.

Evidence may include dependency inventories, supplier risk assessments, contingency plans, and business continuity analysis.

Concentration risk should be assessed in relation to business criticality and the practical availability of alternatives.

### 147. Assessing Third-Party Model Dependencies

Third-party models may introduce risks related to limited transparency, restricted evaluation access, changing behavior, service availability, and data handling.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the supplier's role and the limits of available assurance.

Evidence may include model documentation, contractual commitments, independent reports, service notices, and internal evaluation results.

The organization should establish whether the available information is sufficient for the intended use.

Where assurance is limited, the organization should consider additional testing, compensating controls, restricted use, or alternative providers.

### 148. Assessing Data Dependency Risks

AI systems may depend on internal datasets, external data providers, retrieval corpora, user-submitted information, or continuously updated data sources.

The assessor should determine whether these dependencies are documented and whether data quality, provenance, access, and change risks have been considered.

Evidence may include data inventories, lineage records, quality reports, supplier documentation, and validation procedures.

A change in an upstream data source can affect model behavior even when the model itself has not changed.

The organization should determine how significant data changes are detected and evaluated.

### 149. Assessing External Environment Dependencies

External environmental dependencies may include cloud services, network connectivity, identity providers, regulatory conditions, and other services required for the AI system to operate.

The assessor should determine whether these dependencies are documented and whether their failure or change could affect the system's risks.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, service agreements, continuity plans, and dependency monitoring records.

The assessment should consider whether external dependencies create unrecognized single points of failure or control gaps.

Appropriate risk treatment may require redundancy, fallback procedures, contractual protections, or restrictions on use.

### 150. Assessing Stakeholder Identification

The MAP function includes identifying relevant stakeholders and understanding their interests in the AI system.

Stakeholders may include developers, operators, users, customers, affected individuals, suppliers, regulators, and communities.

The assessor should determine whether stakeholder identification is appropriate to the system's purpose, reach, and potential impact.

Evidence may include stakeholder maps, impact assessments, consultation records, and governance documentation.

The organization should periodically revisit stakeholder assumptions when the system's use or operating context changes.

### 151. Assessing Stakeholder Needs and Expectations

Stakeholders may have different expectations regarding accuracy, privacy, transparency, accessibility, reliability, and accountability.

The assessor should determine whether relevant needs and expectations have been identified and considered in system requirements and risk decisions.

Evidence may include requirements documents, user research, consultation results, service commitments, and documented decisions.

Conflicting expectations should be recognized and evaluated rather than silently ignored.

The organization should be able to explain how significant stakeholder concerns were addressed or why a particular approach was selected.

### 152. Assessing Stakeholder Representation

Stakeholder representation concerns whether the organization has considered the perspectives of groups that may be affected by the system.

The assessor should determine whether engagement methods are appropriate to the system's context and whether relevant groups may have been overlooked.

Potential evidence includes consultation plans, participant selection criteria, feedback records, and impact analysis.

The organization should consider whether the data and evaluation methods reflect the population for which the system is intended.

The absence of reported complaints should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that all affected stakeholders are adequately represented.

### 153. Assessing Feedback From Affected Stakeholders

Feedback from affected stakeholders can reveal harms, usability problems, or unexpected outcomes that may not be visible in internal testing.

The assessor should determine whether appropriate feedback channels exist and whether the organization evaluates the information received.

Evidence may include complaints, support tickets, user research, surveys, incident reports, and remediation records.

The assessment should examine whether feedback is categorized, escalated, and incorporated into risk management decisions where appropriate.

A feedback process is effective only when significant issues can lead to investigation and action.

### 154. Assessing Stakeholder Communication During System Changes

Material system changes may affect users, suppliers, or other stakeholders who rely on the AI system.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has defined when and how changes should be communicated.

Relevant changes may include altered functionality, new data uses, revised limitations, model replacement, or changes to human review requirements.

Evidence may include change communication plans, release notes, user notices, and approval records.

Communication should be proportionate to the significance of the change and the stakeholders who may be affected.

### 155. Assessing Contextual Assumptions

Risk assessments depend on assumptions about users, data, operating conditions, technical behavior, and business processes.

The assessor should determine whether important assumptions are documented and whether their validity is periodically reviewed.

Evidence may include design records, risk assessments, test plans, and change reviews.

Assumptions should be distinguished from verified facts.

Where a critical assumption cannot be validated, the organization should consider the implications for risk acceptance and establish appropriate safeguards or additional evaluation.

### 156. Assessing Contextual Information Quality

Contextual information must be sufficiently accurate and complete to support meaningful risk decisions.

The assessor should evaluate whether system descriptions, architecture diagrams, inventories, and stakeholder analyses are consistent with actual conditions.

Evidence may be corroborated through interviews, configuration reviews, technical testing, and operational records.

Conflicting information should be investigated rather than resolved by selecting the most convenient source.

Material information gaps should be documented because they may limit the reliability of the overall assessment.

### 157. Assessing AI Risk Assumptions About Data

Data-related assumptions may concern representativeness, accuracy, provenance, permissions, freshness, or suitability for the intended purpose.

The assessor should determine whether these assumptions have been evaluated and whether supporting evidence is available.

Relevant evidence may include dataset documentation, data quality metrics, lineage records, and evaluation results.

Where data characteristics are uncertain, the organization should understand how that uncertainty affects system behavior and potential impacts.

Unverified assumptions about data can undermine both model performance and broader risk management decisions.

### 158. Assessing AI Risk Assumptions About Users

Organizations may assume that users have particular levels of expertise, understand system limitations, or follow prescribed procedures.

The assessor should determine whether these assumptions are supported by evidence about actual user behavior and operating conditions.

Evidence may include usability testing, training evaluations, observation, and incident analysis.

Where users may misunderstand or misuse the system, the organization should consider interface changes, workflow safeguards, clearer guidance, or additional training.

Risk management should reflect realistic user behavior rather than idealized expectations.

### 159. Assessing AI Risk Assumptions About Suppliers

Organizations may assume that suppliers maintain certain security controls, notify customers of changes, or provide reliable information about their models.

The assessor should determine whether such assumptions are supported by contractual commitments, independent assurance, technical evidence, or other appropriate verification.

Supplier statements should be evaluated according to their relevance and reliability.

Where the organization cannot verify a critical assumption, it should document the limitation and consider additional safeguards.

The degree of verification should reflect the supplier's importance and the potential consequences of failure.

### 160. Assessing Contextual Information From AI Providers

AI providers may supply model descriptions, usage guidance, safety evaluations, known limitations, and technical documentation.

The assessor should determine whether the organization obtains and reviews information relevant to the intended use.

The assessment should consider the scope, currency, and limitations of provider documentation.

Provider evaluations may not cover the organization's exact application, prompts, data, integrations, or user population.

Organizations should supplement external documentation with their own assessments where necessary.

### 161. Assessing the Suitability of AI for the Use Case

The organization should evaluate whether AI is an appropriate solution for the problem it intends to address.

The assessor should examine whether alternatives, constraints, and foreseeable limitations were considered.

Relevant evidence may include business cases, feasibility studies, proof-of-concept results, and risk assessments.

The assessment should determine whether the expected benefits justify the risks and whether the organization has considered simpler or more controllable alternatives.

The objective is not to reject AI by default but to ensure that adoption is based on an informed and documented decision.

### 162. Assessing Non-AI Alternatives

Some business problems may be addressed through conventional software, deterministic rules, process changes, or human decision-making.

The assessor should determine whether the organization considered alternatives where they could materially reduce risk or complexity.

Evidence may include design decisions, comparative evaluations, business cases, and documented trade-offs.

A non-AI alternative may offer greater predictability in some contexts, while an AI approach may provide meaningful benefits in others.

The decision should be grounded in the intended outcome, operating constraints, and risk profile.

### 163. Assessing Expected Benefits Against Potential Risks

The organization should compare the expected benefits of an AI system with its potential adverse consequences.

Benefits may include improved efficiency, accessibility, consistency, decision support, or new capabilities.

Risks may include unreliable outputs, privacy exposure, security weaknesses, operational disruption, and harmful decisions.

The assessor should determine whether the comparison is documented and whether significant uncertainties are acknowledged.

A favorable business case should not be treated as evidence that the system's risks have been adequately controlled.

### 164. Assessing AI Use-Case Approval Conditions

AI use-case approval may depend on specified conditions, such as restricted data types, mandatory human review, approved user groups, or limits on automated actions.

The assessor should determine whether approval conditions are documented and whether they can be enforced or monitored.

Evidence may include approval records, system configurations, access policies, and operational procedures.

The organization should establish how violations of approval conditions are detected and handled.

An approval decision is not meaningful if the organization cannot determine whether the system continues to operate within the approved boundaries.

### 165. Assessing Prohibited or Restricted AI Uses

Organizations may prohibit or restrict AI uses that are incompatible with policy, risk appetite, contractual obligations, or applicable requirements.

The assessor should determine whether prohibited and restricted uses are clearly defined and communicated.

Evidence may include policies, approved-use catalogues, procurement controls, access restrictions, and training materials.

The organization should evaluate whether technical or procedural safeguards help prevent unauthorized uses.

Where a use is restricted rather than prohibited, the conditions and approval requirements should be explicit.

### 166. Assessing AI System Suitability Over Time

Suitability is not necessarily permanent. A system may become inappropriate when the use case changes, data quality deteriorates, user expectations evolve, or new risks emerge.

The assessor should determine whether the organization periodically reviews whether the system remains suitable for its intended purpose.

Evidence may include performance reviews, risk reassessments, business owner attestations, and change approvals.

The organization should identify triggers for restricting or discontinuing use.

Continued operation should be based on current evidence rather than the assumption that initial approval remains valid indefinitely.

### 167. Assessing MAP Function Completeness

MAP function completeness concerns whether relevant contextual factors, system characteristics, stakeholders, capabilities, limitations, and potential impacts have been identified.

The assessor should compare the available evidence with the applicable assessment criteria and the system's actual risk profile.

Completeness does not mean documenting every imaginable detail. It means covering the information necessary for defensible risk analysis and decision-making.

Material omissions should be recorded and evaluated for their effect on subsequent measurement and management activities.

The assessor should avoid concluding that MAP is effective solely because the organization has completed a standard template.

### 168. Assessing Traceability From Context to Risk

Contextual information should connect to the risk scenarios identified by the organization.

For example, documented external access to a model may inform an assessment of unauthorized use, data disclosure, or service disruption.

The assessor should determine whether material risks can be traced to relevant contextual facts, assumptions, and system characteristics.

Evidence may include risk assessments, threat models, architecture records, and impact analyses.

Weak traceability may indicate that risks were identified generically without sufficient consideration of the system being assessed.

### 169. Assessing Traceability From Risk to Control Requirements

Once a risk is identified, the organization should determine which requirements or safeguards are needed to address it.

The assessor should examine whether control decisions reflect the risk scenario and its consequences.

Evidence may include risk treatment plans, control mappings, design requirements, testing criteria, and approval records.

A control should not be considered adequate merely because it is part of the organization's standard control library.

The assessment should determine whether the control addresses the relevant exposure and whether additional safeguards are required.

### 170. Assessing MAP-to-MEASURE Handoffs

The MAP function provides contextual information that informs the MEASURE function's testing and analysis.

The assessor should determine whether identified risks, assumptions, stakeholder concerns, and performance expectations are translated into measurable evaluation activities.

Evidence may include test plans, evaluation criteria, risk registers, and documented measurement objectives.

If contextual analysis identifies a high-impact failure scenario but the testing plan does not address it, the handoff may be incomplete.

Effective traceability helps ensure that testing focuses on the risks that matter most for the system.

### 171. Assessing MAP-to-MANAGE Handoffs

The MAP function also informs the MANAGE function by identifying risks and impacts that require prioritization and treatment.

The assessor should determine whether contextual analysis results lead to appropriate risk decisions, control implementation, or documented acceptance.

Evidence may include risk treatment plans, deployment decisions, mitigation records, and escalation documentation.

A detailed context assessment provides limited value if identified risks are not translated into action.

The organization should be able to explain how important contextual findings influenced operational decisions.

### 172. Assessing Contextual Reassessment Triggers

Organizations should define when changes in context require the AI system to be reassessed.

Potential triggers include new intended uses, significant model changes, new integrations, material data changes, supplier changes, incidents, and altered operating conditions.

The assessor should determine whether triggers are documented and incorporated into relevant workflows.

Evidence may include change management procedures, risk review records, and release approvals.

Reassessment triggers should be appropriate to the system's risk profile and should not rely exclusively on a fixed annual review schedule.

### 173. Assessing MAP Function Governance

The organization should assign responsibility for collecting, validating, maintaining, and approving contextual information.

The assessor should determine whether system owners, business owners, data owners, and technical teams understand their respective responsibilities.

Evidence may include role descriptions, review procedures, system documentation, and governance records.

The organization should establish who resolves conflicting information and who determines whether contextual changes affect risk decisions.

Clear governance helps ensure that context remains reliable as the system evolves.

### 174. Assessing MAP Documentation Currency

MAP documentation should remain sufficiently current to reflect the system's actual design, use, and operating conditions.

The assessor should compare documented information with current configurations, supplier arrangements, business processes, and user practices.

Evidence may include document revision histories, change tickets, architecture reviews, and inventory updates.

Material discrepancies should be investigated and corrected.

Outdated documentation can cause later risk assessments to rely on assumptions that are no longer valid.

### 175. Assessing MAP Evidence Retention

The organization should retain sufficient evidence to explain how contextual information was gathered and how important assumptions were evaluated.

Relevant records may include system descriptions, stakeholder analyses, impact assessments, architecture decisions, and review approvals.

The assessor should determine whether records are retained according to organizational requirements and are accessible to authorized reviewers.

Evidence should be traceable to the system version or assessment period to which it relates.

Retention practices should balance assurance needs with applicable privacy, security, and data minimization requirements.

### 176. Assessing MAP Gaps and Their Significance

A MAP gap exists when required contextual information or risk analysis is missing, inaccurate, incomplete, or insufficiently supported.

The assessor should describe the gap and explain how it affects the organization's ability to identify or evaluate AI risks.

For example, missing information about an external API may prevent the organization from understanding where sensitive information is transmitted.

The finding should identify the affected criteria, evidence, and relevant consequences.

The severity of a MAP gap should reflect its significance to risk management rather than the number of missing documents alone.

### 177. Assessing MAP Remediation

Remediation should address the cause and consequence of identified contextual weaknesses.

Actions may include completing system documentation, validating assumptions, identifying affected stakeholders, updating architecture diagrams, or conducting additional impact analysis.

The assessor should determine whether remediation has an owner, deadline, and appropriate completion evidence.

Where the gap affects deployment safety or another material risk decision, temporary restrictions may be necessary until adequate information becomes available.

Closure should be based on verification rather than an assertion that the documentation has been updated.

### 178. Assessing MAP Effectiveness Indicators

Organizations may use indicators to evaluate the effectiveness of contextual risk management.

Examples include the percentage of AI systems with current descriptions, completeness of inventory records, coverage of stakeholder assessments, and timeliness of reassessment after material changes.

The assessor should examine whether indicators are clearly defined, reliably measured, and connected to management decisions.

Metrics should reflect meaningful outcomes rather than documentation volume alone.

A complete inventory is useful only if the information is accurate enough to support risk management.

### 179. Assessing MAP Across Multiple AI Systems

Enterprise assessments may include many AI systems with different purposes, architectures, and risk profiles.

The assessor should determine whether contextual analysis is performed at both the individual system level and the portfolio level where appropriate.

Portfolio analysis may identify shared providers, common data dependencies, recurring risk patterns, and systems with similar exposure.

Evidence may include AI inventories, dependency maps, portfolio risk reports, and system-level assessments.

Portfolio-level analysis should not replace detailed contextual evaluation where an individual system presents material or unusual risks.

### 180. Assessing MAP for Generative AI Systems

Generative AI systems may introduce contextual risks associated with variable outputs, prompt sensitivity, retrieval sources, model access, and user reliance.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the system's intended use, permitted inputs, output consumers, and relevant limitations.

For retrieval-augmented generation systems, the assessment should consider the source documents, retrieval process, access permissions, and downstream use of generated responses.

For systems connected to tools, the assessor should identify available actions and permission boundaries.

The assessment should reflect the complete application rather than treating the underlying language model as the entire system.

### 181. Assessing MAP for Agentic AI Systems

Agentic AI systems may plan tasks, call tools, access information, and execute multi-step workflows with varying levels of autonomy.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the agent's objectives, tool permissions, action boundaries, human oversight, and external dependencies.

Risk analysis should consider how a sequence of individually permitted actions may create an unintended or unauthorized outcome.

Evidence may include agent architecture diagrams, tool inventories, permission configurations, workflow definitions, and test scenarios.

The organization should identify conditions under which an agent must request approval, stop execution, or escalate to a human.

### 182. Assessing MAP for Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Retrieval-augmented generation combines a generative model with information retrieved from external or internal sources.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented retrieval sources, indexing processes, access controls, freshness requirements, and output use.

Relevant risks may include unauthorized retrieval, stale information, poisoned content, incorrect source attribution, and exposure of restricted documents.

Evidence may include retrieval architecture, source inventories, indexing configurations, and access-control tests.

The assessment should consider whether retrieval behavior preserves the permissions and restrictions associated with the underlying information.

### 183. Assessing MAP for Fine-Tuned Models

Fine-tuning may change a model's behavior, domain specialization, or response patterns.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the purpose of fine-tuning, training data, evaluation objectives, and expected limitations.

Relevant evidence may include model lineage, training records, data documentation, evaluation results, and version history.

The organization should consider whether fine-tuning introduces new privacy, security, reliability, or misuse risks.

The assessment should distinguish the risks of the base model from those introduced or changed by the fine-tuning process.

### 184. Assessing MAP for Predictive AI Systems

Predictive AI systems estimate outcomes or assign classifications based on input data.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the target variable, intended population, relevant data assumptions, decision context, and consequences of error.

Evidence may include model specifications, dataset documentation, evaluation plans, and business process records.

The assessment should consider whether the target reflects the actual business objective and whether proxy variables may create unintended consequences.

The system's intended use should remain aligned with the population and conditions represented in its evaluation.

### 185. Assessing MAP for AI-Enabled Cybersecurity Tools

AI-enabled cybersecurity tools may classify alerts, detect anomalies, prioritize vulnerabilities, summarize incidents, or recommend defensive actions.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the tool's authority, data sources, operational dependencies, and potential consequences of incorrect outputs.

Relevant considerations include false positives, false negatives, analyst reliance, automated containment, and access to security infrastructure.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, operating procedures, test results, and permission reviews.

The organization should distinguish between tools that provide recommendations and tools authorized to perform consequential security actions.

### 186. Assessing MAP for AI in Critical Processes

AI systems used in critical processes may affect service availability, safety, financial operations, or other essential organizational objectives.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has evaluated the consequences of failure and the dependencies that could affect continuity.

Evidence may include business impact analyses, resilience plans, system dependency maps, and operational risk assessments.

The assessment should consider fallback arrangements and whether personnel can maintain essential operations when the AI system is unavailable or unreliable.

The level of assessment should reflect the system's role and the consequences of interruption or error.

### 187. Assessing MAP for AI in Human Resources

AI systems used in recruitment, employee evaluation, workforce planning, or other human resources processes may affect individuals' employment opportunities and working conditions.

The assessor should determine whether intended use, affected groups, decision authority, data sources, and potential impacts have been documented.

Relevant evidence may include impact assessments, use-case approvals, evaluation results, and human review procedures.

The organization should consider whether the system is appropriate for the proposed decision and whether affected individuals may require additional safeguards or avenues for review.

The assessment should distinguish between administrative assistance and AI use that materially influences consequential employment decisions.

### 188. Assessing MAP for AI in Customer Services

AI systems used in customer service may provide information, generate recommendations, summarize cases, or initiate actions.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the system's permitted tasks, customer data access, escalation routes, and limitations.

Relevant risks may include inaccurate guidance, unauthorized disclosure, inappropriate actions, and failure to escalate sensitive cases.

Evidence may include workflow definitions, access controls, testing records, and customer interaction procedures.

The assessment should consider how users may interpret AI-generated information and what happens when the system cannot reliably address a request.

### 189. Assessing MAP for AI in Financial Processes

AI systems supporting financial processes may influence fraud detection, credit analysis, forecasting, transaction review, or financial reporting.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented the decision context, data dependencies, error consequences, and required human oversight.

Evidence may include model documentation, validation reports, process maps, risk assessments, and approval records.

The organization should consider the consequences of incorrect outputs, potential manipulation, and reliance on shared data or providers.

The assessment should reflect the specific role of the AI system and should not assume that every financial use case carries identical risks.

### 190. Assessing MAP for AI in Healthcare-Related Processes

AI systems used in healthcare-related contexts may support administrative tasks, information retrieval, workflow coordination, or clinical decision support.

The assessor should determine whether the system's purpose, users, operating conditions, and potential consequences have been clearly documented.

Relevant considerations may include patient safety, privacy, reliability, human oversight, and the consequences of incorrect or incomplete information.

Evidence may include system specifications, risk assessments, validation records, and operating procedures.

The assessment should be proportionate to the system's actual role and should not treat an administrative assistant as equivalent to a system that directly influences clinical decisions.

### 191. Assessing MAP for AI Used by External Customers

Externally accessible AI systems may receive unpredictable inputs from a broad range of users and may be exposed to deliberate misuse.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has considered public accessibility, abuse scenarios, input handling, output distribution, and service dependencies.

Evidence may include threat models, user access policies, monitoring plans, and abuse testing.

The assessment should consider how the system behaves when users attempt to exceed intended-use boundaries.

Controls should be designed around the actual exposure and capabilities of the deployed service.

### 192. Assessing MAP for Internal Enterprise AI

Internal enterprise AI systems may have access to corporate documents, communication systems, business applications, or confidential operational data.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has documented access boundaries, data classifications, user groups, and permitted actions.

Relevant risks include inappropriate retrieval, excessive permissions, confidential information disclosure, and overreliance on generated responses.

Evidence may include access-control designs, data-flow diagrams, approved-use guidance, and testing records.

Internal availability should not be treated as inherently safe when the system can access sensitive information or influence important business decisions.

### 193. Assessing MAP for AI Development Environments

AI development environments may contain source code, datasets, model artifacts, credentials, and experimental systems.

The assessor should determine whether development activities and associated assets are included in the contextual analysis.

Relevant considerations include data provenance, access controls, experiment tracking, dependency management, and separation between development and production.

Evidence may include development architecture, repository permissions, dataset inventories, and release procedures.

The assessment should consider whether weaknesses in development environments could affect deployed systems or expose sensitive information.

### 194. Assessing MAP for AI Model Hosting Services

Model hosting services may provide inference endpoints, deployment infrastructure, scaling, logging, and operational management.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the provider's responsibilities, the deployment architecture, and the relevant data flows.

Evidence may include service descriptions, contracts, architecture diagrams, security documentation, and configuration records.

The assessment should identify which controls are managed by the provider and which remain the organization's responsibility.

The use of a managed service does not remove the need to understand the resulting system's risks.

### 195. Assessing MAP for Open-Source AI Components

Open-source AI components may introduce dependencies on external maintainers, community contributions, package repositories, and publicly available model artifacts.

The assessor should determine whether these components and their origins are documented.

Relevant considerations include licensing, provenance, integrity, maintenance status, vulnerabilities, and the availability of support.

Evidence may include software bills of materials, model artifact inventories, repository records, and dependency scanning results.

The organization should assess the specific component and intended use rather than assuming that open-source availability establishes either safety or insecurity.

### 196. Assessing MAP for AI System Retirement

Retirement is part of the AI system lifecycle and may create risks involving data retention, access removal, service dependencies, and loss of audit evidence.

The assessor should determine whether retirement conditions and responsibilities are documented.

Evidence may include decommissioning plans, data deletion records, credential revocation, supplier termination records, and archival procedures.

The organization should consider whether downstream systems still depend on the retired service and whether replacement arrangements are adequate.

Retirement should be treated as a managed lifecycle activity rather than simply turning off the model endpoint.

### 197. Assessing MAP Findings and Evidence Traceability

Each material MAP finding should be traceable to the relevant assessment criteria, evidence, contextual gap, and risk implication.

The assessor should distinguish observed facts from assumptions, interpretations, and unresolved questions.

Evidence references should enable reviewers to understand how the conclusion was reached.

Where information is unavailable, the assessor should explain whether the gap prevents a reliable conclusion or merely limits the depth of the assessment.

Traceability supports defensible reporting and makes remediation more targeted.

### 198. Assessing MAP Function Reporting

MAP assessment reporting should communicate whether the organization has adequately established the context needed to understand and manage AI risks.

The report should identify material strengths, gaps, unsupported assumptions, and limitations in available evidence.

Findings should be prioritized according to their significance for subsequent risk assessment and management.

The report should explain where contextual gaps affect confidence in other assessment conclusions.

A strong report provides management with a clear explanation of what is unknown, why it matters, and what should happen next.

### 199. Assessing MAP Function Continual Improvement

MAP should be revisited as the organization learns more about the system and its operating environment.

New incidents, evaluation results, stakeholder feedback, supplier changes, and changes in intended use may reveal previously unrecognized risks.

The assessor should determine whether the organization incorporates these inputs into updated context records and risk analyses.

Evidence may include revised system documentation, reassessment records, change approvals, and lessons-learned actions.

Continual improvement requires the organization to update its understanding when evidence changes rather than preserving outdated assumptions.

### 200. Core Principles of MAP Function Assessment

The MAP function establishes the contextual foundation for AI risk management. An effective assessment determines whether the organization understands the system's purpose, boundaries, architecture, dependencies, intended users, capabilities, limitations, stakeholders, and potential impacts.

The assessor should evaluate the quality and currency of contextual information, the validity of material assumptions, and the traceability between system context and identified risks.

MAP findings should inform the MEASURE function's testing and evaluation activities and the MANAGE function's risk treatment decisions. Missing or unreliable context can undermine both functions, even when the organization has documented extensive controls.

**The central principle of MAP assessment is that AI risks cannot be evaluated reliably without first understanding the system, its operating environment, and the people and processes it may affect.**

## Part 3 — MEASURE Function Assessment, AI Risk Evaluation, Testing, and Measurement

### 201. Introduction to the MEASURE Function

The MEASURE function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) establishes the processes used to analyze, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risks. While GOVERN establishes organizational oversight and MAP establishes the relevant context, MEASURE provides the evidence needed to understand system behavior and evaluate potential impacts.

An assessment of MEASURE examines whether the organization has defined appropriate evaluation methods, selected meaningful metrics, tested relevant risks, documented limitations, and communicated results to decision-makers.

The objective is not simply to demonstrate that testing occurred. The assessor must determine whether the evaluation methods are appropriate to the intended use, whether the evidence supports the conclusions, and whether identified limitations are incorporated into risk decisions.

### 202. Understanding MEASURE Function Categories

NIST AI RMF 1.0 organizes the MEASURE function into four categories:

- MEASURE 1: Appropriate methods and metrics are identified and applied.
- MEASURE 2: AI systems are evaluated for trustworthiness characteristics.
- MEASURE 3: Mechanisms are established to track identified AI risks over time.
- MEASURE 4: Feedback about the efficacy of measurement is incorporated into assessment and improvement.

The assessor should identify applicable subcategories and determine whether the organization's processes satisfy the relevant assessment criteria.

The evaluation should consider the system's lifecycle, risk profile, deployment context, and available evidence. A single testing method or metric rarely provides sufficient assurance across every AI risk dimension.

### 203. Assessing the AI Measurement Strategy

An AI measurement strategy defines how the organization evaluates system performance, trustworthiness, and risk.

The assessor should determine whether the strategy identifies objectives, methods, metrics, acceptance criteria, responsible parties, and reporting arrangements.

Evidence may include evaluation policies, test strategies, model validation plans, risk assessments, and technical standards.

The strategy should connect identified risks to appropriate evaluation activities. For example, a system exposed to prompt injection should have relevant security testing rather than relying exclusively on accuracy benchmarks.

A measurement strategy is effective when it produces evidence that supports defensible risk decisions.

### 204. Assessing Measurement Objectives

Measurement objectives describe what the organization intends to establish through evaluation.

Objectives may include determining predictive accuracy, robustness, fairness, privacy protection, security resilience, reliability, or suitability for a particular use case.

The assessor should determine whether objectives are explicit and aligned with the system's intended purpose and risk profile.

Evidence may include requirements documents, evaluation plans, risk registers, and approved test criteria.

Vague objectives such as "ensure the model is safe" are insufficient unless translated into specific evaluation questions and observable criteria.

### 205. Assessing the Relationship Between Risk and Measurement

Measurement activities should be selected according to the risks identified during contextual analysis.

The assessor should determine whether material risk scenarios are mapped to evaluation activities and whether the selected methods can meaningfully investigate those scenarios.

For example, the risk of unauthorized access to confidential information may require access-control testing, retrieval permission tests, and data leakage evaluations.

Evidence may include risk-to-test matrices, threat models, test plans, and assessment reports.

Where a material risk has no corresponding evaluation activity, the assessor should determine whether the omission is justified or represents an assurance gap.

### 206. Assessing Measurement Criteria and Acceptance Thresholds

Acceptance criteria define the conditions under which measured results are considered adequate for the intended use.

The assessor should determine whether thresholds are documented, justified, approved, and appropriate to the consequences of failure.

Criteria may include minimum performance levels, maximum error rates, security test requirements, acceptable latency, or limits on unauthorized actions.

Evidence may include technical specifications, risk acceptance records, validation plans, and release criteria.

The organization should not select thresholds solely because they are easy to achieve or because a model performs better than an earlier version.

### 207. Assessing Metric Selection

Metrics should provide meaningful information about the system characteristics and risks being evaluated.

The assessor should determine whether metrics are relevant, interpretable, reproducible, and appropriate to the evaluation objective.

Examples include precision, recall, false-positive rates, false-negative rates, calibration measures, latency, availability, and rates of policy-violating outputs.

A metric should be interpreted in context. High overall accuracy may conceal poor performance on a critical subgroup or a failure mode with serious consequences.

The organization should document the rationale for selecting each material metric and acknowledge its limitations.

### 208. Assessing Metric Validity

Metric validity concerns whether a measure actually provides evidence about the property it is intended to represent.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the assumptions, limitations, and potential distortions associated with its chosen metrics.

For example, user satisfaction may indicate perceived usefulness but does not independently establish factual accuracy, security, or fairness.

Evidence may include metric definitions, validation studies, benchmark documentation, and methodological reviews.

A metric should not be treated as conclusive evidence of trustworthiness merely because it is quantitative.

### 209. Assessing Metric Reliability and Reproducibility

Reliable measurement requires methods that produce sufficiently consistent results under comparable conditions.

The assessor should determine whether test procedures, datasets, model versions, configurations, and evaluation conditions are documented.

Evidence may include experiment records, version-controlled test suites, repeated-run results, and measurement procedures.

For stochastic AI systems, reproducibility may require recording prompts, parameters, seeds where applicable, tool configurations, and relevant environmental conditions.

Where exact reproduction is not possible, the organization should document the variability and explain how it affects confidence in the findings.

### 210. Assessing Measurement Coverage

Measurement coverage describes the extent to which relevant risks, system components, use cases, and operating conditions are evaluated.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified important areas that remain untested or inadequately tested.

Evidence may include risk-to-test matrices, coverage reports, test inventories, and documented exclusions.

Coverage should reflect the system's actual exposure and the consequences of failure rather than the number of test cases executed.

Uncovered areas should be prioritized according to risk and addressed through additional testing, compensating controls, or explicit limitations on use.

### 211. Assessing Test Environment Representativeness

Evaluation results are most useful when the test environment reasonably represents the conditions under which the AI system will operate.

The assessor should determine whether the test environment reflects relevant configurations, data characteristics, integrations, permissions, and user interactions.

Evidence may include environment specifications, deployment diagrams, test records, and configuration comparisons.

Differences between test and production environments should be documented and evaluated for their effect on the results.

Testing a model in isolation may be insufficient when production behavior depends on retrieval systems, external APIs, or workflow orchestration.

### 212. Assessing Test Data Suitability

Test data should be appropriate to the evaluation objective and sufficiently representative of relevant operational conditions.

The assessor should determine whether the data's source, quality, scope, and limitations are documented.

Relevant considerations include data freshness, coverage, class imbalance, sensitive information, and differences between test and production populations.

Evidence may include dataset documentation, data quality reports, test specifications, and data governance records.

The assessor should also determine whether test data handling complies with applicable security, privacy, and access requirements.

### 213. Assessing Test Data Independence

Evaluation data should be sufficiently independent from training or tuning data to support meaningful conclusions about generalization.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has considered data leakage, duplicated examples, overlapping records, and repeated use of the same evaluation set.

Evidence may include dataset split procedures, lineage records, data deduplication reports, and experiment documentation.

Where evaluation data has influenced model development, the organization should assess whether the reported performance remains reliable.

The assessment should focus on whether the methodology supports the conclusions being made rather than assuming that every repeated use invalidates the evaluation.

### 214. Assessing Benchmark Selection

Benchmarks provide standardized or comparative methods for evaluating AI systems.

The assessor should determine whether selected benchmarks are relevant to the intended use and whether their limitations are understood.

Evidence may include benchmark specifications, evaluation reports, comparison studies, and methodological documentation.

A general-purpose benchmark may not reflect the system's domain, language, user population, or operational conditions.

Benchmark results should therefore be interpreted alongside application-specific evaluation rather than treated as sufficient proof of suitability.

### 215. Assessing External Evaluation Methods

Organizations may use independent laboratories, external assessors, specialist testing providers, or published evaluation services.

The assessor should determine whether external methods are appropriate, sufficiently independent, and relevant to the system under assessment.

Evidence may include contracts, methodology descriptions, assessor qualifications, test reports, and statements of scope.

The organization should establish what was actually tested, which version was evaluated, and what limitations apply.

External evaluation can strengthen assurance, but it does not eliminate the organization's responsibility to determine whether the results support its own use case.

### 216. Assessing Internal Evaluation Competence

Effective AI measurement requires personnel with suitable technical, analytical, domain, and risk management competence.

The assessor should determine whether people conducting evaluations understand the methods they use and the limitations of their conclusions.

Evidence may include competency records, training, role descriptions, review procedures, and technical qualifications.

Where specialized evaluation is necessary, the organization should obtain appropriate expertise rather than relying on general familiarity with AI tools.

The assessment should also consider whether reviewers can challenge results independently of the teams responsible for development or deployment.

### 217. Assessing Independence of Measurement Activities

Measurement independence helps reduce conflicts of interest and confirmation bias.

The assessor should determine whether evaluation activities are sufficiently separated from the incentives and responsibilities of the teams being evaluated.

The required level of independence depends on the system's risk, the nature of the assessment, and organizational arrangements.

Evidence may include reporting lines, approval records, independent review results, and documented conflict management.

Where full organizational independence is impractical, the organization should consider alternative safeguards such as peer review, independent validation, or escalation to a separate governance function.

### 218. Assessing Measurement Documentation

Measurement documentation should enable a reviewer to understand what was tested, how testing was conducted, what results were obtained, and what conclusions were drawn.

Relevant records may include test plans, datasets or dataset references, configurations, metrics, results, limitations, and approvals.

The assessor should determine whether documentation is sufficiently complete and traceable to the evaluated system version.

Unexplained summary scores may be inadequate when they conceal important failure cases or methodological limitations.

Documentation should support independent review and subsequent reassessment.

### 219. Assessing Measurement Results Integrity

Measurement results must be protected against unauthorized modification, selective reporting, and loss of context.

The assessor should determine whether results are retained with appropriate access controls, version information, and traceability.

Evidence may include experiment logs, repository history, access permissions, signed reports, and review records.

The assessment should consider whether negative results and material limitations are preserved alongside successful outcomes.

Selective presentation of favorable results can create a misleading impression of system reliability and undermine governance decisions.

### 220. Assessing Measurement Limitations

Every evaluation method has limitations.

Limitations may arise from small sample sizes, incomplete data, constrained testing environments, limited access to model internals, or the inability to simulate all real-world conditions.

The assessor should determine whether limitations are explicitly documented and considered when interpreting results.

Evidence may include evaluation reports, methodology statements, risk assessments, and management decisions.

Where limitations affect a material risk, the organization should consider additional testing, restricted deployment, compensating safeguards, or explicit acceptance by an authorized decision-maker.

### 221. Assessing the Four MEASURE Categories Together

An effective MEASURE assessment considers the relationship among methods and metrics, trustworthiness evaluation, risk tracking, and measurement feedback.

A strong metric without appropriate test data may produce misleading results. A comprehensive evaluation without operational monitoring may fail to identify later deterioration. Monitoring without a process for acting on findings may provide little practical risk reduction.

The assessor should therefore evaluate the measurement lifecycle as a connected process.

Evidence should demonstrate not only that individual activities exist but also that their results inform subsequent evaluation and risk management decisions.

### 222. Assessing Trustworthiness Characteristics

NIST AI RMF identifies several trustworthiness characteristics that organizations should consider in context:

- Valid and reliable.
- Safe.
- Secure and resilient.
- Accountable and transparent.
- Explainable and interpretable.
- Privacy-enhanced.
- Fair, with harmful bias managed.

The assessor should determine which characteristics are relevant to the system and how the organization evaluates them.

Not every characteristic can be reduced to a single metric, and trade-offs may exist between objectives.

The assessment should examine whether the organization has defined a defensible evaluation approach for the characteristics material to its intended use.

### 223. Assessing Validity

Validity concerns whether an AI system performs the task it is intended to perform under the relevant conditions.

The assessor should determine whether the evaluation method reflects the system's intended purpose and whether results support the claims being made.

Evidence may include validation reports, acceptance testing, domain expert review, and comparison against appropriate reference outcomes.

A system may perform well on a generic task while failing to meet the actual business requirement.

Validity should be assessed in relation to the approved use case and the consequences of incorrect outputs.

### 224. Assessing Reliability

Reliability concerns whether the system performs consistently enough for its intended purpose.

The assessor should examine relevant error patterns, performance variation, failure frequency, and behavior across operating conditions.

Evidence may include repeated testing, operational metrics, incident records, and regression evaluations.

For generative AI, reliability assessment may require evaluating the frequency and significance of unsupported claims, inconsistent responses, or failures to follow required constraints.

The organization should define what level of reliability is acceptable for the particular use case.

### 225. Assessing Accuracy

Accuracy measures how often outputs match an accepted reference or expected result under a defined evaluation method.

The assessor should determine whether the selected accuracy metric is appropriate and whether the evaluation set reflects the intended operating context.

Evidence may include confusion matrices, labeled test sets, model evaluation reports, and validation records.

Accuracy alone may be insufficient when errors have unequal consequences or when the dataset is imbalanced.

The assessment should consider which types of errors matter most and whether the metric captures them adequately.

### 226. Assessing Precision and Recall

Precision and recall are useful when evaluating classification and detection tasks.

Precision measures the proportion of predicted positive cases that are correct. Recall measures the proportion of actual positive cases that the system successfully identifies.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the operational implications of both measures.

In security monitoring, low precision may generate excessive false alerts, while low recall may allow genuine threats to go undetected.

The appropriate balance depends on the consequences of each error type and the availability of downstream review or response mechanisms.

### 227. Assessing False-Positive and False-Negative Risks

False positives and false negatives can create different operational and human consequences.

A false positive may trigger unnecessary investigations, deny a legitimate request, or create alert fatigue. A false negative may allow fraud, a security incident, or an incorrect decision to go undetected.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates both error types and their consequences.

Evidence may include confusion matrices, threshold analysis, incident reviews, and operational impact assessments.

The assessment should determine whether decision thresholds and response procedures reflect the relative significance of these errors.

### 228. Assessing Calibration

Calibration concerns whether predicted probabilities correspond appropriately to observed frequencies.

For example, among cases assigned a probability near 80%, a well-calibrated model should produce the relevant outcome at approximately that frequency across a sufficiently large and appropriate set of comparable cases.

The assessor should determine whether calibration is relevant to the system and whether it has been evaluated using appropriate methods.

Evidence may include calibration plots, reliability diagrams, and evaluation reports.

A high-confidence score should not be interpreted as proof of correctness unless the score's meaning and limitations are established.

### 229. Assessing Robustness to Input Variation

AI systems may behave differently when inputs contain noise, unusual formatting, missing values, ambiguous wording, or other variations.

The assessor should determine whether testing covers variations likely to occur in the intended environment.

Evidence may include robustness test cases, boundary testing, malformed-input testing, and evaluation reports.

The organization should establish which variations are expected and how failures are handled.

Testing should distinguish harmless variation from changes that create material risk or cause the system to violate its intended constraints.

### 230. Assessing Generalization Performance

Generalization evaluation examines how well a system performs on data and conditions beyond those directly used to develop it.

The assessor should determine whether evaluation includes relevant populations, environments, time periods, and input characteristics.

Evidence may include holdout test results, cross-validation procedures, out-of-sample evaluations, and domain-specific testing.

The assessment should examine whether the organization has considered overfitting and the limits of the available test data.

Claims about generalization should remain within the conditions supported by the evaluation evidence.

### 231. Assessing Performance Across Subgroups

Overall performance may conceal significant differences between groups, languages, regions, or operating conditions.

The assessor should determine whether subgroup evaluation is appropriate and whether the organization has identified relevant populations.

Evidence may include stratified performance reports, error analysis, dataset documentation, and impact assessments.

The assessment should consider sample sizes and uncertainty when interpreting subgroup results.

Where material disparities are identified, the organization should investigate their causes and determine whether mitigation or restricted use is necessary.

### 232. Assessing Fairness Evaluation

Fairness is context-dependent and may involve different definitions, metrics, legal considerations, and stakeholder expectations.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has explicitly defined the fairness concerns relevant to the use case.

Evidence may include fairness assessment plans, subgroup evaluations, domain expert review, and documented risk decisions.

The assessment should consider whether selected fairness measures are appropriate to the decision context and whether trade-offs are understood.

A single fairness metric should not be treated as a universal measure of fairness across every AI application.

### 233. Assessing Bias Measurement

Bias may arise from data collection, labeling, historical practices, proxy variables, model design, deployment conditions, or user interaction.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified plausible sources of bias and evaluated their potential effects.

Evidence may include data audits, label quality reviews, subgroup analysis, model evaluations, and stakeholder feedback.

The assessment should distinguish statistical differences from harmful or unjustified outcomes and investigate whether observed patterns are relevant to the intended use.

Where bias cannot be fully measured, the limitation should be documented and considered in risk decisions.

### 234. Assessing Data Representativeness

Representativeness concerns whether the evaluation data adequately reflects the population and conditions relevant to the system's intended use.

The assessor should determine whether sampling methods, population coverage, and known exclusions are documented.

Evidence may include dataset profiles, sampling procedures, demographic or domain coverage analysis, and validation reports.

An evaluation dataset may be large but still fail to represent important user groups or rare operating conditions.

The organization should determine whether gaps in representativeness limit the reliability of its conclusions.

### 235. Assessing Rare-Event Performance

Rare events may be difficult to evaluate because relevant examples are scarce.

Examples include uncommon security attacks, unusual operational failures, and infrequent but severe adverse outcomes.

The assessor should determine whether rare-event risks have been considered and whether the evaluation approach is suitable.

Methods may include scenario testing, carefully designed synthetic cases, historical incident analysis, expert review, and controlled simulation.

The limitations of these methods should be documented. Synthetic scenarios can expand test coverage but do not automatically establish performance in real-world conditions.

### 236. Assessing Out-of-Distribution Behavior

Out-of-distribution inputs differ materially from the data or conditions represented during development and evaluation.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified relevant out-of-distribution scenarios and evaluated how the system responds.

Evidence may include anomaly detection tests, stress tests, monitoring rules, and documented fallback behavior.

Depending on the system, suitable behavior may include abstaining, requesting clarification, escalating to a human, or refusing an unsupported action.

The organization should not assume that a model will reliably recognize its own limits without supporting evidence.

### 237. Assessing Safe AI Behavior

Safety evaluation examines whether the system avoids unacceptable harm under relevant operating conditions.

The assessor should determine whether potential hazards, failure conditions, and safeguards have been identified and tested.

Evidence may include hazard analysis, scenario tests, safety requirements, validation reports, and operational procedures.

The evaluation should consider the consequences of both system outputs and downstream actions.

Where the system may influence high-impact processes, the organization should establish appropriate release criteria, oversight, and response arrangements.

### 238. Assessing Hazard Identification

Hazard identification seeks to identify conditions that could contribute to harmful outcomes.

Potential hazards may arise from incorrect outputs, unsafe recommendations, unauthorized actions, inappropriate reliance, or failures in surrounding systems.

The assessor should determine whether hazards have been identified systematically and linked to relevant system functions.

Evidence may include hazard registers, scenario analysis, design reviews, and incident records.

The assessment should examine whether important hazards are evaluated before deployment and reconsidered when the system or its operating context changes.

### 239. Assessing Safety Test Coverage

Safety testing should address plausible hazards and the conditions under which they may occur.

The assessor should determine whether the test plan covers material failure scenarios, expected safeguards, and relevant boundary conditions.

Evidence may include safety test specifications, execution records, defect reports, and acceptance decisions.

Testing should evaluate not only whether the system can produce an unsafe result but also whether safeguards detect, prevent, or mitigate that result.

Unresolved safety failures should be evaluated according to their potential consequences and the system's intended use.

### 240. Assessing Fail-Safe and Fallback Behavior

Fail-safe behavior defines how the system responds when it cannot operate reliably or when a critical condition is detected.

Fallback arrangements may include manual processing, restricted functionality, service suspension, or escalation to a qualified person.

The assessor should determine whether these arrangements are documented and tested.

Evidence may include failure simulations, continuity plans, configuration records, and incident exercises.

A fallback mechanism should remain practical under actual operating conditions and should not introduce a different uncontrolled risk.

### 241. Assessing Security Evaluation

AI security evaluation examines whether the system resists relevant threats and protects its assets, data, interfaces, and operational capabilities.

The assessor should determine whether testing reflects the threat model, architecture, access model, and intended use.

Relevant areas may include authentication, authorization, data protection, API security, model access, prompt injection, malicious inputs, and supply-chain dependencies.

Evidence may include threat models, penetration tests, configuration reviews, vulnerability assessments, and security test reports.

Security evaluation should cover the complete AI application where material risks arise from components surrounding the model.

### 242. Assessing Adversarial Robustness

Adversarial robustness concerns how the system behaves when an attacker deliberately crafts inputs to manipulate its behavior.

The assessor should determine whether relevant attack techniques and threat assumptions are included in testing.

Depending on the system, testing may address adversarial examples, malicious prompts, crafted documents, data poisoning, or attempts to bypass restrictions.

Evidence may include attack scenarios, red-team reports, mitigation testing, and documented limitations.

A successful test against a limited set of attacks does not establish resistance to every possible adversarial technique.

### 243. Assessing Prompt Injection Resistance

Prompt injection occurs when malicious or manipulative instructions influence an AI system through user inputs or retrieved content.

The assessor should determine whether prompt injection is relevant to the system's architecture and whether testing covers the relevant trust boundaries.

For retrieval-augmented systems, tests may examine malicious instructions embedded in retrieved documents. For tool-enabled systems, testing should consider whether manipulated instructions can lead to unauthorized actions.

Evidence may include red-team scenarios, permission tests, application controls, and incident records.

The assessment should focus on whether the complete application limits harmful consequences, not merely whether a prompt-level filter detects known phrases.

### 244. Assessing Data Poisoning Risks

Data poisoning involves manipulating data used to train, fine-tune, or otherwise influence an AI system.

The assessor should determine whether poisoning risks are relevant to the system's data sources and lifecycle.

Potential safeguards include data provenance controls, source validation, access restrictions, integrity verification, anomaly analysis, and controlled retraining procedures.

Evidence may include dataset governance records, pipeline controls, training procedures, and security testing.

The assessment should consider whether malicious or corrupted data could materially change system behavior and whether resulting changes can be detected.

### 245. Assessing Model Extraction and Unauthorized Access

Model extraction attempts seek to obtain information about a model through access to its outputs, interfaces, or associated artifacts.

The assessor should determine whether model confidentiality, intellectual property, or misuse resistance is material to the system.

Relevant evidence may include API access controls, rate limits, monitoring, authentication configuration, and security testing.

The assessment should consider the capabilities available to external users and whether unrestricted access could create unacceptable exposure.

Controls should be proportionate to the model's sensitivity, deployment architecture, and plausible threat scenarios.

### 246. Assessing Sensitive Information Disclosure

AI systems may expose sensitive information through training data memorization, retrieval errors, excessive permissions, logs, generated outputs, or integrations.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has evaluated relevant disclosure scenarios.

Evidence may include data-flow analysis, access-control tests, privacy assessments, red-team results, and logging reviews.

The assessment should consider whether a user can obtain information beyond their authorized access and whether sensitive information is unnecessarily retained.

A general statement that the model was trained securely is insufficient to establish that the deployed application prevents disclosure.

### 247. Assessing Unauthorized Tool Use

AI systems connected to tools may invoke APIs, query databases, create records, send communications, or initiate transactions.

The assessor should determine whether tool permissions are limited to the intended tasks and whether unauthorized or unsafe actions are tested.

Evidence may include tool inventories, identity configurations, permission reviews, execution logs, and approval workflows.

The assessment should consider whether untrusted inputs can influence tool selection or arguments and whether the system can exceed its authorized scope.

Where actions are consequential, safeguards may include explicit authorization, constrained parameters, transaction limits, and human approval.

### 248. Assessing AI Application Access Controls

Access controls should restrict users and system components to the resources and actions they require.

The assessor should determine whether authentication, authorization, privilege separation, and access reviews cover AI-specific components.

Relevant components may include model endpoints, prompt repositories, retrieval indexes, vector databases, evaluation environments, and administrative interfaces.

Evidence may include access policies, configuration records, access reviews, and authorization testing.

The assessment should verify that AI-generated requests or tool calls do not bypass the application's established authorization boundaries.

### 249. Assessing AI API Security

AI APIs may expose model functionality, application data, tool execution, or administrative operations.

The assessor should determine whether API risks are identified and evaluated using appropriate security methods.

Relevant controls may include authentication, authorization, input validation, rate limiting, request monitoring, secret management, and secure error handling.

Evidence may include API specifications, security testing, configuration reviews, and access logs.

The assessment should consider both conventional API vulnerabilities and risks arising from AI-specific input and output behavior.

### 250. Assessing Supply-Chain Security Measurement

AI supply chains may include model providers, software packages, datasets, pretrained artifacts, infrastructure services, and development tools.

The assessor should determine whether relevant supply-chain risks are evaluated and whether evidence supports the organization's confidence in critical dependencies.

Evidence may include supplier assessments, provenance records, software bills of materials, model artifact inventories, vulnerability reports, and contractual documentation.

The assessment should identify where visibility is limited and whether the organization has adopted appropriate compensating safeguards.

Supply-chain assurance should reflect the complete dependency chain where material risks can propagate across components.

### 251. Assessing Privacy Evaluation

Privacy evaluation examines whether the system handles personal and sensitive information appropriately throughout its lifecycle.

The assessor should determine whether relevant privacy risks are identified and whether evaluation methods are suitable for the system's data flows and intended use.

Evidence may include privacy impact assessments, data inventories, retention configurations, access tests, and privacy testing reports.

The assessment should consider collection, training, inference, retrieval, logging, sharing, and deletion where relevant.

Privacy evaluation should address actual system behavior rather than rely solely on policy statements.

### 252. Assessing Data Minimization

Data minimization concerns whether the system collects, uses, retains, and exposes only information necessary for its legitimate purpose.

The assessor should determine whether data requirements are documented and whether unnecessary data is excluded or removed.

Evidence may include data schemas, collection configurations, data retention rules, and application tests.

For retrieval-based systems, the assessment should examine whether the system retrieves information appropriate to the user's task and permissions.

The organization should consider whether reduced data access can achieve the required outcome with lower risk.

### 253. Assessing Memorization and Extraction Risks

Models may retain or reveal aspects of training data, particularly when exposed to repeated or adversarial queries.

The assessor should determine whether memorization and extraction risks are relevant to the data and model type.

Evaluation may include targeted extraction tests, privacy attacks, output analysis, and review of training data sensitivity.

Evidence should document the test methodology, results, limitations, and relevant mitigation measures.

The absence of sensitive information in a small sample of outputs does not prove that extraction risk is eliminated.

### 254. Assessing Privacy-Preserving Techniques

Organizations may use techniques such as data anonymization, pseudonymization, differential privacy, access restrictions, or federated learning to reduce privacy risks.

The assessor should determine whether the selected technique is appropriate to the threat model and supported by evidence.

Evidence may include design documentation, configuration records, validation studies, and privacy analysis.

The assessment should consider the technique's limitations and whether residual risks remain.

The use of a privacy-preserving label or technology should not be treated as proof that all relevant privacy risks have been addressed.

### 255. Assessing Explainability and Interpretability

Explainability and interpretability evaluation examines whether relevant system behavior can be understood sufficiently for the intended use and oversight requirements.

The assessor should determine whether explanation methods are appropriate to the model, audience, and decision context.

Evidence may include explanation reports, usability studies, validation results, and documented limitations.

The assessment should consider whether explanations are stable, meaningful, and faithful enough to support the decisions for which they are used.

An explanation that appears understandable may still be misleading if it does not accurately reflect the system's behavior.

### 256. Assessing Explanation Fidelity

Explanation fidelity concerns whether an explanation accurately represents the behavior of the underlying model or system.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has evaluated the reliability of the explanation method.

Evidence may include comparison studies, sensitivity analysis, perturbation testing, and methodological validation.

The assessment should consider whether the explanation remains meaningful under relevant changes to the input or model.

Where fidelity is limited, the organization should communicate that limitation and avoid using the explanation as conclusive evidence of causation.

### 257. Assessing Interpretability for Decision-Makers

Decision-makers require information appropriate to their responsibilities and the consequences of the decisions they make.

The assessor should determine whether explanations provide sufficient information to support review, challenge, and escalation.

Relevant evidence may include user interface evaluations, operating procedures, decision records, and stakeholder feedback.

The assessment should consider whether users can identify uncertainty, limitations, and situations requiring further investigation.

Technical detail alone may not provide meaningful interpretability to business users who need to make practical decisions.

### 258. Assessing Transparency of Evaluation Results

Evaluation results should be communicated in a way that allows relevant stakeholders to understand performance, limitations, and unresolved risks.

The assessor should determine whether reports clearly describe methods, scope, findings, and confidence limitations.

Evidence may include evaluation reports, management summaries, release decisions, and risk committee records.

The assessment should identify whether important failures or limitations are obscured by aggregate scores or overly broad claims.

Transparency supports informed decisions about deployment, monitoring, and risk acceptance.

### 259. Assessing Accountability Through Measurement

Measurement should produce evidence that supports clear accountability for AI risk decisions.

The assessor should determine whether results are reviewed by authorized personnel and whether unresolved issues have assigned owners.

Evidence may include approval records, risk acceptance decisions, meeting minutes, and remediation tracking.

The assessment should examine whether decision-makers understand the significance of the results and have authority to restrict deployment when necessary.

Technical testing without accountable review may fail to influence actual risk management.

### 260. Assessing Operational Measurement Requirements

Operational measurement concerns the information required to determine whether the AI system continues to behave acceptably after deployment.

The assessor should determine whether operational indicators are defined and linked to material risks.

Indicators may include error rates, service availability, drift signals, security events, override frequency, and user complaints.

Evidence may include monitoring plans, dashboard definitions, alert configurations, and operating procedures.

The organization should establish which conditions require investigation, escalation, or changes to system operation.

### 261. Assessing Risk Tracking Mechanisms

MEASURE includes mechanisms for tracking identified AI risks over time.

The assessor should determine whether the organization maintains records of material risks, their status, supporting evidence, and relevant changes.

Evidence may include risk registers, monitoring records, assessment reports, incident data, and management review records.

Risk tracking should make it possible to determine whether exposure is increasing, decreasing, or remaining outside accepted limits.

A risk register that is updated only during initial approval may not adequately support ongoing AI risk management.

### 262. Assessing AI Risk Indicators

Risk indicators provide information about conditions that may signal a change in exposure.

Examples include increasing error rates, unusual input patterns, rising override frequency, new vulnerabilities, supplier incidents, and changes in data quality.

The assessor should determine whether indicators are connected to identified risks and have defined interpretation criteria.

Evidence may include monitoring specifications, threshold definitions, risk reports, and alert histories.

Indicators should be selected because they provide meaningful information, not merely because the underlying data is easy to collect.

### 263. Assessing Leading and Lagging Indicators

Leading indicators may provide early warning of changing risk, while lagging indicators reflect events or outcomes that have already occurred.

For example, a rise in anomalous input patterns may serve as an early warning, while confirmed data disclosure is a lagging indicator.

The assessor should determine whether the monitoring approach includes suitable indicators for the relevant risks.

Evidence may include monitoring designs, incident metrics, alert definitions, and management reporting.

A balanced approach can improve the organization's ability to identify emerging problems before they result in serious harm.

### 264. Assessing Monitoring Thresholds

Monitoring thresholds define when a measured condition requires attention or action.

The assessor should determine whether thresholds are documented, justified, and appropriate to the risk.

Evidence may include alert rules, baseline analysis, operational procedures, and threshold approval records.

Thresholds should be reviewed when system behavior, operating conditions, or risk tolerance changes.

An alert threshold that is too permissive may delay detection, while one that is too sensitive may create excessive false alarms and reduce response effectiveness.

### 265. Assessing AI Drift Monitoring

Drift monitoring seeks to identify relevant changes in data characteristics or system behavior over time.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified which forms of drift matter to the intended use.

Possible approaches include monitoring input distributions, changes in data quality, changes in model outputs, and performance against reference outcomes when labels become available.

Evidence may include drift detection configurations, monitoring reports, and reassessment records.

Drift signals should be interpreted carefully because a statistical change does not automatically establish harmful performance deterioration.

### 266. Assessing Performance Degradation

Performance degradation occurs when the system no longer meets relevant performance requirements.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has methods for detecting degradation and evaluating its significance.

Evidence may include operational metrics, comparison against baseline performance, validation reports, and incident records.

The assessment should examine whether degradation is evaluated across relevant use cases and populations where appropriate.

The organization should define how material degradation affects continued operation, including whether restrictions, retraining, rollback, or suspension are required.

### 267. Assessing Post-Deployment Validation

Post-deployment validation evaluates whether the system continues to satisfy relevant requirements in its actual operating environment.

The assessor should determine whether validation occurs at an appropriate frequency and after significant changes or incidents.

Evidence may include production evaluations, release records, performance reports, and periodic review documentation.

The validation approach should account for limitations in observing ground-truth outcomes in production.

Where direct performance measurement is unavailable, the organization should document the alternative indicators used and the uncertainty they introduce.

### 268. Assessing Production Data Quality Monitoring

Changes in production data quality can affect AI outputs even when the model remains unchanged.

The assessor should determine whether relevant data characteristics are monitored and whether significant issues trigger investigation.

Evidence may include validation rules, missing-data metrics, schema checks, source quality reports, and pipeline alerts.

The assessment should consider whether corrupted, incomplete, stale, or unexpected data can enter the system without detection.

Data quality monitoring should be linked to the consequences of incorrect inputs and the safeguards available to limit downstream effects.

### 269. Assessing Monitoring of Human Overrides

Human overrides may reveal system errors, inappropriate recommendations, changing operating conditions, or weaknesses in workflow design.

The assessor should determine whether overrides are recorded and analyzed where relevant.

Evidence may include override logs, analyst feedback, quality reviews, and trend reports.

The assessment should distinguish appropriate human judgment from repeated overrides that indicate a systematic system problem.

A high override rate may justify investigation, but its meaning depends on the role of human review and the expected operating process.

### 270. Assessing Monitoring of User Complaints

User complaints can reveal problems not captured by technical metrics.

The assessor should determine whether the organization collects, categorizes, investigates, and escalates relevant complaints.

Evidence may include support tickets, complaint records, issue classifications, and corrective action tracking.

The organization should assess whether complaint patterns indicate changes in risk or recurring failure modes.

Low complaint volume should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of good performance, especially where users lack an effective reporting channel.

### 271. Assessing Monitoring of Security Events

AI security monitoring should address relevant events across the application, model interfaces, data stores, integrations, and administrative components.

The assessor should determine whether security events are collected, correlated, and reviewed in accordance with the threat model.

Relevant signals may include unauthorized access attempts, unusual API usage, suspicious tool calls, repeated prompt attacks, and anomalous data access.

Evidence may include logging configurations, detection rules, security incident records, and monitoring procedures.

Monitoring should support investigation and response without unnecessarily collecting or retaining sensitive information.

### 272. Assessing Monitoring of Privacy Events

Privacy monitoring may involve detecting unauthorized access, unexpected disclosure, excessive data collection, or violations of retention requirements.

The assessor should determine whether relevant events can be identified and escalated.

Evidence may include access logs, data loss prevention alerts, privacy incident records, and retention monitoring.

The assessment should consider whether AI-generated outputs or retrieval behavior can disclose information in ways not captured by conventional monitoring.

Where privacy incidents occur, the organization should determine whether they indicate broader weaknesses in system design or governance.

### 273. Assessing Monitoring of Agentic AI Actions

Agentic systems may perform multi-step actions whose combined effects are difficult to understand from isolated events.

The assessor should determine whether the organization can reconstruct significant agent activities and identify unauthorized or abnormal behavior.

Evidence may include execution traces, tool-call records, approval logs, identity records, and workflow monitoring.

The assessment should consider whether action limits, transaction boundaries, and escalation conditions are monitored.

Where agents can affect external systems, monitoring should support timely interruption and investigation of consequential behavior.

### 274. Assessing Measurement Feedback Loops

MEASURE includes incorporating feedback about the effectiveness of measurement itself.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates whether its metrics, tests, and monitoring methods are detecting relevant problems.

Evidence may include test effectiveness reviews, incident-to-test mappings, false-alarm analysis, and updates to evaluation plans.

For example, an incident that was not detected by existing monitoring may indicate that a relevant indicator or alert rule is missing.

Measurement should evolve when evidence demonstrates that existing methods are inadequate.

### 275. Assessing Test Effectiveness

Test effectiveness concerns whether the testing process identifies the failures and weaknesses it is intended to detect.

The assessor should determine whether test results are reviewed against actual incidents, known defects, and observed operational behavior.

Evidence may include defect escape analysis, incident reviews, regression test additions, and test coverage reports.

The organization should investigate recurring failures that were not identified before deployment.

A large number of executed tests does not necessarily indicate effective testing if important failure modes remain undetected.

### 276. Assessing False Assurance From Metrics

False assurance occurs when measured results appear favorable but fail to represent material risks.

Examples include high average accuracy masking poor subgroup performance or successful benchmark results masking application-level security weaknesses.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates the limitations and possible blind spots of its metrics.

Evidence may include independent review, sensitivity analysis, alternative metrics, and documented assumptions.

The assessment should examine whether management reporting presents limitations alongside favorable results.

Metrics should inform judgment rather than replace it.

### 277. Assessing Measurement Bias

Measurement processes may introduce bias through dataset selection, labeling, benchmark design, evaluation assumptions, or the interpretation of results.

The assessor should determine whether these risks are recognized and whether appropriate safeguards exist.

Evidence may include methodological reviews, dataset documentation, peer review, and independent validation.

The assessment should consider whether the selected measurement method systematically overlooks certain errors or affected populations.

Measurement bias can undermine assurance even when the testing process is technically well executed.

### 278. Assessing Statistical Uncertainty

Evaluation results are subject to uncertainty arising from sample size, data variation, measurement methods, and model behavior.

The assessor should determine whether uncertainty is quantified or described appropriately where it materially affects conclusions.

Evidence may include confidence intervals, sample size justifications, repeated-run analyses, and statistical review.

The organization should avoid presenting small differences as meaningful without considering the uncertainty in the results.

Where uncertainty remains high, decisions may require additional testing, conservative assumptions, or restricted deployment.

### 279. Assessing Sample Size Adequacy

Sample size affects the strength of conclusions drawn from evaluation data.

The assessor should determine whether the number of test cases is appropriate to the evaluation objective, expected error frequency, and consequences of failure.

Evidence may include sampling plans, statistical justifications, test reports, and evaluation methodology.

Rare but severe failures may not be adequately addressed by a large random sample alone.

The organization should explain how its sample design supports the claims made about system behavior.

### 280. Assessing Confidence in Evaluation Conclusions

Confidence in evaluation conclusions depends on the quality of evidence, the appropriateness of the methods, the representativeness of the test conditions, and the uncertainty that remains.

The assessor should determine whether reports distinguish strong evidence from preliminary indications or unsupported assumptions.

Evidence may include methodology reviews, limitations statements, independent assessments, and management decisions.

Conclusions should not exceed what the available evidence supports.

Where confidence is insufficient for a material risk decision, the organization should identify what additional evidence is required.

### 281. Assessing Comparative Model Evaluation

Comparative evaluation examines whether alternative models or versions differ in relevant performance or risk characteristics.

The assessor should determine whether comparisons use appropriate conditions and metrics.

Evidence may include benchmark reports, controlled experiments, version comparisons, and acceptance records.

The assessment should consider whether improvements in one characteristic are accompanied by regressions in another.

A newer or more capable model should not automatically be considered safer or more suitable without supporting evaluation evidence.

### 282. Assessing Regression Testing

Regression testing evaluates whether changes introduce new defects or undermine previously acceptable behavior.

The assessor should determine whether regression tests cover material requirements and known failure modes.

Evidence may include version-controlled test suites, release reports, defect records, and approval criteria.

For generative AI systems, regression testing may need to consider output quality, refusal behavior, security controls, tool use, and retrieval performance.

The assessment should examine whether significant failures discovered during previous evaluations remain covered by subsequent test cycles.

### 283. Assessing Model Update Evaluation

Model updates may change performance, behavior, security exposure, or operational requirements.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates relevant changes before allowing them into production.

Evidence may include change records, release notes, updated model documentation, evaluation reports, and deployment approvals.

The required testing should reflect the significance of the update and the risks associated with the use case.

Where a provider changes a hosted model without detailed disclosure, the organization should consider what monitoring, fallback, or contractual safeguards are needed.

### 284. Assessing Prompt and Configuration Evaluation

Prompts, system instructions, retrieval settings, tool permissions, and application configurations can materially affect AI system behavior.

The assessor should determine whether important configurations are controlled and evaluated.

Evidence may include configuration repositories, change records, test reports, access controls, and approval histories.

The assessment should consider whether configuration changes can bypass safeguards or alter the intended use of the system.

Prompt changes should not be treated as harmless editorial adjustments when they can materially affect outputs or tool execution.

### 285. Assessing Retrieval and Knowledge-Base Evaluation

Retrieval systems can introduce errors through incomplete indexing, irrelevant results, stale documents, access-control failures, or poor source quality.

The assessor should determine whether retrieval quality and security are evaluated.

Relevant measures may include retrieval relevance, source coverage, access enforcement, freshness, and correctness of source attribution.

Evidence may include retrieval test sets, indexing logs, permission tests, and evaluation reports.

The assessment should distinguish retrieval failures from generation failures because the appropriate corrective actions may differ.

### 286. Assessing Output Validation

Output validation examines whether AI-generated results meet relevant requirements before they are used or acted upon.

Validation may involve schema checks, factual verification, policy checks, reference validation, human review, or business-rule enforcement.

The assessor should determine whether validation controls address the actual consequences of incorrect outputs.

Evidence may include application logic, validation test cases, workflow configurations, and exception records.

The organization should evaluate whether validation can be bypassed and what happens when the output fails validation.

### 287. Assessing Content Safety Evaluation

Content safety evaluation examines whether the system produces outputs that violate relevant safety, policy, or use-case requirements.

The assessor should determine whether testing covers plausible harmful outputs and whether the evaluation criteria are appropriate to the application.

Evidence may include safety test suites, red-team reports, content classification results, and mitigation testing.

The assessment should consider false refusals as well as unsafe outputs where both affect the system's intended purpose.

The organization should document the limitations of its safety evaluations and the conditions under which additional review is required.

### 288. Assessing Refusal Behavior

Refusal behavior concerns whether the system declines requests that fall outside permitted or safe operating boundaries.

The assessor should determine whether refusal requirements are defined and tested against relevant misuse scenarios.

Evidence may include refusal test cases, policy configurations, red-team results, and review records.

The assessment should consider whether refusals can be bypassed through indirect instructions, unusual formatting, multi-step interactions, or tool use.

It should also examine whether legitimate requests are unnecessarily refused in ways that undermine the system's intended function.

### 289. Assessing AI Red-Team Evaluation

AI red teaming uses structured adversarial activities to identify weaknesses in AI systems and their surrounding applications.

The assessor should determine whether red-team objectives, scope, authorization, methods, and reporting requirements are documented.

Evidence may include test plans, authorization records, attack scenarios, findings, and remediation reports.

Red teaming may examine prompt injection, data disclosure, unsafe actions, model misuse, and other threats relevant to the system.

The assessment should evaluate whether findings are addressed and whether testing limitations are understood.

### 290. Assessing Red-Team Finding Validation

A red-team finding should be validated to determine whether the observed weakness is reproducible, material, and relevant to the system's risk profile.

The assessor should examine whether findings include sufficient detail to understand the scenario and its consequences.

Evidence may include reproduction steps, affected versions, supporting logs, impact analysis, and remediation testing.

The organization should distinguish confirmed vulnerabilities from speculative attack paths while still considering credible unresolved risks.

Validated findings should feed into risk treatment, regression testing, and subsequent evaluation activities.

### 291. Assessing Adversarial Test Boundaries

Adversarial testing must operate within authorized boundaries and appropriate safeguards.

The assessor should determine whether testing scope, permissions, data handling, and potential operational impacts are controlled.

Evidence may include test authorizations, rules of engagement, environment isolation, and test completion records.

Where testing could affect production systems or sensitive data, the organization should establish suitable restrictions and contingency arrangements.

Testing effectiveness should be balanced with operational safety, legal requirements, and protection of affected stakeholders.

### 292. Assessing Evaluation of Third-Party Models

Third-party models may have limited transparency, restricted access to internal parameters, or changing provider-managed behavior.

The assessor should determine whether available evaluation methods provide sufficient evidence for the intended use.

Possible methods include application-level testing, output evaluation, controlled benchmarking, supplier assurance review, and monitoring after deployment.

Evidence may include provider documentation, contractual commitments, internal test reports, and independent assessments.

Where important properties cannot be verified, the organization should document the resulting uncertainty and determine whether the system remains acceptable for the proposed use.

### 293. Assessing AI System-Level Testing

System-level testing evaluates the combined behavior of models, data sources, prompts, interfaces, tools, and operational workflows.

The assessor should determine whether testing reflects the deployed application rather than only the underlying model.

Evidence may include integration test reports, end-to-end scenarios, authorization tests, and production-like evaluations.

System-level testing is especially important when the application can retrieve sensitive data or initiate actions through external services.

The assessment should verify that component-level assurance is supplemented by testing of relevant interactions and dependencies.

### 294. Assessing End-to-End Evaluation

End-to-end evaluation examines whether the complete process produces acceptable outcomes from initial input through final use or action.

The assessor should determine whether the evaluation includes relevant upstream data, processing steps, model outputs, human review, and downstream systems.

Evidence may include workflow tests, transaction records, business acceptance testing, and scenario-based evaluation.

The assessment should consider failure propagation and whether safeguards operate at the appropriate points in the process.

A model that performs correctly in isolation may still contribute to a failed end-to-end process.

### 295. Assessing Operational Resilience Measurement

Operational resilience measurement evaluates the ability of the AI service and its supporting processes to withstand disruptions and recover appropriately.

The assessor should determine whether availability, recovery, fallback, and dependency risks are measured against defined requirements.

Evidence may include resilience test reports, recovery exercises, service metrics, and continuity records.

The assessment should consider provider outages, network failures, data pipeline disruptions, and failure of supporting components.

The organization should establish how resilience failures affect the continued use of AI in critical processes.

### 296. Assessing Measurement of Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after relevant controls and mitigation measures have been applied.

The assessor should determine whether evaluation results inform the assessment of residual risk and whether remaining uncertainty is documented.

Evidence may include updated risk assessments, control test results, mitigation validation, and formal acceptance records.

Residual risk should reflect the actual effectiveness of safeguards rather than the mere existence of control documentation.

Where residual risk exceeds organizational tolerance or applicable requirements, the organization should consider additional treatment or restriction of use.

### 297. Assessing Measurement-Based Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should be informed by relevant evaluation evidence and authorized according to organizational governance arrangements.

The assessor should determine whether decision-makers understand material failures, limitations, uncertainty, and residual exposure.

Evidence may include risk acceptance forms, assessment reports, approval records, and documented conditions.

The organization should avoid treating a passing benchmark or completed test plan as automatic authorization for deployment.

Risk acceptance should be explicit, traceable, and consistent with applicable requirements and organizational risk tolerance.

### 298. Assessing Measurement-Based Deployment Decisions

Deployment decisions should consider whether evaluation results support the intended use and whether material risks are adequately addressed.

The assessor should examine release criteria, unresolved findings, risk acceptance, and the authority of decision-makers.

Evidence may include release approvals, test summaries, risk assessments, and exception records.

Where critical evaluation failures remain unresolved, the organization should determine whether deployment should be delayed, restricted, or rejected.

A deployment decision should be traceable to the evidence available at the time it was made.

### 299. Assessing Measurement Continual Improvement

Measurement processes should improve as new evidence, incidents, technical methods, and operating conditions become available.

The assessor should determine whether lessons from evaluations and operational experience are incorporated into test plans, metrics, monitoring, and risk assessments.

Evidence may include revised testing standards, updated evaluation suites, post-incident reviews, and documented process changes.

The organization should assess whether previous measurement gaps have been addressed and whether the revised methods are more effective.

Continual improvement requires demonstrated changes to measurement practice, not simply a commitment to improve.

### 300. Core Principles of MEASURE Function Assessment

The MEASURE function provides the evidence needed to evaluate AI system behavior, trustworthiness, and risk. An effective assessment examines whether methods and metrics are appropriate, test conditions are relevant, results are reliable, limitations are documented, and monitoring mechanisms can identify material changes over time.

The assessor should evaluate the relationship between risk scenarios, evaluation activities, observed results, and subsequent decisions. Testing should cover relevant model behavior, application integrations, data dependencies, security and privacy risks, human-AI interactions, and operational conditions where applicable.

Measurement must also account for uncertainty. No benchmark, metric, red-team exercise, or external assurance report proves that an AI system is universally safe or trustworthy.

**The central principle of MEASURE assessment is that risk conclusions must be supported by relevant, traceable, and appropriately interpreted evidence—and that measurement results must influence ongoing AI risk management.**

## Part 4 — MANAGE Function Assessment, Risk Treatment, Governance Integration, and Assurance Conclusions

### 301. Introduction to the MANAGE Function

The MANAGE function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) establishes the processes through which organizations prioritize, respond to, and monitor AI risks. It translates contextual understanding and measurement results into decisions, controls, mitigation activities, and ongoing oversight.

An assessment of MANAGE determines whether identified risks lead to appropriate action, whether treatment decisions are authorized, and whether residual risks remain within acceptable boundaries.

The assessor should examine the complete risk management process, from prioritization and treatment selection to implementation, verification, and continual improvement.

The central question is not whether the organization has documented risk responses, but whether those responses are implemented, effective, and appropriate to the system's intended use and potential impacts.

### 302. Understanding MANAGE Function Categories

NIST AI RMF 1.0 organizes the MANAGE function into four categories:

- MANAGE 1: AI risks based on assessments and other analytical outputs are prioritized, responded to, and managed.
- MANAGE 2: Strategies to maximize AI benefits and minimize negative impacts are planned, prepared, implemented, documented, and informed by relevant AI risks.
- MANAGE 3: AI risks and benefits from third-party entities are managed.
- MANAGE 4: Mechanisms are established to supersede, disengage, or deactivate AI systems that demonstrate performance or outcomes inconsistent with intended use.

The assessor should identify the applicable subcategories and evaluate how the organization implements them.

The assessment should consider the relationship between technical safeguards, operational procedures, governance decisions, and the authority to restrict or discontinue an AI system.

### 303. Assessing Risk Prioritization

Risk prioritization determines which identified risks require the greatest attention and which should be addressed first.

The assessor should determine whether prioritization considers potential impact, likelihood or plausibility, exposure, uncertainty, existing controls, and organizational risk tolerance.

Evidence may include risk registers, scoring methodologies, treatment plans, and governance meeting records.

The assessment should determine whether high-impact risks receive appropriate attention even when their estimated likelihood is low or uncertain.

A prioritization process is ineffective when risk rankings are produced mechanically without influencing resource allocation, treatment decisions, or deployment conditions.

### 304. Assessing Risk Treatment Strategies

Risk treatment strategies define how the organization responds to identified risks.

Common approaches include avoiding the risk, reducing it through controls, transferring or sharing certain consequences where appropriate, and accepting residual risk through authorized decisions.

The assessor should determine whether treatment options are evaluated against the actual risk scenario and intended business outcome.

Evidence may include risk treatment plans, design decisions, control mappings, contracts, and acceptance records.

The selected response should be proportionate to the potential consequences and supported by documented reasoning.

### 305. Assessing Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance involves deciding not to undertake an AI activity or use case because its risks cannot be justified or adequately controlled.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has a process for rejecting proposed use cases or discontinuing uses that are no longer acceptable.

Evidence may include approval criteria, prohibited-use policies, rejected proposals, and documented governance decisions.

Avoidance may be appropriate where the system presents unacceptable risk, the intended use is prohibited, or necessary safeguards cannot be implemented.

The assessment should determine whether commercial pressure or sunk costs improperly override established risk acceptance requirements.

### 306. Assessing Risk Reduction

Risk reduction involves implementing safeguards intended to lower the likelihood or impact of an adverse outcome.

Safeguards may include access restrictions, output validation, human review, monitoring, rate limits, data minimization, or changes to the system's intended use.

The assessor should determine whether the selected controls address the relevant risk mechanisms.

Evidence may include control specifications, implementation records, test results, and residual risk assessments.

A control should not be considered effective solely because it has been implemented. Its operation and effectiveness must be supported by appropriate evidence.

### 307. Assessing Risk Transfer and Sharing

Organizations may allocate certain risks through contractual arrangements, insurance, service agreements, or other mechanisms.

The assessor should determine whether these arrangements genuinely address the relevant consequences and whether important responsibilities remain with the organization.

Evidence may include contracts, insurance terms, supplier agreements, and risk allocation records.

The assessment should distinguish financial risk transfer from the ability to prevent, detect, or mitigate harmful outcomes.

A contract may allocate liability without preventing an AI system from causing operational disruption, privacy harm, or security incidents.

### 308. Assessing Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is a formal decision to tolerate a defined level of residual risk.

The assessor should determine whether acceptance is authorized by the appropriate decision-maker and supported by sufficient evidence.

Acceptance records should identify the risk, rationale, residual exposure, applicable conditions, owner, and review requirements.

The assessment should determine whether the accepted risk is consistent with organizational risk tolerance and applicable obligations.

Informal continuation of an AI system despite known weaknesses should not be treated as equivalent to authorized risk acceptance.

### 309. Assessing Risk Acceptance Authority

Organizations should define who can accept AI risks and the limits of that authority.

The assessor should determine whether authority reflects the severity, scope, and consequences of the risk.

Evidence may include governance policies, delegated authority matrices, approval records, and escalation procedures.

Material risks may require approval by senior management or a designated risk committee rather than an individual system owner.

Risk acceptance must not override legal prohibitions or mandatory requirements that cannot lawfully be waived.

### 310. Assessing Residual Risk Review

Residual risk should be reassessed after controls have been implemented and their effectiveness evaluated.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has updated risk assessments using evidence from testing, operational monitoring, and control verification.

Evidence may include updated risk registers, validation reports, control test results, and acceptance records.

Where treatment does not reduce risk to the expected level, additional action may be necessary.

The assessment should distinguish the intended effect of a control from its demonstrated effect.

### 311. Assessing Risk Treatment Ownership

Every material risk treatment activity should have an accountable owner.

The assessor should determine whether responsibilities, deadlines, dependencies, and expected outcomes are documented.

Evidence may include action plans, project records, risk registers, and management reporting.

Ownership should be assigned to people with sufficient authority and resources to implement the required action.

A risk treatment plan with no accountable owner is unlikely to produce reliable closure.

### 312. Assessing Risk Treatment Deadlines

Deadlines should reflect the severity of the risk and the urgency of the required response.

The assessor should determine whether due dates are defined, monitored, and escalated when missed.

Evidence may include remediation trackers, overdue action reports, governance minutes, and exception approvals.

High-impact risks may require immediate containment or temporary restrictions while permanent remediation is completed.

Repeated deadline extensions should trigger scrutiny of the underlying cause, interim safeguards, and continuing acceptability of the residual risk.

### 313. Assessing Remediation Verification

Remediation verification establishes whether a treatment action has been implemented and whether it addresses the identified weakness.

The assessor should determine whether closure requires objective evidence rather than an owner declaration alone.

Evidence may include retest results, configuration reviews, change records, independent validation, and updated risk assessments.

The verification method should be appropriate to the type of remediation.

For example, correcting an access-control weakness may require testing actual permissions rather than reviewing the updated policy document.

### 314. Assessing Risk Treatment Effectiveness

Risk treatment effectiveness concerns whether the implemented response produces the intended reduction in risk.

The assessor should compare post-treatment evidence with the original risk scenario and acceptance criteria.

Evidence may include before-and-after testing, operational metrics, incident trends, and control effectiveness reviews.

The assessment should determine whether new risks were introduced by the treatment itself.

A treatment should be reconsidered if it fails to address the original risk, creates unacceptable side effects, or becomes ineffective as the system evolves.

### 315. Assessing Unresolved AI Risks

Unresolved risks may arise when mitigation is incomplete, evidence is insufficient, or a treatment is ineffective.

The assessor should determine whether unresolved risks are visible to appropriate decision-makers and subject to explicit controls.

Evidence may include risk registers, exception records, escalation reports, and deployment restrictions.

The organization should evaluate whether continued operation remains justified and whether temporary safeguards are sufficient.

An unresolved risk should not disappear from reporting merely because the original remediation deadline has passed.

### 316. Assessing AI Risk Escalation

Risk escalation ensures that material risks reach people with the authority to make appropriate decisions.

The assessor should determine whether escalation criteria are defined and whether the process operates in practice.

Triggers may include severe evaluation failures, unexpected harmful outcomes, material data exposure, repeated control failures, or risks exceeding accepted tolerance.

Evidence may include escalation procedures, incident records, meeting minutes, and decision logs.

The assessment should establish whether escalation leads to timely decisions and whether urgent restrictions can be imposed when necessary.

### 317. Assessing AI Risk Exceptions

An exception permits a defined deviation from an established requirement or control.

The assessor should determine whether exceptions are documented, justified, authorized, time-limited where appropriate, and subject to review.

Evidence may include exception registers, compensating control records, approvals, and expiration reports.

Exceptions should identify the affected system, requirement, risk, business rationale, and conditions for continued operation.

Repeated renewal of an exception without meaningful reassessment may indicate that a temporary deviation has become an uncontrolled permanent weakness.

### 318. Assessing Compensating Controls

Compensating controls reduce risk when the preferred control cannot be implemented or is not sufficient by itself.

The assessor should determine whether compensating measures address the same relevant risk and are supported by evidence.

For example, where automated validation is limited, additional human review and restricted deployment may reduce exposure.

Evidence may include control mappings, test results, risk assessments, and documented approvals.

Compensating controls should be reviewed for effectiveness and should not be accepted solely because they are convenient or already available.

### 319. Assessing AI Risk Appetite Alignment

Risk appetite expresses the level and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain in support of its objectives.

The assessor should determine whether AI risk decisions align with approved risk appetite and whether relevant limits are understood by system owners.

Evidence may include risk appetite statements, governance policies, risk acceptance records, and management reports.

The assessment should examine whether risk appetite has been translated into practical decision criteria.

Broad statements about innovation or acceptable risk are insufficient when they do not guide concrete decisions about AI deployment and operation.

### 320. Assessing AI Risk Tolerance Thresholds

Risk tolerance establishes more specific boundaries within which exposure may be considered acceptable.

The assessor should determine whether thresholds are defined for relevant performance, security, privacy, safety, and operational risks.

Evidence may include acceptance criteria, escalation thresholds, monitoring rules, and risk treatment plans.

Thresholds should reflect the use case and consequences of failure.

Where a threshold is exceeded, the organization should have a defined response rather than relying on discretionary action without accountability.

### 321. Assessing Risk Treatment Prioritization Across a Portfolio

Organizations may manage many AI systems competing for limited remediation resources.

The assessor should determine whether portfolio prioritization considers severity, exposure, dependencies, business criticality, and shared risks.

Evidence may include portfolio risk reports, remediation roadmaps, resource allocation decisions, and governance records.

The organization should identify systemic weaknesses affecting multiple systems and evaluate whether a common treatment would provide greater risk reduction.

Portfolio prioritization should not allow lower-cost remediation to displace more important risks without a documented rationale.

### 322. Assessing AI Risk Management Integration

AI risk management should integrate with relevant enterprise risk, information security, privacy, compliance, procurement, and operational processes.

The assessor should determine whether AI risks are incorporated into established governance arrangements where appropriate.

Evidence may include integrated risk registers, committee reports, shared control frameworks, and enterprise risk procedures.

Integration should preserve AI-specific considerations rather than forcing every risk into a conventional category that obscures its characteristics.

Effective integration enables consistent oversight while maintaining sufficient detail to understand AI-specific risks.

### 323. Assessing Integration With Enterprise Risk Management

Enterprise risk management provides a broader view of organizational objectives, uncertainties, and exposures.

The assessor should determine whether material AI risks are represented in the enterprise risk process at an appropriate level.

Evidence may include enterprise risk registers, risk committee materials, management reports, and documented escalation routes.

The assessment should examine whether AI risks can be aggregated and compared with other enterprise risks without losing important technical or contextual information.

Significant AI dependencies should be visible to senior management when they affect strategic objectives or organizational resilience.

### 324. Assessing Integration With Information Security Risk Management

AI security risks should connect with the organization's information security risk management processes.

The assessor should determine whether AI assets, threats, vulnerabilities, and controls are considered within relevant security procedures.

Evidence may include threat models, security risk registers, vulnerability reports, and security governance records.

The assessment should consider AI-specific risks such as prompt injection, model extraction, training data poisoning, and unauthorized tool use where relevant.

Conventional security controls remain important, but they may need supplementation to address risks arising from AI behavior and application design.

### 325. Assessing Integration With Privacy Risk Management

AI privacy risks should be addressed through relevant privacy governance, assessment, and incident management processes.

The assessor should determine whether personal data processing, retention, access, disclosure, and individual impacts are considered throughout the lifecycle.

Evidence may include privacy impact assessments, processing records, data-flow documentation, and privacy risk registers.

The assessment should establish whether AI-related processing changes trigger privacy review.

Privacy risks should be considered even where the AI system does not directly make decisions about individuals, if its data processing or outputs create relevant exposure.

### 326. Assessing Integration With Legal and Regulatory Compliance

The organization should identify legal and regulatory requirements applicable to the AI system and its intended use.

The assessor should determine whether compliance obligations are translated into relevant controls, evidence requirements, and accountable responsibilities.

Evidence may include legal assessments, compliance mappings, regulatory registers, and documented approvals.

The assessment should distinguish mandatory requirements from voluntary frameworks and internal policies.

A completed NIST AI RMF assessment does not by itself establish compliance with every applicable law or regulation.

### 327. Assessing Integration With Business Continuity

AI system failures may affect critical business services and processes.

The assessor should determine whether continuity planning accounts for relevant AI dependencies and failure scenarios.

Evidence may include business impact assessments, continuity plans, recovery procedures, and exercise results.

The organization should consider manual fallback, alternative services, recovery priorities, and dependencies on external providers.

Continuity arrangements should be tested where appropriate rather than treated as adequate solely because a plan exists.

### 328. Assessing Integration With Incident Management

AI incidents should be handled through defined processes that support detection, classification, containment, investigation, remediation, and learning.

The assessor should determine whether existing incident procedures address AI-specific failure modes and impacts.

Evidence may include incident response plans, playbooks, incident records, and post-incident reviews.

The assessment should examine whether technical, business, privacy, security, and legal functions coordinate effectively when required.

AI incidents should be classified according to their actual consequences and applicable reporting requirements rather than their novelty alone.

### 329. Assessing AI Incident Severity

Incident severity should reflect the actual and potential consequences of the event.

Relevant factors may include the sensitivity of affected data, number of impacted users, duration, business disruption, safety implications, and possibility of further harm.

The assessor should determine whether classification criteria are documented and applied consistently.

Evidence may include severity matrices, incident reports, escalation records, and review decisions.

The organization should consider whether an event that appears minor technically could have substantial consequences in its business or human context.

### 330. Assessing AI Incident Containment

Containment seeks to limit the consequences of an AI incident while investigation and remediation proceed.

Possible actions include disabling a feature, restricting access, revoking tool permissions, isolating a component, blocking a data source, or suspending a deployment.

The assessor should determine whether appropriate containment options are documented and operationally feasible.

Evidence may include incident playbooks, access configurations, exercise results, and actual incident records.

The organization should establish who can authorize urgent containment and whether action can be taken quickly enough to limit harm.

### 331. Assessing AI Incident Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis seeks to understand why an incident occurred and which technical or organizational conditions contributed to it.

The assessor should determine whether investigations examine more than the immediate model output or triggering input.

Relevant causes may include poor data quality, excessive permissions, weak validation, unclear ownership, inadequate monitoring, or flawed assumptions about system behavior.

Evidence may include investigation reports, causal analysis, remediation plans, and lessons-learned records.

The assessment should determine whether findings lead to corrective action and updates to relevant risk assessments or controls.

### 332. Assessing AI Incident Learning

Incident learning converts operational experience into improvements in governance, design, testing, and monitoring.

The assessor should determine whether lessons are captured and incorporated into relevant processes.

Evidence may include post-incident reviews, revised test cases, updated policies, training changes, and control improvements.

The organization should examine whether similar incidents recur and whether previous corrective actions were effective.

An incident process is incomplete when reports are produced but the underlying weaknesses remain unaddressed.

### 333. Assessing AI Risk Communication

Material AI risks should be communicated to people who need the information to make decisions or perform their responsibilities.

The assessor should determine whether reporting is timely, accurate, proportionate, and directed to appropriate audiences.

Evidence may include risk reports, management dashboards, escalation notices, and governance meeting records.

The assessment should examine whether uncertainty and limitations are communicated alongside risk ratings.

Communication should enable action and accountability rather than merely distribute technical information without explaining its significance.

### 334. Assessing Risk Reporting to Senior Management

Senior management requires a clear view of material AI risks, control effectiveness, unresolved issues, and decisions requiring executive attention.

The assessor should determine whether reports provide sufficient information for informed oversight.

Evidence may include executive dashboards, risk committee materials, approval records, and management decisions.

Reporting should identify significant exposure, risk trends, overdue remediation, and areas where evidence is insufficient.

The assessment should determine whether management decisions influence actual risk treatment and resource allocation.

### 335. Assessing Risk Reporting to the Board

Where AI risks are material to organizational strategy, legal obligations, or enterprise resilience, board-level oversight may be appropriate.

The assessor should determine whether relevant information reaches the board or its designated committee through established governance channels.

Evidence may include board papers, committee minutes, risk summaries, and documented oversight responsibilities.

Reporting should focus on material exposure, strategic implications, significant incidents, and decisions requiring board attention.

The level of reporting should reflect governance arrangements and the organization's circumstances rather than assume that every AI system requires direct board review.

### 336. Assessing Risk Reporting to Operational Teams

Operational teams need actionable information about risks, control requirements, and conditions requiring escalation.

The assessor should determine whether relevant risk decisions are communicated to those responsible for operating and maintaining the system.

Evidence may include operating procedures, team briefings, dashboards, training records, and change notices.

The assessment should examine whether operational personnel understand restrictions and can identify conditions requiring intervention.

Risk decisions have limited practical value if they remain confined to governance documents and are not reflected in operational practice.

### 337. Assessing AI Risk Ownership Across the Lifecycle

AI risk ownership should remain clear as the system moves through design, development, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement.

The assessor should determine whether responsibilities are assigned for identifying, treating, monitoring, and escalating risks at each stage.

Evidence may include lifecycle procedures, role matrices, approval records, and handover documentation.

The assessment should consider whether responsibility gaps arise when systems move between teams or when suppliers change.

Risk ownership should follow actual decision authority and operational responsibility rather than rely on ambiguous collective accountability.

### 338. Assessing Risk Handover Between Teams

AI systems often move between development, security, operations, business, and supplier management teams.

The assessor should determine whether risk information and unresolved issues are transferred during handovers.

Evidence may include deployment checklists, handover records, known-issue registers, and acceptance documentation.

The handover should communicate material limitations, monitoring requirements, rollback conditions, and outstanding remediation.

A technically successful deployment may still create governance failure if operational teams inherit a system without understanding its residual risks.

### 339. Assessing Risk Management for Model Changes

Model changes can affect accuracy, behavior, security, fairness, privacy, and operational requirements.

The assessor should determine whether changes trigger risk review and appropriate testing before release.

Evidence may include change requests, model version records, regression reports, approval decisions, and updated risk assessments.

The required review should reflect the significance of the change and its possible effects.

Where the provider controls updates, the organization should consider mechanisms to detect material changes and respond when the new version no longer meets its requirements.

### 340. Assessing Risk Management for Use-Case Expansion

Expanding an AI system to a new purpose, user population, geographic area, or decision context can introduce new risks.

The assessor should determine whether expansion requires reassessment and approval.

Evidence may include change records, updated impact assessments, revised requirements, and governance decisions.

The assessment should consider whether existing testing remains relevant to the expanded use.

Approval for one use case should not automatically be interpreted as approval for materially different uses.

### 341. Assessing Risk Management for Supplier Changes

Supplier changes may affect model behavior, service availability, data processing, security controls, or contractual obligations.

The assessor should determine whether supplier changes trigger review of relevant risks and dependencies.

Evidence may include supplier notifications, change assessments, contract reviews, updated testing, and approval records.

The organization should determine whether the changed service remains suitable for its intended use.

Material supplier changes may require additional evaluation, contingency planning, or reconsideration of the relationship.

### 342. Assessing AI Risk Treatment in Procurement

Procurement can introduce or reduce AI risk before a system enters the organization.

The assessor should determine whether procurement procedures evaluate AI-specific risks, supplier capabilities, contractual terms, data handling, and available assurance.

Evidence may include due diligence records, security questionnaires, evaluation reports, contracts, and approval workflows.

The assessment should establish whether high-risk use cases receive appropriate scrutiny before purchase or integration.

Procurement approval should not replace ongoing monitoring when supplier services or AI capabilities may change.

### 343. Assessing Third-Party Risk Management

MANAGE includes consideration of risks and benefits associated with third-party entities.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates providers, subcontractors, model suppliers, data suppliers, and other material dependencies.

Evidence may include supplier risk assessments, assurance reports, contractual commitments, monitoring records, and contingency plans.

The assessment should consider the provider's importance, the sensitivity of information processed, and the consequences of service failure.

Third-party assurance should be evaluated for scope and relevance rather than accepted solely because a supplier holds a certification or provides a general security statement.

### 344. Assessing Supplier Assurance Limitations

Supplier assurance may cover only selected systems, locations, periods, or control objectives.

The assessor should determine whether the organization understands the limitations of available assurance.

Evidence may include independent reports, scope statements, exceptions, complementary user controls, and contractual documentation.

Where supplier assurance does not cover a material risk, the organization should consider additional verification or compensating measures.

A supplier's general assurance statement should not be treated as evidence that every aspect of the organization's AI application is adequately controlled.

### 345. Assessing Third-Party Incident Coordination

AI incidents may involve multiple organizations, including cloud providers, model developers, application vendors, and customers.

The assessor should determine whether responsibilities for notification, investigation, containment, and recovery are defined.

Evidence may include contracts, incident response procedures, contact lists, and exercise records.

The assessment should consider whether the organization can obtain sufficient information from suppliers to understand the impact and meet applicable obligations.

Unclear incident coordination can delay response and increase the consequences of a material AI failure.

### 346. Assessing Third-Party Exit Planning

Organizations should consider how they would discontinue a third-party AI service when required.

The assessor should determine whether exit planning addresses data return or deletion, service migration, continuity, access revocation, and replacement options.

Evidence may include exit plans, contracts, data deletion provisions, and continuity exercises.

The assessment should consider whether proprietary formats, technical dependencies, or limited alternatives create significant switching barriers.

Exit planning is particularly important where a provider supports critical processes or handles sensitive organizational information.

### 347. Assessing Benefit Maximization

MANAGE includes strategies intended to maximize the benefits of AI while minimizing negative impacts.

The assessor should determine whether the organization evaluates whether the system achieves its intended objectives and whether benefits justify ongoing risk and cost.

Evidence may include business performance reports, outcome evaluations, stakeholder feedback, and review decisions.

The assessment should distinguish measurable benefits from assumptions about efficiency, productivity, or improved decision-making.

Where expected benefits do not materialize, the organization should reconsider the system's scope, design, or continued use.

### 348. Assessing Harm Minimization Strategies

Harm minimization seeks to prevent or reduce adverse outcomes associated with the AI system.

The assessor should determine whether mitigation measures address relevant impacts and whether their effectiveness is evaluated.

Measures may include restricted functionality, human review, access controls, data safeguards, output validation, and operational monitoring.

Evidence may include treatment plans, test results, impact assessments, and incident trends.

The assessment should consider whether mitigation measures introduce new burdens, inequities, or operational weaknesses.

### 349. Assessing AI Deployment Readiness

Deployment readiness establishes whether the system is sufficiently evaluated, controlled, documented, and approved for its intended use.

The assessor should examine whether required assessments have been completed and material findings have been resolved or formally accepted.

Evidence may include release checklists, validation reports, security approvals, risk acceptance records, and operational readiness reviews.

The organization should establish explicit conditions that prevent deployment when critical requirements are unmet.

Readiness should be based on evidence and authorized decisions, not solely on delivery schedules or business expectations.

### 350. Assessing Conditional Deployment

Conditional deployment permits use only within defined limitations or under specified safeguards.

Conditions may include restricted users, limited data types, mandatory review, transaction caps, enhanced monitoring, or exclusion of high-impact decisions.

The assessor should determine whether conditions are documented, communicated, and enforceable.

Evidence may include deployment approvals, configuration settings, operating procedures, and monitoring records.

The assessment should establish how violations are detected and what happens when the conditions can no longer be maintained.

### 351. Assessing Controlled Rollout

A controlled rollout introduces an AI system gradually to limit exposure and gather evidence before wider deployment.

The assessor should determine whether rollout stages, evaluation criteria, monitoring requirements, and expansion approvals are defined.

Evidence may include rollout plans, pilot evaluations, user feedback, and stage-gate decisions.

The organization should establish conditions for pausing or reversing expansion when results are unsatisfactory.

A limited pilot does not automatically establish suitability for larger populations, additional business processes, or materially different operating conditions.

### 352. Assessing Pilot Evaluation

Pilot evaluations examine whether the system performs acceptably in a limited operational context.

The assessor should determine whether pilot objectives, scope, participants, safeguards, and exit criteria are documented.

Evidence may include pilot plans, evaluation results, incident records, and management decisions.

The assessment should consider whether the pilot conditions are sufficiently representative to inform subsequent deployment.

A successful pilot provides evidence about the tested conditions but should not be treated as proof that every risk has been resolved.

### 353. Assessing Post-Deployment Risk Treatment

Risk treatment continues after deployment because operational evidence may reveal weaknesses that were not apparent during testing.

The assessor should determine whether the organization reviews material findings, changes, incidents, and monitoring signals.

Evidence may include operational risk reviews, remediation records, monitoring reports, and updated acceptance decisions.

The organization should establish how new information affects the continued suitability of the system.

Post-deployment risk treatment should be integrated with normal operational governance rather than performed only after serious incidents.

### 354. Assessing Ongoing Risk Review Frequency

Risk review frequency should reflect system criticality, rate of change, uncertainty, exposure, and potential consequences.

The assessor should determine whether review intervals and event-driven triggers are defined.

Evidence may include review schedules, governance procedures, risk assessment records, and change histories.

High-risk or rapidly changing systems may require more frequent review than stable, low-impact applications.

A fixed annual review may be insufficient where material changes or incidents occur between scheduled reviews.

### 355. Assessing AI Risk Review Triggers

Trigger-based reviews occur when specific events indicate that the risk assessment may no longer be valid.

Triggers may include significant model updates, new data sources, security incidents, performance degradation, changes in intended use, or new regulatory requirements.

The assessor should determine whether triggers are documented and operationalized.

Evidence may include change workflows, incident procedures, monitoring alerts, and reassessment records.

The assessment should verify that triggers result in appropriate action rather than merely generating notifications that remain unresolved.

### 356. Assessing Risk Treatment During Emergencies

Emergency conditions may require immediate decisions to contain AI-related harm.

The assessor should determine whether emergency authority, decision criteria, communication arrangements, and recovery procedures are established.

Evidence may include emergency playbooks, incident exercises, escalation matrices, and actual response records.

The organization should be able to restrict or disable affected functionality when necessary without relying on an impractically slow approval process.

Emergency actions should be recorded and reviewed after the immediate situation is stabilized.

### 357. Assessing AI System Restriction

Restrictions may limit an AI system's functionality, user population, data access, or operating environment.

The assessor should determine whether restriction mechanisms are available and whether they can be implemented effectively.

Evidence may include configuration controls, access policies, deployment settings, and test results.

The organization should establish when restrictions are appropriate and who can authorize them.

Restrictions can provide an intermediate response when immediate full retirement is unnecessary but continued unrestricted use is unacceptable.

### 358. Assessing AI System Suspension

Suspension temporarily stops some or all AI system operations.

The assessor should determine whether suspension criteria, authority, technical procedures, and continuity arrangements are defined.

Evidence may include incident playbooks, service controls, suspension tests, and recovery plans.

The assessment should consider whether suspension can be implemented quickly enough to limit harm and whether dependent processes can continue safely.

The organization should define the conditions under which the system may resume operation and what evidence is required before reactivation.

### 359. Assessing AI System Deactivation

Deactivation disables the AI system or a relevant capability when continued operation is no longer acceptable.

The assessor should determine whether deactivation procedures are documented, tested where appropriate, and assigned to authorized personnel.

Evidence may include shutdown procedures, access revocation records, deployment controls, and continuity plans.

For connected or agentic systems, deactivation may require stopping active tasks, revoking credentials, and preventing further tool execution.

The assessment should establish whether deactivation limits further harm without leaving critical dependencies in an uncontrolled state.

### 360. Assessing AI System Supersession

Supersession replaces an AI system or version with another system or an alternative process.

The assessor should determine whether the replacement decision considers comparative risks, migration requirements, continuity, and data handling.

Evidence may include migration plans, comparative evaluations, change approvals, and decommissioning records.

The organization should verify that the replacement meets the relevant requirements and that the transition does not create unacceptable gaps.

A newer system should not be presumed safer or more effective without suitable evidence.

### 361. Assessing AI System Retirement Decisions

Retirement decisions may be necessary when a system no longer meets its intended purpose, risks become unacceptable, or alternatives provide a better outcome.

The assessor should determine whether retirement criteria are defined and whether decisions are documented.

Evidence may include lifecycle reviews, risk assessments, business cases, and governance approvals.

The organization should consider data disposition, access revocation, supplier termination, and dependencies on downstream processes.

Retirement should be an explicit risk management decision rather than an unplanned response to operational failure.

### 362. Assessing AI System Decommissioning

Decommissioning implements the approved retirement decision.

The assessor should determine whether procedures address infrastructure removal, access revocation, data handling, record retention, and supplier arrangements.

Evidence may include decommissioning checklists, deletion records, asset inventories, and closure approvals.

The assessment should verify that obsolete endpoints, credentials, integrations, and scheduled tasks do not remain active.

Where evidence must be retained for audit or legal purposes, the organization should preserve it according to applicable requirements.

### 363. Assessing AI Rollback Capability

Rollback restores a previously approved version or configuration when a change causes unacceptable behavior.

The assessor should determine whether rollback mechanisms are available, tested, and appropriate to the system's architecture.

Evidence may include deployment procedures, version records, rollback exercises, and recovery test results.

The assessment should consider whether rollback is possible when data schemas, downstream integrations, or external provider behavior have also changed.

Rollback plans should identify who can initiate the action and how the organization confirms that the previous state is safe to restore.

### 364. Assessing AI Kill-Switch Controls

A kill switch is a mechanism intended to stop a system or capability quickly when specified conditions occur.

The assessor should determine whether the mechanism is appropriate to the system's risk and whether it has been tested.

Evidence may include design documentation, access controls, test records, and emergency procedures.

For autonomous systems, the assessment should consider whether the mechanism can stop new actions and address actions already in progress.

A kill switch should be evaluated for practical effectiveness rather than treated as adequate merely because a shutdown command exists.

### 365. Assessing AI Agent Action Limits

Agent action limits constrain what an AI agent can do without additional authorization.

Limits may include restricted tools, read-only access, transaction ceilings, approved destinations, and mandatory human approval for consequential actions.

The assessor should determine whether limits are enforced outside the model's own generated instructions where technically appropriate.

Evidence may include permission configurations, policy enforcement mechanisms, tool-call logs, and adversarial test results.

The assessment should examine whether an agent can bypass limits through alternate interfaces, delegated tools, or chained actions.

### 366. Assessing Emergency Revocation of AI Permissions

Emergency revocation removes permissions that could enable further harm during an incident.

The assessor should determine whether credentials, tokens, service accounts, and tool permissions can be revoked promptly.

Evidence may include identity configurations, incident procedures, revocation tests, and access logs.

The assessment should consider whether revocation affects other critical services and whether emergency changes are recorded.

For agentic systems, permission revocation may be a critical containment measure when behavior becomes unsafe or unauthorized.

### 367. Assessing Human Authority to Intervene

Human intervention requires more than the theoretical existence of an override mechanism.

The assessor should determine whether designated personnel have the authority, knowledge, information, and practical ability to intervene.

Evidence may include role definitions, training records, workflow demonstrations, and incident exercises.

The assessment should consider workload, response time, organizational pressure, and whether the interface makes intervention feasible.

An oversight arrangement is weak if people are expected to intervene but lack the information or authority needed to do so.

### 368. Assessing Reinstatement After Suspension

Reinstatement should occur only when the organization has sufficient evidence that the reasons for suspension have been addressed or that residual risks are otherwise acceptable.

The assessor should determine whether reinstatement criteria and approval authority are documented.

Evidence may include remediation results, regression testing, updated risk assessments, and reactivation approvals.

The assessment should examine whether the organization has evaluated related failure modes and whether monitoring has been strengthened where necessary.

Reactivation should not occur solely because an incident has stopped or a technical fix has been applied.

### 369. Assessing Risk Treatment Communication

Risk treatment decisions may affect users, operators, suppliers, and other stakeholders.

The assessor should determine whether relevant restrictions, changes, and remediation requirements are communicated to those responsible for implementation and oversight.

Evidence may include change notices, operating instructions, training records, supplier communications, and governance decisions.

The assessment should establish whether affected stakeholders understand the practical implications of the treatment.

Uncommunicated risk treatment decisions may fail because operational behavior remains unchanged.

### 370. Assessing Risk Treatment Recordkeeping

Risk treatment records should establish what was decided, why it was decided, who approved it, what actions were taken, and whether the actions were effective.

The assessor should determine whether records are complete, traceable, and retained appropriately.

Evidence may include risk registers, remediation tickets, approvals, test results, and closure reports.

Records should distinguish proposed actions from completed actions and verified outcomes.

Reliable recordkeeping supports accountability, auditability, and future reassessment.

### 371. Assessing AI Risk Management Metrics

Management metrics should provide meaningful information about how effectively AI risks are being treated.

Examples include the number of high-risk findings, remediation aging, recurring incidents, risk acceptance exceptions, and the proportion of material risks with verified treatment.

The assessor should determine whether metrics have clear definitions and whether they support management action.

Evidence may include dashboards, metric specifications, management reports, and review minutes.

Metrics should not reward superficial closure or discourage the reporting of genuine issues.

### 372. Assessing Risk Treatment Backlogs

A risk treatment backlog contains unresolved actions that may compete for resources and attention.

The assessor should determine whether the backlog is prioritized, reviewed, and escalated according to risk.

Evidence may include remediation trackers, overdue reports, resource plans, and governance decisions.

The assessment should identify whether high-impact items are delayed because of resource constraints or competing business priorities.

Where remediation is delayed, the organization should document interim safeguards and reassess whether continued operation remains acceptable.

### 373. Assessing Recurring AI Findings

Recurring findings may indicate ineffective remediation, unclear accountability, systemic weaknesses, or inadequate risk prioritization.

The assessor should determine whether repeated findings are identified and analyzed across systems and business units.

Evidence may include audit reports, issue trend analysis, root-cause reviews, and enterprise remediation plans.

The organization should consider whether a common control improvement can address multiple instances of the same weakness.

Repeatedly closing similar findings without resolving their common cause is evidence of weak risk management effectiveness.

### 374. Assessing Corrective and Preventive Actions

Corrective actions address identified failures, while preventive actions reduce the likelihood of similar problems occurring elsewhere or in the future.

The assessor should determine whether actions address root causes and include appropriate verification.

Evidence may include corrective action plans, root-cause analyses, control changes, and validation records.

The assessment should examine whether lessons from one AI system are applied to other systems with similar architectures or risks.

Actions should be proportionate to the significance of the finding and should have measurable completion criteria.

### 375. Assessing Risk Treatment Dependencies

Some risk treatments depend on other controls, suppliers, projects, or operational changes.

The assessor should determine whether dependencies are documented and whether delays or failures affect the effectiveness of the overall treatment.

Evidence may include implementation plans, dependency maps, project schedules, and risk reviews.

For example, monitoring may depend on logging that has not yet been implemented, making the monitoring treatment incomplete.

The organization should avoid treating a dependent control as effective before the necessary supporting components are operational.

### 376. Assessing Resource Allocation for AI Risk Treatment

Risk treatment requires appropriate staffing, expertise, technology, and budget.

The assessor should determine whether material actions have sufficient resources and whether resource constraints are escalated.

Evidence may include project budgets, staffing plans, remediation schedules, and management decisions.

The assessment should consider whether critical safeguards are delayed because delivery objectives receive priority over risk reduction.

Where resources are insufficient, management should decide whether to provide additional resources, reduce scope, restrict use, or accept residual risk through the appropriate process.

### 377. Assessing AI Risk Management Competence

People responsible for AI risk treatment should understand the risks and controls relevant to their responsibilities.

The assessor should determine whether competence requirements are defined and whether personnel receive appropriate training or support.

Evidence may include training records, role profiles, competency assessments, and technical review records.

Specialized risks may require input from security engineers, privacy specialists, model evaluators, legal experts, or domain professionals.

The assessment should determine whether the organization has the expertise needed to implement and verify the selected treatments.

### 378. Assessing Risk Treatment Culture

Organizational culture influences whether teams disclose weaknesses, challenge unsafe decisions, and prioritize remediation.

The assessor should determine whether governance arrangements encourage responsible escalation and evidence-based decision-making.

Evidence may include staff feedback, escalation records, incident trends, training materials, and management communications.

The assessment should consider whether personnel face incentives to suppress findings or bypass controls to meet delivery targets.

A strong risk management culture supports candid reporting and treats significant findings as inputs to improvement rather than merely obstacles to deployment.

### 379. Assessing Risk Management Across Business Units

Large organizations may operate AI systems under different business units, each with distinct objectives and controls.

The assessor should determine whether common risk management expectations are applied consistently while allowing for relevant contextual differences.

Evidence may include enterprise standards, business unit risk registers, oversight reports, and control assessments.

The organization should identify gaps caused by decentralized procurement, local development, or inconsistent approval practices.

Enterprise oversight should provide sufficient visibility to identify material exposure across the AI portfolio.

### 380. Assessing Shadow AI Risk Treatment

Shadow AI includes AI use that occurs outside approved governance or procurement processes.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has mechanisms to identify unauthorized AI use and address the resulting risks.

Evidence may include discovery processes, access logs, procurement reviews, employee guidance, and remediation records.

Responses may include user education, approved alternatives, access restrictions, or formal onboarding of legitimate use cases.

The assessment should consider why unauthorized use occurs and whether approved tools meet the practical needs of users.

### 381. Assessing AI Risk Treatment for Embedded Capabilities

AI capabilities may be embedded in enterprise applications, productivity tools, security products, and other third-party services.

The assessor should determine whether the organization identifies and evaluates material embedded AI functionality.

Evidence may include supplier inventories, product reviews, contract records, feature assessments, and usage policies.

The organization should consider whether data is processed by an AI feature even when the feature is not procured as a standalone AI product.

Embedded capabilities should be assessed according to their actual function, data access, and potential impact.

### 382. Assessing AI Risk Treatment for Unapproved Models

Unapproved models may introduce risks involving data disclosure, unreliable outputs, unauthorized processing, and unmanaged dependencies.

The assessor should determine whether the organization can identify relevant use and apply appropriate restrictions or onboarding procedures.

Evidence may include approved-tool lists, endpoint controls, procurement records, and access reviews.

The organization should distinguish between prohibited use and use that may be made acceptable through assessment and controls.

Where an unapproved model processes sensitive information, the organization should evaluate the need for containment and incident review.

### 383. Assessing AI Risk Treatment for Sensitive Data

AI systems that process sensitive data may require additional safeguards and restrictions.

The assessor should determine whether data classifications are translated into appropriate rules for model access, processing, retention, and disclosure.

Evidence may include data handling policies, technical configurations, access tests, and risk assessments.

The assessment should consider whether users can submit sensitive information to external services without appropriate authorization.

Controls should address the actual data flows and system capabilities rather than rely solely on user awareness.

### 384. Assessing AI Risk Treatment for High-Impact Decisions

AI systems that materially influence consequential decisions may require stronger evaluation, oversight, and remediation arrangements.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified relevant consequences and established appropriate safeguards.

Evidence may include impact assessments, decision workflows, validation reports, appeal mechanisms, and approval records.

The assessment should consider the ability to challenge or correct adverse outcomes where appropriate.

The organization should not rely solely on aggregate model performance when individual errors can have significant consequences.

### 385. Assessing Risk Treatment for Low-Confidence Outputs

Where an AI system provides confidence or uncertainty indicators, the organization should determine how those signals influence subsequent action.

The assessor should evaluate whether low-confidence or otherwise uncertain outputs trigger suitable review, abstention, or escalation.

Evidence may include threshold configurations, workflow rules, calibration studies, and test results.

If the model does not provide reliable confidence information, alternative safeguards may be needed.

The objective is to prevent unsupported outputs from being treated as reliable merely because the system produced an answer.

### 386. Assessing Risk Treatment for Unsupported Outputs

Unsupported outputs may include fabricated facts, invalid citations, unverified claims, or recommendations outside the system's intended capabilities.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has identified these risks and implemented appropriate validation or review.

Evidence may include output verification tests, source validation, workflow controls, and incident records.

The assessment should consider whether the consequences differ across use cases.

For high-impact uses, the organization may require authoritative source verification or qualified human review before an output is acted upon.

### 387. Assessing Risk Treatment for AI-Generated Code

AI-generated code may introduce vulnerabilities, licensing concerns, functional defects, or insecure design patterns.

The assessor should determine whether generated code is subject to appropriate review and testing before use.

Evidence may include secure development procedures, code review records, static analysis, dependency scanning, and test results.

The assessment should consider whether generated code receives the same or stronger scrutiny as code produced through other development methods.

AI assistance should not bypass established secure development lifecycle requirements.

### 388. Assessing Risk Treatment for AI-Generated Content

AI-generated content may create risks involving factual accuracy, confidentiality, intellectual property, inappropriate disclosures, or misleading communication.

The assessor should determine whether relevant content types and uses have defined review requirements.

Evidence may include publication workflows, content policies, verification procedures, and approval records.

The assessment should consider whether content is distributed internally, externally, or to audiences who may rely on it for important decisions.

Controls should reflect the potential consequences of the content rather than assume all generated text presents the same level of risk.

### 389. Assessing Risk Treatment for Automated Decisions

Automated decisions may require additional safeguards when incorrect outcomes can materially affect individuals or organizational operations.

The assessor should determine whether decision boundaries, approval requirements, error handling, and escalation procedures are defined.

Evidence may include workflow rules, decision logs, validation reports, and control configurations.

The assessment should examine whether automation can continue when required data or validation checks are unavailable.

The organization should ensure that the degree of automation is appropriate to the system's reliability and the consequences of error.

### 390. Assessing Risk Treatment for Autonomous Actions

Autonomous actions may create consequences more rapidly than systems that only provide recommendations.

The assessor should determine whether the organization has established permission boundaries, action limits, monitoring, and interruption mechanisms.

Evidence may include tool permissions, execution logs, approval workflows, test results, and emergency procedures.

The assessment should consider chained actions, delegated tools, and the possibility that individually permitted steps may produce an unintended outcome.

Autonomy should be constrained according to the system's demonstrated capabilities and the consequences of unauthorized or incorrect action.

### 391. Assessing Risk Treatment for Model Retirement and Replacement

Replacing a model may change behavior, dependencies, performance, and the risk profile of the surrounding application.

The assessor should determine whether replacement is treated as a controlled lifecycle change.

Evidence may include comparative evaluations, migration plans, updated documentation, approval records, and rollback arrangements.

The organization should verify that the replacement remains suitable for the intended use and that existing controls continue to operate effectively.

Retirement and replacement should preserve required records and address residual dependencies on the former model.

### 392. Assessing the Effectiveness of MANAGE Governance

Effective MANAGE governance ensures that risk decisions lead to accountable action and that management receives reliable information about remaining exposure.

The assessor should determine whether risk treatment, acceptance, escalation, suspension, and retirement processes operate as intended.

Evidence may include risk committee records, remediation status reports, decision logs, and incident outcomes.

The assessment should examine whether governance arrangements can respond to new evidence and changing risk conditions.

The presence of committees and policies is insufficient if material risks remain unmanaged or decision-makers lack the authority to act.

### 393. Assessing MANAGE Evidence Quality

Evidence supporting MANAGE should demonstrate that risk responses were implemented, reviewed, and evaluated.

Examples include technical test results, configuration records, monitoring data, formal approvals, incident records, and verified remediation outcomes.

The assessor should determine whether evidence is current, traceable, reliable, and relevant to the assessed system.

Management statements should be corroborated where the significance of the risk warrants independent verification.

Evidence should establish both what action occurred and whether the action achieved its intended result.

### 394. Assessing MANAGE Findings and Classification

MANAGE findings may involve ineffective treatment, unauthorized risk acceptance, inadequate escalation, missing safeguards, or failure to restrict an unsafe system.

The assessor should describe the observed condition, applicable criteria, supporting evidence, and potential consequences.

Severity should reflect the significance of the weakness and its relationship to residual risk.

Evidence may support a finding that a process exists but does not operate effectively, or that a control operates but does not adequately address the risk.

The report should distinguish isolated deficiencies from systemic weaknesses affecting multiple systems or governance processes.

### 395. Assessing MANAGE Corrective Action Plans

Corrective action plans should define the steps needed to address identified weaknesses.

The assessor should determine whether each plan includes an accountable owner, target date, expected outcome, dependencies, and verification method.

Evidence may include remediation plans, tracking records, governance approvals, and retest results.

Actions should address root causes where practical and include interim safeguards for material unresolved risks.

A corrective action should not be closed solely because a task has been marked complete; the relevant weakness must be addressed and verified.

### 396. Assessing MANAGE Continual Improvement

MANAGE should evolve as the organization gains experience with AI deployment, monitoring, incidents, and evaluation methods.

The assessor should determine whether feedback from operational experience leads to changes in controls, governance, resources, and risk treatment.

Evidence may include updated procedures, lessons-learned reports, revised risk assessments, and improved monitoring.

The assessment should examine whether previous weaknesses recur and whether management verifies the effectiveness of improvement actions.

Continual improvement requires evidence that the organization adapts its practices in response to what it learns.

### 397. Integrating GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE

The NIST AI RMF functions should be assessed as an integrated risk management approach.

GOVERN establishes policies, accountability, and oversight. MAP identifies context, stakeholders, capabilities, limitations, and potential impacts. MEASURE provides evidence about system behavior and risk. MANAGE prioritizes and treats risks based on the available information.

The assessor should determine whether information flows between these functions and whether decisions remain traceable across the lifecycle.

A weakness in one function can undermine the others. For example, inadequate MAP analysis may cause MEASURE to test the wrong scenarios, while ineffective MANAGE processes may leave serious evaluation findings unresolved.

### 398. Formulating the Overall NIST AI RMF Assessment Conclusion

The overall assessment conclusion should reflect the evidence obtained across the applicable functions, categories, and subcategories.

The assessor should summarize strengths, material gaps, limitations, unresolved risks, and areas requiring further evaluation.

The conclusion should distinguish between documented processes, implemented controls, and demonstrated effectiveness.

It should also identify any scope limitations or evidence gaps that prevent a reliable conclusion.

The assessor should avoid presenting the result as a universal certification of AI safety or regulatory compliance unless a separate, clearly defined assurance scheme supports that claim.

### 399. Developing the AI RMF Assessment Roadmap

An assessment roadmap translates findings into a prioritized improvement plan.

The roadmap should identify the most significant risks, required actions, accountable owners, dependencies, target dates, and verification requirements.

Prioritization should reflect impact, urgency, regulatory significance, control effectiveness, and the consequences of leaving the weakness unresolved.

The roadmap should distinguish immediate containment measures from longer-term governance or architectural improvements.

Progress should be monitored through defined management reporting, and significant delays or changes in residual risk should be escalated.

### 400. Core Principles of NIST AI RMF Assessment

An effective NIST AI RMF assessment evaluates whether an organization can identify, measure, prioritize, treat, and monitor AI risks through a coherent lifecycle process.

The assessment should examine governance accountability, contextual understanding, measurement quality, treatment effectiveness, third-party dependencies, operational monitoring, and the ability to restrict or discontinue systems when necessary.

The four functions are complementary. GOVERN establishes the conditions for accountable action; MAP provides the context for meaningful analysis; MEASURE establishes evidence about system behavior and risk; and MANAGE ensures that findings lead to appropriate decisions and safeguards.

The assessor should evaluate demonstrable practice rather than the mere existence of policies, templates, metrics, or risk registers. Conclusions must be supported by relevant evidence, and uncertainty or scope limitations must be stated transparently.

**The central principle of NIST AI RMF assessment is that effective AI risk management must be contextual, evidence-based, accountable, continuously reviewed, and capable of changing or stopping AI use when the resulting risks are no longer acceptable.**
