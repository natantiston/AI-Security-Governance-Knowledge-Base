# 19.04 — ISO/IEC 42001 Audit

## Part 1 — ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Foundations, AIMS, Scope, Criteria and Core Concepts

### 1. Definition of ISO/IEC 42001 Audit

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit is a systematic, independent, and documented process used to obtain and evaluate evidence against defined audit criteria relating to an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AIMS. It is a management-system standard rather than a technical security standard for individual AI models. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

An audit therefore examines whether the organization's AIMS is established and operated in accordance with the applicable audit criteria.

---

### 2. Purpose of ISO/IEC 42001 Audit

The purpose of an ISO/IEC 42001 audit is to provide structured evidence about the conformity and effectiveness of the AIMS against the applicable audit criteria.

An audit may examine:

* Organizational context
* Leadership
* AI policy
* AI objectives
* Planning
* AI risk management
* AI impact considerations
* Operational controls
* Performance evaluation
* Internal audit
* Management review
* Continual improvement
* Applicable documented information

The audit should evaluate the management system rather than treating a single AI model or application as the entire audit subject.

---

### 3. ISO/IEC 42001 vs ISO/IEC 27001

ISO/IEC 42001 and ISO/IEC 27001 are both management-system standards, but they address different management domains.

### 4. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit vs AI Security Audit

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit evaluates an AIMS against applicable ISO/IEC 42001 audit criteria.

An AI Security Audit focuses more specifically on security-related controls, risks, technologies, architectures, and security outcomes.

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit may include security considerations, but it is not automatically a complete technical AI security audit.


**ISO/IEC 42001 Audit ≠ AI Security Audit.**

### 5. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit vs AI Assessment

An assessment may determine the current state of an AI governance capability against selected criteria.

An audit generally requires a more structured process for obtaining and evaluating evidence against defined audit criteria.

An assessment may be performed for:

* Readiness
* Gap analysis
* Risk evaluation
* Maturity analysis
* Pre-certification preparation

An audit may be performed to determine conformity against formally defined criteria.

**Assessment ≠ Audit.**

**Gap analysis ≠ Certification Audit.**

---

### 6. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit vs Certification Audit

An internal ISO/IEC 42001 audit can be performed by the organization as part of its management-system processes.

A certification audit is performed by an independent certification body operating within the applicable conformity-assessment framework.

The two activities have different purposes and independence requirements.

### 7. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Criteria

Audit criteria are the requirements, policies, procedures, controls, objectives, or other established criteria against which audit evidence is evaluated.

For an ISO/IEC 42001 audit, the primary criteria are the applicable requirements of ISO/IEC 42001.

Additional criteria may apply depending on the audit objective and scope.

Examples include:

* Organizational policies
* Internal procedures
* Contractual requirements
* Regulatory requirements
* Customer requirements
* Approved AI governance requirements
* Risk-treatment commitments

The auditor should identify which criteria apply before drawing audit conclusions.

## Part 2 — ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Controls, Requirement Mapping, Testing, Evidence and Findings

### 51. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Framework

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit control framework organizes the requirements of the Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS), associated organizational controls, implementation evidence, and audit procedures into a structured audit approach.

The framework should enable the auditor to trace each applicable requirement to the relevant organizational process, control, evidence, and conclusion.

```

    AIMS Control

```

The framework should distinguish requirements of the standard from organizational controls developed to implement those requirements.

**Audit Framework ≠ List of Controls Only.**

### 52. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Requirement Inventory

A requirement inventory records the requirements relevant to the audit engagement and provides a basis for planning and testing.

The inventory may include:

- Responsible organizational function
- Related AIMS process

- Expected evidence

Requirement identifiers should preserve the structure of the authoritative source. Internal identifiers should not be presented as official ISO clause numbers.

### 53. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Inventory

A control inventory identifies the controls used by the organization to operate its AIMS.

Potential control domains include:

- AI policy and governance
- AI risk assessment and treatment
- AI impact assessment
- AI lifecycle management
- Data management
- AI system development
- Third-party management
- Human oversight
- Monitoring and measurement
- Internal audit
- Management review
- Corrective action

For each control, the auditor should identify its objective, owner, implementation method, frequency, evidence source, and relationship to applicable requirements.

**Control Inventory ≠ Proof of Control Effectiveness.**

### 54. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Classification

Controls can be classified according to their purpose and operation.

Examples include:

- **Preventive controls:** reduce the likelihood of an undesired condition.
- **Detective controls:** identify deviations, failures, or emerging risks.
- **Corrective controls:** address identified issues.
- **Governance controls:** establish authority, accountability, policy, and oversight.
- **Operational controls:** govern the execution of defined processes.
- **Monitoring controls:** measure conditions or performance over time.

These are useful audit classifications; they should not be mistaken for official ISO/IEC 42001 control categories unless the applicable source explicitly establishes that classification.

### 55. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Ownership

Control ownership identifies the function or individual accountable for ensuring that a control is appropriately designed, implemented, maintained, and evidenced.

- AI governance

- Privacy
- Legal and compliance
- Data governance
- AI engineering
- Business operations

- Internal audit, within its independent assurance role

The auditor should verify whether responsibilities are documented and understood and whether control execution is supported by evidence.

**Control Ownership ≠ Control Execution Automatically.**

### 56. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Frequency

Examples include:

- At AI system onboarding
- Before deployment
- Following material changes
- At defined review intervals
- When risk thresholds are exceeded
- Following incidents
- During periodic management reviews

The appropriate frequency depends on the applicable requirement, risk, organizational process, and approved control design.

An organization-defined review frequency should not be represented as an ISO-mandated interval unless the relevant requirement establishes it.

### 59. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Requirement Hierarchy

Audit criteria can originate from different sources. The auditor should identify their authority and applicability rather than treating them as interchangeable.

A practical hierarchy may distinguish:

1. Applicable legislation and regulation
2. ISO/IEC 42001 requirements
3. Other applicable standards or frameworks
4. Contractual and customer requirements
5. Organizational policies and procedures
6. Internal control objectives and implementation guidance

This is a classification aid, not a universal legal hierarchy. The legal precedence of requirements depends on the applicable jurisdiction and circumstances.

**Internal Policy ≠ Legislation.**

**Contractual Requirement ≠ ISO Requirement Automatically.**

### 60. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Applicability Assessment

Applicability assessment determines which requirements and controls are relevant to the defined audit scope.

The auditor should consider:

- AIMS scope
- Organizational activities
- AI systems and use cases
- Applicable requirements
- Relevant documented information
- Organizational processes
- Outsourced activities
- Dependencies on external providers

Where the organization excludes a control or determines that a requirement is not applicable, the auditor should evaluate the decision against the actual requirements of the standard and the organization's documented rationale.

**Not Applicable ≠ No Evidence Required Automatically.**

### 61. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Regulatory Criteria

Regulatory criteria concern applicable legal or regulatory obligations relevant to the organization and its AI activities.

The auditor should identify the source, applicability, and scope of each criterion.

Potential sources may include:

- Data protection legislation
- AI-specific legislation
- Cybersecurity requirements
- Sector-specific regulation
- Product safety obligations
- Applicable supervisory decisions

ISO/IEC 42001 may support a structured approach to managing such obligations, but conformity with the standard does not itself prove compliance with every applicable law.

**Regulatory Mapping ≠ Legal Opinion Automatically.**

### 62. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Contractual Criteria

Contractual criteria arise from binding agreements and other applicable contractual commitments.

Examples may include:

- Customer AI governance requirements
- Supplier security commitments
- Data processing obligations
- Audit rights
- Service-level commitments
- AI transparency commitments
- Incident reporting requirements

The auditor should verify the relevant contractual language and determine whether the organization has implemented the applicable obligations.

A contractual commitment should not automatically be described as a statutory requirement.

### 63. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Policy Criteria

Policy criteria are internal requirements established by the organization.

They may address:

- Permitted AI use
- AI system approval
- Risk acceptance
- Data handling
- Human oversight
- Supplier selection
- Model changes
- Monitoring
- Incident escalation

The auditor should establish which policies apply and whether they are consistent with higher-authority requirements.

A policy breach may constitute an internal nonconformity without necessarily constituting a legal violation.

### 65. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Design Testing

Control design testing determines whether a control, if implemented and operated as described, is reasonably capable of addressing its intended objective.

Testing may examine:

- Defined control objective
- Assigned responsibility
- Required inputs
- Decision criteria
- Control frequency
- Escalation conditions
- Exception handling
- Evidence requirements
- Review and approval
- Dependencies on other controls

A design deficiency may exist even when the control is consistently performed.

### 66. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Implementation Testing

Implementation testing determines whether the designed control has actually been established.

Potential procedures include:

- Inspecting approved procedures
- Reviewing system configurations
- Examining assigned responsibilities
- Inspecting workflow records
- Observing control execution
- Interviewing responsible personnel
- Verifying implementation dates

The auditor should distinguish planned controls from controls that are operationally established.

**Planned Control ≠ Implemented Control.**

### 67. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Operating Effectiveness Testing

Operating-effectiveness testing determines whether a control operated as intended over the period under review.

Testing should consider the control's frequency, relevant population, risk, and expected evidence.

For example, if an organization requires periodic review of AI risk assessments, the auditor may select review records from the audit period and verify that the reviews were performed by authorized personnel, at the required frequency, with documented outcomes and follow-up.

A single successful execution does not necessarily demonstrate consistent operation throughout the audit period.

### 68. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Outcome Testing

Outcome testing evaluates whether the control or process achieved its intended result.

Examples may include determining whether:

- Material AI risks were identified and treated
- AI systems were subject to required approval processes
- Monitoring identified relevant deviations
- Corrective actions reduced recurrence
- AI objectives achieved their defined targets

Outcome testing complements process and control testing. A positive outcome does not automatically prove that every required process was followed.

**Successful Outcome ≠ Conformity Automatically.**

### 69. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Configuration Testing

Configuration testing examines whether relevant systems and technical settings align with approved requirements and control designs.

Examples include:

- AI system access restrictions
- Model deployment approvals
- Logging configuration
- Monitoring settings
- Data access permissions
- Environment separation
- Model version controls
- Change-management restrictions

Configuration testing may support AIMS audit objectives, but it should be connected to an applicable requirement or control rather than treated as a standalone substitute for management-system auditing.

### 70. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Access-Control Testing

Access-control testing examines whether access to relevant AI management resources and systems is appropriately authorized.

Audit procedures may examine:

- User access approvals
- Role assignments
- Privileged access
- Periodic access reviews
- Joiner, mover, and leaver processes
- Access revocation
- Segregation of duties
- Exceptions

The auditor should assess access controls against the organization's approved requirements and the relevant audit criteria.

**Valid Account ≠ Authorized Access to Every AI Resource.**

### 71. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Privileged-Access Testing

Privileged-access testing examines elevated permissions that can materially affect AI systems, data, controls, or audit evidence.

Examples include privileges to:

- Deploy or modify models
- Change AI system configurations
- Access sensitive datasets
- Modify monitoring
- Approve production releases
- Change risk records
- Alter audit evidence repositories

Testing should establish whether privileged access is authorized, appropriately restricted, monitored, and reviewed according to applicable requirements.

### 72. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Authentication Testing

Authentication testing examines whether the mechanisms used to verify identities operate according to applicable requirements.

Potential evidence includes:

- Identity-provider settings
- Authentication policies
- Multi-factor authentication configuration
- Authentication logs
- Service-account controls
- Exception approvals
- Access reviews

The specific testing scope should be proportionate to the risks and the requirements applicable to the audited AIMS.

**Authentication ≠ Authorization.**

### 73. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Authorization Testing

Authorization testing evaluates whether an authenticated identity has only the permissions appropriate to its approved role and responsibilities.

The auditor may compare:

- Approved access requests
- Role definitions
- Assigned permissions
- Actual resource access
- Privileged operations
- Access-review records

The audit should examine whether permissions align with approved responsibilities and relevant control objectives.

**Authentication Success ≠ Authorization Approval.**

### 75. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Data-Governance Testing

Data-governance testing evaluates whether responsibilities, decision rights, processes, and controls for relevant AI data are established and implemented.

### 89. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Management-Review Testing

### 90. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Corrective-Action Testing

Corrective-action testing evaluates whether identified nonconformities and relevant issues are addressed through an appropriate process.

### 91. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Collection

Evidence collection involves obtaining information relevant to the audit objective and criteria.

Common methods include:

- Inspection
- Inquiry
- Observation
- Reperformance
- Recalculation
- Confirmation
- Data analytics
- System demonstration

The chosen method should be appropriate to the evidence required.

For example, an interview may explain how a control operates, while inspection of records may demonstrate that the control was actually performed.

### 92. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Authentication

Evidence authentication examines whether evidence is genuine and attributable to its claimed source.

Potential checks include:

- Source identity
- Record ownership
- Timestamp
- Version
- Approval history
- System-of-record reference
- Integrity protection
- Collection method

Authenticated evidence is not automatically accurate, complete, or sufficient for an audit conclusion.

**Evidence Authenticity ≠ Evidence Accuracy.**

### 93. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Integrity

Evidence integrity concerns whether evidence has remained protected against unauthorized or undetected alteration.

Controls may include:

- Access restrictions
- Version control
- Audit logs
- Digital signatures
- Hash verification
- Protected repositories
- Chain-of-custody records

Integrity mechanisms support confidence in evidence handling but do not prove that the original information was correct.

**Evidence Integrity ≠ Evidence Correctness.**

### 94. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Relevance

Evidence is relevant when it directly relates to the audit objective, applicable criterion, control, or conclusion being evaluated.

Evidence from a different AI system, business unit, or period may not be relevant to the control under examination.

The auditor should document the relationship between the evidence and the conclusion it supports.

**Evidence Availability ≠ Evidence Relevance.**

### 95. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Reliability

Evidence reliability concerns the degree to which the auditor can rely on information for the intended audit purpose.

Reliability considerations may include:

- Source independence
- Directness of observation
- System reliability
- Evidence provenance
- Record integrity
- Consistency with other evidence
- Limitations in collection or interpretation

No single evidence type is automatically reliable for every audit objective.

### 96. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Sufficiency

Evidence sufficiency concerns whether enough appropriate evidence has been obtained to support the intended audit conclusion.

Sufficiency depends on factors such as:

- Risk
- Materiality
- Control frequency
- Population size
- Nature of the criterion
- Reliability of evidence
- Results of prior testing
- Contradictory information

A large evidence collection may still be insufficient if it does not address the relevant requirement.

### 97. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Appropriateness

Evidence appropriateness concerns whether the evidence is relevant and reliable for the audit purpose.

The auditor should consider both the quality of individual evidence items and how they collectively support the conclusion.

Evidence that demonstrates a procedure exists may not demonstrate that it operated effectively.

**Evidence Sufficiency ≠ Evidence Appropriateness.**

### 98. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Triangulation

Evidence triangulation compares multiple evidence sources or methods to evaluate whether they support a consistent conclusion.

For example, an auditor examining an AI risk review may compare:

- The approved procedure
- The risk register
- Meeting records
- System timestamps
- Interview responses
- Follow-up actions

Contradictions should be investigated rather than resolved through assumption.

Triangulation strengthens evaluation but does not guarantee certainty.

### 99. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Evidence Limitation

Evidence limitations arise when relevant information cannot be obtained, authenticated, interpreted, or evaluated sufficiently.

Potential causes include:

- Incomplete records
- Restricted access
- Missing historical data
- Supplier confidentiality restrictions
- Unreliable system logs
- Inconsistent documentation
- Inaccessible technical environments
- Limitations in audit procedures

The auditor should evaluate whether the limitation affects the scope, testing approach, findings, or conclusion.

**Missing Evidence ≠ Proof of Control Failure Automatically.**

### 100. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Inquiry

Inquiry involves asking relevant personnel or other parties questions about the AIMS, its processes, controls, and operation.

Inquiry may clarify:

- Responsibilities
- Control execution
- Risk decisions
- Exceptions
- Management review
- Corrective actions
- Technical dependencies

Inquiry is useful for understanding processes, but verbal responses should be corroborated where the audit objective requires independent or documentary evidence.

**Inquiry ≠ Independent Proof.**

### 101. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Observation

Observation involves directly witnessing an activity, process, or condition.

Examples include observing:

- A risk review
- An approval workflow
- A control execution
- A monitoring review
- A change-management process

Observation provides evidence of the activity witnessed, but it does not automatically establish that the process operated consistently during the entire audit period.

### 102. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Inspection

Inspection involves examining records, documents, configurations, physical items, or other evidence relevant to the audit.

Inspection may cover:

- Approved policies
- Risk assessments
- AI inventories
- Change records
- Monitoring logs
- Supplier records
- Audit workpapers
- Management-review outputs

The auditor should verify that the inspected item relates to the relevant system, requirement, and period.

### 103. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Reperformance

Reperformance involves independently executing a procedure or control to determine whether it produces the expected result.

For example, the auditor may independently reproduce a risk-score calculation using the documented methodology and source data.

Reperformance can provide strong evidence about the specific procedure tested, but it does not automatically establish that all other control executions were correct.

### 104. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Recalculation

Recalculation verifies the mathematical accuracy of a calculation used in an AIMS process.

Examples include:

- Recalculating risk scores
- Recomputing performance indicators
- Verifying control-exception rates
- Recalculating sampling results
- Checking reported objective-achievement percentages

Correct arithmetic does not establish that the underlying inputs, assumptions, or methodology are appropriate.

**Calculation Accuracy ≠ Input Validity.**

### 105. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Confirmation

Confirmation involves obtaining corroborating information from an appropriate source.

For example, an auditor may confirm a supplier's certification status or verify whether a defined service was delivered during a particular period.

The auditor should evaluate the source's authority, independence, and relevance.

A supplier statement may support a conclusion but should not automatically be treated as independent assurance.

### 106. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Data Analytics

Data analytics can help auditors examine large populations of AIMS records and identify patterns or exceptions.

Potential uses include:

- Missing risk assessments
- Overdue reviews
- Unapproved changes
- Duplicate inventory records
- Unresolved findings
- Inconsistent approval timestamps
- Supplier-review gaps
- Repeated control exceptions

Analytics should be validated against reliable source data and an appropriate test objective.

**Analytical Result ≠ Audit Conclusion Automatically.**

### 107. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Full-Population Testing

Full-population testing examines every record in a defined population for a specified test condition.

For example, an auditor may compare all AI inventory entries against an approved system register to identify discrepancies.

Full-population testing can improve coverage for the selected test, but its reliability depends on population completeness, data quality, test logic, and correct interpretation of results.

**Full-Population Testing ≠ Elimination of Audit Risk.**

### 108. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Sampling

Sampling examines selected items from a defined population to draw conclusions relevant to the audit objective.

Sampling decisions should consider:

- Population definition
- Risk
- Control frequency
- Expected deviation
- Sample-selection method
- Evidence reliability
- Intended conclusion

The auditor should document the basis for the sampling approach and avoid generalizing beyond what the evidence supports.

### 109. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Risk-Based Sampling

Risk-based sampling gives greater attention to items that present higher risk or greater relevance to the audit objective.

Examples include:

- High-risk AI systems
- Significant model changes
- High-impact use cases
- Critical suppliers
- Privileged activities
- Material incidents
- Overdue corrective actions

Risk-based sampling is useful for prioritizing audit effort but does not automatically produce a statistically representative sample.

### 110. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Representative Sampling

Representative sampling seeks to select items that adequately reflect the population for the intended audit purpose.

Representativeness depends on the sampling method, population characteristics, and selection process.

A sample chosen only from high-risk items may be suitable for targeted risk testing but may not represent the entire population.

**Risk-Based Sample ≠ Representative Sample Automatically.**

### 111. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Exception Testing

Exception testing identifies cases in which an observed condition differs from the expected requirement or control outcome.

Examples include:

- Missing approvals
- Overdue reviews
- Unassessed AI systems
- Unapproved changes
- Incomplete documentation
- Failed control executions

An exception should be investigated and evaluated against the applicable criterion before it is classified as an audit finding.

**Exception ≠ Nonconformity Automatically.**

### 112. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Condition

The condition describes what the auditor observed or established through evidence.

A well-supported condition should identify the relevant facts without overstating the evidence.

For example, the condition may state that three sampled AI systems lacked documented risk-review records for the period examined.

The condition should not automatically assert that the entire AIMS is ineffective based on a limited sample.

### 113. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Criteria

The criteria identify the requirement, policy, procedure, control objective, or other established benchmark against which the observed condition is evaluated.

The auditor should identify the authoritative source precisely.

Where a finding relies on an ISO/IEC 42001 requirement, the relevant clause should be verified against the official standard rather than inferred from an internal control identifier.

**Internal Control ID ≠ ISO Clause Number.**

### 114. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Cause

The cause explains why the observed condition occurred, where sufficient evidence supports that explanation.

Potential causes may include:

- Unclear responsibilities
- Inadequate procedures
- Insufficient resources
- Control-design weaknesses
- Inconsistent execution
- Inadequate training
- Weak monitoring
- Technology limitations

The auditor should distinguish verified causes from hypotheses requiring further investigation.

**Finding Condition ≠ Root Cause.**

### 115. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Effect

The effect describes the actual or potential consequence of the observed condition.

Potential effects may include:

- Increased AI risk
- Reduced control reliability
- Incomplete management oversight
- Inaccurate reporting
- Failure to meet an internal requirement
- Potential nonconformity with an applicable standard requirement

Effects should be supported by evidence and appropriately qualified when they are potential rather than confirmed.

### 116. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Risk Evaluation

Finding risk evaluation determines the significance of an issue for the organization and the AIMS.

Factors may include:

- Potential impact
- Likelihood
- Scope
- Duration
- Recurrence
- Control criticality
- Applicable requirement
- Existing compensating controls

An internal severity rating is an organizational audit methodology unless a specific governing framework establishes the classification.

**Finding Severity ≠ Legal Classification Automatically.**

### 117. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Classification

Finding classification should follow the applicable audit methodology and any relevant certification or organizational rules.

Possible classifications include:

- Nonconformity
- Observation
- Opportunity for improvement
- Other defined audit category

Where formal certification rules define categories such as major and minor nonconformities, the auditor should apply the relevant rules rather than inventing classifications.

An internal rating system should be clearly identified as internal.

### 118. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Corrective-Action Mapping

Each finding requiring action should be traceable to an appropriate corrective-action record.

The mapping may include:

- Finding identifier
- Applicable criterion
- Condition
- Cause
- Risk assessment
- Assigned owner
- Required action
- Target date
- Evidence of completion
- Effectiveness-test result
- Closure decision

Traceability supports follow-up but does not by itself demonstrate that the corrective action is effective.

### 119. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Finding Validation

Finding validation checks whether the evidence supports the proposed finding and whether the finding accurately describes the applicable requirement and observed condition.

Validation may examine:

- Evidence authenticity
- Requirement accuracy
- Scope
- Sampling limitations
- Cause analysis
- Risk significance
- Management response
- Consistency with other findings

The auditor should resolve material factual errors before finalizing the finding.

### 120. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Management Response

Management response records how the responsible management function responds to an audit finding.

The response may include:

- Agreement or disagreement
- Explanation
- Immediate correction
- Proposed corrective action
- Assigned owner
- Target completion date
- Risk treatment
- Supporting evidence

Management disagreement should be documented and evaluated rather than automatically treated as proof that the finding is invalid.

### 121. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Corrective-Action Verification

Corrective-action verification determines whether the planned actions have been implemented.

Verification may include inspecting records, reviewing configuration changes, examining revised procedures, or observing the updated process.

Implementation evidence should be matched to the action committed by management.

**Action Reported Complete ≠ Action Verified.**

### 122. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Corrective-Action Effectiveness Testing

Effectiveness testing determines whether corrective action addressed the underlying issue sufficiently to prevent or reduce recurrence.

Testing may examine:

- Revised control design
- Updated procedures
- New system configurations
- Training completion
- Repeated control executions
- Monitoring results
- Recurrence data

The extent of testing should reflect the significance of the issue and the nature of the corrective action.

**Implementation Verification ≠ Effectiveness Testing.**

Audit workpapers should allow an appropriate reviewer to understand how the auditor reached the conclusion.

### 124. ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Quality Review

Audit quality review evaluates whether the engagement was performed and documented in accordance with the applicable audit methodology and professional expectations.

A review may consider:

- Scope and criteria
- Risk-based planning
- Evidence quality
- Sampling rationale
- Finding support
- Conclusion consistency
- Workpaper completeness
- Reporting accuracy
- Independence and objectivity

A quality review can identify deficiencies in the audit itself; it does not automatically establish the effectiveness of the audited AIMS.

### 125. Core ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Control Principle

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit should maintain traceability from the applicable requirement to the organizational control, implementation evidence, audit procedure, test result, finding, and conclusion.

# 19.04 — ISO/IEC 42001 Audit

## Part 3 — Continuous AIMS Auditing, Evidence Evaluation, Findings, Follow-Up and Assurance

### 126. ISO/IEC 42001 Continuous Audit

ISO/IEC 42001 continuous auditing is an internal audit approach that uses recurring or ongoing procedures to evaluate selected controls, risks, management-system processes, and evidence across the Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

Continuous auditing can help an organization identify control failures, overdue actions, changes in AI-system risk, and weaknesses in governance before the next scheduled audit. It does not mean that every requirement is monitored continuously or that a continuous-audit tool automatically establishes conformity.

**Governance Control:** Define which AIMS controls require recurring monitoring, which require periodic audit testing, and which require event-triggered reassessment.

**Implementation Practice:**

- Identify critical AIMS processes and associated risks.
- Define monitoring frequencies and event-based triggers.
- Establish data sources, evidence owners, thresholds, and escalation routes.
- Record exceptions and link them to the applicable audit criteria.
- Validate alerts through appropriate audit procedures.
- Retain evidence of the review, conclusion, and resulting actions.

**Recommended Practice:** Use continuous auditing to supplement a risk-based internal audit programme rather than replacing planned audit coverage.

### 127. Continuous Monitoring vs Continuous Auditing

Continuous monitoring and continuous auditing serve related but different purposes.

- **Continuous monitoring** observes operational conditions, control signals, or performance indicators.
- **Continuous auditing** evaluates evidence against defined audit criteria using planned or recurring audit procedures.
- **Periodic internal audit** evaluates selected areas at scheduled intervals.
- **Event-triggered audit testing** is initiated by a material change, incident, control failure, or other defined trigger.

For example, a monitoring platform may generate an alert when an AI model's performance falls below an internal threshold. An auditor then evaluates whether the organization defined an appropriate threshold, assigned responsibility, investigated the alert, documented its assessment, and implemented required action.

**Continuous monitoring ≠ continuous auditing.** A monitoring alert is an input to audit work, not automatically an audit conclusion.

### 128. Continuous Audit Architecture

A continuous-audit architecture connects operational data, governance records, control requirements, evidence validation, exception management, and audit reporting.

**Governance Control:** Maintain a documented relationship between each automated check and the audit criterion, risk, control, evidence source, and responsible reviewer it supports.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the check's logic, execution time, data coverage, known limitations, exceptions, reviewer decisions, and changes to the detection rule.

An automated check that is poorly designed or based on incomplete data can create false assurance even when it executes successfully.

### 129. Continuous Audit Coverage

Continuous-audit coverage describes the portion of the defined audit universe, relevant risks, controls, populations, or periods addressed by continuous procedures.

Coverage should be measured against an explicit denominator. Counting the number of automated checks is not sufficient because multiple checks may address the same risk while other material risks remain untested.

A coverage assessment should identify:

- In-scope AIMS processes and AI systems.
- Relevant audit criteria and associated controls.
- Controls subject to continuous procedures.
- Controls requiring manual or periodic testing.
- Excluded areas and the rationale for exclusion.
- Data sources that are unavailable, incomplete, or unreliable.
- Residual audit risks caused by coverage limitations.

**Recommended Practice:** Maintain a coverage matrix that distinguishes automated monitoring, automated audit testing, manual audit procedures, and areas not yet covered.

**Control count ≠ risk coverage.** High automation volume does not demonstrate comprehensive AIMS assurance.

### 130. Event-Triggered AIMS Audit

An event-triggered audit is initiated when a defined event indicates that existing audit conclusions, control assumptions, or risk assessments may no longer be adequate.

Potential triggers include:

- Introduction of a materially different AI system or use case.
- Significant changes to model architecture, training data, or deployment.
- Expansion into a new business process or operating environment.
- A serious AI-related incident or recurring control failure.
- A significant supplier or technology change.
- Changes in applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal criteria.
- Repeated overdue corrective actions.
- Material changes to the AIMS scope or organizational responsibilities.

Not every trigger requires a full audit. The response should be proportionate to the event's significance, the affected controls, and the potential consequences.

**Governance Control:** Define event thresholds, decision authority, response timelines, and documentation requirements.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the trigger, preliminary assessment, scope decision, audit procedures performed, rationale for exclusions, and resulting conclusions.

**Material change ≠ automatic nonconformity.** A change creates a reason to reassess relevant controls; it does not by itself prove that a requirement has been breached.

### 131. Audit Trail for Continuous Procedures

A continuous-audit trail should allow a qualified reviewer to reconstruct what the procedure evaluated, what evidence was used, what exceptions were identified, and how the conclusion was reached.

A suitable record may include:

- Unique audit procedure identifier.
- Applicable criterion and control mapping.
- Execution date and period covered.
- Data source and extraction reference.
- Query, rule, script, or test version.
- Population size and data exclusions.
- Execution result and identified exceptions.
- Validation performed by the auditor.
- Reviewer identity and approval, where required.
- Finding reference or documented disposition.
- Evidence retention location and access restrictions.

**Governance Control:** Protect audit records against unauthorized modification, deletion, and access.

**Implementation Practice:** Use version control for audit rules and maintain change records showing who approved changes and why.

An execution log alone may not demonstrate the validity of the underlying data or the appropriateness of the test.

### 132. Continuous-Audit Rule Validation

Automated audit rules must be validated before their results are relied upon for audit conclusions.

Validation should consider:

- Whether the rule accurately represents the intended criterion.
- Whether the selected data fields are relevant and complete.
- Whether the rule covers the intended population.
- Whether expected exceptions are detected.
- Whether valid cases are incorrectly flagged.
- Whether material exceptions can escape detection.
- Whether boundary conditions and unusual cases are handled.
- Whether changes to upstream systems affect the results.

Testing should include known-positive and known-negative cases where feasible, together with independent review of the rule's logic.

**Recommended Practice:** Revalidate rules after material changes to the data source, control design, business process, AI system, or audit methodology.

**Successful execution ≠ valid test.** A procedure can run without technical errors while producing misleading results.

### 133. AI-Assisted ISO/IEC 42001 Audit

AI-assisted auditing uses AI-enabled tools to support activities such as document classification, evidence discovery, control mapping, anomaly detection, interview-note organization, and preliminary finding analysis.

Potential applications include:

- Identifying missing or inconsistent AIMS documentation.
- Comparing policies and procedures against defined audit criteria.
- Locating evidence relevant to selected controls.
- Detecting unusual patterns in control records.
- Identifying potentially duplicated or overdue corrective actions.
- Summarizing large evidence collections for auditor review.
- Suggesting relationships between risks, controls, and findings.

The organization should define permitted uses, access controls, data-handling rules, validation procedures, and responsibility for conclusions.

**Governance Control:** Require appropriate human review of AI-generated audit analysis before it is used to support a formal audit conclusion.

**Implementation Practice:** Retain the source evidence, relevant prompts or processing instructions where appropriate, output reviewed, validation performed, and final auditor decision.

AI-generated summaries can omit qualifications, misinterpret documents, or introduce unsupported claims. Auditors should return to the original evidence when a conclusion depends on a specific statement or record.

**AI-assisted analysis ≠ independent audit evidence automatically.** The reliability of the underlying source and the validity of the auditor's procedure remain central.

### 134. Independence in Continuous Auditing

Continuous-audit arrangements should preserve the distinction between operating a control and independently evaluating its effectiveness.

A team that configures, owns, and operates a control may provide valuable monitoring data, but its operational responsibilities can affect the independence of its assessment. The organization should consider role conflicts, review authority, and the intended level of assurance.

Where internal audit independence is relevant, the audit programme should define reporting arrangements, access rights, conflict-of-interest handling, and escalation routes consistent with the organization's governance framework.

**Governance Control:** Separate control operation from independent audit review where appropriate to the risk and assurance objective.

**Implementation Practice:** Document situations in which the auditor designed, configured, or previously operated the process being audited and determine suitable safeguards.

**Operational ownership ≠ independent assurance.** The fact that a control owner produces a report does not establish that the control has been independently evaluated.

### 135. AIMS Audit Frequency and Risk-Based Scheduling

ISO/IEC 42001 audit scheduling should be based on the organization's defined audit programme, applicable criteria, AIMS scope, and assessment of relevant risks and changes.

A risk-based schedule may consider:

- Significance and potential impact of the AI use case.
- Results of previous audits.
- History of nonconformities and corrective actions.
- Extent of organizational or technical change.
- Reliance on external providers.
- Complexity of the AIMS processes.
- Known limitations in control evidence.
- Relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, and stakeholder expectations.

The schedule should also consider audit capacity, auditor competence, process interdependencies, and the need to assess different organizational units or lifecycle stages.

**Standard Requirement:** Audit-programme planning and internal-audit arrangements should be determined against the applicable requirements of ISO/IEC 42001. The precise obligations should be verified against the licensed, applicable edition of the standard.

**Governance Control:** Document the rationale for audit frequency, priority, scope, and material changes to the schedule.

**High risk ≠ a universally prescribed audit interval.** A risk-based interval is an organizational decision unless a separate applicable requirement establishes a specific frequency.

### 136. AIMS Audit Planning and Replanning

An audit plan translates the approved audit scope into procedures, responsibilities, evidence requirements, and a workable schedule.

The plan should identify:

- Audit objectives and criteria.
- Processes, locations, AI systems, or organizational units in scope.
- Relevant risks and controls.
- Audit team responsibilities and competence.
- Planned interviews, inspections, tests, and sampling.
- Required documents and system access.
- Dependencies on suppliers or other assurance providers.
- Reporting and escalation arrangements.
- Constraints, exclusions, and limitations.

Replanning may be necessary when evidence is unavailable, a critical system changes, an incident occurs, or the initial risk assessment proves inaccurate.

**Implementation Practice:** Record significant changes to the plan, the reason for each change, the approving authority, and the effect on audit coverage.

**Plan completion ≠ audit completion.** Completion must be evaluated against the approved objectives, actual procedures, unresolved limitations, and required reporting.

### 137. AIMS Audit Evidence Lifecycle

Evidence management should follow a controlled lifecycle from identification through collection, validation, analysis, workpaper linkage, retention, and authorized disposal.

A practical lifecycle is:

1. Define the audit question and relevant criterion.
2. Identify the evidence needed to evaluate the criterion.
3. Locate the source and confirm access authorization.
4. Collect or inspect the evidence using an appropriate procedure.
5. Assess relevance, reliability, completeness, and limitations.
6. Link the evidence to the relevant test and conclusion.
7. Protect the record and apply retention requirements.
8. Reassess evidence when material changes or contradictory information emerge.
9. Retain or dispose of records under approved rules.

Evidence requirements should be proportionate to the sensitivity of the information and the potential consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

**Governance Control:** Establish evidence-handling procedures covering confidentiality, integrity, access, retention, and traceability.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain an evidence register with unique references and links to the related audit procedures and findings.

### 138. Evidence Provenance and Source Reliability

Evidence provenance describes where evidence originated, how it was obtained, how it was transformed, and who handled it.

For AIMS audits, evidence may originate from:

- Approved policies and procedures.
- Risk assessments and treatment records.
- AI system inventories and lifecycle records.
- Model development and validation documentation.
- Monitoring platforms and incident-management systems.
- Supplier reports and contractual records.
- Interviews and direct observations.
- System logs, access records, and configuration exports.
- Internal audit and management-review records.

Auditors should evaluate whether the evidence is authentic, relevant, complete enough for the intended test, and sufficiently reliable for the conclusion being drawn.

**Implementation Practice:** Record source identifiers, collection dates, extraction methods, transformations, responsible personnel, and known limitations.

**Provenance ≠ accuracy.** Knowing where evidence came from does not prove that its contents are correct.

### 139. Conflicting AIMS Audit Evidence

Conflicting evidence occurs when two or more sources provide inconsistent accounts of a control, process, event, or outcome.

Examples include:

- A policy requiring approval while workflow records show approval was bypassed.
- A management report stating that all corrective actions are closed while the action register shows overdue items.
- A supplier assurance report describing a control that cannot be corroborated in the organization's own implementation.
- An interview indicating that a procedure is routinely followed while sampled records show exceptions.

The auditor should investigate the inconsistency rather than selecting the source that supports the preferred conclusion.

Appropriate procedures may include:

- Confirming the period covered by each source.
- Checking whether sources refer to the same system, population, and control.
- Reperforming a test using an independent source.
- Obtaining additional records or confirmations.
- Interviewing relevant personnel.
- Assessing whether records were incomplete, outdated, or altered.
- Revising the conclusion where evidence remains insufficient.

**Governance Control:** Require material evidence conflicts to be documented, evaluated, and resolved or explicitly disclosed before final reporting.

**Conflicting evidence ≠ proof of misconduct.** Inconsistency warrants investigation; intent and cause require separate evidence.

### 140. Evidence Sufficiency and Appropriateness Reassessment

Evidence sufficiency concerns whether enough appropriate evidence has been obtained to support a conclusion. Evidence appropriateness concerns its relevance and reliability.

The auditor should reconsider the evidence basis when:

- A control test produces unexpected results.
- A significant exception is discovered.
- The population is incomplete or unreliable.
- A key source cannot be authenticated.
- New evidence contradicts an earlier conclusion.
- The test depends on an unverified assumption.
- The sample does not adequately address the audit objective.
- A supplier's assurance statement lacks sufficient scope detail.

The response may include expanding the sample, testing additional periods, obtaining independent evidence, changing the procedure, or qualifying the conclusion.

**Governance Control:** Define when unresolved evidence limitations require escalation or a limitation to be included in the audit report.

**More evidence ≠ better evidence automatically.** Additional records may not resolve a fundamental reliability problem.

### 141. Audit Findings Across the AIMS Lifecycle

AIMS audit findings may relate to governance, risk management, development, provision, use, monitoring, or retirement of AI systems, as well as the management-system processes supporting these activities.

Potential areas include:

- Incomplete AIMS scope definition.
- Unclear responsibilities for AI risk decisions.
- Missing or inconsistent risk-treatment records.
- Weak control over AI system changes.
- Inadequate supplier oversight.
- Incomplete monitoring or incident records.
- Failure to evaluate the effectiveness of corrective actions.
- Inconsistent management-review inputs.
- Gaps between documented procedures and actual practices.

A finding must be evaluated against defined audit criteria and supported by sufficient evidence. The mere absence of a preferred practice is not automatically an ISO/IEC 42001 nonconformity.

**Standard Requirement:** A nonconformity conclusion must be grounded in an applicable requirement of the standard or another explicitly defined audit criterion.

**Recommended Practice:** Distinguish nonconformities from observations, improvement opportunities, and risk concerns according to the organization's approved audit methodology.

### 142. ISO/IEC 42001 Nonconformity Evaluation

A nonconformity is a failure to fulfil an applicable requirement. In an ISO/IEC 42001 audit, the auditor should identify the specific requirement and establish how the available evidence demonstrates the failure.

A defensible nonconformity record should include:

- The requirement or criterion that applies.
- The objective evidence observed.
- The condition that does not fulfil the requirement.
- The affected process, system, or AIMS scope.
- The extent and potential significance of the issue.
- Any relevant evidence limitations.
- The classification assigned under the audit methodology.

The auditor should avoid relying solely on a general statement that a process is weak or that a control is undesirable.

**Implementation Practice:** Write findings so that another competent auditor can understand the criterion, evidence, and reasoning without relying on undocumented assumptions.

**Weakness ≠ nonconformity automatically.** The conclusion depends on the applicable criterion and the evidence of failure.

### 143. Nonconformity Severity and Classification

Organizations may classify audit findings by severity or priority to support remediation and management attention. Such classifications must not be confused with the underlying requirement or the existence of a nonconformity.

An internal classification model may consider:

- Potential consequences of the failure.
- Scope and number of affected processes or systems.
- Duration and recurrence.
- Whether compensating controls exist.
- Exposure of sensitive data or critical services.
- Effect on AIMS objectives.
- Evidence of systemic or isolated failure.
- Urgency of corrective action.

The organization should define classification criteria before applying them and use them consistently.

**Governance Control:** Maintain documented definitions, approval authority, escalation thresholds, and rules for reclassification.

**Internal severity rating ≠ an official ISO classification automatically.** Do not represent internal labels such as critical, major, or minor as universal regulatory or certification determinations without verifying the applicable audit rules.

### 144. Root-Cause Analysis for AIMS Findings

Root-cause analysis seeks to identify the underlying conditions that allowed a nonconformity or control failure to occur or persist.

Potential causes include:

- Unclear accountability.
- Inadequate process design.
- Insufficient resources or competence.
- Weak change management.
- Incomplete supplier oversight.
- Poorly defined acceptance criteria.
- Inadequate monitoring or escalation.
- Misaligned incentives.
- Deficient documentation or communication.
- Failure to incorporate lessons from previous incidents.

The first visible error may be a symptom rather than the underlying cause. For example, an overdue review may result from unclear ownership, a missing escalation mechanism, or an unrealistic process design.

**Implementation Practice:** Test the proposed root cause against the evidence and consider whether it explains the observed condition, its recurrence, and any related failures.

**Root-cause statement ≠ verified root cause.** Management explanations should be evaluated rather than accepted without scrutiny.

### 145. Corrective Action and Correction

A correction addresses the detected nonconformity or immediate condition. Corrective action addresses its cause to prevent recurrence or occurrence elsewhere, as appropriate.

For example:

- **Correction:** Restore a missing approval record where the underlying approval occurred and can be verified.
- **Corrective action:** Redesign the approval workflow, clarify ownership, and introduce monitoring to prevent repeated approval failures.

The correct response depends on the evidence. A missing record should not be reconstructed as if approval had occurred when that fact cannot be established.

**Governance Control:** Require the responsible owner to distinguish immediate correction, root-cause analysis, corrective action, and effectiveness verification.

**Correction ≠ corrective action.** Resolving the visible symptom does not demonstrate that the underlying cause has been addressed.

### 146. Corrective-Action Ownership and Due Dates

Every corrective action should have an accountable owner, an agreed target date, defined deliverables, and a method for demonstrating completion.

A useful corrective-action record contains:

- Finding reference and related requirement.
- Description of the nonconformity.
- Immediate correction, if applicable.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Proposed corrective action.
- Responsible owner and approval authority.
- Milestones and target dates.
- Required resources and dependencies.
- Evidence expected at completion.
- Effectiveness criteria.
- Verification date and reviewer.
- Closure decision and rationale.

Target dates should reflect the significance of the issue, the time needed for remediation, dependencies, and the risk of leaving the issue unresolved.

**Implementation Practice:** Require a documented rationale and authorized approval for due-date extensions.

**Assigned owner ≠ effective remediation.** Accountability enables follow-up but does not prove that the action is adequate.

### 147. Corrective-Action Verification

Corrective-action verification evaluates whether the agreed action was implemented and whether the completion evidence is credible.

Verification may include:

- Inspecting revised policies and procedures.
- Confirming that a control was deployed.
- Reviewing configuration or workflow records.
- Testing a sample of transactions or decisions.
- Interviewing responsible personnel.
- Inspecting training and competency records.
- Reviewing updated risk assessments.
- Confirming that relevant documentation is controlled and accessible.

The verification procedure should match the nature of the action. A document update may require inspection, while a redesigned control may require operational testing.

**Governance Control:** Define the independence, competence, and approval authority required for closure verification.

**Implementation evidence ≠ effectiveness evidence.** A completed implementation task does not establish that the control operates as intended.

### 148. Corrective-Action Effectiveness

Effectiveness testing determines whether corrective action has addressed the identified cause and reduced the likelihood of recurrence to an acceptable level.

Depending on the control, effectiveness testing may require:

- A suitable observation period.
- Testing multiple transactions or control executions.
- Examination of recurrence and exception trends.
- Confirmation that the control works under realistic operating conditions.
- Review of related systems or processes that may share the same cause.
- Verification that owners understand and follow the revised procedure.

The organization should define effectiveness criteria before closure wherever practical.

If an action fails effectiveness testing, the finding may need to be reopened, the root-cause analysis revisited, or additional action assigned.

**No recurrence observed ≠ effectiveness proven automatically.** The observation period, test coverage, and probability of detecting recurrence affect the strength of the conclusion.

### 149. Overdue Corrective Actions and Escalation

Overdue corrective actions can indicate insufficient ownership, inadequate resources, underestimated complexity, weak governance, or disagreement over the appropriate remediation.

A controlled process should identify overdue items, assess residual risk, determine whether interim measures are needed, and escalate material delays to the appropriate authority.

The escalation decision should consider:

- Finding severity and potential impact.
- Duration of the delay.
- Whether interim controls remain effective.
- Repeated extensions.
- Dependencies outside the owner's control.
- Whether the original corrective action remains adequate.
- Any relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, or certification obligations.

**Governance Control:** Define escalation thresholds and require documented approval of extensions and risk-acceptance decisions.

**Overdue action ≠ automatic legal violation.** The consequences depend on the applicable requirement, the nature of the underlying issue, and the surrounding facts.

### 150. Management Acceptance of Residual Risk

Management may need to make decisions about residual risk, resource allocation, remediation priority, or temporary compensating measures. Such decisions should occur within the organization's authority and risk-management arrangements.

An auditor should evaluate whether the decision was authorized, informed, documented, and consistent with applicable criteria. The auditor should not treat management acceptance as a substitute for fulfilling a mandatory requirement.

Where a nonconformity remains unresolved, its status should remain transparent under the organization's audit and corrective-action process.

**Governance Control:** Record the risk owner, decision authority, rationale, affected systems, compensating controls, review date, and escalation conditions.

**Risk acceptance ≠ requirement waiver.** Internal approval cannot override an applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or standard requirement.

### 151. Follow-Up Audit and Closure

Follow-up audit work determines whether identified findings have been appropriately addressed and whether the evidence supports closure.

Follow-up may be conducted through targeted testing, review of corrective-action evidence, or a broader audit where the issue affects multiple processes or systems.

Closure decisions should consider:

- Whether the original condition was corrected.
- Whether the cause was adequately analyzed.
- Whether the agreed action was implemented.
- Whether effectiveness was tested where required.
- Whether residual issues remain.
- Whether the evidence is sufficient and reliable.
- Whether related findings need reassessment.

A finding may remain open, be closed, or require further action according to the organization's defined methodology and applicable requirements.

**Implementation Practice:** Retain the closure rationale and the evidence supporting it.

**Administrative closure ≠ substantive closure.** Changing a record's status does not prove that the underlying issue has been resolved.

### 152. Repeat Findings and Systemic Weaknesses

Repeat findings occur when similar failures recur across audit periods or processes. They may indicate that prior corrective actions addressed symptoms rather than causes, or that a control has not been sustained.

The auditor should evaluate whether repeated findings share:

- The same root cause.
- A common control owner.
- A common supplier or technology dependency.
- A repeated weakness in governance or process design.
- Similar training or competence gaps.
- Common failures in monitoring or escalation.
- Inadequate verification of earlier actions.

Where a common cause is established, remediation may need to extend beyond the individual finding.

**Recommended Practice:** Maintain trend analysis across findings, control families, organizational units, AI systems, and reporting periods.

**Similar symptoms ≠ identical root cause.** Grouping findings can reveal patterns, but the relationship must be supported by evidence.

### 153. Cross-System and Cross-Process Audit Findings

A single AIMS weakness may affect multiple AI systems, business units, or lifecycle stages. Conversely, similar findings across different systems may arise from unrelated causes.

Cross-system analysis should identify the affected population, shared dependencies, relevant control owners, and the potential effect on audit conclusions.

For example, an inadequate model-change approval process may affect multiple AI applications if they use the same release workflow. The auditor should determine whether the issue is limited to one application or represents a broader management-system weakness.

**Implementation Practice:** Use an affected-system matrix linking findings to AI inventory records, risk assessments, controls, suppliers, and responsible owners.

**One finding ≠ one affected system.** Scope the issue based on evidence rather than the system where it was first discovered.

### 154. Supplier Evidence in AIMS Audits

Supplier evidence may include independent assurance reports, certifications, control descriptions, testing summaries, contractual commitments, and incident notifications.

The auditor should evaluate whether the evidence:

- Covers the relevant service and period.
- Includes the control or process being relied upon.
- Identifies meaningful exclusions and limitations.
- Applies to the relevant organizational entity.
- Provides adequate information about exceptions.
- Supports the specific audit conclusion.
- Is sufficiently current for the intended purpose.

A supplier report may support an assessment but may not demonstrate that the customer has implemented its own responsibilities or that every relevant AI lifecycle risk is addressed.

**Governance Control:** Define the criteria for accepting, supplementing, or rejecting supplier assurance evidence.

**Supplier certification ≠ complete customer AIMS assurance.** The organization's own governance, integration, oversight, and applicable obligations still require evaluation.

### 155. External Audit and Certification Findings

External auditors and certification bodies may evaluate conformity within their defined scope and under the applicable audit or certification arrangements.

Internal AIMS audit teams should understand the scope and status of external findings, but they should not assume that an external report covers every internal control, AI system, legal obligation, or contractual commitment.

When external findings are received, the organization should determine:

- Which requirement and scope are affected.
- Whether the issue also affects internal audit conclusions.
- Whether related systems or processes may be exposed.
- Which actions are required by the external process.
- What evidence is needed for internal follow-up.
- Whether internal reporting or escalation is necessary.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain traceability between external findings, internal findings, corrective actions, and relevant management-review records.

**External audit result ≠ universal assurance.** Assurance is bounded by the criteria, scope, period, evidence, and procedures of the engagement.

### 156. Internal Audit Findings and Regulatory Notifications

An internal audit finding may reveal an issue that requires further legal, regulatory, contractual, or operational assessment. Whether a notification, report, or other formal response is required depends on the applicable obligation and the facts.

The audit team should not independently assume that every nonconformity is reportable or that no notification is needed because a finding has been classified as low severity.

**Implementation Practice:**

- Escalate potentially reportable matters through the organization's established process.
- Involve competent legal, compliance, privacy, security, or regulatory specialists as appropriate.
- Preserve relevant evidence and decision records.
- Evaluate applicable deadlines and responsible authorities.
- Document the rationale for decisions made by authorized personnel.

**Audit finding classification ≠ legal reportability determination.** These are related but distinct decisions.

### 157. Audit Workpaper Quality and Traceability

Audit workpapers should provide a clear and reviewable record of the audit work performed, evidence obtained, reasoning applied, and conclusions reached.

A well-structured workpaper typically contains:

- Audit objective and criterion.
- Control or process under examination.
- Test procedure and population.
- Sampling rationale, where applicable.
- Evidence references.
- Test results and exceptions.
- Evaluation of evidence limitations.
- Auditor conclusion and rationale.
- Reviewer comments and resolution.
- Related finding or follow-up reference.

Workpapers should be sufficiently detailed to enable an appropriately qualified reviewer to understand the basis of the conclusion.

**Governance Control:** Establish workpaper preparation, review, retention, access, and quality-assurance rules.

**Workpaper volume ≠ workpaper quality.** Completeness, relevance, clarity, and traceability matter more than the number of pages.

### 158. Audit Review and Challenge

Audit review provides an additional check on whether the work performed supports the reported conclusions and whether the audit methodology was applied consistently.

Reviewers should challenge:

- Unsupported assumptions.
- Weak or contradictory evidence.
- Incomplete population definitions.
- Unexplained exclusions.
- Inappropriate sampling.
- Unclear criterion-to-finding links.
- Conclusions that exceed the evidence.
- Unresolved conflicts of interest.
- Findings whose severity is inconsistent with the defined methodology.
- Corrective-action closures without adequate verification.

The level of review should be proportionate to the complexity, significance, and risk of the audit.

**Implementation Practice:** Document material review comments, the auditor's response, the resolution, and approval of the final workpaper or report.

**Review completed ≠ review effective.** A review process is useful only when it meaningfully evaluates the quality and supportability of the work.

### 159. Audit Scope Limitations and Qualifications

An audit limitation arises when the auditor cannot obtain or evaluate evidence needed to complete a planned procedure or support a conclusion.

Potential limitations include:

- Inaccessible systems or records.
- Missing or unreliable data.
- Supplier restrictions.
- Incomplete audit logs.
- Insufficient observation periods.
- Inability to test a relevant control.
- Unavailable personnel or subject-matter expertise.
- Restrictions imposed on the audit scope.

The auditor should assess the impact on the engagement, consider alternative procedures, and determine whether the limitation must be escalated or disclosed.

**Governance Control:** Require significant scope and evidence limitations to be documented with their effect on audit coverage and conclusions.

**No exception detected ≠ control effective** when the relevant test could not be performed adequately.

### 160. Audit Conclusion Formulation

An audit conclusion should reflect the objectives, criteria, scope, procedures performed, evidence evaluated, findings identified, and unresolved limitations.

A defensible conclusion distinguishes:

- Areas where the evidence supports conformity.
- Confirmed nonconformities against specified criteria.
- Observations or improvement opportunities.
- Unresolved questions.
- Areas excluded from the audit.
- Material limitations affecting the level of confidence.

The conclusion should not imply that every AI system, every legal obligation, or every control was evaluated unless the engagement actually covered those areas.

**Implementation Practice:** Link each material conclusion to the relevant workpapers and findings, and ensure the report states the applicable scope and period.

**Audit conclusion ≠ guarantee of absence of failure.** An audit provides a conclusion within defined boundaries; it does not establish that no unobserved issue exists.

### 161. AIMS Audit Reporting

An AIMS audit report communicates the results of the engagement to relevant management and governance stakeholders.

A report should normally identify:

- Audit objectives, scope, and criteria.
- Period and organizational areas covered.
- Audit methodology and major procedures.
- Significant findings and their supporting criteria.
- Positive practices or strengths, where relevant.
- Evidence limitations and scope exclusions.
- Corrective-action owners and required follow-up.
- Overall conclusion and material qualifications.
- Distribution, confidentiality, and approval details.

The report should be clear enough for decision-makers while preserving sufficient precision for technical and compliance follow-up.

**Governance Control:** Establish reporting timelines, review responsibilities, escalation rules, and approved distribution lists.

**Concise reporting ≠ omission of material qualifications.** A short report must still communicate the boundaries and significance of the conclusion.

### 162. Management Response to Audit Findings

Management responses should address the substance of the finding, the proposed action, the responsible owner, the target date, and the evidence needed for closure.

Management may agree with a finding, dispute its interpretation, propose alternative remediation, or request clarification. Disagreement should be evaluated through the defined audit process rather than resolved solely through hierarchy or negotiation.

Where management disputes a finding, the auditor should:

- Revisit the applicable criterion.
- Review the supporting evidence.
- Consider additional evidence supplied by management.
- Clarify any factual errors.
- Document unresolved differences.
- Escalate material disagreements according to the audit charter or governance process.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain a transparent record of the auditor's conclusion and management's response.

**Management disagreement ≠ finding invalid.** Equally, an auditor's initial position should remain open to correction when new evidence changes the analysis.

### 163. AIMS Audit Metrics

Audit metrics can help evaluate programme execution, coverage, timeliness, recurring weaknesses, and corrective-action performance.

Potential metrics include:

- Planned audits completed.
- Risk-weighted audit coverage.
- Findings by type and severity.
- Repeat-finding rate.
- Corrective actions overdue.
- Median time to corrective-action closure.
- Effectiveness-test failure rate.
- Evidence limitations by cause.
- Audit-plan changes.
- Percentage of high-priority findings with verified interim measures.

Metrics should have defined formulas, data sources, ownership, reporting frequency, and interpretation rules.

**Recommended Practice:** Pair completion metrics with quality and outcome measures.

**Audit volume ≠ assurance quality.** Completing many audits does not demonstrate that the most significant risks were adequately evaluated.

### 164. Audit Metrics and Incentive Distortion

Audit metrics can create undesirable incentives when performance is assessed using a single target.

For example:

- A target to reduce findings may discourage appropriate reporting.
- A target to close actions quickly may encourage premature closure.
- A target to complete audits may reward shallow coverage.
- A target to automate procedures may reduce attention to unautomated risks.

Metrics should therefore be interpreted in context and reviewed for unintended consequences.

**Governance Control:** Ensure that audit performance measures do not reward suppressing findings, weakening testing, or closing actions without evidence.

**Lower finding count ≠ lower risk automatically.** A reduction may reflect genuine improvement, weaker detection, changed scope, or reporting differences.

### 165. AIMS Audit Programme Effectiveness

The audit programme should be evaluated periodically to determine whether it continues to address relevant AIMS risks, organizational changes, and assurance needs.

Evaluation may consider:

- Whether significant risks receive appropriate coverage.
- Whether audits are completed with adequate competence.
- Whether evidence is reliable and traceable.
- Whether findings identify meaningful control weaknesses.
- Whether corrective actions address causes.
- Whether repeat findings decline for substantiated reasons.
- Whether reporting supports management decisions.
- Whether the programme adapts to new AI systems and changes.
- Whether stakeholders receive the assurance they need.

The evaluation should also consider lessons from incidents, external assessments, supplier issues, and changes to applicable criteria.

**Governance Control:** Define programme review criteria, responsibilities, frequency, and improvement actions.

**Audit programme completion ≠ audit programme effectiveness.** A programme can meet its schedule while leaving material risks insufficiently examined.

### 166. Audit Competence and Specialist Support

AIMS audits may require knowledge of management systems, AI governance, risk assessment, data management, information security, privacy, technical AI lifecycle processes, and applicable legal or contractual criteria.

The required competence depends on the audit objectives and scope. Not every auditor must be an expert in every AI technology, but the audit team must collectively possess sufficient competence to perform the planned work.

Specialist support may be needed to evaluate:

- Model development and validation.
- Data quality and lineage.
- AI security architecture.
- Human oversight and operational workflows.
- Cloud and supplier controls.
- Statistical testing and monitoring.
- Applicable regulatory or contractual requirements.

**Governance Control:** Assess competence against the audit assignment and document how significant gaps will be addressed.

**Auditor seniority ≠ technical competence for every audit scope.** Competence should be evaluated against the actual procedures and risks.

### 167. Audit Tools and Configuration Governance

Audit tools may include evidence repositories, workpaper systems, workflow platforms, analytics tools, and automated testing scripts.

Their use should be governed to ensure that results remain reliable and records remain protected.

Controls may cover:

- Authorized access and role separation.
- Version control and change approval.
- Configuration management.
- Data protection and retention.
- Validation of calculations and test logic.
- Logging of execution and reviewer actions.
- Backup and recovery.
- Vendor dependency and service availability.
- Restrictions on uploading confidential evidence to external services.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain an inventory of significant audit tools, their owners, purposes, dependencies, and validation status.

**Tool availability ≠ reliable audit output.** Tool governance and procedure validation remain necessary.

### 168. Audit Data Protection and Confidentiality

AIMS audit evidence may contain personal data, proprietary model information, security configurations, trade secrets, supplier records, and confidential business information.

The organization should handle evidence according to applicable law, contractual commitments, internal policies, and legitimate audit requirements.

Practical measures include:

- Limiting access to authorized personnel.
- Collecting only evidence needed for the audit purpose.
- Redacting unnecessary personal or sensitive information.
- Using approved transfer and storage mechanisms.
- Applying retention and deletion rules.
- Recording authorized disclosures.
- Protecting evidence shared with external auditors.
- Evaluating risks when using external or AI-assisted audit tools.

**Legal Requirement:** Applicable data-protection and confidentiality obligations must be assessed independently; an audit purpose does not automatically remove those obligations.

**Governance Control:** Define secure evidence-handling procedures and assign responsibility for access, disclosure, and retention decisions.

**Audit access ≠ unrestricted access.** Evidence collection should remain authorized and proportionate to the audit objective.

### 169. Audit Evidence Retention

Evidence-retention rules should reflect the purpose of the record, the organization's documented information controls, applicable legal and contractual requirements, and relevant certification or audit arrangements.

A retention schedule may distinguish:

- Audit plans and approved criteria.
- Evidence and workpapers.
- Findings and corrective-action records.
- Management responses.
- Review and approval records.
- Reports and distribution logs.
- Records subject to legal hold or investigation.

The organization should identify the applicable retention period and authority rather than applying an assumed universal duration.

**Implementation Practice:** Define retention triggers, record owners, access restrictions, deletion authorization, and legal-hold procedures.

**Retained evidence ≠ valid evidence automatically.** Retention preserves a record; it does not establish its authenticity, relevance, or sufficiency.

### 170. Audit Evidence Reuse

Evidence collected for one audit may be relevant to another audit, but reuse requires an assessment of whether it remains appropriate for the new objective.

The auditor should consider:

- Whether the source and scope are the same.
- Whether the evidence covers the required period.
- Whether the control or system has changed.
- Whether the evidence was independently validated.
- Whether the original procedure was sufficient for the new objective.
- Whether the evidence is subject to restrictions.
- Whether reliance on the original auditor's work is justified.

For example, a supplier assurance report used in a previous audit may no longer cover the current service configuration or reporting period.

**Recommended Practice:** Record why reused evidence is relevant and identify any additional procedures required.

**Previously accepted evidence ≠ permanently sufficient evidence.** Reuse depends on the new audit question and the continuing validity of the source.

### 171. Audit Evidence from Management Review

Management-review records may demonstrate that the organization evaluated AIMS performance, considered relevant information, and made decisions within its management-system processes.

An auditor should examine whether the records are appropriate to the applicable criteria and whether the documented decisions are supported by the underlying information.

Potential evidence includes:

- Review agendas and inputs.
- Decisions and action records.
- Relevant performance information.
- Risk and opportunity updates.
- Audit results and corrective-action status.
- Resource or governance decisions.
- Follow-up on previous review actions.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected management-review decisions to their source inputs, assigned actions, and subsequent outcomes.

**Meeting minutes ≠ effective management review automatically.** The evidence should support the substance of the review and the applicable requirements.

### 172. Audit Evidence from Internal Audit

Records of prior internal audits can support assessment of the organization's audit process and identify recurring weaknesses. However, the existence of previous audit reports does not independently establish that every reported conclusion is correct.

When relying on prior work, consider:

- The original audit scope and criteria.
- Auditor competence and objectivity.
- Procedures performed.
- Evidence quality.
- Unresolved limitations.
- Review and approval records.
- Changes since the audit.
- Status and effectiveness of corrective actions.

**Governance Control:** Define when prior audit work can be reused and when independent retesting is required.

**Prior audit report ≠ substitute for current testing automatically.** The relevance of earlier work must be assessed against the current audit objective.

### 173. Audit Evidence from AI Risk Assessments

AI risk assessments may provide evidence of the organization's risk-management process, assumptions, risk criteria, and treatment decisions.

The auditor should determine whether the assessment is appropriate to the AI system and its context, whether it is maintained as circumstances change, and whether it connects to actual decisions and controls.

Testing may examine:

- Defined risk criteria and assumptions.
- Identified AI systems and use cases.
- Relevant stakeholders and lifecycle stages.
- Potential impacts and affected parties.
- Risk evaluation and prioritization.
- Treatment decisions and control mapping.
- Ownership and approval.
- Reassessment triggers.
- Residual-risk decisions.
- Links to monitoring and incident records.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected risks from identification through treatment, implementation evidence, monitoring, and reassessment.

**Risk register entry ≠ risk effectively managed.** A documented assessment must be evaluated alongside the decisions and actions it produces.

### 174. Audit Evidence from AI System Documentation

AI system documentation may describe intended purpose, design, data, model behavior, limitations, validation, deployment, monitoring, and operational responsibilities.

The auditor should establish which documentation is expected under the applicable criteria and determine whether the records are accurate, current, approved where required, and consistent with actual practice.

Useful procedures include:

- Comparing documents with deployed configurations.
- Tracing model versions to release records.
- Comparing intended use with observed use.
- Reviewing limitations and assumptions.
- Checking links between risk assessments and technical controls.
- Confirming ownership and update responsibilities.
- Examining change history and approvals.

**Governance Control:** Define documentation ownership, update triggers, version control, and evidence of review.

**Documentation completeness ≠ operational conformity.** Documents should be evaluated against actual implementation and the applicable criteria.

### 175. Audit Evidence from AI Monitoring

AI monitoring records may support evaluation of whether the organization observes defined operational risks and responds to relevant changes.

Depending on the system, records may address performance, data quality, drift, access, security events, human overrides, complaints, incidents, or other defined indicators.

The auditor should examine:

- Whether monitoring objectives are defined.
- Whether indicators have suitable thresholds.
- Whether data sources are appropriate.
- Whether alerts are reviewed and assigned.
- Whether exceptions lead to proportionate action.
- Whether monitoring gaps are identified.
- Whether the evidence covers the relevant period.
- Whether monitoring logic has changed.
- Whether monitoring results inform risk reassessment.

**Implementation Practice:** Sample monitoring alerts from detection through investigation, disposition, escalation, and closure.

**Monitoring dashboard ≠ effective monitoring control.** Evidence of detection must be distinguished from evidence of review and response.

### 176. Audit Evidence from AI Incidents

Incident records may help an auditor evaluate whether the organization identifies, assesses, escalates, investigates, and learns from AI-related events under its defined processes.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Incident reports and timelines.
- Initial classification and impact assessment.
- Escalation and notification decisions.
- Containment and recovery actions.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Communications and approvals.
- Corrective actions.
- Lessons learned.
- Risk reassessment and control changes.

The auditor should distinguish an incident's technical facts from management's classification and any legal determination.

**Legal Requirement:** Where notification or reporting duties may apply, the relevant legal criteria and facts must be evaluated by competent personnel.

**Incident record ≠ complete incident assurance.** The audit should assess the process and supporting evidence, not merely confirm that a record exists.

### 177. Audit Evidence from Human Oversight

Human-oversight arrangements may include defined responsibilities, review procedures, intervention authority, escalation rules, competence requirements, and records of human decisions.

Audit procedures should evaluate whether the arrangements are appropriate to the AI system's intended purpose and applicable criteria.

Possible tests include:

- Reviewing assigned roles and authority.
- Inspecting training and competence evidence.
- Sampling human-review records.
- Confirming that intervention mechanisms are available.
- Examining override and escalation records.
- Checking whether reviewers receive relevant information.
- Evaluating whether exceptions lead to action.
- Comparing documented procedures with actual workflows.

**Implementation Practice:** Test whether human reviewers can exercise the responsibilities assigned to them, rather than relying only on role descriptions.

**Human present ≠ meaningful human oversight.** Oversight depends on authority, competence, information, opportunity to intervene, and the actual operation of the process.

### 178. Audit Evidence from AI Change Management

AI systems may change through model updates, data changes, prompt modifications, retrieval-source updates, infrastructure changes, configuration changes, or alterations to intended use.

Audit evidence should support evaluation of whether relevant changes are identified, assessed, authorized, tested, documented, and monitored under the organization's defined process.

Testing may include:

- Reviewing change classifications.
- Tracing changes to approvals.
- Inspecting risk reassessment records.
- Verifying test and validation results.
- Comparing approved versions with deployed versions.
- Examining rollback or recovery arrangements.
- Reviewing post-deployment monitoring.
- Sampling emergency changes and retrospective approvals where permitted.

**Governance Control:** Define which changes require reassessment, approval, validation, or escalation.

**Change record ≠ adequate change control.** The auditor must evaluate whether the required process was followed and whether the evidence supports its effectiveness.

### 179. Audit Evidence from Supplier Management

Supplier-management evidence may support evaluation of how the organization identifies, assesses, monitors, and manages relevant external dependencies within its AIMS.

Audit procedures may examine:

- Supplier identification and classification.
- Due diligence and risk assessment.
- Contractual control requirements.
- Responsibility allocation.
- Access and data-handling arrangements.
- Change and incident notifications.
- Assurance reports and their limitations.
- Performance monitoring.
- Remediation of supplier findings.
- Exit and continuity arrangements where relevant.

The required depth depends on the supplier's role, the organization's reliance, the risks involved, and applicable criteria.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected supplier risks to due diligence, contractual measures, operational monitoring, and reassessment.

**Supplier assessment completed ≠ supplier risk eliminated.** Residual risk and continuing oversight remain relevant.

### 180. Audit Evidence from AI Data Governance

Data-governance evidence may address data sources, ownership, permitted use, quality, lineage, access, retention, transformations, and lifecycle controls.

The auditor should establish which data-governance criteria apply to the AI system and evaluate whether the records correspond to actual data flows and use.

Procedures may include:

- Tracing selected datasets to their sources.
- Reviewing access and authorization records.
- Comparing documented lineage with actual processing.
- Examining data-quality checks.
- Reviewing transformations and version history.
- Checking relevant approvals and restrictions.
- Evaluating retention and deletion evidence.
- Inspecting exception handling.
- Confirming that changes trigger reassessment where required.

**Legal Requirement:** Data protection, intellectual property, confidentiality, and other legal requirements should be assessed separately against the facts and applicable law.

**Dataset documentation ≠ lawful or appropriate data use automatically.** The underlying rights, purpose, controls, and evidence require assessment.

### 181. Audit Evidence from AI Security Controls

AI security evidence may include identity and access records, configuration baselines, vulnerability assessments, threat models, security tests, incident logs, and change records.

The auditor should identify the security criteria within scope and determine whether the selected procedures provide adequate evidence for the specific conclusion.

Potential testing areas include:

- Access authorization and privilege management.
- Protection of training, validation, and operational data.
- Model and pipeline integrity.
- Secrets and key management.
- Environment separation.
- Dependency and supply-chain controls.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability handling.
- Incident response.
- Secure deployment and change management.

**Implementation Practice:** Connect security test results to the relevant AIMS risk, control owner, remediation record, and verification evidence.

**Security test passed ≠ complete AIMS conformity.** Security testing addresses a defined technical or procedural scope and does not automatically cover the full management system.

### 182. Audit Evidence from AI Performance and Impact Evaluation

AI performance and impact evaluations may provide evidence that the organization assesses selected outcomes, limitations, risks, or effects against defined criteria.

The auditor should establish whether the evaluation's scope, methods, assumptions, and data are appropriate to the intended conclusion.

Potential considerations include:

- Defined evaluation objectives.
- Relevant performance and impact criteria.
- Data and test-set suitability.
- Representativeness and known limitations.
- Documented assumptions.
- Evaluation results and exceptions.
- Approval and interpretation.
- Links to risk treatment.
- Reassessment after material changes.

The auditor should avoid treating a single metric as proof of overall safety, fairness, reliability, or compliance.

**Governance Control:** Require evaluation methods and limitations to be documented and reviewed according to the significance of the AI system and applicable criteria.

**Metric achieved ≠ overall AI assurance.** The conclusion is bounded by what was measured and how it was evaluated.

### 183. Audit Evidence from AI Training and Competence

Competence evidence may include role requirements, training records, experience, qualifications, assessments, authorization records, and demonstrations of capability.

An auditor should determine whether the evidence is appropriate to the person's assigned responsibilities and the applicable requirements.

Potential procedures include:

- Comparing role profiles with required competence.
- Sampling training and assessment records.
- Confirming that required training preceded assigned work.
- Examining authorization and supervision arrangements.
- Checking how competence gaps are addressed.
- Reviewing whether training remains current after material changes.
- Confirming that relevant personnel understand escalation duties.

**Implementation Practice:** Use interviews or practical demonstrations where documentary records alone cannot establish the competence needed for the audit objective.

**Training attendance ≠ demonstrated competence.** Attendance is evidence of participation, not necessarily of understanding or capability.

### 184. Audit Evidence from AI Objectives and Performance Indicators

AIMS objectives and related indicators can support evaluation of whether the organization defines intended outcomes, assigns responsibility, monitors progress, and acts on material deviations.

Audit procedures may assess:

- Whether objectives are defined and approved.

- Whether measurement methods are documented.
- Whether indicators are relevant to the objective.
- Whether data is reliable and sufficiently complete.
- Whether results are reviewed.
- Whether deviations trigger action.
- Whether objectives are revised when circumstances change.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace a sample of objectives from approval through measurement, management review, and resulting action.

**Objective documented ≠ objective achieved.** The auditor should evaluate performance evidence and the organization's response to deviations.

### 185. Audit Evidence from AIMS Scope and Boundaries

AIMS scope evidence should support an understanding of the organizational boundaries, activities, processes, and relevant AI-related responsibilities included in the management system.

The auditor should compare the documented scope with actual operations, AI-system inventory information, organizational responsibilities, and relevant external dependencies.

Potential tests include:

- Comparing the scope statement with business activities.
- Checking whether relevant AI development, provision, or use activities are represented.
- Reviewing organizational and supplier boundaries.
- Investigating systems or processes excluded from the scope.
- Evaluating whether exclusions are justified against applicable criteria.
- Checking consistency between scope, policy, objectives, and audit programme.

**Governance Control:** Require scope changes to be assessed, approved, documented, and reflected in related AIMS records.

**Scope statement ≠ complete scope assurance.** Actual organizational activities must be considered alongside the written statement.

### 186. Audit Evidence from Interested Parties and Context

Context and interested-party records may support evaluation of whether the organization has identified relevant internal and external issues and requirements for its AIMS.

The auditor should assess whether the analysis is appropriate to the organization's circumstances and whether relevant changes are considered.

The audit should distinguish between identifying an interested party, identifying its relevant requirements, and deciding which requirements apply within the AIMS.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected material requirements or context changes to their assessment, assigned owner, and relevant AIMS actions.

**Interested-party list ≠ complete requirements analysis.** The list must be evaluated for relevance and the decisions arising from it.

### 187. Audit Evidence from AI Policy and Governance

AI policy evidence should be evaluated against the applicable standard requirements, organizational commitments, and other defined audit criteria.

**Governance Control:** Maintain controlled policy records and define responsibility for communication, review, and update.

**Policy approval ≠ policy implementation.** Operational evidence is needed where the audit objective concerns actual application.

### 188. Audit Evidence from AIMS Resources

Resource evidence may include personnel, competence, infrastructure, technology, tools, information, and other resources used to establish, implement, maintain, and improve the AIMS.

**Implementation Practice:** Link material resource constraints to affected controls, risks, decisions, and corrective actions.

**Resource budget approved ≠ resources adequate.** Adequacy depends on actual availability and the demands of the processes in scope.

### 189. Audit Evidence from AIMS Communication

Communication records may support evaluation of whether relevant information is communicated to the appropriate people at suitable times through defined channels.

**Implementation Practice:** Test selected communications from the triggering event through delivery, acknowledgment where relevant, and resulting action.

**Message sent ≠ message understood.** The evidence needed depends on whether the audit objective concerns delivery, receipt, comprehension, or action.

### 190. Audit Evidence from Documented Information Control

Documented information controls may cover identification, format, review, approval, access, distribution, storage, version control, retention, and disposition.

The auditor should identify the applicable criteria and test whether the organization's arrangements are implemented and maintained as required.

Potential procedures include:

- Reviewing approval records.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace a document from creation or receipt through approval, use, revision, and retention or disposal.

**Document control system installed ≠ document control effective.** The actual process and records must support the conclusion.

### 191. Audit Evidence from Operational Control

Operational-control evidence should show how relevant AIMS processes are planned, implemented, and controlled against applicable criteria.

Depending on the process, evidence may include approved procedures, execution records, validation results, change approvals, exception logs, monitoring outputs, and recovery records.

Auditors should evaluate whether the controls address the risks and requirements in scope and whether the records reflect actual operations.

**Implementation Practice:** Select representative operational activities and trace them through the defined process, including exceptions and deviations.

**Procedure exists ≠ operational control demonstrated.** The audit should test the process in practice.

### 192. Audit Evidence from Performance Evaluation

Performance evaluation involves examining information used to determine whether relevant AIMS processes and objectives are achieving their intended results.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected performance results to the underlying data, interpretation, decision, and resulting action.

**Performance report produced ≠ performance adequately evaluated.** The relevance and use of the information matter.

### 193. Audit Evidence from Continual Improvement

Continual-improvement evidence may include improvement proposals, lessons learned, trend analysis, process changes, corrective actions, resource decisions, and evaluations of resulting outcomes.

The auditor should examine whether improvement activity is connected to relevant performance information and whether the organization evaluates the results where appropriate.

Potential procedures include:

**Improvement activity completed ≠ improvement effectiveness demonstrated.** The organization should have evidence appropriate to the intended outcome.

### 194. AIMS Audit Lessons Learned

- Unanticipated dependencies.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the lesson, supporting evidence, proposed improvement, owner, decision, and subsequent evaluation.

**Lesson recorded ≠ lesson applied.** Improvement requires an appropriate response and, where relevant, evidence of its effectiveness.

### 195. Audit Programme Adjustments After Significant Change

Material changes to the organization, AIMS scope, AI systems, suppliers, or applicable criteria may require changes to the audit programme.

An adjustment should consider whether previous assumptions remain valid and whether additional procedures are needed.

The organization may need to:

- Reassess audit priorities.
- Add a targeted audit.
- Expand the scope of planned work.
- Change sampling or evidence requirements.
- Obtain specialist competence.
- Review previously accepted evidence.
- Reassess dependencies and limitations.
- Communicate changes to relevant stakeholders.

**Governance Control:** Require significant audit-programme changes to have a documented rationale and appropriate approval.

**Programme stability ≠ programme adequacy.** A programme should remain responsive to material changes.

### 196. Audit Assurance Boundaries

An audit provides assurance only within the boundaries of its defined criteria, scope, period, procedures, evidence, and competence.

For ISO/IEC 42001, an audit may examine the AIMS as a management system without independently validating every model, every operational decision, or every legal obligation affecting the organization.

The report should avoid extrapolating beyond the work performed.

**Implementation Practice:** State significant boundaries and exclusions in the audit plan and report, and explain their effect on the conclusion.

**AIMS audit ≠ exhaustive AI system validation.** Separate technical, legal, security, privacy, and operational assessments may be needed to address questions outside the audit's scope.

### 197. Audit Assurance and Residual Uncertainty

Residual uncertainty remains when evidence is incomplete, sampling is used, controls change over time, or unobserved conditions may affect the conclusion.

The auditor should assess whether uncertainty is acceptable for the engagement's objectives and disclose material limitations.

Sources of uncertainty may include:

- Sampling risk.
- Incomplete populations.
- Measurement limitations.
- Changing AI-system behavior.
- Inaccessible third-party evidence.
- Unverified assumptions.
- Conflicting records.
- Limited observation periods.
- Inherent limitations in control design.

**Recommended Practice:** Explain material uncertainty in terms that help management understand what was tested, what remains unknown, and what additional work may be warranted.

**Assurance conclusion ≠ certainty.** The strength of assurance depends on the scope and quality of the work and evidence.

### 198. Audit Assurance and Risk Acceptance

Audit assurance informs management decisions but does not transfer accountability for risk acceptance from authorized decision-makers to the auditor.

The auditor's role is to evaluate the evidence against criteria, report findings, and communicate limitations. Management remains responsible for decisions within its authority, including remediation priorities and residual-risk treatment.

Where a decision conflicts with a mandatory requirement, internal acceptance cannot make the requirement disappear.

**Governance Control:** Define decision authority and escalation arrangements for material risks identified through audit.

**Audit recommendation ≠ management decision.** The report should distinguish the auditor's conclusion from management's response and approved action.

### 199. Assurance from Multiple Audit Periods

Comparing audit results across periods can reveal trends, but comparisons are meaningful only when the underlying scope, criteria, methods, and populations are sufficiently comparable.

When comparisons are made, the organization should consider:

- Changes in AIMS scope.
- New or retired AI systems.
- Changes in audit criteria.
- Differences in sampling and testing.
- Changes in evidence sources.
- Changes in severity classification.
- Revised audit methodology.
- Changes in reporting thresholds.

**Implementation Practice:** Document material differences before interpreting trends in findings, coverage, or corrective-action performance.

**Lower findings over time ≠ improved conformity automatically.** Differences in scope or detection capability may explain the trend.

### 200. AIMS Audit Assurance Map

An assurance map identifies which risks, controls, or governance objectives are covered by internal audit, management monitoring, compliance reviews, security assessments, supplier assurance, and other assurance activities.

Its purpose is to identify gaps, overlaps, dependencies, and areas where management may be relying on evidence that does not fully address its assurance needs.

An assurance map may include:

- AIMS objectives and significant risks.
- Relevant controls and processes.
- Assurance provider and role.
- Scope and period of work.
- Criteria and methods.
- Evidence and limitations.
- Reporting line.
- Findings and action status.
- Planned future assurance.

**Governance Control:** Assign responsibility for maintaining the map and reviewing it when the AIMS, assurance environment, or significant risks change.

**Assurance map complete ≠ assurance adequate.** The quality and relevance of each assurance activity still require evaluation.

### 201. Coordination with Compliance Functions

Compliance functions may evaluate legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal obligations relevant to AI systems and the AIMS. Internal audit may independently evaluate selected governance and control arrangements.

Coordination can reduce duplication and improve coverage, but it should not compromise audit independence or confuse different functions' responsibilities.

The organization should define:

- Which function maintains the requirements inventory.
- Which function interprets specialized obligations.
- How compliance assessments inform audit planning.
- How potential violations are escalated.
- How evidence is shared securely.
- How conflicting conclusions are resolved.
- Which function owns corrective actions.

**Implementation Practice:** Use a responsibility matrix that distinguishes compliance advice, control ownership, operational execution, and independent audit evaluation.

**Compliance monitoring ≠ independent internal audit automatically.** The purpose, objectivity, criteria, and reporting arrangements differ.

### 202. Coordination with Security and Privacy Assurance

AI security and privacy assessments can provide relevant inputs to an ISO/IEC 42001 audit when they address controls or risks within the defined AIMS scope.

However, their results should be interpreted according to their own scope, criteria, period, and methods.

For example, a penetration test may support evaluation of selected technical security controls but does not establish that the organization has adequately defined AI governance roles, management review, or continual-improvement processes.

Similarly, a privacy assessment may address specific data-processing risks without establishing conformity across the entire AIMS.

**Recommended Practice:** Map assurance results to the precise AIMS criteria they support and identify uncovered requirements.

**Security assurance + privacy assurance ≠ complete AIMS assurance automatically.**

### 203. Coordination with Certification Audits

An organization seeking ISO/IEC 42001 certification may use internal audit results to prepare for external certification activities. Internal audit should still evaluate the AIMS against its approved criteria and should not be reduced to a checklist designed solely to anticipate external auditor questions.

Internal audit should examine the system's actual implementation, effectiveness, and evidence within its defined scope.

**Implementation Practice:** Track certification-related observations and findings alongside internal audit results while preserving the origin, criteria, status, and owner of each item.

**Certification preparation ≠ independent internal audit.** The audit programme should provide useful assurance about the AIMS, not merely prepare documentation for an external event.

### 204. Audit of AI Governance Responsibilities

Governance responsibility records should establish who is accountable for relevant decisions, who performs controls, who approves exceptions, and who receives escalation.

The auditor should assess whether the assigned responsibilities are clear, appropriate to the organization's arrangements, and reflected in actual decision-making.

Potential procedures include:

- Reviewing governance charters and role descriptions.
- Comparing responsibility matrices with process records.
- Sampling approvals and escalations.
- Interviewing role holders.
- Checking delegated authority.
- Examining unresolved ownership conflicts.
- Tracing material decisions to authorized personnel.

**Governance Control:** Define decision rights and escalation routes for material AIMS matters.

**Responsibility assigned on paper ≠ responsibility exercised in practice.** Operational records should support the conclusion.

### 205. Audit of AI Risk-Treatment Decisions

Risk-treatment decisions should be assessed against the organization's defined risk criteria, applicable requirements, assigned authority, and documented process.

The auditor should evaluate whether the decision is supported by the risk assessment, whether selected controls are implemented as intended, and whether residual risk is handled appropriately.

Potential procedures include:

- Tracing selected risks to treatment plans.
- Checking the rationale for selected controls.
- Verifying assigned owners and target dates.
- Inspecting implementation evidence.
- Reviewing residual-risk decisions.
- Confirming required approvals.
- Testing monitoring and reassessment.
- Examining treatment actions that remain overdue.

**Implementation Practice:** Select material risks and follow them through assessment, decision, implementation, monitoring, and reassessment.

**Risk-treatment plan approved ≠ risk treated effectively.** Evidence of implementation and resulting risk management remains necessary.

### 206. Audit of AI Impact-Assessment Processes

Where the organization uses AI impact assessments, an audit may evaluate whether the process is defined, appropriately scoped, supported by evidence, and connected to governance decisions.

The precise obligations depend on the applicable criteria and context. An internal impact-assessment method should not automatically be presented as a legal requirement or as equivalent to every legally mandated assessment.

Audit procedures may examine:

- Trigger criteria for performing an assessment.
- Scope and affected stakeholders.
- Assumptions and data sources.
- Potential benefits and harms considered.
- Review and approval arrangements.
- Treatment decisions and controls.
- Reassessment triggers.
- Links to monitoring and incidents.

**Governance Control:** Define when assessments are required by the organization's framework and how separate legal assessment duties are identified.

**Internal impact assessment ≠ every statutory impact assessment.** The applicable purpose, scope, method, and legal basis must be verified.

### 207. Audit of AI System Lifecycle Governance

Lifecycle governance covers relevant controls and decisions from conception and development through deployment, operation, change, and retirement.

An audit should define the lifecycle stages and responsibilities relevant to the AI systems in scope rather than assuming every system follows an identical process.

Potential procedures include:

- Reviewing lifecycle governance and stage gates.
- Sampling approval and release records.
- Tracing risk assessments across stages.
- Examining validation and deployment decisions.
- Checking change control and monitoring.
- Reviewing incident and rollback arrangements.
- Evaluating retirement and data-disposition records where applicable.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected systems through relevant lifecycle records and compare actual practices with approved procedures.

**Lifecycle documentation ≠ lifecycle control effectiveness.** Records should demonstrate how decisions and controls operate.

### 208. Audit of AI System Retirement

Retirement or decommissioning can create governance, security, privacy, contractual, and operational risks. The applicable procedures depend on the system, its data, dependencies, and the organization's requirements.

Audit evidence may include:

- Approved retirement decisions.
- Identification of dependent services.
- Access revocation and credential handling.
- Data retention and disposal records.
- Supplier or contractual closure activities.
- Transfer of necessary business records.
- Monitoring and incident-handling changes.
- Confirmation that obsolete services are no longer used.
- Updates to inventories and governance records.

**Legal Requirement:** Retention, deletion, and other end-of-life obligations must be determined from applicable law and contracts.

**System disabled ≠ complete retirement.** Associated data, access, dependencies, and records may require separate treatment.

### 209. Audit of Exceptions and Approved Deviations

An exception is a departure from a defined process, control, policy, or criterion. Whether it is permitted, temporary, or constitutes a nonconformity depends on the applicable requirement and the authority governing the exception.

The auditor should evaluate:

- The rule from which the exception departs.
- Whether exceptions are permitted under that rule.
- Approval authority and rationale.
- Duration and scope.
- Risk assessment and compensating controls.
- Expiry and review arrangements.
- Evidence of implementation.
- Repeated or expired exceptions.
- Whether the exception affects a mandatory requirement.

**Governance Control:** Maintain an exception register with owners, rationale, approval, expiry, risk treatment, and closure evidence.

**Approved exception ≠ permitted departure from every requirement.** Internal authorization cannot override a binding external obligation.

### 210. Audit of Compensating Controls

A compensating control is an alternative measure intended to reduce risk when a preferred or expected control is unavailable, unsuitable, or temporarily ineffective.

The auditor should assess whether the compensating control addresses the relevant risk, operates within the required scope, and has sufficient supporting evidence.

Evaluation should consider:

- The risk addressed by the original control.
- The reason the original control is unavailable or ineffective.
- The design of the alternative measure.
- Coverage and operating frequency.
- Dependencies and failure modes.
- Monitoring and escalation.
- Residual risk.
- Approval and review requirements.

**Implementation Practice:** Test the compensating control directly rather than inferring effectiveness from its approval record.

**Compensating control approved ≠ risk adequately mitigated.** Its suitability must be supported by evidence and applicable criteria.

### 211. Audit of Control Dependencies

AIMS controls often depend on other processes, technologies, teams, or external providers. A control may appear effective in isolation but fail when a dependency is unavailable or unreliable.

Examples include:

- Audit logs depending on correct system time and complete event capture.
- Human oversight depending on access to relevant information.
- Monitoring depending on reliable data pipelines.
- Change approval depending on accurate inventory and version records.
- Supplier assurance depending on accurate service-scope mapping.
- Corrective action depending on ownership and resources.

**Governance Control:** Identify material control dependencies and include them in risk assessment and audit planning.

**Individual control test passed ≠ end-to-end process effective.** Material dependencies should be evaluated where they affect the conclusion.

### 212. Audit of Control Failures and Compensating Evidence

When a control fails, the auditor should determine the failure's nature, scope, duration, and effect on the audit conclusion.

Other evidence may reduce uncertainty about the underlying risk, but it should not be used to conceal or automatically erase a failure against an applicable criterion.

The auditor should consider:

- Whether the failure is isolated or systemic.
- Whether the test population is affected.
- Whether another control addresses the same risk.
- Whether the alternative control is relevant and effective.
- Whether the failed control remains a requirement.
- Whether the conclusion or finding must be revised.
- Whether additional testing is needed.

**Implementation Practice:** Document the failed control, alternative evidence considered, rationale, residual uncertainty, and final conclusion.

**Compensating evidence ≠ automatic cancellation of a nonconformity.** The underlying requirement and condition must still be evaluated.

### 213. Audit of AIMS Change Impact

Changes to the organization or its AI environment may affect previously assessed risks, controls, responsibilities, evidence sources, and audit coverage.

A change-impact review should identify affected components and determine whether additional testing is required.

Potential triggers include:

- Acquisitions or organizational restructuring.
- New AI services or use cases.
- Changes in supplier relationships.
- New data sources.
- Material model or infrastructure updates.
- Revised policies or governance responsibilities.
- Changes in applicable criteria.
- New incidents or recurring control failures.

**Governance Control:** Define a change-impact process that links material changes to risk reassessment, documentation updates, and audit-programme adjustments.

**Change approved ≠ change impact fully assessed.** Approval of the change does not replace evaluation of its consequences.

### 214. Audit of AIMS Governance Forums

Governance forums may oversee AI risk, approve material decisions, review performance, allocate resources, and escalate issues. Their authority and responsibilities vary by organizational design.

Audit procedures may assess:

- Terms of reference and decision authority.
- Membership and competence.
- Meeting cadence and required inputs.
- Decision and action records.
- Escalation arrangements.
- Conflict-of-interest handling.
- Follow-up of previous actions.
- Links between decisions and actual implementation.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected material decisions from agenda or proposal through approval, assignment, implementation, and follow-up.

**Committee established ≠ governance effective.** The evidence should show that the forum performs its assigned role.

### 215. Audit of Management-Review Actions

Management-review actions should be evaluated for clear ownership, defined outcomes, implementation evidence, and follow-up appropriate to the action.

The auditor should determine whether actions address the decisions made and whether material unresolved matters are visible to management.

Potential procedures include:

- Sampling actions from management-review records.
- Checking assignment and target dates.
- Reviewing implementation evidence.
- Examining overdue actions.
- Confirming escalation of material delays.
- Assessing whether outcomes were evaluated.
- Tracing recurring issues to subsequent review.

**Governance Control:** Maintain a controlled register of management-review actions and define how completion is verified.

**Action marked complete ≠ intended outcome achieved.** Completion status should be supported by evidence appropriate to the decision.

### 216. Audit of AIMS Management-System Integration

An AIMS may interact with existing information-security, quality, privacy, risk-management, compliance, and business-continuity processes.

Integration can reduce duplication and improve consistency, but the organization should still demonstrate that AI-specific risks and applicable requirements receive appropriate attention.

Audit procedures may examine:

- Shared policies and governance processes.
- Integrated risk and control registers.
- Responsibilities across management systems.
- Consistency of criteria and definitions.
- Shared audit and corrective-action processes.
- Handling of AI-specific risks not covered elsewhere.
- Evidence that shared controls apply to the relevant AI activities.

**Recommended Practice:** Map shared controls to the requirements and risks they actually address, including limitations and exclusions.

**Integrated management system ≠ all requirements satisfied automatically.** Integration does not eliminate the need to evaluate the applicable AI management-system criteria.

### 217. Audit of AIMS Scope Expansion

Scope expansion may occur when the organization adds new AI activities, business units, services, or responsibilities to its management system.

The auditor should assess whether the expansion is reflected consistently in the scope statement, inventory, risk assessments, governance responsibilities, controls, and audit programme.

Potential tests include:

- Comparing new activities with the approved scope.
- Checking whether relevant risks have been assessed.
- Reviewing ownership and resource arrangements.
- Confirming applicable controls are identified.
- Evaluating documentation and operational readiness.
- Checking whether the audit programme addresses the expanded scope.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace a recently added AI activity from the scope decision through its associated governance and control records.

**Scope expanded on paper ≠ expanded scope implemented.** Actual processes and responsibilities should support the claim.

### 218. Audit of AIMS Scope Reduction

Scope reduction may be appropriate when organizational activities change, but it should not be used to obscure unresolved weaknesses or exclude relevant activities without a defensible basis.

The auditor should examine the reason for the reduction, affected responsibilities, applicable criteria, and consistency with actual operations.

Potential concerns include:

- Continued use of systems that were removed from the inventory.
- Undocumented transfer of responsibilities.
- Unresolved risks that remain within the organization's activities.
- Misalignment between the scope statement and operational reality.
- External claims that overstate the scope of the management system.

**Governance Control:** Require documented rationale and authorized approval for material scope changes.

**Scope reduction approved ≠ exclusions justified.** The decision must be assessed against actual activities and applicable criteria.

### 219. Audit of Assurance Statements and Claims

Organizations may make statements about AIMS conformity, certification, AI governance maturity, or assurance results. Such statements should accurately reflect the underlying evidence and defined scope.

The auditor may evaluate whether claims:

- Identify the relevant organization and systems.
- Accurately describe the audit or certification status.
- State material scope boundaries.
- Avoid implying universal coverage.
- Distinguish internal assessment from independent certification.
- Reflect the applicable period and criteria.
- Avoid unsupported statements about legal compliance or AI safety.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected public or internal assurance claims to their source reports, approved wording, scope, and supporting evidence.

**Certification claim ≠ universal compliance claim.** The statement should not exceed what the underlying certification or audit actually establishes.

### 220. Audit of AI Governance Maturity Assessments

Maturity assessments may help organizations evaluate the development and consistency of their governance capabilities. Their scales are often internally defined or based on a selected external model.

The auditor should examine whether the assessment criteria, scoring rules, evidence expectations, and review procedures are documented and applied consistently.

Potential tests include:

- Reviewing the maturity model and definitions.
- Checking evidence supporting selected scores.
- Comparing similar processes for consistency.
- Examining assessor competence and independence.
- Reviewing treatment of missing evidence.
- Checking approval and challenge processes.
- Tracing improvement actions to identified gaps.

**Recommended Practice:** Treat maturity ratings as a structured assessment result, not as a substitute for evaluating conformity with specific requirements.

**High maturity score ≠ ISO conformity or legal compliance automatically.** The conclusions are different and must be supported separately.

### 221. Audit of AI Governance Benchmarking

Benchmarking compares selected practices or results across organizations, business units, time periods, or defined reference models.

An auditor should evaluate whether the benchmark is appropriate, whether comparisons use compatible definitions, and whether the data is sufficiently reliable.

Potential limitations include:

- Different AIMS scopes.
- Different AI-system populations.
- Different legal or operating contexts.
- Inconsistent severity classifications.
- Different measurement methods.
- Incomplete or self-reported data.
- Different maturity models.
- Different reporting periods.

**Implementation Practice:** Document the comparison basis, normalization assumptions, exclusions, and limitations before drawing conclusions.

**Benchmark position ≠ conformity evidence.** Being above an industry average does not demonstrate that an applicable requirement has been fulfilled.

### 222. Audit of AI Assurance Providers

External assurance providers may offer assessments, certifications, testing, or specialized reviews relevant to an organization's AIMS.

The organization should evaluate whether the provider's competence, independence, methodology, and scope are appropriate to the assurance purpose.

Audit considerations may include:

- Defined engagement scope and criteria.
- Provider competence and qualifications.
- Independence and conflicts of interest.
- Evidence access and testing methodology.
- Reporting limitations.
- Treatment of exceptions.
- Applicability to the organization's systems.
- Recency and continuing relevance.
- Restrictions on use or distribution.

**Governance Control:** Define criteria for selecting and relying on external assurance providers.

**Provider reputation ≠ evidence of engagement suitability.** Reliance should be based on the specific assurance activity and its limitations.

### 223. Audit of Assurance Reliance

Assurance reliance occurs when an organization uses another party's assessment or report to support its own decisions or conclusions.

Before relying on external assurance, the auditor should evaluate whether the report addresses the relevant objective and whether any gaps require additional procedures.

Relevant questions include:

- Does the report cover the correct service or entity?
- Is the reporting period appropriate?
- Are the relevant controls included?
- Are material exceptions disclosed?
- Is the provider's method sufficiently clear?
- Are the controls operated by the supplier or customer?
- Are complementary controls required?
- Has the service changed since the report?
- Are there restrictions on reliance or distribution?

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain a reliance assessment showing the report used, intended purpose, covered controls, limitations, and supplementary evidence needed.

**Report received ≠ assurance reliance justified.** The organization must evaluate the suitability of the report for its specific purpose.

### 224. Audit of Evidence Gaps

An evidence gap occurs when available information does not adequately support a planned test or conclusion.

The auditor should distinguish among:

- A control that is not implemented.
- A control that may operate but lacks sufficient records.
- Records that exist but cannot be authenticated.
- Evidence that does not cover the required period.
- A test that cannot be completed.
- A requirement whose applicability remains unclear.

Each condition may require a different response.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the missing evidence, the reason it is needed, the attempts made to obtain it, alternative procedures considered, and the effect on the conclusion.

**Evidence gap ≠ proof of control failure automatically.** However, inability to demonstrate conformity may itself be significant when evidence or documented information is required by the applicable criterion.

### 225. Audit of Missing Documented Information

Missing documented information should be evaluated against the applicable requirement and the organization's documented information arrangements.

The auditor should determine whether the missing record is required, whether equivalent evidence exists, whether the underlying activity occurred, and whether the absence prevents a reliable conclusion.

For example, missing approval documentation may indicate a documentation failure, an approval failure, or both. Additional evidence is needed to distinguish these possibilities.

**Governance Control:** Define how missing records are investigated, escalated, corrected where possible, and evaluated against applicable criteria.

**Missing record ≠ underlying activity definitely never occurred.** Conversely, an unsupported assertion that the activity occurred does not replace evidence required by the criterion.

### 226. Audit of Retrospective Documentation

Retrospective documentation is created after the event it describes. It may be useful for explaining facts or recording a reconstruction, but it should not be represented as a contemporaneous record.

The auditor should assess:

- Why the original record is missing.
- Who prepared the retrospective record.
- Which source evidence supports it.
- Whether the reconstruction is complete and reliable.
- Whether the timing is clearly disclosed.
- Whether the applicable criterion requires a contemporaneous record.
- Whether the absence indicates a control weakness.

**Implementation Practice:** Label retrospective records clearly and preserve the distinction between original evidence and later explanation.

**Retrospective explanation ≠ original contemporaneous evidence.** Its evidential value depends on the criterion, corroboration, and transparency.

### 227. Audit of Management Representations

Management representations can clarify responsibilities, explain processes, and provide context. They should be evaluated alongside other available evidence when the conclusion requires objective support.

The auditor should consider:

- The person's knowledge and authority.
- Consistency with other evidence.
- Specificity of the representation.
- Potential conflicts or incentives.
- Whether corroboration is available.
- Whether the representation concerns a fact, intention, or opinion.

**Implementation Practice:** Record material representations and obtain corroboration where appropriate to the audit objective and risk.

**Management representation ≠ sufficient evidence automatically.** A statement may be useful but may not independently establish control implementation or effectiveness.

### 228. Audit of Evidence Authenticity

Authenticity concerns whether evidence is what it is represented to be and originates from the stated source.

Procedures may include:

- Checking document metadata and version history.
- Confirming records with the originating system.
- Reviewing digital signatures or approval logs where relevant.
- Comparing records against independent sources.
- Examining access and modification history.
- Validating export or extraction procedures.
- Investigating inconsistencies or signs of alteration.

The procedure should be proportionate to the evidence's significance and the risk of manipulation.

**Implementation Practice:** Document the basis for treating material evidence as authentic.

**Authentic record ≠ accurate content automatically.** A genuine record can contain errors or reflect an incorrect process.

### 229. Audit of Evidence Completeness

Completeness concerns whether the evidence or population includes the relevant records needed for the audit objective.

An incomplete population can undermine both automated testing and manual sampling.

Auditors may:

- Reconcile totals to an independent source.
- Compare source-system counts with exported records.
- Check date ranges and filter conditions.
- Examine missing identifiers or sequence gaps.
- Validate inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Confirm that the population covers the relevant systems and organizational units.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the population definition, source, extraction method, exclusions, reconciliation procedures, and known limitations.

**Large dataset ≠ complete population.** Completeness must be demonstrated for the intended test.

### 230. Audit of Evidence Reliability

Reliability concerns the degree to which evidence can be trusted for the intended audit purpose.

Reliability may be affected by:

- Source-system integrity.
- Manual intervention.
- Data transformation.
- Incomplete logging.
- Unclear ownership.
- Uncontrolled document versions.
- Conflicts of interest.
- Limited independence.
- Unverified extraction methods.
- Errors in automated processing.

Evidence reliability should be evaluated in context. No single evidence type is automatically reliable for every audit question.

**Recommended Practice:** Corroborate material conclusions using different evidence sources or procedures where appropriate.

**Independent sources agree ≠ truth guaranteed.** Corroboration strengthens a conclusion but does not remove the need to evaluate the sources and their dependencies.

### 231. Audit of Evidence Relevance

Evidence is relevant when it helps answer the specific audit question or evaluate the selected criterion.

A record may be accurate and authentic yet irrelevant to the control or period being tested.

Auditors should consider:

- The criterion being evaluated.
- The process or system in scope.
- The period covered.
- The control objective.
- The population represented.
- The relationship between the evidence and the conclusion.

**Implementation Practice:** Document why material evidence supports the test result rather than merely listing the records collected.

**Relevant document ≠ sufficient evidence.** Relevance is necessary, but sufficiency and reliability must also be considered.

### 232. Audit of Evidence Timeliness

Evidence timeliness concerns whether the record or observation covers the period and conditions relevant to the audit objective.

An old approval, test result, or supplier report may be insufficient where the system, control, or risk has changed materially.

The auditor should consider:

- The period under review.
- The date the evidence was created.
- Subsequent system changes.
- Changes in the control environment.
- The frequency of the control.
- Whether the evidence remains valid.
- Whether additional current testing is needed.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the evidence period and assess whether subsequent events undermine the original conclusion.

**Recent evidence ≠ reliable evidence automatically.** Timeliness and reliability are separate dimensions.

### 233. Audit of Evidence Triangulation

Evidence triangulation uses different procedures or sources to assess whether a conclusion is consistently supported.

For example, a control's implementation may be evaluated using a policy, configuration evidence, operational records, and an interview with the responsible owner.

Triangulation should be purposeful. Collecting several records that all derive from the same unverified source may provide less independent corroboration than expected.

**Implementation Practice:** Identify the conclusion being tested, the sources used, their dependencies, and any remaining contradictions.

**Multiple sources ≠ independent corroboration automatically.** Independence and common-source limitations should be considered.

### 234. Audit of Evidence Contradictions After Reporting

New evidence may emerge after an audit report has been issued. The organization should have a process to evaluate whether the information materially affects the prior conclusion or requires further action.

The response should consider:

- The nature and credibility of the new evidence.
- The affected criterion and scope.
- Whether the original audit procedure was reasonable.
- Whether the information was available at the time.
- Whether the conclusion or finding requires correction.
- Whether management or other stakeholders need notification.
- Whether further audit work is necessary.

**Governance Control:** Define how material post-report evidence is assessed, documented, and escalated.

**Report issued ≠ conclusion immune to revision.** Material new evidence may justify reassessment under the organization's audit-governance process.

### 235. Audit Finding Communication

Findings should be communicated to relevant management and control owners in a way that supports accurate understanding, accountability, and timely remediation.

Communication should distinguish the observed condition from its potential effect and from the proposed action.

The auditor should:

- Confirm factual accuracy.
- Identify the applicable criterion.
- Explain the supporting evidence.
- Describe material risk implications.
- State the finding's classification and rationale.
- Identify required follow-up.
- Record management's response.
- Escalate unresolved disagreements appropriately.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain traceability between preliminary discussion, validated finding, final report, and corrective-action record.

**Finding communicated ≠ finding understood or resolved.** Follow-up should verify the agreed response and resulting action.

### 236. Audit Finding Acceptance and Dispute

A management owner may dispute a finding because of factual disagreement, different interpretation of a criterion, incomplete evidence, or disagreement about severity.

The organization should have a defined process for resolving disputes without suppressing valid findings or ignoring credible new evidence.

The process should identify:

- The specific disputed statement or conclusion.
- The relevant criterion.
- Evidence supporting each position.
- The authority for final interpretation or escalation.
- Any additional procedures required.
- The final decision and rationale.

**Governance Control:** Ensure that material disputes are resolved through documented evidence-based review.

**Finding acceptance by management ≠ finding validity.** Validity depends on the applicable criteria and supporting evidence.

### 237. Audit Finding Risk Communication

Risk communication should explain why a finding matters without overstating what the evidence establishes.

A useful risk statement identifies the relevant condition, plausible consequences, affected objectives, and uncertainty.

Auditors should distinguish actual observed effects from potential effects and avoid treating every possible scenario as a confirmed outcome.

**Implementation Practice:** Link risk statements to evidence and clearly label assumptions or unconfirmed consequences.

**Potential impact ≠ realized harm.** The audit should communicate both the significance of plausible outcomes and the facts actually established.

### 238. Audit Finding Aggregation

Finding aggregation groups related observations to identify common themes, systemic weaknesses, or broader exposure.

Aggregation can improve management understanding, but it should preserve traceability to the original evidence and applicable criteria.

The auditor should evaluate:

- Whether the findings share a common criterion or cause.
- Whether they affect the same control environment.
- Whether the affected population is known.
- Whether severity changes when findings are considered together.
- Whether aggregation obscures meaningful differences.
- Whether each original finding remains traceable.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain both individual finding records and the rationale for any consolidated reporting.

**Aggregated finding ≠ proven systemic cause automatically.** A systemic conclusion requires evidence of a common underlying weakness.

### 239. Audit Finding Closure Criteria

Closure criteria should be defined clearly enough to support consistent decisions across auditors and reporting periods.

Criteria may include:

- Correction completed.
- Root cause adequately addressed.
- Corrective action implemented.
- Required evidence retained.
- Effectiveness testing passed, where appropriate.
- Residual risk assessed and handled.
- Related records updated.
- Required approvals obtained.

The criteria should be proportionate to the nature of the finding and consistent with applicable requirements.

**Governance Control:** Define closure authority and require a recorded rationale for closure, rejection, or reopening.

**Closure criteria met on paper ≠ evidence of effectiveness.** The closure decision should be supported by the actual results of the required verification.

### 240. Core Continuous AIMS Audit Principle

The core principle of continuous ISO/IEC 42001 auditing is to maintain a traceable, risk-based, evidence-driven evaluation of the AIMS while adapting audit coverage to material changes and preserving clear boundaries between standards conformity, legal compliance, internal governance, and assurance conclusions.

An effective continuous-audit approach connects audit criteria to risks and controls, validates automated and manual procedures, evaluates evidence quality, documents findings, verifies corrective actions, and communicates limitations transparently.

**Internal taxonomy ID:** `AI-42001-AUD-240`.

The identifier is an internal knowledge-base reference. It is not an ISO clause, legal provision, regulatory article, certification requirement, or independent statement of a mandatory obligation.

**Core distinctions:**

- Continuous monitoring ≠ continuous auditing.
- Automated execution ≠ valid audit testing.
- Evidence existence ≠ evidence sufficiency.
- Evidence authenticity ≠ accuracy.
- Management representation ≠ sufficient evidence automatically.
- Control implementation ≠ operating effectiveness.
- Finding classification ≠ legal reportability determination.
- Correction ≠ corrective action.
- Corrective-action implementation ≠ effectiveness.
- Risk acceptance ≠ requirement waiver.
- Supplier certification ≠ complete customer AIMS assurance.
- Internal maturity score ≠ ISO conformity automatically.
- AIMS audit ≠ exhaustive AI system validation.

### 301. Enterprise ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Governance

Enterprise AIMS audit governance establishes how the organization directs, coordinates, oversees, and improves its ISO/IEC 42001 audit activities across business units, AI systems, suppliers, and organizational boundaries.

The objective is to ensure that audit activities remain aligned with the organization's AIMS scope, risk profile, governance responsibilities, and assurance needs.

An enterprise audit-governance model should define:

- Audit authority, objectives, and reporting arrangements.
- Responsibilities for audit planning and execution.
- Independence and conflict-of-interest safeguards.
- Criteria for prioritizing audit coverage.
- Competence requirements for audit personnel.
- Evidence-management and retention arrangements.
- Finding classification and escalation rules.
- Corrective-action monitoring and closure authority.
- Coordination with other assurance providers.
- Reporting to appropriate management and governance bodies.

**Governance Control:** Maintain an approved audit-governance framework that identifies decision rights, responsibilities, escalation routes, and oversight arrangements.

**Implementation Practice:** Review the framework when the organization changes its AIMS scope, operating model, AI portfolio, or assurance requirements.

**Enterprise audit governance ≠ centralized execution of every audit.** Organizations may distribute audit execution while maintaining consistent oversight, methodology, and reporting.

### 302. Enterprise AIMS Audit Charter

An audit charter establishes the authority, purpose, responsibilities, and operating boundaries of the audit function or programme.

For an AIMS audit programme, the charter should explain how audit work relates to the management system, who authorizes the programme, and how findings are reported and escalated.

Relevant charter provisions may include:

- Audit objectives and authority.
- Access to relevant records, personnel, and systems, subject to applicable restrictions.
- Organizational independence and objectivity safeguards.

- Responsibilities of process and control owners.

**Governance Control:** Approve the charter through the organization's designated authority and maintain a controlled version.

**Recommended Practice:** Reassess the charter when significant changes affect audit authority, independence, scope, or access.

### 304. Enterprise AIMS Audit Universe

The audit universe is the structured collection of auditable activities, processes, systems, controls, and organizational areas considered by the audit programme.

An enterprise AIMS audit universe may include:

- AI policies, objectives, and documented information.

- Supplier and third-party governance.

**Implementation Practice:** Reconcile the audit universe with the AI inventory, process inventory, risk register, supplier inventory, and AIMS scope statement.

### 305. Enterprise Audit Risk Assessment

Enterprise audit risk assessment prioritizes audit work according to the likelihood that significant weaknesses, nonconformities, or other relevant issues may remain undetected or insufficiently addressed.

The assessment should consider the organization's risks and the limitations of available assurance.

Potential factors include:

- Significance of AI use cases.
- Potential impact on people, operations, and business objectives.
- Complexity of AI development and deployment.
- Previous findings and incidents.
- Control maturity and known weaknesses.
- Supplier dependencies.
- Material changes in systems or processes.
- Quality and availability of evidence.
- Reliance on other assurance providers.
- Changes to applicable audit criteria.

The resulting priorities should inform audit scheduling, specialist involvement, testing depth, and resource allocation.

**Governance Control:** Document the risk factors, scoring method where used, decision authority, and rationale for prioritization.

**Recommended Practice:** Reassess priorities after significant incidents, material system changes, or evidence of recurring control failure.

**Risk-based audit priority ≠ regulatory risk classification automatically.** An internal audit-priority score is a planning mechanism, not a legal determination.

### 306. Enterprise AIMS Audit Coverage Model

An enterprise audit coverage model connects the audit universe and risk assessment to actual audit work performed over a defined period.

Coverage should consider whether material risks and relevant criteria receive sufficient attention, not merely whether an audit has been scheduled.

A coverage model may distinguish:

- Audited areas with completed and reviewed work.
- Areas covered by targeted testing.
- Areas covered through reliance on other assurance.
- Areas scheduled but not yet audited.
- Areas with incomplete or limited evidence.
- Areas excluded from the programme.
- Areas requiring specialist review.
- Areas with overdue follow-up work.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain a coverage matrix connecting risks, controls, audit criteria, procedures, reporting periods, and conclusions.

Coverage reports should identify their measurement basis. A count of completed audits cannot establish that the most significant risks have been adequately addressed.

**Audit completed ≠ risk fully covered.** The adequacy of coverage depends on the objective, scope, criteria, and evidence.

### 307. Enterprise Audit Resource Allocation

Audit resource allocation determines how personnel, specialist skills, time, tools, and budget are assigned to the AIMS audit programme.

The allocation should reflect risk, complexity, required competence, audit deadlines, and the evidence needed to support reliable conclusions.

Relevant considerations include:

- Size and complexity of the AIMS.
- Number and diversity of AI systems.
- Technical and regulatory specialization.
- Geographic and organizational distribution.
- Availability of internal and external expertise.
- Supplier and system-access dependencies.
- Required independence safeguards.
- Expected evidence volume.
- Corrective-action follow-up workload.
- Contingency capacity for material events.

**Governance Control:** Approve resource plans and define escalation when resource constraints threaten audit quality or coverage.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the effect of resource limitations on the audit plan and obtain appropriate decisions about reprioritization or additional support.

**Budget allocated ≠ audit capability available.** Effective capacity depends on competence, access, timing, and the ability to perform the required work.

### 308. Enterprise Auditor Competence Framework

An auditor competence framework defines the knowledge, skills, experience, and professional behaviors required for different AIMS audit assignments.

The framework should reflect the audit objectives rather than assuming that one qualification proves competence for every AI-related audit.

Competence domains may include:

- Management-system auditing.
- ISO/IEC 42001 requirements and interpretation.
- AI governance and risk management.
- AI lifecycle and system architecture.
- Information security and privacy.
- Data governance and documentation.
- Supplier and third-party risk.
- Evidence evaluation and sampling.
- Interviewing and professional judgment.
- Audit reporting and corrective-action evaluation.
- Relevant legal or contractual criteria.

Specialist support should be obtained where the assigned team lacks the expertise necessary to evaluate material issues.

**Governance Control:** Define competence expectations by audit role and retain evidence supporting assignment decisions.

**Implementation Practice:** Use training, supervised assignments, peer review, and continuing professional development to address competence gaps.

**Qualification held ≠ competence for every audit.** Competence must be appropriate to the scope and procedures performed.

### 309. Enterprise Audit Independence and Objectivity

Independence and objectivity help ensure that audit conclusions are based on evidence and criteria rather than operational pressure, personal interests, or responsibility for the controls being examined.

Potential threats include:

- Auditing controls designed or operated by the auditor.
- Financial or personal interests in the outcome.
- Management pressure to change findings.
- Restricted access to relevant evidence.
- Dependence on the control owner for the audit conclusion.
- Conflicts involving supplier or consulting relationships.
- Inappropriate incentives linked to audit results.

Safeguards may include reassignment, independent review, disclosure of conflicts, external specialist involvement, and escalation to an appropriate governance authority.

**Governance Control:** Establish a documented process for identifying, assessing, and mitigating threats to audit objectivity.

**Implementation Practice:** Record material conflicts and the safeguards applied before relying on the resulting audit work.

**Independence declaration ≠ independence demonstrated.** Practical safeguards and actual conduct matter.

### 310. Enterprise Audit Quality Assurance

Audit quality assurance evaluates whether audit work is planned, performed, reviewed, documented, and reported consistently with the approved methodology and applicable criteria.

A quality-assurance process may assess:

- Audit planning and scope.
- Criteria selection and interpretation.
- Sampling rationale.
- Evidence quality.
- Workpaper traceability.
- Finding consistency.
- Corrective-action verification.
- Report accuracy.
- Reviewer competence.
- Compliance with evidence-handling rules.
- Treatment of limitations and disagreements.

Quality assurance should identify both engagement-level deficiencies and systemic weaknesses in the audit methodology.

**Governance Control:** Define quality-review responsibilities, review thresholds, documentation expectations, and remediation for quality deficiencies.

**Implementation Practice:** Use periodic file reviews, thematic reviews, and lessons-learned analysis to improve consistency.

**Quality review performed ≠ audit quality assured automatically.** The review must be substantive and proportionate to the engagement.

### 311. Enterprise Audit Methodology Governance

Audit methodology governance maintains consistent procedures for planning, testing, evaluating evidence, classifying findings, and reporting results.

The methodology should be sufficiently standardized to support comparability while allowing auditors to adapt procedures to the specific risks and scope.

Methodology components may include:

- Sampling approaches.
- Finding definitions.
- Risk and severity models.
- Workpaper standards.
- Corrective-action verification.
- Reporting requirements.

**Governance Control:** Assign an owner for the audit methodology and require controlled approval of material changes.

**Implementation Practice:** Validate revised templates and procedures before broad deployment and communicate material changes to audit personnel.

**Standardized methodology ≠ identical procedures for every audit.** Procedures must remain appropriate to the specific engagement.

### 312. Enterprise Requirement-to-Control Traceability

Requirement-to-control traceability connects the criteria used in an audit to the controls and evidence intended to demonstrate conformity.

For an AIMS audit, the traceability model should distinguish requirements arising from ISO/IEC 42001 from legal, regulatory, contractual, policy, and other organizational criteria.

A practical traceability chain is:

1. Identify the applicable criterion.
2. Determine its scope and applicability.
3. Identify the relevant risk or objective.
4. Map the criterion to the relevant control or process.
5. Identify the control owner.
6. Define the evidence needed for evaluation.
7. Test implementation and operation as appropriate.
8. Record the result and any limitation.
9. Link findings and corrective actions.
10. Retain the basis for the conclusion.

**Governance Control:** Maintain controlled mappings and document unresolved interpretation or applicability questions.

**Implementation Practice:** Review mappings after changes to the standard, applicable law, organizational scope, or control environment.

**Requirement-to-control mapping ≠ conformity evidence.** Traceability supports testing but does not prove that a control is implemented or effective.

### 313. Enterprise Audit Criteria Governance

Audit criteria define the requirements or benchmarks against which evidence is evaluated.

An enterprise audit may use several types of criteria, but their authority and consequences must remain distinguishable.

| Criterion type                     | Typical basis                                           | Audit consideration                                                     |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Standard requirement               | Applicable edition of ISO/IEC 42001                     | Verify the exact requirement and its applicability                      |
| Legal requirement                  | Applicable legislation and authoritative interpretation | Verify jurisdiction, scope, and facts                                   |
| Regulatory requirement or decision | Applicable regulatory instrument or decision            | Confirm authority, scope, and binding effect                            |
| Contractual requirement            | Agreement or binding contractual term                   | Verify parties, scope, and relevant provisions                          |
| Internal policy                    | Approved organizational policy                          | Verify approval, applicability, and consistency with higher obligations |
| Governance control                 | Organization-defined control arrangement                | Evaluate against its stated objective and approved criteria             |
| Recommended practice               | Guidance or voluntarily adopted practice                | Do not automatically classify deviation as nonconformity                |

**Governance Control:** Require audit criteria to be identified, sourced, and approved or reviewed through the organization's defined process.

**Implementation Practice:** Record criterion versions, relevant sections, applicability decisions, and interpretation assumptions.

**Criterion selected ≠ criterion legally binding.** Binding effect depends on the source and applicable circumstances.

### 314. ISO/IEC 42001 Version and Amendment Management

Audit programmes must identify the applicable edition and relevant amendments or corrigenda when determining audit criteria.

The organization should establish how it:

- Monitors relevant standard updates.
- Determines the applicable edition.
- Evaluates the effect of changes.
- Updates requirement mappings.
- Revises audit procedures and training.
- Communicates material changes.
- Handles transition arrangements where applicable.
- Retains the basis for historical audit conclusions.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain a controlled source register for standards and related audit criteria.

The auditor should verify the applicable text rather than relying exclusively on an old checklist, secondary summary, or internal mapping.

**Standard edition change ≠ automatic conclusion that existing controls are nonconforming.** The effect depends on the applicable text, transition arrangements, and actual implementation.

### 315. Enterprise Legal and Regulatory Criteria Integration

An ISO/IEC 42001 audit may need to consider legal or regulatory criteria where they are included in the audit scope, relevant to the AIMS, or necessary to understand the organization's governance arrangements.

**Implementation Practice:** Where an audit finding may indicate a legal issue, involve appropriately qualified legal or compliance personnel and preserve the distinction between an audit observation and a formal legal determination.

**ISO/IEC 42001 conformity ≠ legal compliance automatically.** A management-system audit and a legal compliance assessment have related but distinct objectives.

### 318. Enterprise Audit Integration with AI Regulation

### 319. Enterprise Audit of AI Conformity-Assessment Readiness

### 321. Enterprise AI Audit Automation Strategy

An enterprise AI audit automation strategy identifies which audit activities can be automated safely and reliably, which require human judgment, and which require specialist procedures.

Potential automation candidates include:

- Evidence inventory and classification.
- Detection of missing or overdue records.
- Comparison of selected documents.

**Governance Control:** Approve automation use cases based on defined objectives, risk, validation, and accountability.

**Implementation Practice:** Pilot significant automation, compare outputs with independent procedures, and document the conditions under which results may be relied upon.

### 322. Enterprise Audit Automation Risk Register

Audit automation introduces risks that should be evaluated as part of tool governance.

Potential risks include:

- Incorrect mapping of criteria to controls.
- Incomplete source data.
- Undetected extraction failures.
- Incorrect rule logic.
- False-positive and false-negative results.
- Uncontrolled tool updates.
- Unauthorized evidence disclosure.
- Excessive reliance on generated summaries.
- Loss of traceability.
- Inadequate human review.
- Unclear accountability for automated conclusions.

**Governance Control:** Maintain a risk register for material audit automation use cases and assign accountable owners.

**Implementation Practice:** Record each use case's purpose, inputs, limitations, validation method, approval status, monitoring approach, and retirement conditions.

**Automated audit output ≠ independently validated result.** Reliability must be established for the intended use.

### 323. Governance of Generative AI Used in Auditing

Generative AI may assist auditors with document summarization, classification, drafting, and evidence navigation. Its outputs can also introduce unsupported statements, omissions, or inaccurate interpretations.

A controlled use policy should address:

- Approved tools and environments.
- Permitted and prohibited uses.
- Confidentiality and data protection.
- Source traceability.
- Human validation requirements.
- Retention of relevant processing records.
- Handling of hallucinations and uncertainty.
- Conflicts of interest and reviewer accountability.
- Restrictions on autonomous finding classification or closure.

**Implementation Practice:** Require material factual statements in AI-assisted work products to be checked against the source evidence before approval.

Where confidential evidence is involved, the organization should evaluate the tool's data handling, access controls, retention, and contractual terms.

**Generative AI summary ≠ source evidence.** The original records remain the authoritative basis for the audit conclusion unless the audit objective specifically concerns the generated output itself.

### 324. Governance of AI-Based Audit Analytics

AI-based audit analytics may identify patterns, anomalies, or relationships in large evidence collections. These outputs can help direct audit attention but should not automatically determine conformity or nonconformity.

The organization should evaluate:

- Intended analytical purpose.
- Input data quality and representativeness.
- Model or rule limitations.
- Validation and performance.
- False-positive and false-negative behavior.
- Explainability appropriate to the use.
- Reviewer competence.
- Security and privacy risks.
- Version changes and ongoing performance.

**Governance Control:** Define when analytics results may trigger further testing and who is authorized to approve the final audit conclusion.

**Implementation Practice:** Retain the relevant model or rule version, input-population description, output, validation evidence, and auditor disposition.

**Anomaly detected ≠ control failure established.** An anomaly is an indicator that requires interpretation and, where appropriate, corroboration.

### 325. Audit Automation Validation and Revalidation

Validation determines whether an automated audit procedure is suitable for its intended purpose. Revalidation determines whether that suitability remains after relevant changes.

Revalidation may be required when:

- The underlying source system changes.
- Data fields or schemas change.
- The audit criterion changes.
- A rule or model is updated.
- A new population or business unit is added.
- Known error patterns emerge.
- The tool's performance degrades.
- The audit procedure is used for a materially different purpose.

**Governance Control:** Define validation and revalidation triggers, approval responsibilities, and documentation expectations.

**Implementation Practice:** Maintain test cases, expected outcomes, actual results, known limitations, and approval records for material automated procedures.

**Previously validated tool ≠ permanently valid tool.** Validity depends on the continuing match between the procedure, its inputs, and the intended audit use.

### 326. Human Oversight of Automated Audit Conclusions

Human oversight establishes who evaluates automated outputs, challenges uncertain results, approves findings, and remains accountable for the audit conclusion.

The level of oversight should reflect the complexity of the procedure and the consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

Appropriate safeguards may include:

- Independent checking of high-impact results.
- Review of exceptions and borderline cases.
- Access to source evidence.
- Clear escalation of uncertain results.
- Defined approval authority.
- Monitoring of error patterns.
- Periodic evaluation of reviewer competence.

**Governance Control:** Assign a named role accountable for approving material conclusions derived with automation.

**Implementation Practice:** Record the human review performed, significant challenges raised, and the basis for accepting or rejecting the automated output.

**Human review checkbox ≠ meaningful oversight.** The reviewer must have sufficient competence, information, and authority to challenge the result.

### 327. Enterprise Audit Evidence Architecture

An enterprise evidence architecture defines how audit evidence is identified, stored, linked, protected, searched, and retained across the organization.

- Test procedures.
- Source records.
- Findings.
- Corrective actions.

**Governance Control:** Define evidence ownership, access, retention, integrity, and cross-system traceability.

**Implementation Practice:** Use unique references and metadata that enable reviewers to locate the source evidence and understand how it supports a conclusion.

### 328. Enterprise Audit Data Lineage

Audit data lineage describes how information moves from its source through extraction, transformation, analysis, and reporting.

### 329. Enterprise Audit Evidence Interoperability

Evidence interoperability allows records from different business systems and assurance platforms to be linked and compared without losing their meaning or context.

**Implementation Practice:** Retain the original source reference alongside normalized or mapped values.

### 330. Enterprise Audit Reporting Architecture

Enterprise audit reporting consolidates engagement-level results into a coherent view of AIMS conformity, control weaknesses, audit coverage, and corrective-action progress.

**Implementation Practice:** Reconcile aggregated metrics to underlying engagement records and document material differences in scope or classification.

**Enterprise dashboard ≠ complete audit evidence.** Summary metrics should link to the records and analysis that support them.

### 331. Enterprise Audit Finding Taxonomy

### 332. Enterprise Root-Cause Trend Analysis

Root-cause trend analysis evaluates whether findings reveal recurring weaknesses across processes, business units, suppliers, or reporting periods.

Potential themes include unclear accountability, deficient change management, inadequate training, unreliable evidence, weak supplier oversight, insufficient monitoring, and ineffective corrective action.

The organization should distinguish:

- Repeated symptoms.
- Common contributing factors.
- Verified common root causes.
- Correlated events.
- Independent failures that happen to share a category.

**Implementation Practice:** Validate proposed enterprise-level root causes against individual findings, evidence, and management investigations.

**Trend correlation ≠ causal relationship.** Aggregated analysis can identify where further investigation is warranted but cannot establish causation by itself.

### 333. Enterprise Corrective-Action Governance

- Opportunities for improvement.

**Governance Control:** Define how audit results are escalated into management review and how resulting decisions are tracked.

**Implementation Practice:** Trace selected management-review decisions to assigned actions, implementation evidence, and follow-up outcomes.

**Audit report presented ≠ management action demonstrated.** Management review should lead to appropriate decisions where the evidence warrants them.

### 335. Enterprise Board and Executive Reporting

### 336. Enterprise Assurance Coordination

**Assurance coordination ≠ consolidation of every conclusion into one opinion.** Each provider's scope, criteria, competence, and limitations remain relevant.

### 337. Enterprise Assurance Gap Analysis

**Reliance decision approved ≠ evidence automatically sufficient.** The auditor must determine whether the work supports the specific conclusion.

### 339. Enterprise AIMS Audit Maturity Model

An audit maturity model can help an organization evaluate the development of its audit capabilities and identify improvement priorities.

An internal model may use stages such as:

- **Level 1 — Ad hoc:** Audit activities are reactive and inconsistently documented.
- **Level 2 — Defined:** Core procedures, responsibilities, and reporting templates exist.
- **Level 3 — Standardized:** Risk-based planning, controlled methodology, and consistent evidence practices are established.
- **Level 4 — Integrated:** Audit coverage, assurance coordination, trend analysis, and corrective-action oversight operate across the enterprise.
- **Level 5 — Adaptive:** The programme systematically uses validated analytics, lessons learned, and changing risk information to improve coverage and quality.

These levels are an illustrative internal maturity model, not an official ISO/IEC 42001 maturity scale.

**Governance Control:** Define scoring criteria and evidence expectations for each maturity level.

**Implementation Practice:** Require ratings to be supported by observed practices, records, and outcome evidence rather than self-assessment alone.

**Maturity level ≠ conformity determination.** Maturity and compliance answer different questions.

### 354. Enterprise Audit Sampling Governance

### 393. Enterprise Audit of Assurance Debt

### 399. Enterprise ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Readiness and Sustainability

Audit readiness means the organization can provide appropriate, controlled evidence of its AIMS processes and their operation within the defined scope. Sustainability means those arrangements continue to operate and improve over time.

**Governance Control:** Maintain ongoing ownership of AIMS evidence, internal audit, and corrective-action processes.

**Implementation Practice:** Periodically test evidence retrieval, traceability, and the ability to demonstrate actual operation of selected controls.

**Audit readiness ≠ temporary documentation exercise.** Sustainable readiness requires continuing implementation and evaluation.

### 400. Core Enterprise ISO/IEC 42001 Audit Principle

The core principle of enterprise ISO/IEC 42001 auditing is to maintain a risk-based, independent where appropriate, evidence-driven, and traceable evaluation of the Artificial Intelligence Management System within its defined scope and against clearly identified criteria.

Enterprise audit governance should connect leadership oversight, audit planning, competence, evidence management, testing, reporting, corrective action, assurance coordination, and continual improvement. It should also preserve the distinction between ISO/IEC 42001 conformity, legal compliance, contractual obligations, internal governance controls, certification, and broader AI assurance.

The organization should be able to explain not only which audits were completed, but also which risks and criteria were addressed, what evidence supports the conclusions, what limitations remain, and how identified weaknesses are being resolved.
