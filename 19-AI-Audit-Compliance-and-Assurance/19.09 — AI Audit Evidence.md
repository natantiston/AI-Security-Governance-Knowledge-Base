# 19.09 — AI Audit Evidence

## Part 1 — AI Audit Evidence Foundations, Evidence Quality, Collection, Reliability and Chain of Custody

### 1. Introduction to AI Audit Evidence

AI audit evidence is the information collected and evaluated to determine whether AI-related governance processes, risk-management activities, security safeguards, technical controls, and compliance obligations satisfy defined assessment criteria.

Evidence supports the conclusions reached by internal auditors, external auditors, compliance assessors, security assurance teams, and other authorized reviewers.

Unlike conventional information systems, AI environments may involve probabilistic outputs, changing model versions, dynamic prompts, retrieval pipelines, autonomous tool execution, external model providers, and complex data dependencies. These characteristics can make evidence collection and interpretation more demanding.

AI audit evidence may include:

- Governance policies and approved procedures
- AI inventories and risk assessments
- Model and system documentation
- Data lineage and data-quality records
- Model evaluation and validation results
- Access-control configurations
- Application and infrastructure logs
- AI interaction records
- Security testing and red-team reports
- Human oversight and approval records
- Incident and corrective-action records
- Supplier assurance documentation
- Monitoring dashboards and performance metrics
- Regulatory applicability assessments

Evidence must support a specific audit objective or control criterion. The mere existence of a document, dashboard, log, or test report does not establish that the relevant control is effective.

The central principle is that audit conclusions must be traceable to evidence that is appropriate for the claim being made.

### 2. Objectives of AI Audit Evidence Collection

Evidence collection establishes the factual basis for evaluating AI governance, control effectiveness, risk treatment, and compliance.

The principal objectives are to:

1. Verify that required policies, procedures, and controls exist.
2. Determine whether controls have been implemented.
3. Evaluate whether controls operated during the period under review.
4. Verify whether technical configurations match approved requirements.
5. Establish whether AI-related decisions and activities are traceable.
6. Identify control deviations and potential weaknesses.
7. Assess whether risk treatments operate as intended.
8. Support findings with objective and reproducible information.
9. Enable independent review of the auditor's reasoning.
10. Support remediation verification and follow-up testing.

Evidence collection should be planned around the audit objective rather than around whichever documents are easiest to obtain.

**Example:** If the objective is to verify that production AI deployments receive appropriate authorization, a policy describing the approval requirement is insufficient on its own. The auditor may also need deployment records, approval timestamps, change tickets, and technical evidence demonstrating that the approval process was followed.

### 3. AI Audit Evidence Versus AI System Documentation

AI system documentation and AI audit evidence are related but distinct.

System documentation describes how an AI system is intended to operate, how it is designed, and how it should be governed. Audit evidence demonstrates relevant facts about the system, its controls, or its actual operation.

| Attribute          | AI system documentation                                 | AI audit evidence                                     |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary purpose    | Explain design, intended use, or operation              | Support an assessment conclusion                      |
| Examples           | Architecture diagrams, model cards, system descriptions | Configuration exports, approval records, test results |
| Temporal relevance | May describe an intended or historical state            | Must be relevant to the assessed period and claim     |
| Typical limitation | May not reflect the deployed configuration              | May be incomplete or require corroboration            |
| Audit use          | Establish context and expected behavior                 | Verify implementation and operation                   |

A system design document may specify that all sensitive retrieval requests must be authorized. A captured test result and supporting access-control configuration may provide evidence of whether that requirement is enforced.

Documentation can itself be audit evidence when it directly supports the criterion being assessed. Its evidential value depends on its relevance, authenticity, completeness, and relationship to actual practice.

### 4. AI Audit Evidence and Audit Criteria

Evidence is meaningful only in relation to defined criteria.

Criteria may originate from:

- Applicable legislation and regulations
- Contractual obligations
- Internal policies
- Approved risk-management procedures
- Technical security baselines
- Organizational control frameworks
- Standards and assessment requirements
- System-specific acceptance criteria
- Approved business requirements

The auditor should identify the applicable criterion before determining whether the evidence demonstrates conformity.

**Example:** An internal procedure requires privileged access to AI production environments to be reviewed quarterly. The auditor should determine the relevant review period, identify the expected population, obtain the review records, and verify whether the required reviews occurred.

The criterion, evidence, and conclusion should be linked explicitly.

A useful traceability structure is:

**Requirement → Control Objective → Evidence Request → Evidence Obtained → Testing Procedure → Result → Conclusion**

If the criterion is ambiguous, the auditor should clarify its interpretation before reaching a definitive conclusion. Legal or regulatory interpretation may require consultation with appropriately qualified personnel.

### 5. Fundamental Characteristics of Reliable Audit Evidence

Reliable AI audit evidence should possess characteristics appropriate to the audit objective.

**Relevance:** The evidence directly addresses the control, requirement, risk, or assertion being evaluated.

**Authenticity:** The evidence can reasonably be attributed to its stated source and has not been improperly altered or misrepresented.

**Completeness:** The evidence contains the information necessary to evaluate the assertion, including relevant exceptions and limitations.

**Accuracy:** The evidence correctly represents the underlying event, configuration, transaction, or system state.

**Timeliness:** The evidence relates to the period and system version under assessment.

**Traceability:** The evidence can be linked to its source, collection method, associated test, and conclusion.

**Integrity:** Appropriate safeguards protect the evidence against unauthorized alteration, deletion, or substitution.

**Reproducibility:** Where practicable, another qualified reviewer can understand the procedure and reproduce or independently verify the result.

These characteristics are interrelated. A technically authentic log may still be incomplete if the relevant service was not configured to record all required events.

Likewise, a complete report may have limited relevance if it assesses a different model version or environment from the one under review.

### 6. Evidence Sufficiency and Appropriateness

Evidence sufficiency concerns the quantity of evidence obtained. Evidence appropriateness concerns its quality, relevance, and reliability.

Both dimensions matter.

A large volume of weak evidence does not necessarily support a strong conclusion. Conversely, a small number of highly reliable records may be sufficient for a narrowly defined objective, depending on the testing method and applicable assurance requirements.

The auditor should consider:

- Significance of the risk
- Nature of the control
- Complexity of the AI system
- Reliability of the evidence source
- Population size and variability
- Sampling method
- Potential impact of failure
- Degree of independent corroboration
- Unresolved inconsistencies
- Limitations affecting the conclusion

For high-impact controls, stronger or more extensive evidence may be required.

**Example:** A single successful prompt-injection test cannot establish that an AI application is resistant to prompt injection generally. The auditor may need a defined test suite, representative attack scenarios, system configuration evidence, documented acceptance criteria, and information about the test environment.

The final conclusion must remain proportionate to the evidence obtained.

### 7. Evidence Hierarchy and Source Reliability

Evidence sources differ in reliability according to how they are generated, controlled, collected, and verified.

A practical evidence hierarchy may distinguish among:

1. Direct observation or independently performed testing.
2. System-generated records whose origin, completeness, and integrity have been validated.
3. Independently corroborated operational records.
4. Approved organizational records supported by evidence of actual operation.
5. Management explanations corroborated by other sources.
6. Unsupported verbal representations or unverified assertions.

This hierarchy is a practical assessment aid, not a universal rule that automatically ranks every item of evidence.

The reliability of a source depends on the circumstances. A system-generated log is not automatically reliable if administrators can alter it without detection or if event collection is incomplete.

Similarly, direct observation may establish that an activity occurred during the observation period but may not demonstrate consistent operation over several months.

Auditors should evaluate evidence in context and use corroboration where appropriate.

### 8. Direct, Indirect, and Corroborative Evidence

AI audits may rely on several evidence categories.

**Direct evidence** supports an assertion through direct observation, inspection, or testing.

Example: An authorized test demonstrates that an AI application blocks a prohibited tool action.

**Indirect evidence** supports an assertion through related information or an inference.

Example: A deployment report indicates that the approval workflow was used, but it does not independently establish that every required approval was valid.

**Corroborative evidence** independently supports or confirms information obtained from another source.

Example: A change-management ticket is compared with the deployment platform's release history and approval timestamps.

Where the consequences of an incorrect conclusion are significant, auditors should seek sufficient corroboration to address material uncertainty.

Conflicting evidence should be investigated rather than selectively accepting whichever source supports the preferred conclusion.

### 9. AI Audit Evidence Classification

An evidence classification scheme helps auditors organize collected material and apply appropriate handling and evaluation procedures.

Evidence may be classified by source, purpose, or technical form.

| Classification       | Examples                                                | Primary assessment concern                               |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Governance evidence  | Policies, committee minutes, risk acceptance records    | Approval, accountability, and implementation             |
| Process evidence     | Workflows, checklists, operating procedures             | Whether required activities occurred                     |
| Technical evidence   | Configurations, API logs, access records                | Actual technical behavior and control enforcement        |
| Model evidence       | Evaluation results, validation reports, model metadata  | Model-specific performance and limitations               |
| Data evidence        | Lineage records, quality reports, access logs           | Data origin, quality, permissions, and handling          |
| Security evidence    | Vulnerability reports, penetration tests, incident logs | Security posture and treatment of weaknesses             |
| Supplier evidence    | Assurance reports, contracts, provider attestations     | Scope, reliability, and relevance to outsourced services |
| Monitoring evidence  | Metrics, alerts, trend reports                          | Ongoing control operation and detection                  |
| Remediation evidence | Change records, retest results, closure approvals       | Whether identified deficiencies were corrected           |

The classification should not replace the assessment of evidential quality. A document categorized as technical evidence may still be unsuitable if it cannot be associated with the relevant system version.

### 10. Evidence Collection Planning

Evidence collection should be planned before fieldwork begins.

The auditor should establish:

- Audit objectives and scope
- Applicable criteria
- Relevant AI systems and components
- Evidence required for each control
- Evidence owners and source systems
- Assessment period
- Collection methods
- Required technical access
- Confidentiality and handling restrictions
- Evidence retention requirements
- Validation and corroboration procedures
- Known dependencies and limitations

A structured evidence request list reduces incomplete submissions and unnecessary collection.

**Example evidence request — AI model change management**

- Approved change-management procedure
- Inventory of model changes during the assessment period
- Selected change requests
- Risk assessment records
- Validation results
- Approval records
- Deployment timestamps
- Model registry history
- Post-deployment monitoring results
- Records of exceptions and emergency changes

The auditor should define the evidence needed to support each test rather than collecting large volumes of material without a clear assessment purpose.

### 11. AI Audit Evidence Request Management

An evidence request register tracks outstanding requests, submissions, clarifications, and unresolved gaps.

A typical register includes:

| Field                | Description                                    |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Request ID           | Unique identifier                              |
| Audit objective      | Purpose of the request                         |
| Control reference    | Related control or requirement                 |
| Evidence description | Specific material requested                    |
| Evidence owner       | Responsible individual or team                 |
| Source system        | Expected origin of the evidence                |
| Period covered       | Relevant dates or reporting period             |
| Requested date       | Date of the request                            |
| Due date             | Agreed submission deadline                     |
| Status               | Open, submitted, under review, or closed       |
| Quality assessment   | Completeness and relevance evaluation          |
| Follow-up            | Clarifications or additional evidence required |

Evidence requests should be specific enough to minimize ambiguity.

For example, requesting “AI security logs” is less precise than requesting “authentication and privileged-action logs for the production AI orchestration service covering the defined assessment period, including event timestamps, actor identities, and relevant source identifiers.”

The auditor should record when evidence is unavailable, incomplete, delayed, or rejected as unsuitable.

### 12. Evidence Provenance and Source Identification

Evidence provenance describes where evidence originated and how it reached the audit workpapers.

For each material item, the auditor should record relevant information such as:

- Source system or repository
- Original file or record identifier
- Date and time collected
- Collector or responsible person
- Collection method
- System or environment
- Relevant version or configuration
- Period covered
- Transformations performed
- Validation procedures
- Storage location and access restrictions

Provenance is especially important for AI evidence because the same application may behave differently across model versions, environments, prompts, datasets, and configuration states.

**Example:** A model evaluation report should be associated with the tested model version, evaluation dataset, test configuration, execution date, and acceptance criteria.

Without that context, the report may not support conclusions about the currently deployed system.

### 13. Evidence Authenticity and Integrity Verification

Authenticity verification determines whether evidence originates from the claimed source. Integrity verification evaluates whether the evidence has been altered or compromised in an unauthorized manner.

Depending on the evidence type, procedures may include:

- Obtaining records directly from the authoritative system
- Verifying digital signatures
- Comparing record identifiers
- Reviewing system audit logs
- Validating export metadata
- Comparing independently maintained records
- Calculating and recording cryptographic hashes
- Reviewing access and modification history
- Confirming the collection process with the source owner

Cryptographic hashes can help detect changes to a collected file when a trusted reference hash is available. However, a hash does not independently establish that the original file was accurate, complete, or authentic.

Similarly, a digital signature may establish a relationship to a signing key without proving that the underlying statement is factually correct.

Integrity controls should therefore be combined with appropriate source verification and evidence evaluation.

### 14. AI Audit Evidence Collection Methods

Evidence may be collected through several methods, selected according to the audit objective and source characteristics.

**Inspection:** Examine policies, configurations, records, source code where authorized, or other artifacts.

**Observation:** Observe a control or process being performed.

**Inquiry:** Obtain explanations from relevant personnel.

**Reperformance:** Independently repeat a control activity or calculation.

**Technical testing:** Execute approved test cases against an AI system or supporting infrastructure.

**Data analysis:** Analyze structured records to identify patterns, exceptions, missing items, or anomalies.

**Reconciliation:** Compare independent records to identify inconsistencies.

**Confirmation:** Obtain corroboration from an independent or authoritative source where appropriate.

Methods may be combined. For example, an auditor may inspect a deployment record, reconcile it with the model registry, interview the release owner, and independently verify the relevant approval configuration.

The chosen method should provide evidence suitable for the intended conclusion.

### 15. AI System and Model Version Identification

Version identification is a critical part of AI audit evidence management.

The auditor should determine which model, application, configuration, and supporting components were active during the assessment period.

Relevant identifiers may include:

- Model name and version
- Model provider and deployment identifier
- Application release
- System instruction or prompt version
- Retrieval index version
- Dataset version
- Tool and connector configuration
- API version
- Infrastructure configuration
- Evaluation framework version
- Deployment environment

For systems using hosted models that can change behind a provider-managed endpoint, the auditor may need provider documentation, release notices, service-version records, or other available evidence of the underlying change history.

Where exact version identification is unavailable, that limitation should be documented.

Evidence obtained from one version should not automatically be treated as evidence for another version without evaluating whether the change could affect the control or behavior under review.

### 16. Evidence Relevance to the Assessment Period

Audit evidence must relate to the period being assessed.

A policy approved after the assessment period may demonstrate a later improvement but does not necessarily prove that the control existed earlier.

Similarly, a configuration captured today may not establish how the system was configured several months ago.

The auditor should distinguish among:

- Evidence of the state at a specific point in time
- Evidence of activities performed during a period
- Evidence of a change between two states
- Evidence of current operation
- Evidence of remediation after an identified issue

Historical evidence may include retained logs, approved change records, archived configurations, deployment histories, and dated review records.

Where evidence is only available for the current state, the auditor should avoid making unsupported claims about historical operating effectiveness.

### 17. Evidence Completeness and Population Reconciliation

Evidence completeness determines whether the collected material includes the relevant records needed to evaluate the assertion.

For population-based testing, the auditor should verify that the population is sufficiently complete and accurate before selecting samples.

Potential procedures include:

- Reconciling AI inventory records with deployment platforms
- Comparing model registry entries with production endpoints
- Reconciling identity records with privileged-access listings
- Comparing incident-management records with security alerts
- Comparing change tickets with deployment histories
- Reviewing log collection coverage across relevant services

**Example:** An audit of AI model approvals begins with a list of approved model changes. The auditor independently obtains deployment records and compares the two populations to identify production changes missing from the approval list.

This approach can reveal unauthorized changes that would not appear in a population generated exclusively by the approval workflow.

If completeness cannot be established, the auditor should consider additional procedures and restrict the conclusion as necessary.

### 18. Evidence Reliability in AI-Generated Content

AI-generated content may be used in business decisions, reports, analyses, summaries, or automated workflows. Its evidential value depends on the assertion it is intended to support and the controls surrounding its creation.

An AI-generated summary of a meeting may help identify relevant topics, but it may not independently establish the exact wording of an approval decision.

A model-generated explanation of a security event may be useful for triage, but the underlying event records should be examined before relying on the explanation as factual evidence.

The auditor should consider:

- Whether the original source is available
- Whether the output can be independently verified
- Whether material information was omitted
- Whether the model introduced unsupported statements
- Whether the output was altered after generation
- Whether the model version and relevant inputs are recorded
- Whether human review was required and performed

AI-generated explanations should not be treated as a substitute for authoritative source records when the audit objective requires direct evidence of what occurred.

### 19. Evidence Reliability in AI-Assisted Audit Work

AI tools may assist auditors with document classification, evidence summarization, anomaly detection, control mapping, or workpaper preparation.

When AI is used in audit activities, the audit team should evaluate the reliability of the output and the suitability of the tool for the task.

Potential safeguards include:

- Using approved tools and data sources
- Restricting access to confidential evidence
- Validating extracted information against original records
- Reviewing generated summaries for omissions
- Checking control mappings against authoritative criteria
- Recording material transformations
- Maintaining human accountability for conclusions
- Avoiding unsupported automated severity decisions
- Preserving sufficient audit trails

**Example:** An AI tool extracts approval dates from a collection of deployment records. The auditor should validate the extracted dates against the source records before using them to determine whether approvals preceded deployment.

The use of an AI tool does not transfer responsibility for the audit conclusion to the tool or its provider.

### 20. Evidence Collection for Generative AI Systems

Generative AI systems may produce variable outputs from similar inputs and may rely on external models, retrieval sources, tools, and dynamic context.

Evidence collection should account for the system's relevant operating conditions.

Potential evidence includes:

- System instructions and prompt templates
- Model and application versions
- Input and output records, where appropriately retained
- Retrieval-source identifiers
- Tool invocation records
- Output validation results
- Safety and security test results
- Human-review decisions
- Monitoring events
- Configuration and change records

The auditor should consider whether collected records preserve enough context to interpret the event.

For example, an output log without the relevant system version, retrieved sources, or tool invocation history may be insufficient to explain why a particular response or action occurred.

Collection must remain proportionate to the audit objective and comply with applicable privacy, confidentiality, and data-minimization requirements.

### 21. Evidence Collection for Agentic AI Systems

Agentic AI systems can plan and execute multi-step activities, invoke tools, interact with external systems, and perform actions that affect business processes.

Evidence should allow the auditor to reconstruct material actions and determine whether authorization and oversight controls operated as intended.

Relevant records may include:

- User or initiating identity
- Agent and session identifiers
- Relevant model and application versions
- Tool selection and invocation records
- Action parameters
- Authorization decisions
- Human approvals
- Execution outcomes
- Error and retry events
- Relevant timestamps
- Downstream transaction identifiers

The auditor should establish whether the available records capture the important stages of the action lifecycle.

A final response stating that a task was completed may not prove which actions were executed, which systems were accessed, or whether the agent stayed within its authorized boundaries.

Evidence should support the actual control objective rather than merely documenting the agent's self-reported account of its actions.

### 22. Evidence Collection for AI Data Governance

Data governance evidence supports assessments of data ownership, access, quality, lineage, retention, and permitted use.

Potential evidence includes:

- Dataset inventories
- Data classification records
- Data-source approvals
- Data lineage documentation
- Data-quality assessment results
- Access permissions
- Data-sharing agreements
- Retention schedules
- Deletion records
- Dataset change histories
- Privacy assessment records

The auditor should verify that documentation corresponds to the data actually used by the AI system.

For example, a documented training dataset may not fully describe the external retrieval sources accessed by a production AI assistant.

Data lineage evidence should establish relevant relationships between source data, transformations, derived datasets, and AI components.

Where the system uses dynamic external data sources, the auditor should determine whether the evidence captures the source configuration and changes relevant to the audit period.

### 23. Evidence Collection for AI Security Controls

Security-control evidence demonstrates how safeguards protect AI applications, models, data, interfaces, infrastructure, and connected services.

Potential evidence includes:

- Identity and access configurations
- Privileged-access records
- Network restrictions
- API authorization settings
- Secrets-management records
- Vulnerability scan results
- Penetration-test reports
- Prompt-injection test results
- Tool permission policies
- Logging and alerting configurations
- Incident records
- Security change approvals

The auditor should evaluate whether the evidence covers the actual attack surface.

A cloud infrastructure security report may not address prompt injection, retrieval authorization, unsafe tool execution, or AI-specific data leakage.

Conversely, a prompt-injection test may not demonstrate that the infrastructure, identities, and external integrations are securely configured.

Evidence collection should therefore follow the defined control objectives and relevant system architecture.

### 24. Evidence Collection for AI Human Oversight

Human-oversight evidence demonstrates whether people can understand, review, intervene in, or override AI-supported activities as required.

Potential evidence includes:

- Review policies
- Reviewer assignments
- Approval and rejection records
- Escalation records
- Override logs
- User interface screenshots
- Training records
- Review timestamps
- Quality assurance results
- Records of decisions made after escalation

The auditor should assess whether the evidence demonstrates meaningful oversight rather than merely the presence of a human approval step.

For example, a record showing that a reviewer clicked an approval button does not establish that the reviewer received sufficient information, understood the relevant risks, or had the authority to reject the proposed action.

Where human judgment is an essential control, the audit procedure should evaluate the information, decision rights, and workflow available to the reviewer.

### 25. Evidence Collection for AI Incident Management

Incident-management evidence supports assessment of detection, triage, escalation, investigation, containment, recovery, and lessons learned.

Potential evidence includes:

- Incident reports
- Security alerts
- Case-management records
- Investigation timelines
- Escalation communications
- Containment actions
- Relevant AI interaction records
- Root-cause analysis
- Recovery validation
- Corrective-action tracking
- Post-incident review records

The auditor should verify that records are consistent across relevant systems and that material timestamps and actions can be reconstructed.

For AI-related incidents, the investigation may need to consider model behavior, system instructions, retrieval sources, tool actions, external provider dependencies, and configuration changes.

Where an incident has potential legal or regulatory reporting implications, the auditor should verify that the responsible personnel assessed the applicable requirements and documented their decisions.

### 26. Evidence Collection for AI Third-Party Assurance

Third-party evidence is important when an organization relies on external model providers, AI platforms, cloud services, datasets, or specialized AI vendors.

Potential evidence includes:

- Supplier contracts
- Service descriptions
- Independent assurance reports
- Security certifications
- Penetration-test summaries
- Data-processing agreements
- Subprocessor information
- Service-level reports
- Incident notifications
- Business continuity evidence
- Model documentation
- Change notifications

The auditor should evaluate whether the evidence covers the services, environments, and controls relevant to the organization's actual use.

A supplier certification may establish that a defined scope was assessed against particular criteria, but it does not automatically establish that every AI service offered by the supplier is covered.

The organization should document material assurance gaps and determine whether additional evidence, contractual protections, technical controls, or alternative arrangements are required.

### 27. Evidence Gaps, Limitations, and Unavailable Records

An evidence gap exists when the available material is insufficient to evaluate an assertion or complete a planned procedure.

Common causes include:

- Missing logs
- Incomplete inventories
- Expired retention periods
- Restricted supplier access
- Unavailable historical configurations
- Inconsistent recordkeeping
- Unclear ownership
- Technical limitations
- Inadequate evidence collection procedures

The auditor should record the missing evidence, the reason it is unavailable where known, the affected control objective, and the impact on the assessment.

Where practical, alternative procedures may be used to obtain sufficient evidence. However, alternative evidence should not be assumed equivalent without evaluation.

An inability to obtain evidence does not automatically prove that a control failed. It may nevertheless represent an assurance limitation or, where evidence retention is itself required, a separate control deficiency.

The conclusion should distinguish a confirmed control failure from an inability to determine whether the control operated effectively.

### 28. Evidence Contradictions and Inconsistency Resolution

Contradictory evidence occurs when two or more sources provide materially inconsistent information about the same event, configuration, or control activity.

Examples include:

- A policy indicates approval was required, but deployment logs show a release before approval.
- A model registry identifies one version, while production records indicate another.
- Management reports that logging is enabled, but sampled events are absent from the monitoring platform.
- A remediation ticket is marked complete, but the original failure remains reproducible.

The auditor should investigate material contradictions before reaching a conclusion.

Potential procedures include:

1. Confirming that the records refer to the same system and period.
2. Verifying timestamps and time-zone assumptions.
3. Checking the authenticity and completeness of each source.
4. Obtaining additional independent records.
5. Interviewing relevant personnel.
6. Reperforming the affected test where appropriate.
7. Documenting the resolution or remaining uncertainty.

The auditor should not selectively discard contradictory evidence merely because it conflicts with management's explanation or the initial audit hypothesis.

Unresolved contradictions should be disclosed and reflected in the conclusion where material.

### 29. AI Audit Evidence Retention and Preservation

Evidence retention supports review, follow-up, incident investigation, and verification of historical conclusions.

Retention requirements should reflect applicable legal obligations, contractual commitments, organizational policies, professional standards, and the sensitivity of the material.

The evidence-management process should address:

- Approved storage locations
- Access restrictions
- Retention periods
- Record integrity
- Backup and recovery
- Legal holds where applicable
- Secure deletion
- Version history
- Evidence retrieval
- Disposal authorization

AI evidence may contain personal data, confidential business information, source code, sensitive prompts, proprietary model details, or security-relevant configuration information.

Retention should therefore balance auditability with privacy, confidentiality, security, and data-minimization obligations.

The organization should avoid retaining sensitive AI interaction content indefinitely merely because it could be useful for a future audit.

Where detailed content is not necessary, appropriately designed metadata or redacted records may provide sufficient evidence for the defined objective.

### 30. Core Principle of AI Audit Evidence

AI audit evidence is the foundation of defensible AI assurance.

Reliable evidence connects defined requirements and control objectives to verifiable facts about governance, technical implementation, system operation, and corrective action.

The auditor must evaluate not only what evidence says, but also where it originated, what period and system version it represents, whether it is complete and reliable, and whether it actually supports the intended conclusion.

**The governing principle is simple: an AI audit conclusion must never exceed what the available evidence can reasonably support.**

**Taxonomy note:** Internal evidence identifiers, where used by the knowledge base, are organizational labels and should not be represented as official ISO/IEC clauses, NIST identifiers, or statutory article numbers.

## Part 2 — AI Audit Evidence Collection by Domain, Technical Artifacts, Validation Procedures and Evidence Reliability

### 31. AI Governance Evidence Collection

AI governance evidence demonstrates whether the organization has established and implemented the accountability structures, policies, decision-making processes, and oversight mechanisms required to manage AI responsibly.

Relevant evidence may include:

- AI governance policies and standards
- AI governance committee terms of reference
- Meeting minutes and decision records
- Assigned AI system owners
- Risk acceptance approvals
- AI inventory records
- Governance reporting
- Roles and responsibility matrices
- AI policy exceptions
- Management review records
- Escalation procedures
- Evidence of policy communication

The auditor should evaluate whether the documented governance arrangements operate in practice.

For example, an AI governance committee may have formally approved terms of reference, but the auditor should also determine whether it meets as required, reviews material AI risks, records decisions, and follows up on unresolved issues.

**Evidence validation procedure:**

1. Obtain the approved governance framework.
2. Identify the responsibilities and decision rights relevant to the audit scope.
3. Select governance decisions from the assessment period.
4. Trace selected decisions to supporting records and assigned owners.
5. Verify whether required actions were completed or escalated.
6. Compare documented responsibilities with actual operational practice.

A governance document alone does not establish that governance activities are consistently performed.

### 32. AI Inventory Evidence Validation

An AI inventory provides the population of AI systems that the organization knows about and manages. Its reliability directly affects the completeness of risk assessment, control testing, and compliance reporting.

Relevant evidence may include:

- AI inventory exports
- Cloud and application discovery records
- Procurement records
- Software-as-a-service inventories
- Model registry entries
- Production deployment records
- Business application catalogs
- Supplier registers
- AI system onboarding records
- Retirement and decommissioning records

The auditor should establish whether the inventory includes the AI systems relevant to the assessment and whether key attributes are accurate.

Important attributes may include system owner, business purpose, deployment environment, provider, model version, data categories, risk classification, and lifecycle status.

**Evidence validation procedure:**

1. Obtain the AI inventory and identify its authoritative source.
2. Select independent sources of AI deployment information.
3. Reconcile the sources against the inventory.
4. Investigate systems that are missing, duplicated, retired, or incorrectly classified.
5. Trace selected inventory entries to actual deployments.
6. Document unresolved population limitations.

A complete-looking inventory may still omit shadow AI applications or embedded AI functionality in third-party products.

Inventory completeness should therefore be assessed using appropriate independent sources rather than relying exclusively on the inventory itself.

### 33. AI Risk Assessment Evidence

AI risk assessment evidence demonstrates how risks are identified, analyzed, evaluated, and documented throughout the system lifecycle.

Relevant artifacts may include:

- AI risk assessment methodology
- System-specific risk assessments
- Threat models
- Impact assessments
- Stakeholder analyses
- Risk registers
- Risk scoring worksheets
- Risk acceptance records
- Risk treatment plans
- Residual risk evaluations
- Assessment review and approval records

The auditor should determine whether the assessment reflects the system's intended purpose, deployment context, affected stakeholders, technical architecture, and reasonably foreseeable misuse.

**Example:** An AI assistant connected to internal documents and business tools may introduce risks related to unauthorized retrieval, prompt injection, excessive permissions, sensitive-data disclosure, and unintended actions.

The auditor should verify whether these risks were considered in the documented assessment and whether the selected treatment measures are supported by evidence.

Evidence of a completed risk assessment does not automatically establish that the assessment was sufficiently comprehensive or that the identified risks were adequately treated.

### 34. AI Risk Treatment and Acceptance Evidence

Risk treatment evidence establishes whether identified risks have been addressed through mitigation, avoidance, transfer, or informed acceptance, as appropriate to the organization's risk-management methodology.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Risk treatment plans
- Control implementation records
- Remediation tickets
- Technical change records
- Risk acceptance approvals
- Compensating control assessments
- Residual risk calculations
- Risk owner sign-offs
- Exception registers
- Monitoring plans

The auditor should trace selected risks from identification through treatment and determine whether the intended safeguards were implemented.

For accepted risks, the auditor should verify that the approval came from an authorized risk owner, that the decision was informed by relevant evidence, and that the acceptance remains valid under the organization's rules.

Risk acceptance does not override mandatory legal requirements or make an otherwise prohibited activity permissible.

### 35. AI Model Development Evidence

### 59. AI Evidence Quality Review Checklist

- Is the evidence relevant to the audit objective?
- Does it relate to the correct system and environment?
- Is the model or application version identifiable?
- Does it cover the required assessment period?
- Is the source authoritative or independently corroborated?
- Can its authenticity and integrity be reasonably established?
- Is the population complete where population-based testing is used?
- Are material limitations documented?
- Does the evidence support the specific assertion being made?
- Are contradictory records investigated?
- Is sensitive information handled appropriately?
- Can the evidence be traced to the workpaper and conclusion?
- Is the evidence retained according to approved requirements?

Evidence that fails one or more checks should not automatically be discarded. The auditor should determine whether the issue can be resolved through additional verification, corroboration, or alternative procedures.

If the limitation remains material, it should be reflected in the testing conclusion.

### 60. AI Audit Evidence — Domain-Based Assurance Principle

AI audit evidence must reflect the specific control domain, architecture, data flows, operational context, and assessment criteria under review.

A mature evidence process connects each material conclusion to traceable artifacts, validates evidence against authoritative sources, investigates contradictions, preserves relevant context, and documents limitations.

**The governing principle is that evidence must demonstrate the control or condition being assessed—not merely the existence of documentation describing what should happen.**

## Part 3 — AI Audit Evidence Integrity, Chain of Custody, Advanced Validation, Sampling and Forensic Preservation

### 61. AI Audit Evidence Chain of Custody

The chain of custody is the documented history of evidence from its original source through collection, transfer, storage, examination, and final disposition.

For AI audits, chain-of-custody controls are particularly important when evidence may be used in security investigations, regulatory examinations, internal audit findings, legal proceedings, or disputed compliance assessments.

Evidence may include model artifacts, prompts, AI interaction records, access logs, configuration exports, datasets, incident records, and technical test outputs.

A defensible chain of custody should record:

- Unique evidence identifier
- Description and classification
- Original source
- Collection date and time, including time zone
- Collector identity
- Collection method and tools
- Original filename or record identifier
- File size and cryptographic hash, where appropriate
- Transfer history
- Storage location
- Access and examination history
- Transformations or redactions
- Integrity verification results
- Final retention or disposal action

**Example:** An auditor collects application logs associated with a suspected unauthorized AI tool invocation. The auditor records the source system, query parameters, collection timestamp, file hash, storage location, and subsequent access by the investigation team.

This record helps establish how the evidence was handled and whether the collected copy remained unchanged after acquisition.

A documented chain of custody does not independently prove that the original source was accurate or complete. Source reliability must still be evaluated.

### 62. Original Evidence and Working Copies

Evidence management should distinguish original source material from working copies used for analysis.

An original may be a source-system record, an exported log, a model artifact, or a document received from an evidence owner. A working copy is created for activities such as filtering, parsing, redaction, correlation, or technical analysis.

The evidence-management procedure should specify:

1. How the original is acquired.
2. How its integrity is verified.
3. Where it is preserved.
4. How working copies are created.
5. How transformations are recorded.
6. How analytical outputs are linked to the original.
7. How access and retention are controlled.

For example, an auditor may receive a large AI interaction log and create a filtered dataset containing only events relevant to the assessment. The filtered dataset should retain a reference to the original evidence and document the filtering criteria.

The auditor should not overwrite the original evidence with a transformed version.

Where evidence must be redacted to protect personal data or confidential information, the unredacted original and redacted working copy should be handled according to approved access and retention requirements.

### 63. Cryptographic Hashes for AI Audit Evidence

A cryptographic hash produces a fixed-length value calculated from digital content. Hashes can help detect changes to a file or artifact after a reference hash has been established.

Commonly used hash algorithms include SHA-256 and SHA-512.

A typical evidence-preservation process may involve:

- Calculating a hash at collection
- Recording the algorithm and result
- Storing the evidence in an access-controlled repository
- Recalculating the hash when integrity verification is required
- Comparing the result with the recorded reference value
- Investigating any unexpected difference

**Example:** An auditor collects a model evaluation report and records its SHA-256 hash. Before a later review, the auditor recalculates the hash and compares it with the original value.

Matching hashes provide evidence that the compared file contents are unchanged, assuming the hash calculation and reference record are trustworthy.

Hashing has important limitations:

- It does not prove that the original file was accurate.
- It does not prove that the source system was trustworthy.
- It does not establish that the evidence collection was complete.
- It does not prove who created the file unless supported by additional evidence.
- It does not independently establish the truth of the file's contents.

Hashing should be used as part of an evidence-integrity process rather than as a substitute for source verification.

### 64. Digital Signatures and Signed Evidence

Digital signatures can help verify the integrity of signed content and establish that the signature was generated using a particular private key.

For AI audit evidence, signed artifacts may include:

- Approved release manifests
- Software or model artifact packages
- Configuration baselines
- Supplier attestations
- Formal approvals
- Evidence transfer records

The auditor should determine whether the signature is valid, whether the signing key can reasonably be associated with the claimed signer, and whether the signature covers the relevant content.

Where material, the auditor should also evaluate certificate validity, revocation status, trusted timestamps, and the applicable signature-verification process.

A valid signature does not automatically establish that the signer was authorized to approve the activity or that the signed information was factually correct.

The assessment should therefore combine signature verification with authorization records, source information, and the relevant control criteria.

### 65. Trusted Timestamps and Evidence Chronology

Timestamps help establish the sequence of events and whether a control activity occurred within the required timeframe.

AI environments may produce timestamps across multiple systems, regions, cloud platforms, and external services. Differences in clock configuration or event processing can complicate chronology.

The auditor should consider:

- Time zone and UTC offset
- Clock synchronization
- Event generation time
- Event ingestion time
- Processing and alert time
- Record modification time
- Time source reliability
- Delayed or asynchronous processing

For example, an AI deployment record may show that a release occurred before the approval timestamp in a separate workflow. Before concluding that the release was unauthorized, the auditor should verify whether the timestamps use consistent time zones and whether either record reflects delayed processing rather than the actual event time.

Where chronology is material, the auditor should use reliable timestamps and corroborating records.

A timestamp field alone does not establish that a record was created at that time or that its clock was accurate.

### 66. Evidence Acquisition from Cloud-Based AI Services

Cloud-based AI systems often distribute evidence across provider-managed services, customer-controlled resources, and external integrations.

Evidence acquisition should account for the shared-responsibility model and the access rights available to the organization.

Potential sources include:

- Cloud audit logs

- API gateways
- Storage audit records
- Configuration-management services
- Security monitoring platforms
- Provider assurance portals
- Support and incident records

Collection procedures should document the service, account or tenant, region where relevant, time range, query criteria, filters, and export method.

Where the provider controls evidence generation or retention, the organization should evaluate the provider's capabilities and any resulting assurance limitations.

The auditor should not assume that a customer-visible dashboard exposes every relevant event or that provider-managed logging covers all customer-specific control objectives.

### 67. Evidence Acquisition from APIs and Programmatic Interfaces

Application programming interfaces can provide structured access to AI service records, configuration data, model metadata, and audit events.

API-based evidence acquisition can improve repeatability, particularly where large populations or recurring assessments are involved.

- Time range
- Pagination handling
- Rate limits
- Returned record counts
- Error responses
- Data transformations
- Export and storage method

The auditor should verify whether the query retrieves the intended population and whether pagination, filtering, or permission restrictions could omit records.

**Example:** An auditor extracts AI deployment events through a cloud management API. The auditor compares the extracted count with the platform's reported count, checks for incomplete pages, and validates selected records against the source console or another authoritative record.

API evidence is not automatically complete or reliable. The collection method, permissions, query logic, and source behavior must be assessed.

### 68. Evidence Collection from Model Registries and Artifact Stores

Model registries and artifact repositories may contain important evidence about model lineage, versioning, approval, validation, and deployment.

- Retirement records

Where possible, the auditor should compare registry information with deployment records and inspect whether access controls prevent unauthorized changes.

If an artifact's hash changes unexpectedly, the auditor should investigate whether the difference reflects an authorized rebuild, a new version, a packaging change, or an integrity issue.

The presence of a model in an approved registry does not by itself establish that the approved model is running in production.

### 69. Evidence Preservation for AI Incidents

AI incidents may require evidence preservation to support root-cause analysis, security investigations, regulatory reporting, or legal review.

- Supplier communications
- Investigation notes

The preservation process should be initiated according to the organization's incident-response and evidence-handling procedures.

The team should determine which records are likely to be relevant, protect them from unauthorized modification or deletion, and document any gaps.

Where personal data or legally protected information is involved, preservation and access should be coordinated with the appropriate privacy, legal, and security functions.

The auditor should distinguish contemporaneous records from later-created summaries or reconstructions. Both may be useful, but they support different assertions.

### 70. Volatile Evidence in AI Environments

Volatile evidence is information that may disappear or change rapidly during normal system operation.

Examples may include:

- Temporary application state
- Short-lived authentication tokens
- Ephemeral container information
- In-memory execution state
- Temporary tool results
- Short-retention logs
- Dynamic session context
- Transient monitoring events

Where volatile evidence is relevant, the organization should define appropriate collection and preservation procedures in advance.

Collection should be authorized, proportionate, and designed to minimize disruption to the system.

The auditor should document the source, collection time, method, and any limitations caused by the transient nature of the information.

Not every AI audit requires volatile evidence acquisition. The need depends on the control objective, investigation context, technical architecture, and applicable evidence-handling requirements.

### 71. AI Evidence Transformation and Data Normalization

Evidence transformation converts source records into a format suitable for analysis. Examples include parsing JSON logs, normalizing timestamps, extracting selected fields, joining records, or converting documents into searchable text.

Transformations can introduce errors if they omit fields, alter values, change encoding, or incorrectly associate records.

A defensible process should preserve the original evidence and document material transformations.

The audit workpaper should identify:

- Source evidence
- Transformation tool or procedure
- Version of the processing method where relevant
- Selected fields
- Filtering criteria
- Data normalization rules
- Excluded records
- Known parsing limitations
- Output location
- Validation procedures

**Example:** An auditor converts AI interaction logs into a table to analyze tool invocations. The auditor preserves the original logs, records the parsing method, checks for parsing errors, and reconciles the number of processed records with the source population.

Analytical outputs should remain traceable to the underlying evidence.

### 72. Evidence Deduplication and Record Reconciliation

Deduplication identifies repeated records, while reconciliation compares related populations or sources to determine whether they agree.

These activities can improve audit efficiency but must be performed carefully.

Duplicate records may represent repeated exports, duplicated ingestion, legitimate retries, or distinct events with similar attributes.

The auditor should not remove records merely because their content appears similar.

A suitable procedure may involve:

1. Defining what constitutes a duplicate.
2. Identifying the fields used for comparison.
3. Preserving original record identifiers.
4. Separating true duplicates from legitimate repeated events.
5. Recording excluded records and the reason for exclusion.
6. Reconciling the resulting population with the source.

For AI agents, repeated tool calls may indicate retry behavior, a workflow loop, or separate authorized actions. Deduplication should not conceal these operational patterns.

Where deduplication affects a testing population, the method and impact should be documented.

### 73. AI Evidence Sampling and Sample Integrity

Evidence sampling selects items from a population for examination. The reliability of the conclusion depends partly on whether the population and selection method are appropriate.

The auditor should document:

- Population definition
- Population source
- Assessment period
- Completeness checks
- Sampling objective
- Selection method
- Sample size rationale
- Selected item identifiers
- Exclusions and substitutions
- Results for each selected item
- Limitations on inference

Random, stratified, judgmental, and targeted sampling serve different purposes.

A targeted sample of high-risk AI interactions may help investigate specific risks but should not automatically be used to estimate the failure rate across all interactions.

If sample items are replaced because evidence is unavailable, the auditor should record the reason and evaluate whether the substitution could bias the results.

Sampling limitations should be reflected in the final conclusion.

### 74. Full-Population Analytics for AI Audit Evidence

Full-population analytics evaluates all records available within a defined population rather than examining only a sample.

Potential applications include:

- Identifying AI deployments without assigned owners
- Detecting production changes without matching approval records
- Finding missing log fields
- Identifying access reviews that are overdue
- Detecting unusual tool invocation patterns
- Comparing model registry versions with production deployments
- Identifying unresolved high-severity findings

Full-population analysis can improve coverage, but the result is only as complete as the underlying population and the quality of the analytical logic.

The auditor should validate the input data, test the analytical rules, document exclusions, and investigate unexpected results.

**Example:** A query identifies all deployment events without a corresponding approval record. The auditor should validate the matching logic, examine false positives, and determine whether approval records may exist under alternate identifiers.

A full-population query does not eliminate the need for professional judgment or independent verification.

### 75. AI Evidence Correlation and Event Reconstruction

Event reconstruction combines records from multiple systems to establish the sequence of activities associated with an AI transaction, control event, or incident.

A reconstructed timeline may include:

1. User authentication.
2. AI request submission.
3. Application processing.
4. Retrieval of relevant information.
5. Tool selection.
6. Authorization decision.
7. Tool execution.
8. Downstream transaction.
9. Monitoring alert.
10. Human intervention or incident response.

The auditor should determine whether the records can be reliably linked using timestamps, identities, session IDs, request identifiers, or transaction references.

Missing events should be investigated rather than silently inferred.

Where multiple plausible sequences remain, the workpaper should describe the uncertainty and avoid presenting an inferred timeline as a fully verified fact.

### 76. AI Evidence Reliability for Non-Deterministic Systems

Non-deterministic or probabilistic AI systems may produce different outputs under apparently similar conditions.

This creates challenges when attempting to reproduce an earlier event or verify a control based solely on repeated outputs.

The auditor should preserve relevant context where available, including:

- Model and application versions
- System instructions
- Input content
- Retrieval context
- Tool permissions
- Configuration
- Relevant runtime parameters
- Execution timestamps
- Evaluation criteria

Even when these details are recorded, exact reproduction may not be possible because of provider-side changes, stochastic behavior, hidden system state, or external dependencies.

The auditor should distinguish between reproducing the exact historical output and independently evaluating whether the control objective is met under defined test conditions.

A different output during retesting does not automatically prove that the original evidence was inaccurate. The cause and significance of the difference must be evaluated.

### 77. Evidence Reproducibility and Independent Verification

Reproducibility enables another qualified reviewer to understand the evidence collection and analytical process and, where practicable, obtain a consistent result.

The audit file should contain enough information to explain:

- What was tested
- Which system and version were involved
- Which inputs and criteria were used
- How the evidence was obtained
- Which analytical steps were performed
- How exceptions were identified
- How the conclusion was reached

For AI systems, reproducibility may be limited by external model updates, inaccessible provider components, stochastic outputs, or unavailable historical context.

Where exact reproduction is not feasible, the auditor should document the limitation and identify alternative verification procedures.

Examples include validating the historical record's integrity, checking associated deployment metadata, or independently testing the control using an equivalent authorized configuration.

Reproducibility should be treated as a quality objective, not an unsupported guarantee that every AI behavior can be recreated exactly.

### 78. Evidence Redaction and Privacy-Preserving Collection

AI audit evidence may contain personal data, confidential prompts, customer information, credentials, trade secrets, or sensitive security details.

Evidence collection should follow applicable data-protection requirements and organizational handling rules.

Potential techniques include:

- Redaction
- Pseudonymization
- Data minimization
- Field-level filtering
- Controlled test identities
- Synthetic test records
- Restricted evidence repositories
- Separation of identity mappings
- Limited reviewer access

The auditor should ensure that redaction does not remove information essential to the assessment.

For example, replacing a user identifier with a consistent pseudonymous value may preserve the ability to correlate events while reducing unnecessary exposure of identity information.

Where a detailed record is necessary for a restricted investigation, the organization should use appropriate authorization and access controls rather than distributing the unredacted evidence broadly.

The workpaper should document material transformations and any resulting limitations.

### 79. Evidence Access Control and Segregation of Duties

Audit evidence must be protected against unauthorized access, modification, deletion, and disclosure.

Evidence repositories should apply access controls proportionate to the sensitivity of the records.

Potential safeguards include:

- Role-based access
- Least-privilege permissions
- Multifactor authentication
- Read-only access to preserved originals
- Separate administrative and review roles
- Access logging
- Controlled evidence exports
- Periodic permission reviews
- Secure transfer mechanisms

Segregation of duties can reduce the risk that one individual collects, alters, approves, and disposes of the same evidence without independent oversight.

Where practical, evidence collection, technical analysis, and final review should have clearly defined responsibilities.

These controls are especially important when evidence is supplied by the team whose controls are being assessed or when the audit involves a disputed incident.

### 80. Evidence Retention, Legal Holds, and Secure Disposal

Evidence retention should comply with applicable legal obligations, contractual requirements, organizational policies, and professional standards.

A retention process should define:

- Required retention periods
- Evidence categories
- Storage locations
- Legal-hold procedures
- Access restrictions
- Retention extensions
- Disposal approval
- Secure deletion methods
- Disposal records

Where an investigation, litigation, regulatory request, or other preservation obligation applies, normal deletion schedules may need to be suspended according to the organization's legal procedures.

The auditor should not independently decide to delete evidence subject to a legal hold.

At the end of the authorized retention period, evidence should be disposed of securely and the disposal documented where required.

Retention should be proportionate. Preserving every AI prompt and response indefinitely may create unnecessary privacy and security risks.

### 81. Evidence Collection Failure and Recovery Procedures

Evidence collection can fail because of permissions, API errors, service outages, incomplete exports, expired logs, or unsupported formats.

The auditor should distinguish a collection failure from a control failure.

For example, a failed API query may indicate incorrect authentication or an unavailable endpoint. It does not automatically demonstrate that the underlying control is ineffective.

A recovery procedure should include:

1. Recording the failure and its time.
2. Identifying the affected evidence request.
3. Determining the likely cause.
4. Correcting authorized collection parameters or permissions.
5. Repeating the collection where appropriate.
6. Verifying completeness and integrity.
7. Considering alternative evidence sources.
8. Documenting unresolved limitations.

Repeated collection attempts should not overwrite earlier records of failed or partial extraction when those records are relevant to understanding the evidence history.

If the underlying source no longer retains the required information, the auditor should assess whether the missing evidence affects the conclusion or indicates a separate retention-control weakness.

### 82. Evidence Reliability Assessment Matrix

An evidence reliability matrix provides a structured way to assess material evidence before using it in a conclusion.

| Dimension        | Assessment question                                       | Potential warning sign                            |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Source authority | Is the source appropriate for the assertion?              | Unverified or secondary source                    |
| Authenticity     | Can the source be reasonably verified?                    | Unknown origin                                    |
| Integrity        | Is unauthorized modification detectable or controlled?    | Unprotected files or mutable records              |
| Completeness     | Does the evidence cover the required population or event? | Missing records or unexplained gaps               |
| Accuracy         | Does it correctly represent the underlying activity?      | Conflicting or inconsistent values                |
| Timeliness       | Does it relate to the required period?                    | Stale configuration                               |
| Context          | Can the system, version, and environment be identified?   | Unclear deployment reference                      |
| Traceability     | Can the evidence be linked to the test and conclusion?    | Missing identifiers or workpaper references       |
| Corroboration    | Is independent confirmation needed and available?         | Unsupported management assertion                  |
| Handling         | Was the evidence collected and stored appropriately?      | Unrecorded transformations or uncontrolled access |

The matrix should be applied proportionately. Not every item requires identical verification procedures, but material limitations should be addressed before a conclusion is finalized.

### 83. AI Evidence Conflict Resolution and Escalation

Some evidence conflicts cannot be resolved through technical verification alone.

Examples include disagreements about approval authority, uncertain interpretation of a legal requirement, disputed supplier representations, or inconsistent accounts of an AI incident.

The auditor should document:

- The conflicting assertions
- The evidence supporting each position
- The procedures performed
- The remaining uncertainty
- The potential impact on the audit conclusion
- The responsible escalation path

Technical discrepancies may require engineering or forensic expertise. Legal applicability questions may require qualified legal review. Disputes about management authorization may require review by the relevant governance authority.

The auditor should maintain independence and avoid resolving substantive disagreements through unsupported assumptions.

Where the conflict remains unresolved, the conclusion should clearly describe the limitation and its significance.

### 84. AI Evidence Handling for External Assurance

External assurance engagements may require evidence to be shared with auditors, assessors, customers, regulators, or other authorized parties.

Evidence-sharing procedures should define:

- Authorized recipients
- Purpose and scope of disclosure
- Confidentiality restrictions
- Data minimization
- Approved transfer channels
- Evidence integrity requirements
- Access expiration
- Retention responsibilities
- Restrictions on onward disclosure

Organizations should distinguish between evidence required for an assessment and information that is merely convenient to provide.

For example, an external assessor may need proof that an AI service enforces access restrictions without needing unrestricted access to all customer prompts or confidential model artifacts.

Where supplier or provider restrictions limit direct access, alternative assurance mechanisms may be considered, subject to the assessment objective and applicable requirements.

The resulting evidence limitations should be documented rather than hidden.

### 85. Evidence Collection for Regulatory Investigations

Regulatory investigations may require a more formal evidence-preservation and handling process than routine control testing.

The organization should follow applicable legal requirements, regulator instructions, and established investigation procedures.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Policies and procedures
- Decision and approval records
- Model and system documentation
- Risk assessments
- Technical logs
- Relevant AI interactions
- Change histories
- Incident records
- Supplier communications
- Internal investigation findings
- Corrective-action records

The collection process should establish the source, scope, integrity, and relevance of each material item.

Where evidence is exported or transformed, the organization should preserve appropriate provenance and document the method used.

Auditors should not represent an internal evidence-handling process as satisfying a particular legal evidentiary standard without confirming the applicable requirements.

### 86. Evidence Reliability for AI-Generated Reports and Summaries

AI-generated summaries may simplify large volumes of audit evidence, but they can omit important qualifications, merge distinct events, or introduce unsupported details.

When using generated summaries, the auditor should:

1. Identify the underlying source records.
2. Verify material statements against those records.
3. Check that exceptions and contradictory evidence are preserved.
4. Confirm that dates, identifiers, and numerical results are accurate.
5. Record material transformation or summarization steps.
6. Retain the source evidence needed to support the conclusion.
7. Ensure that a qualified reviewer approves material conclusions.

An AI-generated summary should not become the sole evidentiary basis for a significant finding when the underlying records are available and require direct examination.

If the source material is incomplete, the summary should not be treated as a substitute for the missing evidence.

### 87. Evidence Integrity Monitoring and Tamper Detection

Evidence-integrity monitoring detects or helps investigate unauthorized changes to evidence repositories and relevant source records.

Potential safeguards include:

- Immutable or write-once storage
- Append-only audit logs
- File integrity monitoring
- Cryptographic hash verification
- Digital signatures
- Restricted administrative access
- Separation of evidence administration
- Access and modification alerts
- Independent backups

The auditor should evaluate whether the safeguards cover the evidence sources and repositories relevant to the assessment.

A system may protect exported files while leaving the original source records vulnerable to alteration. Conversely, source records may be protected while exported evidence is stored in an uncontrolled location.

Integrity controls should be assessed across the relevant evidence lifecycle.

### 88. Evidence Quality Exceptions and Corrective Actions

Evidence-quality weaknesses may themselves indicate control deficiencies.

Examples include:

- Missing required logs
- Incomplete approval records
- Unreliable version identification
- Inadequate evidence retention
- Unauthorized evidence modification
- Inability to trace decisions to source records
- Incomplete audit trails
- Inconsistent recordkeeping
- Uncontrolled evidence exports

The auditor should determine whether the issue represents an isolated documentation problem, a failure of an operational control, or a systemic weakness in evidence governance.

Corrective actions may include improved logging, standardized evidence templates, stronger access controls, automated collection, revised retention rules, or better source-system reconciliation.

The auditor should validate the corrective action against the original evidence requirement.

For example, introducing a new log format is not sufficient if the system still fails to capture the events needed to reconstruct relevant AI actions.

### 89. Advanced AI Evidence Review Checklist

Before finalizing a material AI audit conclusion, the reviewer should verify that:

- The evidence supports the specific assertion being assessed.
- The relevant AI system, model version, and environment are identifiable.
- The assessment period is clear.
- The source and collection method are documented.
- Material integrity checks have been performed where appropriate.
- Population completeness has been evaluated where relevant.
- Sampling and analytical methods are documented.
- Transformations and redactions are traceable.
- Contradictions and unexplained gaps have been investigated.
- Sensitive information has been handled appropriately.
- Chain-of-custody requirements have been followed where applicable.
- The workpaper links the evidence to the test result.
- The conclusion reflects material limitations.
- Required evidence has been retained according to approved rules.

The depth of review should reflect the significance of the assertion, the sensitivity of the evidence, and the consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

### 90. AI Audit Evidence Integrity — Core Principle

AI audit evidence must remain trustworthy throughout its lifecycle, from source identification and collection to analysis, reporting, retention, and disposal.

Strong evidence governance combines provenance, integrity verification, appropriate access control, reproducible methods, privacy-conscious handling, and explicit documentation of limitations.

For AI systems, these practices are especially important because evidence may span multiple services, model versions, data pipelines, external providers, and probabilistic interactions.

**The governing principle is that an audit conclusion is defensible only when the evidence can be traced to its source, interpreted in its correct context, and shown to be sufficiently reliable for the assertion being made.**

## Part 4 — AI Audit Evidence Evaluation, Reporting, Evidence Governance Maturity, Continuous Assurance and Enterprise Integration

### 91. AI Audit Evidence Evaluation and Conclusion Formation

Evidence evaluation is the process of determining whether collected information sufficiently supports an audit conclusion against established criteria.

For AI governance and security audits, evaluation must connect the evidence to the control objective, the assessed AI system, the relevant period, and the applicable risk or compliance requirement.

The auditor should evaluate:

- Whether the evidence directly addresses the control objective.
- Whether the evidence is sufficiently reliable and complete.
- Whether the evidence covers the relevant AI system and model version.
- Whether the evidence demonstrates control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness as applicable.
- Whether exceptions or contradictory records affect the conclusion.
- Whether the evidence supports the scope and wording of the finding.
- Whether any limitations prevent a definitive conclusion.

Evidence should not be considered sufficient merely because a large volume of documentation has been collected.

For example, an organization may provide an approved AI security policy, a model risk assessment, and a management presentation. These artifacts may support the existence of a governance framework, but they do not necessarily demonstrate that access reviews, model approvals, or monitoring controls operated effectively during the assessment period.

**Core principle:** Evidence must support the specific assertion being made, not merely demonstrate that related documentation exists.

### 92. Evidence-to-Control Traceability Matrix

An evidence-to-control traceability matrix establishes the relationship between audit criteria, control objectives, test procedures, evidence artifacts, results, and conclusions.

This matrix helps reviewers determine whether each material conclusion is supported by identifiable evidence.

A practical structure is:

| Field               | Description                                                     |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Control ID          | Unique identifier for the control under assessment              |
| Control objective   | Intended risk-reduction or compliance outcome                   |
| Assessment criteria | Policy, framework, contractual, or regulatory requirement       |
| AI system scope     | System, model, application, or workflow being assessed          |
| Test procedure      | Method used to evaluate the control                             |
| Evidence reference  | Unique identifier for the supporting evidence                   |
| Evidence period     | Dates covered by the evidence                                   |
| Result              | Pass, fail, partial pass, not tested, or another defined status |
| Exception reference | Identifier for any deviation or deficiency                      |
| Conclusion          | Assessment outcome and supporting rationale                     |
| Reviewer            | Person responsible for review or approval                       |

**Example:**

| Control objective                                         | Evidence                                                  | Test result                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Only authorized personnel can deploy production AI models | IAM role export, deployment logs, approval records        | One deployment identity lacked documented approval                                   |
| AI interactions are monitored for security events         | Monitoring configuration, alert records, incident tickets | Monitoring was enabled, but selected test events did not generate the expected alert |
| High-risk AI changes receive formal approval              | Change tickets, risk assessments, release records         | Two sampled releases lacked evidence of required risk approval                       |

The matrix should preserve direct references to the underlying workpapers and source artifacts.

Internal identifiers such as `AI-CT-001` may be used to organize the knowledge base, but they should not be represented as official ISO, NIST, or statutory identifiers.

### 93. Evidence Sufficiency for Design, Implementation, and Operating Effectiveness

AI control assessment should distinguish among three separate questions.

**Control design:** Is the control appropriately designed to address the identified risk?

Evidence may include control descriptions, architecture decisions, risk assessments, approved procedures, and responsibility assignments.

**Control implementation:** Has the control been put into operation in the relevant environment?

Evidence may include configuration records, deployed technical settings, workflow definitions, access assignments, and production release records.

**Operating effectiveness:** Did the control operate as intended over the relevant period?

Evidence may include transaction samples, recurring access reviews, alert records, approval histories, incident handling records, and repeatable test results.

These dimensions should not be conflated.

For example, an AI deployment approval procedure may be well designed and formally approved. However, if production deployment logs show that releases bypassed the approval workflow, the auditor cannot conclude that the control operated effectively based solely on the procedure.

Where the assessment scope requires only design review or implementation verification, the auditor should state that limitation explicitly rather than imply that operating effectiveness was tested.

### 94. AI Audit Findings and Evidence-Based Root-Cause Analysis

An AI audit finding should be based on a documented difference between an applicable criterion and the observed condition.

A structured finding commonly includes:

1. **Criterion:** What requirement or control expectation applies?
2. **Condition:** What did the auditor observe?
3. **Evidence:** Which records or test results substantiate the condition?
4. **Cause:** Why did the deficiency occur, where the cause can be established?
5. **Risk or impact:** What actual or potential consequence could arise?
6. **Recommendation:** What action could address the identified weakness?
7. **Management response:** How does the responsible owner respond?
8. **Corrective-action owner and target date:** Who is accountable, and when is remediation expected?

**Example:**

- **Criterion:** Production AI model changes must receive documented approval before deployment.
- **Condition:** Two releases in the selected assessment population lacked corresponding approval records.
- **Evidence:** Deployment events, change tickets, and approval workflow exports.
- **Potential cause:** The release process permitted an alternative deployment path that did not enforce approval validation.
- **Risk:** Unauthorized or inadequately assessed changes could affect model behavior, security, or regulatory compliance.
- **Recommendation:** Enforce approval validation across all production deployment paths and implement exception monitoring.

The cause should be labeled as confirmed, probable, or undetermined where appropriate. The auditor should not present speculation as an established fact.

### 95. Evidence Limitations and Qualified Audit Conclusions

Evidence limitations arise when the auditor cannot obtain or validate enough information to support a definitive conclusion.

Common causes include:

- Incomplete logging
- Missing historical records
- Restricted provider access
- Unavailable model artifacts
- Unclear model-version mappings
- Inconsistent timestamps
- Unreconciled populations
- Unreliable management representations
- Inability to reproduce a historical event
- Technical constraints that prevent a required test

The auditor should assess whether each limitation is isolated or affects a material part of the audit scope.

Possible reporting outcomes may include:

- An unqualified conclusion where sufficient appropriate evidence supports it.
- A qualified or limited conclusion for a specific area where the methodology permits such reporting.
- A statement that the control could not be assessed.
- A scope limitation or disclaimer where the missing evidence prevents an overall conclusion.
- A finding concerning the evidence-management control itself, where justified by the criteria.

The terminology used must follow the organization's audit methodology and the applicable professional or regulatory requirements.

An inability to obtain evidence does not automatically prove that the underlying control failed. However, failure to retain required records may itself constitute a control deficiency.

### 96. Management Representations and Supporting Evidence

Management representations can provide context about control ownership, operating procedures, risk acceptance, and system behavior.

Examples include statements that:

- All production AI systems are registered.
- High-risk model changes require approval.
- AI-generated outputs are reviewed by qualified personnel.
- Security alerts are investigated within defined timeframes.
- Third-party AI providers undergo risk assessments.

Such representations should be evaluated according to their significance and corroborated where appropriate.

The auditor may compare them with:

- System configuration
- Transaction records
- Audit logs
- Change histories
- Risk registers
- Incident tickets
- Independent assurance reports
- Interviews with control operators

If management states that all AI systems are inventoried, the auditor should consider reconciling the inventory against available cloud resources, procurement records, application catalogs, or other relevant discovery sources.

A signed representation does not automatically establish operating effectiveness or eliminate the need for other evidence.

### 97. AI Audit Workpapers and Evidence Documentation

Audit workpapers provide the documented basis for the procedures performed, evidence examined, results obtained, and conclusions reached.

A complete workpaper should enable a qualified reviewer to understand the work without relying solely on the original auditor's memory or undocumented explanations.

Typical elements include:

- Audit objective and scope
- Applicable criteria
- Risk and control references
- System and model identifiers
- Assessment period
- Test procedure
- Population and sample methodology
- Evidence references
- Execution details
- Results and exceptions
- Evidence limitations
- Conclusion and rationale
- Reviewer comments
- Review and approval history

For technical AI tests, the workpaper may also include test inputs, expected results, actual results, environment details, relevant configuration, and links to supporting logs.

Sensitive prompts, personal data, credentials, and confidential model artifacts should not be copied into workpapers unnecessarily. References to appropriately controlled evidence repositories may be used where suitable.

The level of detail should be sufficient to support the conclusion and enable independent review while respecting confidentiality and data-protection obligations.

### 98. Evidence Review and Independent Quality Assurance

Independent review helps detect unsupported conclusions, incomplete testing, incorrect interpretations, and evidence-handling errors.

The reviewer should assess whether:

- The audit objective is clearly defined.
- The selected criteria are applicable.
- The test procedure addresses the control objective.
- The population and sample are appropriate.
- Evidence references are accurate.
- Evidence is sufficiently reliable for the conclusion.
- Exceptions are correctly classified.
- Contradictory information has been considered.
- Findings distinguish facts from assumptions.
- Limitations are transparently reported.
- The conclusion follows logically from the evidence.

For complex AI assessments, specialist review may be needed for model behavior, cybersecurity architecture, data protection, statistical methods, or regulatory interpretation.

Independence requirements should be appropriate to the assurance function and engagement.

A reviewer should not approve a conclusion merely because the workpaper is complete or the evidence volume is substantial.

### 99. AI Evidence Governance Roles and Accountability

Effective evidence governance requires defined responsibilities across the AI lifecycle.

Potential roles include:

- **AI system owner:** Identifies system-specific evidence and maintains relevant operational records.
- **Control owner:** Demonstrates control operation and addresses deficiencies.
- **AI governance function:** Defines evidence expectations and coordinates governance records.
- **Security operations:** Preserves relevant security events and investigation records.
- **Data governance function:** Maintains data lineage, quality, access, and provenance records.
- **Model development team:** Maintains model development, evaluation, and release artifacts.
- **Privacy function:** Advises on lawful, proportionate handling of personal data.
- **Legal function:** Advises on legal holds, disclosure obligations, and applicable legal requirements.
- **Internal audit:** Independently evaluates evidence and control effectiveness within its mandate.
- **External assessor or auditor:** Examines evidence within the agreed engagement scope.

The organization should establish clear ownership for evidence creation, collection, preservation, access, review, and disposal.

Responsibility for producing evidence should not be confused with responsibility for independently assessing its reliability.

### 100. AI Evidence Repository Architecture

A centralized or federated evidence repository can improve traceability, access governance, and audit readiness.

The appropriate design depends on the organization's size, architecture, confidentiality requirements, and regulatory obligations.

A repository may contain:

- Evidence request registers
- Evidence metadata
- Control mappings
- Source references
- Collection timestamps
- Integrity verification records
- Test workpapers
- Exception records
- Reviewer approvals
- Retention classifications
- Access logs
- Corrective-action references

In a federated model, the evidence may remain in the source system while the repository stores metadata, controlled references, and verification results.

This can reduce unnecessary duplication of sensitive information.

The organization should evaluate availability, integrity, confidentiality, access segregation, retention, backup, and recovery requirements.

A repository should not be treated as authoritative merely because it is centrally managed. Its own access controls, change history, and evidence-ingestion procedures must be assessed.

### 102. Automated Evidence Collection and Validation

Automation can improve consistency and reduce the manual effort required to collect recurring evidence.

Potential use cases include:

- Scheduled configuration exports
- Automated access-review reports
- Collection of deployment histories
- Evidence of policy enforcement
- Log-retention verification
- AI inventory reconciliation
- Model-version validation
- Security alert testing
- Evidence completeness checks
- Automated integrity verification

Automated collection should be governed as a control process rather than treated as inherently reliable.

The organization should validate:

1. The source and scope of the collection.
2. The permissions used by the collection mechanism.
3. The accuracy of filters and query logic.
4. Pagination and record-count handling.
5. Failure detection and alerting.
6. Output integrity and storage.
7. Versioning of collection scripts or workflows.
8. Independent review of material exceptions.

A successful job status does not prove that the correct records were collected.

For example, an automated export may complete successfully while excluding a newly introduced AI deployment region. Completeness checks and reconciliation remain necessary.

### 103. Continuous Evidence Collection and Assurance Readiness

Continuous evidence collection captures selected control information on a recurring or event-driven basis.

It can help organizations detect control degradation before the next formal audit.

Examples include:

- Daily reconciliation of production AI deployments with the inventory
- Continuous monitoring of privileged AI service identities
- Event-driven capture of model release approvals
- Scheduled validation of log retention
- Periodic checks of high-risk AI control exceptions
- Automated notification of overdue risk reviews
- Collection of evidence following significant configuration changes

The frequency should reflect the risk, volatility, and operational characteristics of the control.

Continuous collection does not mean that every record must be reviewed manually in real time. Automated rules may identify exceptions for investigation.

The organization should define how alerts are triaged, how false positives are handled, and how unresolved exceptions are escalated.

Continuous evidence supports assurance readiness, but it does not eliminate the need for periodic independent evaluation of the evidence process itself.

### 104. Evidence Governance for AI Changes and Releases

AI systems may change through model updates, prompt modifications, retrieval-index refreshes, tool integrations, data pipeline changes, or modifications to access permissions.

Evidence governance should ensure that material changes remain traceable to the relevant approvals and risk assessments.

For each significant release, the organization may retain:

- Change request
- Change owner
- Risk assessment
- Security and privacy review
- Testing and evaluation results
- Approval records
- Artifact or version identifiers
- Deployment timestamp
- Rollback plan
- Post-deployment validation
- Relevant monitoring results

The auditor should verify that the evidence corresponds to the version actually deployed.

An approval associated with an earlier model version does not necessarily support a later release.

Where emergency changes are permitted, the organization should define the required authorization, documentation, and retrospective review process.

### 105. Evidence Governance for AI Suppliers and External Providers

AI services may depend on external models, cloud platforms, data providers, retrieval services, software libraries, or specialized AI vendors.

The organization should define which evidence it expects from each supplier and how that evidence will be evaluated.

Potential evidence includes:

- Security assurance reports
- Relevant certifications and scope statements
- Service descriptions
- Subprocessor information
- Incident notifications
- Vulnerability management summaries
- Change notifications
- Data-processing terms
- Service-level reports
- Model documentation
- Risk assessment responses
- Business continuity information

The auditor should determine whether supplier evidence is current, relevant to the service, and sufficient for the organization's risk exposure.

A supplier certification does not automatically demonstrate that every relevant control is effective in the customer's deployment.

Where direct testing or evidence access is unavailable, the organization should document the limitation and determine whether alternative assurance procedures are adequate.

### 106. Evidence Governance for AI Regulatory Obligations

AI evidence requirements vary according to the applicable jurisdiction, system classification, sector, contractual commitments, and organizational role.

A regulatory evidence matrix should map each applicable obligation to:

- The relevant legal or regulatory source
- The applicability rationale
- The responsible owner
- Required records or technical artifacts
- Retention requirements, where specified
- Review and approval requirements
- Evidence location
- Testing method
- Identified gaps
- Remediation status

The organization should distinguish explicit legal recordkeeping requirements from internal evidence practices adopted to demonstrate governance maturity.

For example, where the EU AI Act applies, the organization should assess the documentation, logging, monitoring, and recordkeeping duties applicable to its particular role and AI system classification. Requirements should be verified against the applicable legal text and relevant implementation provisions rather than inferred from a generic checklist.

The auditor should record the version and effective period of the criteria used for the assessment.

### 107. Evidence Quality Metrics and Key Risk Indicators

Evidence governance can be monitored using quantitative measures.

Potential indicators include:

| Metric                           | Example calculation or definition                                                    | Purpose                                  |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| Evidence request completion rate | Completed requests divided by total requests                                         | Measures evidence availability           |
| Evidence completeness rate       | Items meeting defined completeness criteria divided by items reviewed                | Measures record completeness             |
| Evidence rejection rate          | Items rejected during quality review divided by items reviewed                       | Identifies quality problems              |
| Unresolved evidence gaps         | Count of material gaps still open                                                    | Highlights assurance limitations         |
| Traceability coverage            | Material findings with linked supporting evidence divided by total material findings | Measures conclusion traceability         |
| Integrity verification success   | Successful verifications divided by verification attempts                            | Monitors integrity controls              |
| Collection failure rate          | Failed collection jobs divided by total collection jobs                              | Identifies collection reliability issues |
| Overdue evidence actions         | Open evidence-related corrective actions past target date                            | Monitors remediation                     |

Metrics should have defined owners, data sources, calculation rules, reporting frequency, and escalation thresholds.

A high evidence completion rate does not automatically imply high evidence quality. Measures should be interpreted together and supported by periodic qualitative review.

### 108. AI Evidence Governance Maturity Model

An evidence governance maturity model helps an organization assess the consistency and reliability of its evidence practices.

The following model is an illustrative internal framework, not an official maturity scale issued by ISO, NIST, or a regulator.

| Level | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                                            |
| ----- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Ad hoc     | Evidence is collected reactively, with inconsistent ownership and limited traceability                     |
| 2     | Repeatable | Basic evidence requests, storage practices, and responsibility assignments exist                           |
| 3     | Defined    | Standardized evidence procedures, metadata, quality criteria, and retention rules are established          |
| 4     | Measured   | Evidence completeness, reliability, collection performance, and exceptions are measured                    |
| 5     | Optimized  | Risk-based automation, continuous assurance, advanced analytics, and systematic improvement are integrated |

An organization may operate at different maturity levels across evidence domains.

For example, cloud security logs may be collected automatically with strong integrity controls, while model evaluation documentation remains fragmented across development teams.

The assessment should identify domain-specific gaps rather than assign a high overall maturity rating based solely on the strongest areas.

### 109. Common AI Audit Evidence Anti-Patterns

Evidence anti-patterns are recurring practices that weaken the reliability or defensibility of audit conclusions.

Common examples include:

- **Policy substitution:** Treating an approved policy as proof that controls operate effectively.
- **Screenshot dependence:** Using screenshots without sufficient source, date, context, or completeness verification.
- **Version ambiguity:** Failing to identify the model or system version represented by the evidence.
- **Uncontrolled exports:** Storing sensitive evidence without adequate access restrictions.
- **Selective evidence:** Reviewing only favorable records while ignoring exceptions.
- **Unsupported summaries:** Relying on management or AI-generated summaries without checking material assertions.
- **Population uncertainty:** Drawing conclusions from a dataset whose completeness is unknown.
- **Unrecorded transformation:** Filtering or modifying evidence without preserving the original or documenting the method.
- **False reproducibility:** Assuming a probabilistic AI output can always be recreated exactly.
- **Unqualified conclusions:** Reporting successful control operation despite material evidence gaps.
- **Unverified supplier claims:** Accepting provider statements without assessing their relevance or support.
- **Retention mismatch:** Deleting evidence before the required retention period or preserving it longer than justified.

These anti-patterns should be addressed through audit methodology, training, quality review, and evidence-governance controls.

### 110. AI Audit Evidence and the Three Lines Model

Evidence governance supports the distinct responsibilities commonly described by the Three Lines Model.

**First line — operational management:** Owns AI systems, operates controls, maintains operational records, and provides evidence of control execution.

**Second line — risk and compliance functions:** Establishes requirements, monitors risk exposure, challenges control owners, and evaluates evidence quality within its mandate.

**Third line — internal audit:** Provides independent assurance on governance, risk management, and control effectiveness, subject to its charter and applicable professional standards.

External auditors, regulators, certification bodies, and independent assessors may perform additional assurance activities outside this internal model.

The organization should preserve the distinction between management's responsibility for controls and internal audit's responsibility for independent assessment.

Internal audit should not assume ownership of the operational evidence-production process in a way that compromises its independence.

### 111. AI Audit Evidence and Risk-Based Audit Planning

Evidence planning should reflect the organization's AI risk profile and the assurance objectives of the engagement.

Higher-risk systems may require more extensive evidence procedures because of their potential impact, regulatory classification, autonomy, access to sensitive information, or ability to execute consequential actions.

Planning considerations include:

- Potential harm and business impact
- AI system classification
- Exposure to external users
- Access to sensitive data
- Degree of autonomy
- Tool execution and transaction capabilities
- Supplier dependency
- Change frequency
- Historical incidents
- Control maturity
- Applicable legal obligations
- Availability and reliability of evidence

The auditor should prioritize evidence that supports material risk assertions rather than collecting artifacts indiscriminately.

For an agentic AI system that can execute financial or administrative actions, evidence concerning identity, authorization, tool invocation, transaction approval, and monitoring may be more important than general descriptive documentation.

Risk-based evidence planning should be revisited when significant changes or new information emerge during the engagement.

### 112. AI Audit Evidence and Control Failure Analysis

Evidence should help establish not only whether a control failed, but also the nature and extent of the failure.

The auditor should distinguish among:

- Control design deficiency
- Implementation failure
- Operating failure
- Evidence-retention failure
- Monitoring failure
- Exception-management failure
- Governance or accountability weakness
- Supplier assurance limitation

These categories may overlap.

For example, missing records of AI deployment approval could result from an inadequately designed workflow, an operational bypass, an incomplete logging configuration, or premature log deletion.

The auditor should perform sufficient procedures to distinguish among plausible explanations before assigning a root cause.

Where the cause cannot be established, the report should describe the observed condition and the remaining uncertainty.

### 113. Evidence-Based Corrective-Action Validation

Corrective-action validation determines whether management's remediation addresses the identified deficiency and operates as intended.

A remediation claim should be supported by evidence appropriate to the original finding.

A typical validation process includes:

1. Reconfirming the original finding and control objective.
2. Reviewing the approved remediation plan.
3. Verifying implementation in the relevant environment.
4. Inspecting updated configurations or procedures.
5. Testing the revised control.
6. Reviewing evidence from a relevant operating period where required.
7. Confirming that the original deficiency has been addressed.
8. Assessing whether residual risk remains.
9. Documenting the validation result.
10. Recording formal closure or continued monitoring.

**Example:** Management introduces a mandatory approval gate for production AI deployments. Validation should confirm that the gate is enabled, that authorized approval is required, and that attempted bypasses are blocked or detected. Depending on the original deficiency, evidence from subsequent releases may also be required.

A policy update or management confirmation alone may be insufficient to close an operational control finding.

### 114. Evidence-Based Management Review

Management review should consider evidence about AI governance performance, control effectiveness, emerging risks, and unresolved deficiencies.

Relevant inputs may include:

- AI inventory and risk-register status
- Control-testing results
- Security incidents
- Monitoring trends
- Model evaluation outcomes
- Supplier assurance findings
- Regulatory changes
- Audit findings
- Corrective-action status
- Evidence-quality metrics
- Resource constraints
- Significant changes to AI systems

The review should produce documented decisions, assigned actions, accountable owners, and target dates where appropriate.

Evidence of management review should establish more than the existence of a meeting. It should demonstrate that relevant information was considered and that decisions or actions were recorded.

Where management accepts residual risk, the auditor should verify the required authority, rationale, scope, and review conditions according to organizational policy.

### 115. Enterprise AI Assurance and Evidence Reuse

Organizations may be able to reuse evidence across internal audit, security assessments, compliance reviews, supplier assessments, and management assurance activities.

Reuse can reduce duplicated effort, but only when the evidence remains appropriate for the new purpose.

Before reusing evidence, the organization should assess:

- Whether the evidence covers the same system and environment
- Whether the assessment period remains relevant
- Whether the control criteria are equivalent
- Whether the original collection method was reliable
- Whether the evidence has changed
- Whether the original assessor's scope is sufficient
- Whether the intended user can rely on the original assurance
- Whether contractual or confidentiality restrictions permit reuse

A security certification or supplier assurance report may support part of an AI assessment, but it should not be treated as conclusive evidence of all AI-specific requirements.

Evidence reuse should be documented, including the original source, intended use, limitations, and any supplementary procedures performed.

### 116. Evidence Interoperability Across Governance Frameworks

Organizations often map AI assurance evidence to several overlapping frameworks and requirements.

Examples may include ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, internal security policies, contractual obligations, and applicable AI regulation.

A common evidence repository can support multiple mappings, provided the organization does not assume that similar wording establishes identical requirements.

For each mapping, the organization should evaluate:

- The purpose of each criterion
- The applicable organizational scope
- Required control outcomes
- Evidence requirements
- Assessment methods
- Relevant time periods
- Differences in terminology
- Additional requirements not covered by the shared evidence

For example, evidence of information-security access control may support an AI security assessment, but it may not demonstrate that human oversight, model validation, or AI-specific risk-management requirements are satisfied.

Framework mapping should therefore identify both reusable evidence and remaining assessment gaps.

### 117. AI Audit Evidence Program Operating Model

An enterprise evidence program defines how evidence is governed across AI systems, assurance functions, and business units.

Core operating-model components include:

- Evidence governance policy
- Evidence taxonomy and metadata standards
- Defined roles and responsibilities
- Risk-based collection requirements
- Approved collection tools
- Evidence repository architecture
- Integrity and access controls
- Retention and disposal procedures
- Evidence quality standards
- Control and framework mappings
- Exception and escalation procedures
- Reporting metrics
- Independent quality assurance
- Continuous improvement

The operating model should integrate with existing governance, risk, compliance, security, privacy, records-management, and internal-audit processes where appropriate.

It should also distinguish mandatory evidence requirements from recommended practices.

A mature evidence program reduces fragmented documentation, improves audit readiness, and supports consistent conclusions without encouraging unnecessary collection or indefinite retention.

### 118. AI Audit Evidence Program Implementation Roadmap

A practical implementation roadmap can be organized into five phases.

**Phase 1 — Discover and assess**

- Identify AI systems and assurance stakeholders.
- Review existing evidence repositories and collection processes.
- Identify recurring evidence gaps and retention weaknesses.
- Establish the initial risk-based priorities.

**Phase 2 — Define standards**

- Approve evidence taxonomy and metadata.
- Define ownership and handling requirements.
- Establish evidence-quality and traceability criteria.
- Map key controls to required evidence.

**Phase 3 — Implement governance**

- Configure approved repositories and access controls.
- Establish collection and preservation procedures.
- Introduce workpaper and evidence-request templates.
- Define exception handling and review responsibilities.

**Phase 4 — Automate and measure**

- Automate suitable recurring collection activities.
- Implement completeness and integrity checks.
- Establish evidence-quality metrics.
- Integrate collection failures with operational monitoring.

**Phase 5 — Assure and improve**

- Perform independent reviews of the evidence program.
- Test evidence reliability and control traceability.
- Address recurring deficiencies.
- Update standards for new AI architectures and regulatory requirements.

Implementation should be proportionate to organizational scale, system risk, regulatory exposure, and available resources.

### 119. AI Audit Evidence Program Effectiveness Assessment

The effectiveness of the evidence program should be assessed periodically using both quantitative and qualitative procedures.

The assessment should determine whether:

- Material AI controls have defined evidence requirements.
- Evidence owners and responsibilities are established.
- Required records are available for the relevant period.
- Evidence can be traced to authoritative sources.
- Integrity and access controls operate effectively.
- Sensitive information is handled appropriately.
- Evidence transformations are documented.
- Control findings are supported by appropriate evidence.
- Collection failures are detected and addressed.
- Retention and disposal requirements are followed.
- Corrective actions are validated.
- Evidence supports timely and reliable assurance conclusions.

The assessment should include representative evidence from different AI risk domains rather than relying only on repository statistics or management reports.

Results should be used to prioritize improvement initiatives and determine whether the evidence program continues to support the organization's assurance needs.

### 120. AI Audit Evidence — Enterprise Assurance Principle

AI audit evidence is a foundational component of defensible AI governance, security, compliance, and assurance.

An effective evidence program connects requirements to controls, controls to tests, tests to source records, and source records to conclusions. It preserves integrity and provenance, addresses privacy and confidentiality, documents limitations, and supports independent review.

The objective is not to collect the largest possible volume of AI documentation. It is to maintain **sufficient, appropriate, traceable, and reliable evidence** to support decisions about AI risk and control effectiveness.

An enterprise AI assurance program should therefore treat evidence governance as a lifecycle capability integrated with AI development, deployment, monitoring, incident response, third-party management, regulatory compliance, and internal audit.

**Final principle:** No material AI assurance conclusion should exceed what the available evidence can reasonably support.
