# 19.11 — AI Documentation Standards, Regulatory Records, Technical Transparency and Evidence Requirements

# Part 1 — Documentation Standards, Regulatory Obligations, Transparency and Accountability

## 1. Understanding AI Documentation as a Governance Control

AI documentation is a foundational governance control that enables organizations to demonstrate how AI systems are designed, developed, assessed, approved, deployed, monitored, and maintained. It provides a structured record of system behavior, technical characteristics, risks, control implementation, and governance decisions.

In AI security governance, documentation serves three primary purposes:

- **Operational purpose:** Enables engineers, security teams, system owners, and operators to understand and manage AI systems.
- **Assurance purpose:** Provides evidence that defined controls, assessments, approvals, and monitoring activities have been performed.
- **Accountability purpose:** Establishes who made decisions, which information informed those decisions, and what responsibilities were assigned.

AI documentation should not be treated as a collection of static files prepared only for audits. It should reflect the actual system and remain synchronized with material changes throughout the AI lifecycle.

For example, a model risk assessment completed before deployment may become unreliable after the model receives new capabilities, gains access to additional data sources, or begins executing external tools. Documentation governance must ensure that such changes trigger appropriate reassessment.

A mature documentation control establishes clear ownership, authoritative sources, version control, review requirements, evidence traceability, and retention rules.

**Governance principle:** Documentation is effective when it enables stakeholders to understand the system, verify relevant claims, reconstruct material decisions, and establish whether applicable controls are operating as intended.

## 2. Distinguishing Documentation, Records, and Evidence

The terms documentation, records, and evidence are related, but they serve different purposes within AI governance.

**Documentation** describes a system, process, requirement, architecture, methodology, or intended operating procedure.

Examples include:

- AI governance policies.
- Security architecture documents.
- Model cards.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Incident-response procedures.
- Model evaluation methodologies.

**Records** capture activities, events, approvals, or decisions that have occurred.

Examples include:

- Deployment approvals.
- Access-review records.
- Model release histories.
- Incident timelines.
- Change requests.
- Risk acceptance decisions.

**Evidence** supports a particular assertion or conclusion.

Examples include:

- Configuration exports demonstrating an enabled security control.
- Test results supporting a validation conclusion.
- Audit logs demonstrating a privileged action.
- Signed approval records.
- Monitoring data supporting an operational assessment.

A single artifact may function as documentation, a record, and evidence depending on its context. An approved risk assessment is documentation of identified risks, a record of the assessment activity, and potentially evidence that the assessment process was performed.

However, the existence of an artifact does not automatically make it sufficient evidence for every claim associated with it.

For example, a security architecture document may describe the intended access-control design but cannot, by itself, prove that production permissions match the documented design.

Organizations should identify the purpose of each artifact and define the assurance claims it is intended to support.

## 3. Establishing the Relationship Between AI Documentation and Governance Accountability

Documentation provides the mechanism through which governance responsibilities can be demonstrated and challenged.

AI systems may involve multiple stakeholders, including business owners, model developers, data providers, platform operators, security teams, risk managers, compliance officers, and external AI service providers. Without clear records, responsibility for critical decisions can become fragmented.

An accountability record should establish:

- The person or function responsible for the system.
- The owner of the relevant risk.
- The individuals responsible for implementing controls.
- The person authorized to approve deployment or material changes.
- The reviewers responsible for independent challenge.
- The authority responsible for accepting residual risk.
- The process for escalating unresolved issues.

For example, an engineering team may implement a security control, but the designated risk owner may remain accountable for the decision to operate the system with a known residual risk.

Documentation should preserve that distinction.

An accountability model should also identify delegated authorities and their boundaries. A project manager may approve routine project changes without having authority to accept material cybersecurity or regulatory risks.

When governance decisions are made by committees, records should identify the decision-making body, applicable quorum or approval rules where relevant, the decision, and any conditions imposed.

The objective is to establish a defensible chain from operational activity to accountable governance decisions.

## 4. Mapping AI Documentation to the System Lifecycle

AI documentation requirements vary throughout the lifecycle because different risks and decisions arise at different stages.

A lifecycle-based documentation model identifies the artifacts required before a system progresses to the next stage.

| Lifecycle stage | Typical documentation                                          | Governance objective                  |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Initiation      | Use-case description, system owner, preliminary risk screening | Establish scope and accountability    |
| Procurement     | Provider assessment, contractual review, service documentation | Evaluate external dependencies        |
| Design          | Architecture, data flows, threat model                         | Identify technical and security risks |
| Development     | Version history, model lineage, development records            | Preserve technical traceability       |
| Validation      | Test plan, evaluation results, limitations                     | Assess system suitability             |
| Deployment      | Approval records, release configuration, operating procedures  | Authorize controlled production use   |
| Operation       | Monitoring records, access reviews, incident records           | Maintain ongoing assurance            |
| Change          | Impact assessments, regression tests, revised approvals        | Manage evolving risk                  |
| Decommissioning | Shutdown approval, access revocation, data disposition         | Ensure controlled retirement          |

Not every AI system requires the same depth of documentation. Requirements should be proportionate to system risk, complexity, intended purpose, data sensitivity, autonomy, and applicable obligations.

For instance, an internal assistant that summarizes public documents may require a different evidence package from an AI agent that accesses confidential records and executes transactions.

The lifecycle model should identify mandatory artifacts, conditional requirements, responsible owners, and the events that trigger reassessment.

## 5. Understanding the Purpose of AI Documentation Standards

AI documentation standards establish consistent requirements for creating, maintaining, reviewing, and preserving information about AI systems.

They help organizations avoid inconsistent templates, missing metadata, ambiguous ownership, undocumented changes, and unreliable evidence.

A documentation standard should define:

- Required artifact types.
- Minimum content and metadata.
- Naming and identification conventions.
- Ownership and approval responsibilities.
- Version-control requirements.
- Review and update triggers.
- Classification and access restrictions.
- Evidence integrity expectations.
- Retention and disposal requirements.
- Traceability to risks, controls, and decisions.

Standards should distinguish between mandatory organizational requirements and recommended practices.

A mandatory internal rule may require every production AI system to have an assigned owner and approved risk assessment. A recommended practice may suggest additional technical details for systems with particular architectures.

External frameworks may inform the documentation standard, but the organization must determine how each external requirement applies to its specific context.

A documentation standard should not simply copy terminology from multiple frameworks without reconciling overlaps, differences, and applicability.

The objective is a coherent internal standard that provides consistent operational requirements while preserving necessary distinctions between legal obligations, contractual requirements, and voluntary guidance.

## 6. Understanding ISO/IEC 42001 and AI Management System Documentation

ISO/IEC 42001 establishes requirements for an AI management system (AIMS). Its management-system approach addresses organizational policies, objectives, processes, responsibilities, performance evaluation, and continual improvement relating to AI.

Documentation supports the management system by preserving information about how governance arrangements are established and operated.

Relevant documented information may include:

- AI management system scope.
- AI policy and objectives.
- Assigned roles and responsibilities.
- AI risk assessment and treatment processes.
- Applicable control and governance arrangements.
- Operational planning and change management.
- Performance evaluation and internal audit.
- Management review.
- Corrective actions and continual improvement.

The exact documentation required depends on the applicable standard clauses, organizational context, and the way the management system is implemented.

Organizations should distinguish between maintaining documented information and retaining documented information. Some information must remain current and controlled, while other records must be preserved as evidence that an activity occurred.

For example, a current AI policy should be maintained as a controlled document, while completed internal audit results should be retained as records.

An ISO/IEC 42001-aligned documentation program should demonstrate that the AI management system operates as a coherent governance process rather than as an isolated collection of documents.

**Important distinction:** Maintaining documentation that appears aligned with ISO/IEC 42001 does not, by itself, establish conformity or certification. The management system must be evaluated against the applicable requirements.

## 7. Understanding the Role of ISO/IEC 23894 in AI Risk Documentation

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on AI risk management. It helps organizations integrate AI-specific risk considerations into their risk management activities.

Documentation is important because AI risks may arise from data, model behavior, system integration, operational context, human interaction, and changes over time.

A risk documentation process should establish:

- Risk identification methods.
- Risk analysis assumptions.
- Risk evaluation criteria.
- Risk treatment options.
- Risk owners and responsibilities.
- Residual risk decisions.
- Monitoring and review activities.
- Communication and consultation arrangements.

For example, a generative AI application may create risks involving prompt injection, confidential-data disclosure, hallucinated outputs, unauthorized tool execution, or reliance on inaccurate information.

A risk record should identify the relevant scenario, affected assets, plausible consequences, likelihood or other evaluation factors, existing controls, and required treatment.

Risk documentation should distinguish inherent risk from residual risk. Inherent risk reflects exposure before considering relevant controls, while residual risk reflects the risk remaining after the controls and treatments considered in the assessment.

The organization should also record material assumptions and uncertainties. Where the behavior of an external model cannot be fully explained, the assessment should state the limitation and identify how it affects the risk decision.

Risk records must remain linked to the controls, evidence, and decisions used to address the identified risks.

## 8. Understanding the Role of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides a voluntary framework for managing AI-related risks. Its core functions are **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**.

Documentation supports each function.

| NIST AI RMF function | Documentation contribution                                                  |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Govern               | Policies, roles, accountability records, risk-management procedures         |
| Map                  | System context, intended use, stakeholders, impacts, and dependencies       |
| Measure              | Evaluation methods, test results, performance measurements, risk indicators |
| Manage               | Risk treatment plans, decisions, monitoring records, and incident responses |

Under **Govern**, organizations document the structures that assign responsibility and establish risk-management expectations.

Under **Map**, they record the system's context, intended purpose, affected stakeholders, and potential impacts.

Under **Measure**, they preserve the methods and results used to assess performance, security, reliability, and other relevant characteristics.

Under **Manage**, they document how identified risks are prioritized, treated, monitored, accepted, or escalated.

The NIST AI RMF should be applied according to the organization's context and selected implementation approach. It does not automatically prescribe one universal set of artifacts for every AI system.

Organizations should map their internal documentation requirements to the relevant AI RMF concepts while preserving clear references to the source framework and the organization's own control identifiers.

A mapping demonstrates how internal processes relate to the framework; it does not independently establish that risks have been adequately managed.

## 9. Distinguishing Legal Requirements from Standards and Internal Policies

AI documentation programs commonly combine requirements from legislation, regulations, contracts, external standards, and internal policies.

These sources have different authority and applicability.

**Legal requirements** arise from applicable laws and regulations. Their applicability depends on factors such as jurisdiction, system classification, intended purpose, organizational role, and relevant circumstances.

**Contractual requirements** arise from binding agreements, such as data-processing agreements, supplier contracts, or customer commitments.

**External standards and frameworks** may establish formal requirements or provide voluntary guidance, depending on the instrument and how it is used.

**Internal policies** establish organizational expectations and may impose requirements beyond external minimums.

A documentation mapping should identify the source of each requirement and the rationale for its applicability.

For example, an organization may impose a requirement for independent validation of certain high-risk AI systems as an internal control. That requirement should not be described as a universal legal obligation unless an applicable law or regulation actually imposes it.

Similarly, a voluntary framework mapping should not be presented as proof of statutory compliance.

A well-governed requirements register should record:

- Requirement identifier.
- Source and reference.
- Requirement wording or controlled summary.
- Applicability rationale.
- Responsible owner.
- Related risks and controls.
- Evidence requirements.
- Assessment status.
- Known gaps and remediation actions.

This separation supports defensible compliance conclusions and reduces the risk of overstating regulatory obligations.

## 10. Establishing AI Documentation Requirements Under the EU AI Act

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes a risk-based legal framework for AI systems and assigns obligations according to factors including the relevant system category, intended purpose, and the roles of the organizations involved.

Documentation obligations are not identical for all AI systems. Depending on applicability, relevant requirements may concern technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, instructions for use, quality management, human oversight, monitoring, and post-market activities.

For systems classified as high-risk under the applicable provisions, technical documentation can be particularly important for demonstrating conformity with relevant requirements.

Depending on the applicable legal provisions and system characteristics, documentation may need to address matters such as:

- General system description and intended purpose.
- Design specifications and system architecture.
- Development and validation processes.
- Data-related information.
- Risk management.
- Performance and accuracy.
- Robustness and cybersecurity.
- Human oversight measures.
- Relevant testing and validation results.
- Changes and version history.
- Post-deployment monitoring arrangements.

The precise requirements depend on the relevant provisions, applicable dates, system classification, and organizational role. Not every listed item applies identically to every AI system.

Organizations should also distinguish between the documentation required of providers and the obligations that may apply to deployers or other actors.

A compliance assessment should therefore document the system's legal classification, organizational role, applicable provisions, evidence requirements, and any relevant transitional arrangements.

**Governance principle:** EU AI Act documentation must be mapped to applicable legal provisions and supported by actual implementation evidence. A generic AI documentation package is not sufficient to establish compliance.

## 11. Understanding Technical Documentation for High-Risk AI Systems

Technical documentation explains how an AI system is constructed, what it is intended to do, how it was assessed, and how relevant risks and performance characteristics are addressed.

For high-risk AI systems subject to applicable technical documentation requirements, the documentation should be sufficiently detailed to support the required conformity assessment and regulatory scrutiny.

Depending on the applicable requirements, the technical package may describe:

- System purpose and intended users.
- Provider and system identification.
- System architecture and component relationships.
- Development methodology.
- Data requirements and data-processing practices.
- Model characteristics and relevant limitations.
- Validation and testing methods.
- Performance and robustness results.
- Risk management measures.
- Cybersecurity controls.
- Human oversight arrangements.
- Instructions for use.
- Version history and relevant modifications.

Technical documentation should be consistent with the system actually supplied or deployed.

For example, a technical file that describes one model version cannot automatically be assumed to describe a materially different version introduced later.

The documentation should identify applicable versions, relevant configuration details, and the evidence supporting material claims.

Organizations should also control access to proprietary or security-sensitive information while ensuring that records remain available to authorized reviewers and competent authorities when required.

A technical file is not simply a product description. It is a structured source of information supporting the relevant legal and technical assessment.

## 12. Documenting Intended Purpose, Scope, and Foreseeable Use

The intended purpose of an AI system is central to its risk assessment, documentation, validation, and applicable legal classification.

The intended-purpose record should define what the system is designed to accomplish, the context in which it will operate, the intended users, and the boundaries of its approved use.

Relevant details may include:

- Business objective.
- Supported tasks and decisions.
- Intended user groups.
- Expected operating environment.
- Input and output types.
- Data access and processing boundaries.
- Human involvement.
- Prohibited or unsupported uses.
- Known limitations.
- Relevant assumptions.

The assessment should also consider reasonably foreseeable misuse where relevant to security and risk management.

For example, an AI assistant intended to summarize internal documents may be exposed to attempts to retrieve confidential information, follow malicious instructions embedded in source documents, or generate content outside its authorized purpose.

Documentation should explain the intended use and identify material foreseeable misuse scenarios.

A vague description such as “improves productivity using AI” is insufficient for a meaningful risk assessment because it does not define the actual activities, data, users, or consequences involved.

Changes to intended purpose should trigger review of the relevant risk assessments, validation evidence, operating restrictions, and legal applicability analysis.

## 13. Establishing AI System Identity and Inventory Documentation

A reliable AI inventory establishes which systems exist, who owns them, where they operate, and how they relate to organizational processes.

Without an accurate inventory, governance teams may overlook unapproved deployments, duplicate assessments, miss material dependencies, or fail to identify systems affected by new requirements.

An AI inventory record should typically include:

- Unique system identifier.
- System name and description.
- Business owner.
- Technical owner.
- Provider or developer.
- Model and relevant version information.
- Intended purpose.
- Deployment environment.
- Data categories and sensitivity.
- Connected services and dependencies.
- Risk classification.
- Lifecycle status.
- Applicable governance requirements.
- Approval status.
- Last review date.
- Planned retirement date, where known.

The inventory should distinguish an AI model from an application that uses the model and from the broader business service that depends on the application.

One model may support several applications with different users, data flows, controls, and risks. Conversely, one application may depend on several models or external AI services.

Inventory processes should integrate with procurement, software asset management, cloud governance, development pipelines, and security discovery mechanisms where appropriate.

Records should be updated when systems are introduced, materially changed, transferred to another owner, suspended, or retired.

## 14. Documenting AI Data Governance and Provenance

Data governance documentation establishes where AI-related data originates, how it is processed, who is responsible for it, and which restrictions govern its use.

This is particularly important because data quality, provenance, permissions, and processing practices can influence model behavior, security, privacy, and compliance.

A data governance record may cover:

- Data source and owner.
- Collection or acquisition method.
- Purpose and permitted use.
- Dataset identifier and version.
- Data quality assessment.
- Transformation and preprocessing.
- Labeling or annotation procedures.
- Personal or sensitive data considerations.
- Licensing and contractual restrictions.
- Retention and deletion requirements.
- Access permissions.
- Relevant lineage and downstream dependencies.

Organizations should distinguish training data, fine-tuning data, evaluation data, retrieval sources, inference inputs, and generated outputs because they may have different purposes and governance requirements.

For example, a dataset approved for internal evaluation may not be authorized for model training or external disclosure.

Where complete data provenance is unavailable, the limitation should be documented and assessed. Third-party models may have incomplete public information about their training data, and organizations should not invent missing details.

Data lineage should link datasets to relevant models, applications, evaluations, and decisions so that material changes can be assessed systematically.

## 15. Documenting AI Model Characteristics and Limitations

Model documentation helps users, operators, developers, and governance stakeholders understand a model's capabilities, limitations, intended use, and relevant evaluation results.

Depending on the system, model documentation may include:

- Model name, provider, and version.
- Architecture or model family where available.
- Intended use and excluded use cases.
- Training or fine-tuning information where available.
- Input and output characteristics.
- Performance evaluation results.
- Known weaknesses and failure modes.
- Relevant fairness or robustness assessments.
- Security considerations.
- Environmental and operational dependencies.
- Version history.
- Recommended safeguards.

A model card or similar artifact can communicate important characteristics, but it should not be treated as a substitute for system-level documentation.

A model's behavior may be materially affected by application logic, prompts, retrieval mechanisms, tool permissions, data access, and deployment configuration.

For example, a model with acceptable standalone evaluation results may still be unsafe when integrated into an application that grants unrestricted access to confidential information.

Documentation should therefore distinguish model-level characteristics from system-level properties.

When a provider does not disclose certain technical information, the record should identify the limitation, available evidence, and its implications for the intended use.

## 16. Documenting AI Security Architecture and Threat Models

Security architecture documentation describes the components, trust boundaries, identities, data flows, and security mechanisms used to protect an AI system.

Threat-model documentation identifies plausible attack scenarios and helps establish the controls needed to address them.

For generative AI systems, relevant threat scenarios may include:

- Prompt injection.
- Sensitive-data disclosure.
- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Excessive agent privileges.
- Training-data or retrieval-source poisoning.
- Model extraction or abuse.
- Insecure output handling.
- Compromised external dependencies.
- Inadequate tenant isolation.
- Authentication or authorization failures.

A threat model should describe the assets, actors, entry points, trust boundaries, attack paths, consequences, and mitigations relevant to the system.

Security architecture documentation should identify which controls are implemented at the model, application, identity, data, infrastructure, and monitoring layers.

For example, instructions telling an AI agent not to access unauthorized data should not replace server-side authorization controls that prevent the agent from obtaining that data.

The documentation should link each material threat to the relevant control and evidence used to assess that control.

Threat models should be revisited when the system gains new tools, new data sources, additional privileges, or materially different operating conditions.

## 17. Establishing AI Evaluation and Validation Documentation

AI evaluation and validation documentation records how the organization assessed whether a system satisfies defined requirements and is suitable for its intended purpose.

The documentation should establish the test objective, system version, methodology, acceptance criteria, results, limitations, and approval outcome.

Depending on the system, evaluation may cover:

- Functional performance.
- Accuracy and reliability.
- Robustness to unexpected inputs.
- Security against relevant attacks.
- Privacy and data leakage.
- Bias or fairness considerations where applicable.
- Human oversight effectiveness.
- Operational resilience.
- Performance under expected workload.
- Compliance with defined acceptance criteria.

For security testing, the report should identify the relevant attack scenarios, testing environment, tools or procedures, findings, severity, remediation, and retest results.

The report should distinguish a successful test from an untested requirement or an inconclusive result.

For example, if a system passes a limited set of prompt-injection tests, the report should describe the scope of those tests rather than claiming that prompt injection has been eliminated.

Evaluation records should also identify the model and application versions used during testing. A material update may require regression testing or a new validation decision.

The resulting evidence should support a clearly defined conclusion, such as approval, conditional approval, restricted use, remediation required, or rejection.

## 18. Documenting Human Oversight and Operational Responsibilities

Human oversight documentation establishes how people supervise AI behavior, intervene when necessary, and remain accountable for decisions within their assigned authority.

Oversight requirements vary according to the system's purpose, autonomy, impact, and applicable legal obligations.

Relevant documentation may define:

- Oversight roles and responsibilities.
- Decisions requiring human review.
- Escalation thresholds.
- Conditions requiring intervention.
- Override or shutdown mechanisms.
- Access to supporting information.
- Required competence and training.
- Recording of human interventions.
- Review of overrides and consequential decisions.
- Procedures for handling uncertainty or unexpected behavior.

The organization should distinguish between nominal oversight and effective oversight.

Nominal oversight exists when a person is assigned responsibility but lacks sufficient information, time, competence, or authority to challenge the system's output.

Effective oversight requires an appropriate ability to understand the relevant limitations, assess the output, intervene when necessary, and escalate unresolved concerns.

Documentation should describe how the oversight process operates in practice and what evidence demonstrates its effectiveness.

Where human review is used as a risk control, the organization should assess whether the review process is reliable under realistic workload and operating conditions.

## 19. Establishing AI Transparency and Information Disclosure Records

Transparency documentation explains what relevant information should be communicated to users, operators, affected individuals, customers, or other stakeholders.

The information required depends on the system, intended use, applicable law, contractual commitments, and organizational policy.

Transparency records may address:

- The identity and purpose of the AI system.
- Relevant limitations and expected reliability.
- The role of AI in generating content or recommendations.
- Human involvement in consequential decisions.
- Known conditions under which outputs may be unreliable.
- Applicable usage restrictions.
- Available human escalation or complaint channels.
- Required disclosures or notices.
- The basis for material decisions where explanation is required.

Transparency should be meaningful for its intended audience. Highly technical documentation may be useful to engineers but inadequate for ordinary users who need to understand the system's role and limitations.

Conversely, a simplified user-facing explanation should not replace the detailed technical records needed for security assurance or regulatory review.

The organization should record which disclosures are required, where they are presented, who approves them, and how they are kept current.

For example, if an application begins using a new external model or changes the role AI plays in a customer-facing process, existing disclosures may require review.

Transparency claims should be consistent with actual system behavior and should not overstate the degree of human control, model reliability, or explainability.

## 20. Building an Integrated AI Documentation Assurance Framework

An integrated AI documentation assurance framework connects standards, lifecycle artifacts, risk records, technical evidence, approvals, and monitoring into one governed operating model.

The framework should establish a repeatable way to demonstrate that documentation is complete, accurate, current, appropriately protected, and sufficient for its intended assurance purpose.

Core components include:

- Approved documentation standards and templates.
- Defined ownership and approval authority.
- An accurate AI system inventory.
- Risk-based documentation requirements.
- Controlled repositories and authoritative sources.
- Version control and change management.
- Links between requirements, risks, controls, and evidence.
- Evidence integrity and retention safeguards.
- Quality review and independent assurance.
- Exception and remediation management.
- Performance indicators and management reporting.
- Continuous improvement.

An integrated assurance chain can be represented as follows:

```
Applicable Requirements
          |
          v
AI System Scope and Intended Purpose
          |
          v
Risk Assessment
          |
          v
Control Design and Implementation
          |
          v
Technical and Operational Evidence
          |
          v
Validation and Assurance Review
          |
          v
Governance Decision
          |
          v
Monitoring, Change Management, and Review
          |
          +----------------------------+
                                       |
                                       v
                              Updated Assessment
```

Each stage should have defined acceptance criteria, accountable owners, and traceable records.

The framework should also distinguish between the existence of documentation and the effectiveness of the underlying control. A complete evidence package can still reveal a failed control, an unacceptable risk, or a need to restrict the system.

**Final governance principle:** AI documentation standards provide the structure for accountable AI management, but assurance depends on the quality of the records, the reliability of the supporting evidence, and the extent to which documentation reflects the system's actual behavior and operating conditions.

**Illustrative taxonomy note:** Any internal identifiers introduced in subsequent parts should be treated as organization-defined identifiers unless they are explicitly mapped to an authoritative external standard, regulation, or other source.

# Part 2 — Documentation Governance, Evidence Integrity, Traceability and Record Management

## 21. Establishing Documentation Ownership and Accountability

AI documentation requires clearly assigned ownership. Without defined accountability, records may become outdated, contradictory, incomplete, or unavailable when required for audits, regulatory reviews, incident investigations, or operational decisions.

Organizations should assign responsibility according to the nature of each artifact. AI system owners may maintain system-level documentation, data owners may validate data provenance and permitted use, model owners may maintain model specifications and evaluation records, security teams may maintain threat models and security assessments, and compliance functions may verify alignment with applicable obligations.

A documentation ownership model should define:

- The accountable owner for each artifact.
- The person or function responsible for preparing and updating it.
- The reviewer or approver.
- The required review frequency and triggering events.
- The authoritative repository.
- The escalation process for missing or inaccurate records.

Ownership should remain explicit when responsibilities are distributed across internal teams, vendors, cloud providers, and external AI service providers. Contractual responsibilities should identify which party creates, maintains, supplies, and retains each required record.

## 22. Defining Documentation Approval and Authorization Workflows

Documentation should not be considered approved merely because a file exists in a repository. Formal approval workflows establish whether the content has been reviewed by an authorized person and is suitable for its intended purpose.

Approval requirements should be proportionate to the document's risk and significance. A low-risk internal operating procedure may require a functional owner’s approval, while a high-risk AI system’s technical documentation may require coordinated review by engineering, security, privacy, legal, risk management, and compliance stakeholders.

An approval workflow should specify:

- Submission and review requirements.
- Required technical and governance reviewers.
- Approval authority and delegation rules.
- Rejection, revision, and resubmission procedures.
- Effective dates and expiration or review dates.
- Evidence of approval and any outstanding conditions.

Organizations should distinguish document approval from system authorization. Approving a risk assessment does not automatically authorize deployment, and approving technical documentation does not establish that all applicable legal obligations have been fulfilled.

## 23. Implementing Document Version Control

Version control preserves the history of documentation changes and enables reviewers to determine which information applied at a particular point in time.

AI systems can change through model updates, revised prompts, new datasets, modified retrieval sources, infrastructure changes, altered access permissions, and changes to intended purpose. Documentation must reflect these changes without obscuring the records that supported earlier decisions.

A controlled versioning process should capture:

- Unique document identifier.
- Version number and status.
- Author and approver.
- Creation and revision timestamps.
- Summary and rationale for changes.
- Links to relevant system versions or releases.
- Superseded versions and their retention status.

Organizations should prohibit uncontrolled overwriting of approved records where historical evidence is necessary. Version identifiers should be consistent enough to support reliable retrieval and comparison across repositories.

## 24. Establishing Document Change Classification

Not every documentation change has the same governance significance. Organizations should classify changes according to their potential effect on system behavior, risk, compliance, security, and accountability.

A typographical correction may require limited review, while a change to an AI system’s intended purpose, training data, model architecture, decision thresholds, or human oversight arrangements may require a formal impact assessment.

An internal classification scheme could include:

- **Editorial change:** Corrects wording or formatting without changing meaning.
- **Administrative change:** Updates owners, contacts, or repository references.
- **Technical change:** Alters technical descriptions, configurations, or system dependencies.
- **Risk-significant change:** May affect identified risks, control effectiveness, or risk acceptance.
- **Regulatory-significant change:** May affect conformity assessments, required disclosures, registration, or other legal obligations.

These categories are illustrative internal governance categories, not universal regulatory classifications. Organizations should map them to applicable legal requirements and established change-management processes.

## 25. Maintaining an Authoritative Documentation Repository

AI documentation should be stored in a controlled repository that provides reliable access, appropriate protection, and clear identification of authoritative records.

Documents distributed across personal drives, email attachments, ticketing systems, shared folders, and vendor portals can create uncertainty about which version is current. A central repository or governed federated architecture reduces this risk.

Repository requirements should address:

- Role-based access and least privilege.
- Version history and change logs.
- Searchability and metadata.
- Approval and publication status.
- Retention and disposal rules.
- Backup and recovery.
- Audit logging.
- Secure external sharing.
- Integration with AI inventory, risk registers, and change-management systems.

A federated model may be appropriate where different functions must retain records in specialized platforms. In that case, the organization should maintain a reliable index linking each artifact to its authoritative location, owner, version, and access requirements.

## 26. Designing a Consistent Documentation Metadata Standard

Metadata enables documentation to be discovered, interpreted, related to other records, and assessed for currency. Without consistent metadata, organizations may possess extensive documentation but struggle to establish whether it applies to a particular AI system or release.

A minimum internal metadata schema may include:

| Metadata field              | Purpose                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Artifact ID                 | Uniquely identifies the record                                    |
| AI system ID                | Links the artifact to an inventoried system                       |
| Artifact type               | Identifies the record’s purpose                                   |
| Owner                       | Identifies the accountable function or person                     |
| Version                     | Establishes the document revision                                 |
| Status                      | Distinguishes draft, approved, superseded, or archived records    |
| Effective date              | Identifies when the record becomes applicable                     |
| Review date                 | Indicates when reassessment is due                                |
| Related control IDs         | Connects the artifact to relevant controls                        |
| Related risk IDs            | Connects the artifact to assessed risks                           |
| Classification              | Defines handling and access restrictions                          |
| Source or lineage reference | Identifies originating data, systems, or records where applicable |

This schema is an illustrative internal design. Organizations should extend it where required by the applicable regulatory regime, industry, contractual obligations, or technical environment.

## 27. Linking Documentation to the AI System Inventory

Documentation must be traceable to the correct AI system. A document that cannot be associated with a known system, component, deployment, or service may provide limited assurance value.

The AI system inventory should act as a primary reference point for system identity and lifecycle status. Relevant records may include system descriptions, model cards, data lineage records, security assessments, privacy impact assessments, evaluation reports, incident records, and supplier documentation.

Organizations should define how documentation relates to:

- The AI application or business use case.
- The model or model family.
- The deployed model version.
- The datasets and external data sources.
- The supporting infrastructure.
- The AI provider and relevant subcontractors.
- The production environment and deployment location.
- The system owner and business process owner.

Where one model supports several applications, organizations should distinguish shared model documentation from application-specific documentation. This prevents assumptions that a single assessment automatically covers every deployment and use case.

## 28. Establishing Traceability Between Requirements and Evidence

Traceability demonstrates how a requirement is addressed and what evidence supports the associated conclusion. It is central to auditability because the existence of a policy or control description does not establish that the control operates effectively.

A traceability structure should connect each applicable obligation or internal requirement to its implementation, supporting artifact, accountable owner, and verification result.

A practical relationship is:

Requirement → Control objective → Implemented control → Evidence artifact → Test result → Finding or conclusion → Remediation, if needed.

For example, an internal requirement to restrict access to sensitive AI evaluation data may map to an access-control standard, a platform configuration, approved access records, periodic access-review evidence, and a test confirming that unauthorized access is prevented.

Traceability should distinguish:

- A requirement that has been identified.
- A control that has been designed.
- A control that has been implemented.
- Evidence that the control operated.
- A test that assessed its effectiveness.
- A conclusion that has been formally approved.

These states should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 29. Maintaining Regulatory Requirement-to-Evidence Matrices

A requirement-to-evidence matrix helps organizations organize compliance obligations and demonstrate how each requirement is addressed.

The matrix should identify the applicable legal provision, standard clause, contractual commitment, or internal policy requirement. It should then reference the responsible owner, implementation mechanism, supporting evidence, review status, and any identified gap.

A useful matrix may contain:

| Field                   | Description                                   |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Requirement reference   | Exact source identifier where available       |
| Requirement summary     | Concise interpretation of the obligation      |
| Applicability rationale | Why the requirement applies or does not apply |
| Control reference       | Relevant control or procedure                 |
| Evidence reference      | Location and identifier of supporting records |
| Accountable owner       | Responsible function                          |
| Assessment status       | Assessed implementation state                 |
| Gap or exception        | Known deficiency or unresolved question       |
| Remediation reference   | Action plan and target date                   |
| Validation date         | Date of the latest assessment                 |

The matrix should preserve links to authoritative source text rather than relying solely on paraphrased summaries. Legal applicability decisions should be reviewed by suitably qualified personnel, especially where classification, jurisdiction, exemptions, or transitional provisions affect the result.

## 30. Preserving Evidence Integrity and Authenticity

Documentation used for assurance must be sufficiently reliable to support the conclusions drawn from it. Evidence integrity concerns whether a record has remained protected against unauthorized alteration, deletion, substitution, or misrepresentation.

Evidence controls may include access restrictions, immutable or tamper-evident storage, cryptographic hashes, trusted timestamps, signed approvals, source-system references, and audit logs. The appropriate measures depend on the sensitivity of the information, the applicable retention requirements, and the consequences of evidence loss or alteration.

For important evidence artifacts, organizations should record:

- The source system or originating process.
- The person or service that generated the record.
- The collection date and time.
- The applicable system and software version.
- Any transformations applied to the evidence.
- The storage location and access history.
- Integrity-verification information, where appropriate.

A cryptographic hash can help detect whether a file has changed, but it does not independently prove that the original file was accurate, complete, or generated by an authorized source. Integrity mechanisms must therefore operate alongside provenance, access control, and collection procedures.

## 31. Establishing Evidence Collection Procedures

Evidence collection should follow repeatable procedures so that records are complete, relevant, and collected in a manner suitable for their intended use.

Unstructured collection can result in missing context, incomplete time periods, inconsistent extraction methods, or records that cannot be reproduced. These weaknesses can undermine investigations and audit conclusions.

A documented collection procedure should define:

- The objective and scope of collection.
- The required source systems and data fields.
- The authorized collector.
- The collection period and time zone.
- The extraction method and applicable filters.
- The validation and completeness checks.
- The secure transfer and storage process.
- Any limitations or collection failures.
- The review and acceptance criteria.

For AI systems, relevant evidence may include model evaluation outputs, inference logs, access events, prompt and response records, human review decisions, configuration snapshots, data-quality results, and incident timelines. Collection must remain proportionate and consistent with privacy, confidentiality, security, and data-minimization requirements.

## 32. Documenting Data Provenance and Lineage

Data provenance records where data originated, how it was obtained, and under what conditions it may be used. Data lineage describes how data moves through systems and how it is transformed, combined, filtered, labeled, or otherwise processed.

Both are important to AI governance because data characteristics can influence model performance, fairness, privacy exposure, security, and legal compliance.

Documentation should address, where relevant:

- Data source and provider.
- Collection or acquisition method.
- Collection date or relevant time period.
- Ownership, licensing, and permitted use.
- Consent or other relevant legal basis, where applicable.
- Data classification and sensitivity.
- Labeling and annotation processes.
- Transformation and preprocessing steps.
- Quality checks and known limitations.
- Retention, deletion, and access restrictions.
- Links to training, validation, testing, and production use.

Organizations should not claim complete lineage when the underlying information is unavailable. Known gaps, estimated provenance, inherited supplier limitations, and unverified assumptions should be explicitly documented and evaluated for their effect on risk and compliance.

## 33. Recording Dataset Versioning and Data Changes

AI behavior may change when datasets are replaced, expanded, filtered, relabeled, or corrected. Dataset versioning enables teams to associate model training and evaluation results with the data actually used.

A dataset record should identify the dataset version, source, relevant composition, processing pipeline, quality assessment, and approval status. Where feasible, the record should include reproducible references to the underlying data and transformation code or configuration, subject to intellectual-property, privacy, and security constraints.

Changes should be assessed for their potential effects on:

- Data representativeness and coverage.
- Label accuracy and consistency.
- Distribution shifts.
- Protected or sensitive attributes.
- Data leakage between training and evaluation sets.
- Licensing and permitted use.
- Personal data exposure.
- Model performance and known limitations.

When a dataset cannot be retained because of legal, contractual, or privacy restrictions, the organization should document an appropriate alternative, such as controlled source references, approved metadata, transformation records, or reproducible dataset manifests. Such alternatives may not fully reproduce the original dataset, and that limitation should be recorded.

## 34. Documenting Model Identity, Configuration, and Dependencies

Model documentation should make it possible to determine which model was developed, evaluated, approved, and deployed. Model names alone may be insufficient where providers publish multiple revisions or where deployment configurations materially affect behavior.

Relevant records may include:

- Model identifier and provider.
- Model version or release reference.
- Model architecture or model family, where available.
- Training or fine-tuning information, where known and permitted.
- Configuration parameters.
- Inference settings and safety controls.
- Prompt templates and system instructions, where applicable.
- Retrieval indexes and knowledge sources.
- External tools and API dependencies.
- Deployment environment and integration points.
- Known limitations and provider disclosures.

For externally hosted or proprietary models, some technical details may not be available. Organizations should document the information received from the provider, the information that remains unknown, the assurance measures used to address that uncertainty, and any resulting limitations on use.

## 35. Maintaining Model and Deployment Configuration Baselines

A configuration baseline defines the approved technical state against which changes can be assessed. In AI environments, relevant configuration may extend beyond model weights to include prompts, retrieval settings, guardrails, access controls, orchestration logic, data connectors, and monitoring thresholds.

Organizations should identify which configuration elements are material to system behavior or risk and preserve an appropriate record of their approved state.

Baseline documentation should specify:

- The configuration item and version.
- The approved value or reference.
- The approval authority.
- The implementation environment.
- The date of verification.
- The change history.
- The method for detecting unauthorized drift.
- The process for restoring an approved configuration.

Baselines should be updated through controlled change management. A difference from the baseline should trigger assessment rather than automatic classification as a defect; some differences may be authorized, while others may indicate unapproved change or control failure.

## 36. Documenting AI Evaluation and Test Evidence

Evaluation records should establish what was tested, why the tests were selected, how they were conducted, what results were obtained, and how the results influenced deployment or continued-use decisions.

A report containing only aggregate accuracy or a general statement that a model passed testing may be inadequate for a consequential system. Evaluation should be aligned with intended purpose, operating conditions, relevant risks, and known limitations.

Documentation may include:

- Evaluation objectives and acceptance criteria.
- Test datasets and their versions.
- Test environment and configuration.
- Test methods and tools.
- Performance and robustness metrics.
- Security and abuse-testing results.
- Fairness or subgroup analyses where relevant.
- Human evaluation methodology.
- Known limitations and unresolved failures.
- Reproducibility constraints.
- Reviewer conclusions and approval decisions.

Organizations should distinguish test execution from successful test outcomes. Failed tests, inconclusive results, deviations from the planned method, and excluded test cases should be retained and assessed rather than omitted from the final record.

## 37. Recording Risk Acceptance and Residual Risk Decisions

AI documentation should preserve the rationale for accepting residual risk after controls have been implemented or planned. A risk register that identifies risks without recording decisions may be insufficient to demonstrate accountable governance.

A risk acceptance record should identify:

- The risk and affected system.
- The assessment method and assumptions.
- Inherent and residual risk ratings, where the methodology uses them.
- Existing and planned controls.
- Supporting evidence.
- Remaining uncertainty.
- The decision owner and approval date.
- Conditions or restrictions on acceptance.
- The next review date or reassessment trigger.

Acceptance authority should be defined by the organization’s risk governance framework. Risks exceeding delegated authority, involving mandatory legal obligations, or presenting unacceptable safety or security consequences must be escalated rather than treated as acceptable solely because a business owner approves them.

Risk acceptance should be revisited when the system, operating environment, threat landscape, applicable obligations, or evidence materially changes.

## 38. Documenting Exceptions, Waivers, and Control Deviations

Exceptions permit a defined departure from an internal requirement or control, where the organization’s policy and applicable law allow such a departure. They should be explicit, time-bounded where appropriate, and subject to authorized approval.

An exception record should state:

- The requirement or control affected.
- The business and technical rationale.
- The scope of the deviation.
- The risks introduced or increased.
- Compensating controls.
- The approving authority.
- The effective date and expiration date, if applicable.
- Required monitoring and review.
- Conditions for closure or renewal.

An internal waiver cannot override a statutory or regulatory obligation that is legally mandatory. Where an apparent conflict exists, the organization should obtain appropriate legal and compliance assessment before authorizing the deviation.

Expired exceptions should be reviewed and closed, renewed through an authorized process, or escalated. A recurring exception should trigger analysis of whether the underlying control or operating model requires redesign.

## 39. Maintaining Corrective Action and Remediation Records

Documentation gaps and control failures should lead to tracked corrective actions. Remediation records enable governance teams to monitor whether identified weaknesses are addressed and whether the corrective measures are effective.

A remediation record should include the finding, severity, root-cause analysis, accountable owner, planned actions, due date, dependencies, status, and closure evidence.

For AI-related findings, corrective action may involve revising documentation, improving data lineage, changing a model configuration, updating access controls, conducting additional evaluations, renegotiating supplier terms, or restricting a deployment.

Closure should require more than a status update. The responsible reviewer should verify that the action was implemented, that the evidence supports the claimed outcome, and that residual risk has been reassessed where necessary.

Overdue high-risk findings should be escalated according to defined governance procedures. Organizations should also analyze recurring findings to identify systemic problems in documentation standards, ownership, tooling, or assurance processes.

## 40. Establishing Documentation Quality Assurance

Documentation quality assurance evaluates whether records are accurate, complete, consistent, current, understandable, traceable, and appropriate for their intended use.

Quality assurance should be proportionate to the document’s significance. A routine operational guide may receive a standard peer review, while documentation supporting a high-impact AI deployment may require multidisciplinary review and independent verification of critical claims.

A documentation quality checklist may assess:

- Correct identification of the AI system and version.
- Clear purpose, scope, and assumptions.
- Completeness of required fields.
- Consistency with system configuration and inventory records.
- Traceability to requirements, risks, controls, and evidence.
- Validity of references and supporting links.
- Appropriate approvals and version history.
- Identification of limitations and unresolved issues.
- Compliance with access, confidentiality, and retention rules.
- Defined review triggers and ownership.

Quality checks should be recorded where necessary to demonstrate that review occurred. Automated validation can identify missing metadata, broken references, expired reviews, or inconsistent identifiers, but it does not replace expert assessment of technical accuracy, legal interpretation, or the adequacy of governance decisions.

The objective is to treat documentation as a maintained assurance capability rather than a collection of static files. Reliable records help the organization explain how an AI system is governed, establish the basis for decisions, identify weaknesses, and demonstrate accountability throughout the system lifecycle.
