# 19.15 — System Cards

## Part 1 — System Card Foundations, Documentation Structure, and Governance Requirements

### 1. Definition of an AI System Card

An **AI system card** is a structured governance and technical documentation artifact that describes an AI system, its intended purpose, operating context, capabilities, limitations, risks, safeguards, and accountability arrangements.

It provides a consolidated view of how an AI system is designed to be used, what it can and cannot reliably do, which stakeholders are accountable for it, and what controls are required throughout its lifecycle.

A system card translates technical and operational information into a form that can be reviewed by multiple stakeholders, including:

- Business owners responsible for the outcomes produced by the AI system.
- AI governance teams responsible for policy alignment and oversight.
- Risk management teams responsible for identifying and evaluating risks.
- Information security teams responsible for protecting AI assets and services.
- Privacy teams responsible for personal data processing and privacy obligations.
- Legal and compliance teams responsible for regulatory and contractual requirements.
- Internal auditors responsible for independently evaluating governance and control effectiveness.
- Senior management responsible for accepting or escalating material risks.

A system card is not simply a description of a model. It documents the AI system as deployed or intended to be deployed within a particular business and governance context.

For example, a customer-service chatbot may use a foundation model, a retrieval-augmented generation architecture, a vector database, internal knowledge repositories, identity controls, and a human escalation process. A model-only description would not adequately explain the risks of the complete solution. A system card should describe the relevant components and their interactions.

The system card should therefore answer five fundamental questions:

1. **What is the AI system?**
2. **What is it intended to do, and under what conditions may it be used?**
3. **What are its limitations, risks, dependencies, and potential impacts?**
4. **What safeguards and accountable roles govern its operation?**
5. **What evidence demonstrates that the system remains suitable for its approved purpose?**

The depth of documentation should be proportionate to the system's complexity, intended use, potential impact, and risk exposure.

### 2. Purpose of System Cards in AI Governance

System cards support AI governance by making important information about AI systems explicit, structured, reviewable, and traceable.

AI systems frequently cross organizational boundaries. A business unit may define the use case, an engineering team may integrate the model, a third-party provider may supply the underlying technology, and a risk team may assess the resulting exposure. Without structured documentation, each stakeholder may operate with an incomplete understanding of the system.

A system card provides a common reference point for these stakeholders.

Its principal governance purposes include the following.

**2.1 Establishing a documented system baseline**

The system card records the approved configuration and intended operating conditions against which subsequent changes can be assessed.

**2.2 Supporting risk assessment**

It provides information about the system's purpose, users, data, architecture, dependencies, limitations, and potential consequences. This information supports risk identification and evaluation.

**2.3 Demonstrating governance decisions**

The card can record approval status, review dates, accountable owners, required safeguards, and outstanding conditions. This helps demonstrate that decisions were made through an established governance process.

**2.4 Supporting audit and assurance**

Auditors can use the system card to understand the system's scope, identify relevant controls, determine which evidence should exist, and assess whether actual operation is consistent with documented approvals.

**2.5 Improving transparency**

A well-structured card helps authorized stakeholders understand the system without requiring every reviewer to reconstruct its technical design from source code, architecture diagrams, vendor documents, and informal communications.

**2.6 Supporting lifecycle management**

The card provides a baseline for evaluating material changes, reassessing risks, reviewing performance, managing incidents, and determining whether a system should remain in operation.

A system card does not independently establish that an AI system is safe, compliant, or effective. It establishes a documented basis for review and helps connect governance decisions to supporting evidence.

### 3. System Cards Versus Model Cards

System cards and model cards are related but serve different documentation purposes.

A **model card** primarily describes a model's characteristics, intended uses, evaluation results, limitations, and relevant performance considerations. A **system card** describes the broader AI-enabled solution and the environment in which the model operates.

| Dimension            | Model card                                                           | AI system card                                                                         |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary subject      | Individual model                                                     | Complete AI system or defined AI use case                                              |
| Scope                | Model characteristics and evaluation                                 | Model, application, integrations, data flows, users, controls, and operational context |
| Intended use         | Model-level use recommendations                                      | Business purpose and approved system use                                               |
| Dependencies         | Model architecture and training-related information, where available | Models, APIs, data stores, tools, infrastructure, vendors, and connected applications  |
| Risk considerations  | Model behavior, limitations, and evaluation results                  | Model risks plus workflow, security, privacy, operational, human, and business risks   |
| Accountability       | Often emphasizes model developers and providers                      | Covers business ownership, technical ownership, operations, oversight, and approval    |
| Operational controls | May describe model-level safeguards                                  | Documents system-level safeguards and operational responsibilities                     |
| Lifecycle focus      | Model development, evaluation, and release                           | System design, integration, deployment, monitoring, change management, and retirement  |

Consider an organization that deploys a commercially available language model to summarize confidential legal documents.

The model card may describe the model's capabilities, known limitations, evaluation results, and recommended uses. The system card should additionally describe:

- Which legal documents the application can access.
- Whether documents contain personal or confidential information.
- How users authenticate and obtain access.
- Whether prompts and outputs are retained by the organization or provider.
- Whether generated summaries are reviewed by qualified personnel.
- Whether the application can send information to external services.
- What happens when the model produces an inaccurate or incomplete summary.
- Who approves the use case and who is responsible for ongoing monitoring.

The distinction is important because the governance risk of an AI deployment cannot be determined solely from the characteristics of its underlying model.

### 4. System Cards Versus AI Inventories and Risk Registers

A system card is part of a broader governance documentation ecosystem. It should not be treated as a replacement for the AI inventory, AI risk register, control library, or audit evidence repository.

Each artifact serves a different purpose.

| Governance artifact        | Primary question answered                                         | Typical information                                                       |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI inventory               | Which AI systems and use cases exist?                             | System identifier, owner, provider, business unit, lifecycle status       |
| AI system card             | What is this system, how does it operate, and how is it governed? | Purpose, architecture, data, limitations, safeguards, accountability      |
| AI risk register           | What could go wrong, and how are the risks managed?               | Risk statements, impact, likelihood, treatment, residual risk, risk owner |
| AI control library         | What requirements and controls should apply?                      | Control objectives, control descriptions, ownership, testing criteria     |
| Audit workpapers           | What did the auditor examine and conclude?                        | Samples, evidence, test procedures, exceptions, conclusions               |
| Corrective-action register | What must be remediated?                                          | Finding, action owner, target date, remediation status, validation        |
| Assurance report           | What conclusion can authorized stakeholders rely on?              | Scope, criteria, methodology, findings, limitations, conclusion           |

These artifacts should be linked through stable identifiers and governed processes.

For example, the system card for an AI-assisted recruitment platform may reference the relevant inventory record, the risk register entries concerning discriminatory outcomes and inappropriate data access, and the controls established to mitigate those risks.

The risk register should retain the detailed risk analysis, while the system card should summarize the material risks and point reviewers to the authoritative records.

This approach avoids maintaining several competing versions of the same information.

### 5. Defining the Scope of a System Card

The organization must define what constitutes the subject of a system card before creating the documentation.

Depending on the operating environment, the subject may be:

- A complete AI-enabled business application.
- An AI service embedded in an existing enterprise platform.
- A specific AI use case within a broader application.
- An internally developed machine-learning solution.
- A generative AI application using an externally hosted model.
- An AI agent that interacts with tools, APIs, and business systems.
- A high-impact component within a larger AI-enabled workflow.

The appropriate scope depends on whether the system has a distinct intended purpose, ownership structure, risk profile, deployment boundary, or approval decision.

A single system card may cover several tightly integrated components when they operate as one governed system. Separate cards may be appropriate when components have materially different purposes, owners, risks, approval requirements, or lifecycle arrangements.

For example, an enterprise may use one foundation model in three different applications:

1. An internal employee knowledge assistant.
2. A customer-facing support chatbot.
3. An AI-assisted credit assessment application.

Although the underlying model may be the same, the three applications have different users, data access patterns, consequences, and governance requirements. A single model description cannot adequately document all three operational contexts.

The organization may maintain a shared model-level record and separate system cards for each deployment. The documentation architecture should make their relationship explicit.

#### 5.1 Scope boundaries

The system card should identify what is included and excluded from its scope.

Relevant boundaries may include:

- Application components and interfaces.
- Model endpoints and inference services.
- Data sources and repositories.
- Retrieval systems and vector databases.
- Agent tools and connected business applications.
- Human review and escalation procedures.
- Third-party services and external APIs.
- Production, testing, and development environments.
- Monitoring, logging, and incident-management components.

A documented boundary prevents reviewers from assuming that important dependencies have been assessed when they are actually outside the review.

If an external model provider does not disclose its training data or internal model architecture, the system card should record that limitation rather than imply that the information is known.

### 6. Establishing the Intended Purpose

The intended purpose is one of the most important elements of a system card because it defines the context against which system suitability, risk, controls, and authorization are evaluated.

A vague purpose statement makes it difficult to determine whether the system is being used appropriately.

For example, the statement *“The AI system improves business productivity”* does not establish a sufficiently specific governance boundary.

A more useful statement would be:

*“The AI system assists authorized customer-service personnel by retrieving approved knowledge articles and drafting responses to routine product inquiries. Employees must review generated responses before sending them to customers. The system is not authorized to make binding contractual commitments, approve refunds independently, or determine customer eligibility for regulated services.”*

This statement identifies the business objective, intended users, principal function, review requirement, and restrictions.

A documented intended-purpose statement should address the following elements:

| Element                 | Governance question                                                                    |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business objective      | What business problem is the system intended to address?                               |
| Intended users          | Who is authorized to use the system?                                                   |
| Target population       | Whose information, interests, or decisions may be affected?                            |
| Core functionality      | What outputs, recommendations, classifications, or actions does it produce?            |
| Operating context       | In which processes and environments may it operate?                                    |
| Human involvement       | Which decisions require human review or approval?                                      |
| Restrictions            | What uses, users, or actions are prohibited?                                           |
| Success criteria        | How will the organization determine whether the system performs its approved function? |
| Consequences of failure | What happens if the system produces incorrect, misleading, or unavailable outputs?     |

The intended purpose must reflect actual deployment conditions, not merely the original project proposal or a provider's generic description.

If the system's actual use diverges from its approved purpose, the organization should evaluate whether the difference constitutes unauthorized use, a material change, or a trigger for renewed risk assessment and approval.

### 7. Documenting System Capabilities and Limitations

A system card should describe both what the AI system can do and the conditions under which its outputs may be unreliable.

Capability statements should be specific enough to support governance decisions. General statements such as *“the model is intelligent”* or *“the system is highly accurate”* are not sufficient evidence of operational suitability.

Relevant capabilities may include:

- Text classification and extraction.
- Summarization and content generation.
- Semantic search and retrieval.
- Image, audio, or video processing.
- Forecasting and anomaly detection.
- Recommendations and prioritization.
- Tool execution or workflow automation.
- Multi-step planning and agentic actions.

Limitations may include:

- Inaccurate or fabricated outputs.
- Inconsistent results for similar inputs.
- Reduced performance on underrepresented populations or unusual cases.
- Limited contextual understanding.
- Dependence on the quality and completeness of source data.
- Sensitivity to prompt manipulation or adversarial inputs.
- Inability to explain particular outputs adequately.
- Restrictions on language, domain, data types, or operating conditions.
- Dependence on provider availability, API behavior, or model versions.
- Inadequate performance outside the evaluated operating environment.

The card should distinguish between documented limitations, observed failures, untested assumptions, and known information gaps.

For example:

| Category              | Example statement                                                                                           | Governance implication                                                                                   |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Documented limitation | The model may produce unsupported statements when source documents do not contain the requested information | Require source-grounded responses and escalation when evidence is insufficient                           |
| Observed failure      | Testing identified incorrect extraction of certain date formats                                             | Implement validation and regression testing                                                              |
| Untested assumption   | Performance has not been evaluated for all supported languages                                              | Restrict use to evaluated languages or conduct further testing                                           |
| Information gap       | The provider does not disclose sufficient details about model training data                                 | Document the dependency, assess its significance, and determine whether additional assurance is required |

A limitation should not be treated as adequately managed simply because it has been documented. Where a limitation creates material risk, the organization must determine whether the risk can be mitigated, accepted by an authorized person, restricted through operating conditions, or avoided by not deploying the system.

### 8. Describing the System Architecture and Dependencies

The system card should contain enough architectural information to help governance, security, risk, and audit stakeholders understand the principal system components and their relationships.

It does not necessarily need to reproduce every implementation detail in a technical design document. Instead, it should summarize the architecture relevant to the system's intended purpose, risk profile, and control environment.

A typical architecture description may cover:

1. User interfaces and entry points.
2. Application services and orchestration logic.
3. Model providers, model identifiers, and deployed versions where available.
4. Data sources, retrieval mechanisms, and processing pipelines.
5. External APIs and third-party dependencies.
6. Identity, authorization, and privileged-access mechanisms.
7. Input and output validation.
8. Human review and approval points.
9. Logging, monitoring, and incident detection.
10. Deployment environments and operational dependencies.

Consider a retrieval-augmented generation application:

```
Authorized Employee
        |
        v
Enterprise Application
        |
        v
Identity and Access Controls
        |
        v
Retrieval and Orchestration Layer
        |
        +------> Approved Knowledge Repository
        |
        v
External or Internal Language Model
        |
        v
Output Validation and Safety Controls
        |
        v
Human Review, Where Required
        |
        v
Final Business Response
```

This diagram illustrates the principal interactions. The system card should also identify material trust boundaries, data transfers, external service dependencies, and control points.

For AI agents, the architecture description should additionally identify the tools the agent can invoke, the permissions available to those tools, the conditions under which actions require approval, and the mechanisms for stopping or limiting execution.

An architecture description supports governance by revealing dependencies that may not be apparent from the model name alone.

For example, an agent that can only summarize documents has a different operational exposure from an agent that can also send emails, modify customer records, create purchase orders, or execute administrative commands.

### 9. Identifying Data Sources and Information Flows

Data documentation is essential because the risks associated with an AI system often depend on what information it receives, where that information originates, how it is processed, and where outputs are sent.

The system card should identify material data categories and the principal information flows.

Relevant information includes:

- Data sources and their business owners.
- Whether data contains personal, confidential, regulated, or commercially sensitive information.
- The purpose for which each data category is processed.
- Whether information is retrieved dynamically or incorporated into model development.
- Whether prompts and outputs are retained in logs.
- Whether information is transferred to external providers.
- Applicable access restrictions and retention requirements.
- Whether generated outputs are stored, shared, or used to trigger further processing.
- Known limitations in data quality, representativeness, provenance, or completeness.

The documentation should distinguish between training data, fine-tuning data, retrieval data, runtime inputs, system prompts, operational logs, and generated outputs where these distinctions are relevant to the system.

These data categories are not interchangeable.

For example, a business application using a third-party foundation model may not train that model on the organization's runtime prompts. However, the application may still send confidential information to the provider for inference, retain prompts in application logs, or store generated outputs in a connected business system.

Each of those flows creates a different governance consideration.

A data-flow description should make the relevant processing boundaries understandable to reviewers and identify where additional evidence is required.

If the organization cannot establish whether a provider retains submitted information, the system card should record the uncertainty and link it to the appropriate vendor due diligence, contractual review, privacy assessment, or risk treatment.

### 10. Recording Human Oversight and Decision Authority

A system card should explain how humans participate in the system's operation and where decision-making authority resides.

The existence of a human user does not automatically establish meaningful human oversight. A person may technically be able to reject an output but lack the time, expertise, information, or authority required to challenge it effectively.

The card should therefore describe the actual oversight arrangement, including:

- Which outputs or actions require human review.
- Who performs the review and what competence is required.
- Which information is available to support the review.
- Whether the reviewer can reject, correct, or override an output.
- Which actions are prohibited without approval.
- How exceptions are escalated.
- How review decisions are recorded.
- Who remains accountable for the resulting business decision.

For example, an AI-assisted recruitment system may summarize applications and suggest candidates for further review. The system card should clarify whether the AI output is merely advisory, whether recruiters independently assess the underlying evidence, and whether any employment decision can be made solely from the AI recommendation.

The documentation should also identify the consequences of inadequate oversight, including automation bias, excessive reliance on model outputs, unclear accountability, and the possibility that human reviewers approve outputs without meaningful examination.

For an autonomous or agentic system, the card should describe the permitted level of autonomy and the boundaries on execution.

A practical oversight model may distinguish among:

| Operating mode                           | Human involvement                                                                     | Typical governance consideration                                     |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Human-in-the-loop                        | Human approval is required before a defined decision or action                        | Approval authority, review quality, and evidence of approval         |
| Human-on-the-loop                        | The system operates within defined boundaries while humans monitor and can intervene  | Monitoring coverage, intervention capability, and escalation         |
| Human-in-command                         | Humans establish operating objectives, constraints, and governance boundaries         | Authority over deployment, permitted activities, and suspension      |
| Fully automated within an approved scope | The system executes predefined activities without routine case-by-case human approval | Authorization boundaries, monitoring, failure handling, and recovery |

These categories are descriptive governance concepts rather than a substitute for assessing the actual system design or applicable legal requirements. The appropriate model depends on the system's intended purpose, risk, and operating context.

### 11. Documenting Risks, Safeguards, and Residual Risk

A system card should summarize material risks and identify the principal safeguards used to manage them.

The purpose is not to duplicate the complete AI risk register. Instead, the card should provide enough information to understand the system's principal risk exposures and locate the authoritative risk records.

Potential risk categories include:

- Incorrect, misleading, or fabricated outputs.
- Bias, discrimination, or inappropriate differential treatment.
- Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.
- Prompt injection and manipulation of model behavior.
- Excessive permissions or unauthorized agent actions.
- Insecure integrations and vulnerable dependencies.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Model or service degradation.
- Provider outages or unexpected model changes.
- Noncompliance with applicable legal, regulatory, or contractual requirements.
- Inadequate transparency, traceability, or recordkeeping.
- Unclear accountability for AI-supported decisions.

For each material risk, the system card should identify the relevant risk-register reference, the principal safeguards, and any significant limitations or unresolved conditions.

For example:

| Risk                                          | Example safeguard                                                         | System-card documentation                                       |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized access to confidential documents | Identity-based access control and document-level authorization            | Describe the access-control design and link to control evidence |
| Fabricated answers                            | Retrieval grounding, output checks, and human review for defined cases    | Describe the known limitation and required validation           |
| Prompt injection                              | Input handling, retrieval isolation, and restrictions on tool permissions | Identify the threat scenario and the applicable safeguards      |
| Unauthorized agent action                     | Least-privilege tool access and approval gates for sensitive actions      | Document permitted actions, prohibited actions, and escalation  |
| Performance degradation                       | Production monitoring and defined reassessment triggers                   | Identify monitored indicators and the response process          |

The presence of a safeguard in the system card is not proof that the safeguard is implemented or effective. The organization should link important control statements to implementation records, test results, monitoring evidence, or other appropriate sources.

The card should also distinguish inherent risk from residual risk where that distinction is used by the organization's risk-management methodology.

Residual risk represents the risk remaining after the relevant safeguards are considered. Where residual risk exceeds the organization's acceptance criteria, the system should not be treated as adequately governed merely because a mitigation plan exists.

The card should reference the authorized risk decision, applicable conditions, and required follow-up where material risks remain unresolved.

### 12. Assigning Ownership and Accountability

A system card should identify the individuals or roles accountable for the system throughout its lifecycle.

Ownership must be sufficiently explicit to support approvals, incident response, risk decisions, control operation, and retirement.

Depending on the organization, relevant roles may include:

- **Business owner:** Accountable for the business purpose, operational outcomes, and continued business justification.
- **System owner:** Responsible for the overall system and coordination of its lifecycle.
- **Technical owner:** Responsible for architecture, technical configuration, integrations, and technical changes.
- **AI or model owner:** Responsible for model-related evaluation, version management, and model-specific documentation where applicable.
- **Data owner:** Responsible for relevant data access, quality, classification, and authorized use.
- **Risk owner:** Accountable for managing a defined risk and ensuring appropriate treatment or escalation.
- **Control owner:** Responsible for operating a particular governance or technical control.
- **AI governance function:** Responsible for applying the organization's governance framework and oversight requirements.
- **Approving authority:** Authorized to approve deployment or accept risks within defined delegation limits.

One individual may hold multiple roles in a small organization, but the responsibilities should remain distinguishable. Combining roles does not eliminate the need to address conflicts of interest or independent review where required.

The system card should identify how ownership changes are handled. If a system owner leaves the organization or a business unit transfers responsibility, the record should be updated through an established process.

Unassigned ownership is a governance weakness because no clearly accountable party may exist to maintain documentation, address findings, approve changes, or respond to material incidents.

### 13. Defining Lifecycle Status and Governance Milestones

The system card should identify the system's current lifecycle status and the governance milestones relevant to its authorization and continued operation.

A practical lifecycle model may include:

1. Proposed.
2. Under assessment.
3. Approved for development or testing.
4. Under validation.
5. Approved for production.
6. In production.
7. Restricted or suspended.
8. Retired.

The organization may use different terminology, provided that statuses are clearly defined and consistently applied.

Lifecycle status should not be confused with risk rating or regulatory classification. A high-risk system may be approved for a specific purpose under appropriate conditions, while a low-risk system may still be suspended because of a security incident or an unresolved operational failure.

The system card should record relevant dates and decision references, such as:

- Initial registration.
- Risk assessment completion.
- Required technical or business validation.
- Governance approval.
- Production deployment.
- Last material change.
- Last periodic review.
- Next scheduled review.
- Suspension or retirement, where applicable.

A system card should also record the conditions attached to an approval.

For example, a system might be approved for internal use only, subject to restricted data access and mandatory human review. Those conditions must remain visible to relevant operators and reviewers rather than being buried in meeting notes.

If the system's operating conditions change materially, the organization should determine whether the existing approval remains valid or whether renewed assessment and authorization are required.

### 14. Establishing a Standard System Card Structure

A standardized template improves consistency, reviewability, and completeness across an organization's AI portfolio.

The template should be sufficiently comprehensive to capture material governance information while allowing the depth of documentation to vary according to risk and complexity.

A practical structure may include the following sections.

| Section                              | Required information                                                               |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Identification                    | System name, unique identifier, version, business unit, owner, status              |
| 2. Purpose and scope                 | Business objective, intended use, prohibited uses, system boundaries               |
| 3. Users and affected parties        | Authorized users, affected populations, access conditions                          |
| 4. Architecture                      | Principal components, models, integrations, deployment environment                 |
| 5. Data and information flows        | Data categories, sources, transfers, retention, processing boundaries              |
| 6. Capabilities and limitations      | Supported functions, known weaknesses, tested operating conditions                 |
| 7. Evaluation and validation         | Test scope, evaluation results, acceptance criteria, unresolved limitations        |
| 8. Risks and safeguards              | Material risks, control references, risk treatment, residual-risk decisions        |
| 9. Human oversight                   | Review responsibilities, intervention mechanisms, approval boundaries              |
| 10. Security and privacy             | Access controls, information protection, privacy considerations, incident handling |
| 11. Third-party dependencies         | Providers, services, contractual constraints, assurance limitations                |
| 12. Accountability                   | Business, technical, data, risk, and control owners                                |
| 13. Approval and restrictions        | Approval status, approving authority, conditions, exceptions                       |
| 14. Monitoring and change management | Indicators, review triggers, version changes, reassessment requirements            |
| 15. Evidence and references          | Risk-register entries, control records, test results, assessments, decisions       |
| 16. Review history                   | Review dates, material changes, approvers, outstanding actions                     |

Not every system requires the same level of detail in every section. However, the organization should define which fields are mandatory, which are conditional, and how missing information is handled.

A field should not be marked complete simply because text has been entered. The information must be sufficiently accurate and specific to support its intended governance purpose.

For example, entering *“security controls implemented”* does not adequately describe the controls, their scope, or the evidence supporting the statement.

A stronger entry would identify the applicable control references, the responsible owners, the relevant implementation records, and any material exceptions.

### 15. Distinguishing Required, Conditional, and Unknown Information

System card templates should distinguish between information that must always be recorded, information that is required only in certain circumstances, and information that cannot currently be established.

This distinction improves completeness assessment and prevents unknown information from being mistaken for an absence of risk.

**Required information** is necessary for every applicable system card. Typical examples include the system identifier, intended purpose, business owner, lifecycle status, and approval status.

**Conditional information** is required when a relevant feature or condition exists. For example, details about automated external actions are necessary when the system can execute actions through connected tools.

**Unknown information** represents a genuine information gap. Examples include undisclosed provider details, incomplete evidence about data provenance, or an evaluation that has not yet been performed.

Unknown information should include an explanation of the gap, its significance, the person responsible for resolving it, and the expected resolution date where appropriate.

The following distinctions are important:

| Entry                    | Meaning                                                                      | Appropriate treatment                                       |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Not applicable           | The requirement does not apply to this system, with a defensible reason      | Record the rationale                                        |
| Unknown                  | The organization cannot currently establish the answer                       | Record the gap, owner, and required action                  |
| Not assessed             | The relevant assessment has not been completed                               | Track assessment completion and any deployment restrictions |
| Not implemented          | The required capability or control is absent                                 | Assess risk and determine treatment or authorization        |
| Implemented but untested | The control is believed to exist but its effectiveness has not been verified | Obtain appropriate testing or assurance                     |
| Verified                 | Suitable evidence supports the stated implementation or result               | Reference the evidence and its review date                  |

These distinctions support more reliable governance reporting. A dashboard that treats all populated fields as complete may create a misleading impression of readiness.

For instance, a system with all template fields filled in may still have unresolved privacy questions, untested safeguards, or undocumented provider dependencies.

Completeness must therefore be assessed in terms of information quality and evidential support, not merely the percentage of fields containing text.

### 16. Managing System Card Versions and Changes

A system card must be maintained as the system evolves. It should not become a static document that reflects only the original design or initial approval.

Material changes may affect the system's intended purpose, architecture, data processing, risk profile, operating conditions, or authorization.

Examples include:

- Replacing the underlying model.
- Changing model versions or provider services.
- Introducing new data sources.
- Expanding access to a new user population.
- Connecting the system to additional enterprise applications.
- Increasing the level of autonomy.
- Allowing the system to perform actions previously requiring human approval.
- Changing retention or data-sharing arrangements.
- Moving from an internal pilot to customer-facing production.
- Discovering a material performance limitation or security weakness.

The organization should define which changes require documentation updates, renewed risk assessment, testing, or formal approval.

A version history should identify the change, the date, the responsible person, the impact assessment, and the approval or review decision where applicable.

A simple version-control table may be structured as follows:

| Version | Change                                         | Governance impact                                | Approval or review                              |
| ------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| 1.0     | Initial production release                     | Initial risk assessment and authorization        | Approved by the designated authority            |
| 1.1     | Added a new internal knowledge source          | Data access and output reliability reassessed    | Reviewed under change management                |
| 2.0     | Enabled external actions through an agent tool | Increased autonomy and potential business impact | Subject to renewed risk assessment and approval |

These entries are illustrative. Actual version histories should reflect documented changes and real governance decisions.

Version control is particularly important when an AI system depends on a third-party service whose behavior, model version, or configuration may change independently of the organization's application release cycle.

The system card should identify how the organization detects such changes and determines whether reassessment is required.

### 17. Defining Review and Approval Requirements

System cards should be reviewed by stakeholders with responsibilities appropriate to the system's purpose and risk.

The review process should establish whether the documented information is accurate, the system is suitable for its approved purpose, material risks have been assessed, required controls are identified, and the relevant authority has approved the intended deployment.

Reviewers may include business owners, technical owners, AI governance specialists, security and privacy teams, legal and compliance personnel, and designated risk authorities.

Not every reviewer must approve every system. The organization should define approval criteria and escalation requirements according to its governance framework.

A review should not be treated as a purely administrative exercise. Reviewers should challenge unsupported assumptions, identify missing evidence, and confirm that material limitations and restrictions are reflected in operational arrangements.

Approval requirements may depend on factors such as:

- The significance of decisions influenced by the system.
- The sensitivity of processed information.
- The degree of automation and autonomy.
- The scale and type of potential impact.
- The maturity of the technology and operating environment.
- The applicable legal and regulatory requirements.
- The extent of third-party dependency.
- The level of residual risk and the authority required to accept it.

The system card should record the approval outcome and any conditions.

Possible outcomes include approval, conditional approval, return for remediation, restriction to a narrower use case, suspension, or rejection.

Conditional approval should identify the required conditions, accountable owners, deadlines, and consequences of non-compliance.

A documented approval does not remove the need for continued monitoring or periodic reassessment.

### 18. Integrating System Cards into the Governance Operating Model

System cards should be incorporated into established governance processes rather than maintained as isolated documents.

Their value increases when they are connected to the activities that authorize, operate, monitor, and review AI systems.

Important integration points include:

- AI intake and registration.
- AI risk assessment.
- Security and privacy reviews.
- Third-party due diligence.
- Model and system validation.
- Deployment approval.
- Change management.
- Operational monitoring.
- Incident management.
- Internal audit and compliance testing.
- Corrective-action management.
- Periodic management review.
- System retirement.

A typical governance workflow can be represented as follows:

```
AI Use Case Identified
          |
          v
Initial Registration
          |
          v
System Card Created
          |
          v
Risk, Security, Privacy,
and Compliance Assessments
          |
          v
Required Controls and
Evaluation Evidence Established
          |
          v
Governance Review and Approval
          |
          v
Authorized Deployment
          |
          v
Monitoring, Change Management,
and Periodic Review
          |
          v
Reassessment, Restriction,
or Retirement When Required
```

The workflow is not necessarily linear in every organization. Assessment may identify missing information that requires the system card to be revised, or testing may reveal a risk that requires a change in design.

The important governance principle is that documentation, assessment, approval, and operational control should inform one another.

For example, if a privacy assessment determines that certain data categories must not be submitted to an external model provider, the system card should reflect that restriction. The application design, user instructions, and monitoring controls should then support enforcement of the restriction.

A restriction recorded only in documentation but not reflected in operational controls may be insufficient.

### 19. Designing System Cards for Auditability

System cards should enable an independent reviewer to trace important statements to authoritative records or supporting evidence.

This does not mean every statement requires a separate attachment. It means material claims should be verifiable through identifiable sources.

Examples include:

- Architecture statements linked to approved architecture documentation.
- Evaluation claims linked to test reports and acceptance decisions.
- Risk summaries linked to the risk register.
- Control statements linked to control descriptions and evidence.
- Approval status linked to the relevant decision record.
- Provider claims linked to applicable contracts, attestations, or due diligence records.
- Monitoring commitments linked to operational procedures and monitoring records.
- Change history linked to release and change-management records.

A system card should identify the evidence source, version, date, or reference needed to locate the supporting record.

Where evidence is unavailable, the card should not present the associated claim as independently verified.

For example, a statement that *“the system has been tested for bias”* should be supported by an identifiable evaluation record describing the scope, method, results, limitations, and relevant decision.

The mere existence of a test report does not prove that the testing was adequate. The reviewer must still determine whether the evidence supports the claim being made.

System cards should also be subject to access controls and appropriate version management. They may contain sensitive architectural information, security-control details, provider arrangements, or information about business processes.

Auditability therefore requires both traceability and appropriate protection of the documentation itself.

### 20. Common System Card Governance Failures

Organizations can create system cards and still fail to establish effective AI governance if the documentation is incomplete, inaccurate, disconnected from operations, or unsupported by evidence.

Common failure patterns include the following.

**20.1 Treating the system card as a marketing document**

The card emphasizes capabilities and business benefits while omitting limitations, failure conditions, and material risks.

**20.2 Documenting the model instead of the complete system**

The card identifies the model but fails to describe integrations, data flows, user permissions, external services, or automated actions.

**20.3 Using vague intended-purpose statements**

The purpose is too broad to establish authorized uses, prohibited uses, or the consequences of misuse.

**20.4 Copying provider documentation without deployment-specific analysis**

Generic model documentation is reused without addressing the organization's data, workflows, users, controls, or business consequences.

**20.5 Recording controls without evidence**

The card states that safeguards exist but provides no way to establish whether they are implemented or effective.

**20.6 Failing to identify accountable owners**

The card names a department or project rather than identifying a responsible role with the authority to manage the system.

**20.7 Treating unknown information as not applicable**

Material gaps are concealed by marking fields as irrelevant, preventing appropriate risk evaluation and escalation.

**20.8 Failing to update the card after material changes**

The card continues to describe an earlier model version, data flow, or level of autonomy.

**20.9 Confusing approval with permanent authorization**

The organization assumes that an initial approval remains sufficient despite changes in system behavior, risk, or operating conditions.

**20.10 Maintaining disconnected documentation**

The system card, risk register, inventory, control records, and audit evidence contain inconsistent information or cannot be reliably cross-referenced.

These failures weaken governance because stakeholders cannot confidently determine what the system does, which safeguards apply, who is accountable, or whether the system remains within its approved boundaries.

### 21. Part 1 Core Principle

An AI system card is a **structured, lifecycle-oriented governance record for a defined AI system or use case**. It connects the system's intended purpose, architecture, data, capabilities, limitations, risks, safeguards, human oversight, ownership, and approval conditions into a reviewable source of information.

Its effectiveness depends on more than completeness of documentation. Material claims must be accurate, deployment-specific, traceable to supporting evidence, and maintained when the system changes.

A system card does not replace an AI inventory, risk register, control library, or audit workpapers. It connects those artifacts so that governance decisions can be understood, challenged, and independently assessed.

## Part 2 — System Card Content, Technical Evidence, and Risk-Based Documentation

### 22. Documenting Model Identity and Provenance

A system card should identify the models used by the AI system and document their provenance to the extent that reliable information is available. Model identity and provenance are important because model behavior, security characteristics, licensing conditions, and operational dependencies can vary between providers, versions, configurations, and deployment arrangements.

The documentation should distinguish the model itself from the application, orchestration layer, and supporting services that use it.

Relevant model information may include:

- Model name and identifier.
- Model provider or developer.
- Model family and version.
- Deployment type, such as provider-hosted API, self-hosted model, or managed cloud service.
- Release or deployment date, where available.
- Model configuration and relevant inference parameters.
- Known version dependencies or provider-controlled updates.
- Licensing and usage restrictions.
- Available technical documentation and evaluation reports.
- Known limitations and material information gaps.

For internally developed models, the organization may also document training-data provenance, model-development methods, evaluation datasets, fine-tuning arrangements, and release controls. For third-party foundation models, equivalent information may be unavailable or restricted by contractual confidentiality.

The system card must distinguish between information supplied by the provider, information independently verified by the organization, and information that remains unknown.

For example, a provider may state that a model has undergone safety evaluation. The system card can record that statement, but it should not automatically characterize the model as independently validated for the organization's use case. Provider evaluation and deployment-specific validation are different forms of evidence.

A model provenance record should therefore support the following questions:

1. Can the organization identify the model currently serving the application?
2. Can it determine whether the model has changed?
3. Does the model's provenance create material security, legal, licensing, or operational concerns?
4. Is sufficient information available to assess suitability for the intended use?
5. Are material information gaps reflected in the system's risk assessment and approval conditions?

Where a provider does not expose an exact model version or disclose important development details, the organization should document the limitation and determine whether compensating controls, contractual commitments, additional testing, or usage restrictions are necessary.

### 23. Documenting Model Configuration and Deployment Context

Model identity alone is insufficient to describe how an AI system behaves in production. Configuration and deployment context can materially influence outputs, security exposure, and operational risk.

A system card should identify the configuration elements that materially affect the system's intended purpose or risk profile.

Depending on the implementation, these may include:

- System prompts and instruction templates.
- Sampling parameters and output-length limits.
- Model routing and fallback logic.
- Retrieval settings and source-selection rules.
- Fine-tuning or adapter configurations.
- Content filters and safety policies.
- Tool definitions and function-calling permissions.
- Context-window and input-size restrictions.
- Model endpoint configuration.
- Deployment region and relevant processing boundaries.
- Rate limits and resource constraints.
- Version-pinning or provider-update arrangements.

Not every parameter requires inclusion in the main system card. The objective is to identify configuration elements that affect governance decisions and provide references to authoritative technical records for implementation detail.

For example, a customer-support application may use the same foundation model for two workflows. One workflow generates draft responses for employee review, while another is permitted to issue automated responses for a narrowly defined set of routine questions.

The difference in risk does not arise exclusively from the underlying model. It also arises from the application configuration, response restrictions, permitted actions, and level of human oversight.

The system card should therefore explain the deployment context rather than assume that a model's general characteristics determine the risk of every implementation.

Where configuration details are sensitive, the card can reference a controlled technical repository instead of reproducing secrets or privileged implementation data. API keys, passwords, private credentials, and other secrets should never be included in the system card.

### 24. Recording Evaluation Scope and Evidence

An AI system card should summarize the evaluations performed to determine whether the system is suitable for its intended purpose.

Evaluation documentation should distinguish between tests of the underlying model, tests of the integrated application, and tests of the system under realistic operating conditions.

These forms of evaluation address different questions.

**Model-level evaluation** examines characteristics such as general capability, accuracy, robustness, or known safety limitations.

**Application-level evaluation** examines how the model interacts with prompts, retrieval components, application logic, data sources, and configured safeguards.

**Operational evaluation** examines how the complete system behaves under actual deployment constraints, including access controls, monitoring, user workflows, failure handling, and third-party dependencies.

The system card should identify the evaluation scope and provide references to supporting evidence.

Relevant fields include:

| Evaluation element   | Documentation requirement                                                                 |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Evaluation objective | What property, risk, or acceptance criterion was evaluated?                               |
| Scope                | Which model, application components, workflows, and configurations were included?         |
| Test method          | Which testing approach or evaluation procedure was used?                                  |
| Test data            | What datasets, scenarios, or representative inputs were used?                             |
| Acceptance criteria  | What results were required for the system to be considered suitable?                      |
| Results              | What did the evaluation demonstrate?                                                      |
| Limitations          | Which conditions, populations, scenarios, or failure modes were not adequately covered?   |
| Findings             | Which failures, exceptions, or residual concerns were identified?                         |
| Decision             | Was the system approved, conditionally approved, restricted, or returned for remediation? |
| Evidence reference   | Where can the test results and approval decision be examined?                             |

The system card should not rely on unsupported descriptions such as *“the model passed testing”* or *“accuracy is high.”* Such statements do not identify the test conditions, the metric used, the acceptance threshold, or the limitations of the result.

A useful evaluation statement might explain that a system was tested against a defined set of representative support questions, achieved a specified performance threshold, and remained subject to human review because certain complex questions produced unsupported answers.

The exact results must come from actual test evidence. Illustrative examples should not be presented as real performance claims.

### 25. Documenting Performance Metrics and Acceptance Criteria

Performance metrics help establish whether an AI system performs adequately for its approved purpose. The relevant metrics depend on the system's functionality and the consequences of its errors.

A system card should identify the principal measures used to evaluate suitability and, where appropriate, ongoing performance.

Examples include:

- Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score for classification tasks.
- False-positive and false-negative rates for detection systems.
- Calibration measures for probabilistic predictions.
- Task completion and error rates for automated workflows.
- Retrieval relevance and source coverage for retrieval-augmented generation.
- Factual consistency and unsupported-claim rates for generated responses.
- Latency, availability, and throughput for operational performance.
- Disaggregated performance measures where differences between relevant groups may affect risk.
- Human override, escalation, and correction rates where these measures are meaningful.

Metrics must be interpreted in the context of the task. A single aggregate score may conceal important weaknesses.

For example, a fraud-detection model may achieve high overall accuracy because most transactions are legitimate, while failing to identify a significant proportion of fraudulent transactions. The system card should identify the measures that reflect the business objective and the consequences of errors rather than relying on accuracy alone.

Acceptance criteria should be established before the evaluation results are used to justify deployment, wherever practicable. Otherwise, teams may select thresholds after observing results in a way that makes weak performance appear acceptable.

The documentation should identify:

1. The metric and its definition.
2. The required acceptance threshold.
3. The evaluation conditions.
4. The observed result.
5. Material uncertainty or limitations.
6. The authority responsible for accepting exceptions.
7. The circumstances that require reassessment.

Acceptance criteria may include minimum performance thresholds, maximum error rates, mandatory human review, restricted operating conditions, or requirements for further validation.

For high-impact applications, the organization should consider whether the evaluation covers the relevant operating populations, edge cases, distribution shifts, and foreseeable misuse scenarios.

Performance metrics are not a substitute for risk assessment. A system may perform well against a technical metric while remaining unsuitable because of privacy exposure, security weaknesses, unacceptable bias, or inappropriate use.

### 26. Documenting Fairness, Bias, and Representativeness

Where an AI system can affect individuals or groups differently, the system card should describe relevant fairness considerations, the evaluation performed, and the limitations of the available evidence.

Bias can arise from multiple sources, including:

- Historical patterns in training or reference data.
- Underrepresentation of relevant populations.
- Inaccurate or incomplete input data.
- Features that act as proxies for sensitive characteristics.
- Differences in how the system performs across contexts.
- Human decisions embedded in labels, workflows, or acceptance criteria.
- Changes in the population or operating environment after deployment.

The system card should identify the groups, scenarios, or conditions considered relevant to the system's intended purpose and the rationale for that scope.

It should also record whether the evaluation was performed at model level, application level, or both.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Dataset representativeness assessments.
- Performance comparisons across relevant groups.
- Error analysis.
- Review of potentially discriminatory features or proxies.
- Evaluation of decision thresholds.
- Human review procedures.
- Documented limitations in available demographic or outcome data.
- Remediation plans for identified disparities.

Fairness is context-dependent. Different metrics can produce conflicting conclusions, and the appropriate evaluation method depends on the use case, available evidence, and applicable requirements.

For example, an AI-assisted recruitment system may produce different screening outcomes across demographic groups. The system card should not simply state that the system is unbiased because a general model evaluation found acceptable aggregate performance.

It should document the relevant evaluation scope, the observed results, the limitations of the analysis, and the controls used to prevent inappropriate reliance on model outputs.

Where reliable demographic data is unavailable or cannot lawfully be processed for evaluation, the organization should document the limitation and assess what other forms of assurance are appropriate. It should not claim that fairness has been established merely because no disparity was measured.

### 27. Documenting Robustness and Failure Behavior

Robustness describes the extent to which an AI system continues to behave acceptably under foreseeable variations, disturbances, or attempts to manipulate its behavior.

A system card should identify the robustness properties relevant to the intended use and the failure scenarios examined.

Depending on the system, these may include:

- Unexpected or malformed inputs.
- Missing, incomplete, or contradictory information.
- Distribution shifts in operational data.
- Adversarial examples.
- Prompt injection and indirect prompt injection.
- Retrieval of irrelevant or malicious content.
- Excessively long inputs or context saturation.
- Tool failures and API errors.
- Model-provider outages.
- Unexpected model-version changes.
- Repeated requests that cause resource exhaustion.
- Inconsistent outputs across similar cases.

The documentation should explain how the system is expected to behave when it cannot reliably complete its task.

Appropriate failure behavior may include refusing an unsupported request, returning a controlled error, escalating to a human, disabling a sensitive action, or reverting to a validated alternative process.

The system card should distinguish between a failure being detected, a failure being contained, and a failure being recovered from. These are different operational capabilities.

For example, an AI system that detects an invalid output but still transmits it to a customer has not adequately contained the failure. A system that blocks the output but provides no recovery path may prevent immediate harm while leaving the business process unavailable.

Where robustness testing identifies a material weakness, the system card should record the finding, the relevant risk-register reference, the mitigation status, and any operating restrictions.

### 28. Documenting Security Threats and Safeguards

The system card should describe the principal security threats relevant to the system architecture, data flows, integrations, and permitted actions.

AI systems introduce security considerations that overlap with conventional application security while also creating risks related to model behavior and the interaction between natural-language inputs and system capabilities.

Relevant threat categories may include:

- Unauthorized access to prompts, outputs, or connected data.
- Prompt injection and instruction manipulation.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Insecure model or application interfaces.
- Excessive permissions assigned to AI agents.
- Unsafe execution of model-generated code or commands.
- Poisoning of training, fine-tuning, or retrieval data.
- Model or service supply-chain compromise.
- Inadequate isolation between users or tenants.
- Abuse of model endpoints and resource exhaustion.
- Inadequate logging or incident detection.
- Insecure handling of generated outputs by downstream systems.

The system card should identify material safeguards and reference the corresponding control evidence.

Examples include least-privilege access, input and output validation, retrieval authorization checks, isolation of untrusted content, restrictions on tool execution, secure secret management, rate limiting, monitoring, and incident-response procedures.

The documentation should avoid implying that any single safeguard eliminates a threat.

For example, a system prompt instructing a model not to reveal confidential information is not equivalent to an enforced authorization boundary. If the model can retrieve documents, the retrieval layer should enforce the user's actual permissions independently of the model's natural-language instructions.

Likewise, a content filter may reduce certain unsafe outputs but should not be treated as a complete defense against data exfiltration or unauthorized tool execution.

The system card should describe the security architecture at a level sufficient for governance review while referencing detailed threat models, penetration-test reports, configuration records, and security assessments where necessary.

Material weaknesses, accepted exceptions, and unresolved security findings should remain visible to the appropriate risk and approval authorities.

### 29. Documenting Privacy and Data Protection Considerations

A system card should summarize privacy considerations associated with the AI system and identify the authoritative privacy records relevant to its operation.

The depth of documentation depends on the data processed, the system's purpose, the parties involved, and the applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

Relevant considerations may include:

- Categories of personal data processed.
- Purpose and legal basis where applicable.
- Data minimization and purpose limitation.
- Data subject categories.
- Data sources and collection methods.
- Retention and deletion arrangements.
- Provider access and onward disclosure.
- International transfers and processing locations.
- Access and correction mechanisms where applicable.
- Profiling or automated decision-making considerations.
- Privacy risks arising from prompts, outputs, and logs.
- Applicable privacy impact assessments.
- Restrictions on secondary use or model training.

The system card should not replace a formal privacy assessment or data protection impact assessment where one is required. It should identify the relevant assessment, its status, and any material conditions affecting system operation.

For example, a generative AI application may process customer correspondence containing names, contact details, and account information. The card should explain the relevant data flow, identify the external services receiving the information, and reference the controls and contractual arrangements governing that processing.

It should also describe relevant operational restrictions, such as prohibiting users from submitting certain categories of information or requiring masking before data is sent to an external service.

A privacy statement in the system card is not evidence that processing is lawful or that privacy risks have been adequately mitigated. Those conclusions require appropriate analysis and supporting evidence.

Where the organization cannot determine how a provider processes or retains personal data, the uncertainty should be escalated through the applicable privacy, legal, procurement, and risk-management processes.

### 30. Part 2 Core Principle

A governance-grade system card must describe the AI system as it actually operates, not merely as it was designed or marketed to operate. Model identity, configuration, evaluation, performance, fairness, robustness, security, and privacy information should be documented at a level proportionate to the system's intended purpose and risk.

Material claims should be linked to reliable evidence, and limitations must remain visible to decision-makers. Provider statements, internal assumptions, independently verified results, and unresolved information gaps must not be presented as equivalent forms of assurance.

The system card becomes a useful governance instrument when it provides an accurate basis for determining whether the AI system is suitable for its approved use, which safeguards are necessary, and what conditions must remain in place throughout operation.

## Part 3 — System Card Governance, Lifecycle Maintenance, and Assurance Integration

### 31. Establishing System Card Governance Requirements

An AI system card must operate within a defined governance framework that establishes who creates it, who reviews it, who approves it, and who is responsible for keeping it accurate throughout the system lifecycle.

Without defined governance requirements, system cards can become inconsistent records that depend on individual project teams rather than organizational policy. Different business units may document similar systems using different terminology, omit important information, or apply inconsistent approval criteria.

A formal system card governance standard should define the minimum documentation requirements, ownership responsibilities, review criteria, approval authority, and lifecycle triggers applicable to the organization's AI portfolio.

The governance standard should address the following elements:

- **Scope:** Which AI systems, AI-enabled applications, and use cases require system cards.
- **Documentation requirements:** Which fields are mandatory, conditional, or optional.
- **Ownership:** Who is accountable for the accuracy and maintenance of each card.
- **Review requirements:** Which stakeholders must review the documentation.
- **Approval authority:** Who can authorize a system for development, testing, production, or continued operation.
- **Evidence requirements:** Which claims require supporting records or independent verification.
- **Lifecycle maintenance:** Which events trigger an update or reassessment.
- **Access and confidentiality:** Who may access, modify, approve, or export system card information.
- **Quality assurance:** How completeness, accuracy, and consistency are checked.
- **Exceptions:** How missing information, deviations, and overdue reviews are managed.

The organization should distinguish between requirements that apply to every system and additional requirements that depend on risk classification.

For example, every registered AI system may require a unique identifier, intended purpose, accountable owner, lifecycle status, and documented approval status. A system that makes consequential recommendations or performs autonomous actions may additionally require detailed evaluation evidence, documented human oversight, explicit operating restrictions, and formal risk acceptance.

The governance standard should make these requirements enforceable through established business processes. A mandatory field in a template is useful, but it does not establish accountability unless someone is responsible for verifying that the information is accurate and sufficient.

### 32. Designing a Risk-Based System Card Methodology

System card requirements should be proportionate to the potential consequences of the AI system's use.

Applying the same documentation depth to every system may create unnecessary administrative work for low-impact applications while failing to provide sufficient scrutiny for high-impact systems.

A risk-based methodology establishes a common minimum standard and introduces additional documentation and assurance requirements where justified.

The methodology should consider factors such as:

- Potential harm to individuals or groups.
- Influence over consequential decisions.
- Sensitivity and volume of processed information.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Access to privileged systems or business functions.
- Scale of deployment.
- Complexity and opacity of the technology.
- Reliance on external providers.
- Exposure to adversarial inputs.
- Reversibility of system actions.
- Ability to detect, contain, and recover from failures.
- Applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements.

An organization may implement a tiered approach.

| Documentation tier | Illustrative system characteristics                                                                                        | Typical documentation expectations                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Basic              | Low-impact internal assistance with limited access and no consequential automated decisions                                | Core system identification, intended purpose, owner, data overview, limitations, and basic safeguards                                                     |
| Enhanced           | Systems integrated with enterprise data or used in material business workflows                                             | Detailed architecture, data flows, risk references, security and privacy assessments, evaluation evidence, and change controls                            |
| High assurance     | Systems with significant potential consequences, extensive autonomy, sensitive processing, or elevated regulatory exposure | Comprehensive system documentation, stronger validation evidence, formal approval, documented oversight, explicit risk decisions, and enhanced monitoring |

These tiers are illustrative rather than universal classifications. An organization should define its own criteria and map them to applicable legal obligations, internal risk appetite, and governance policies.

The tier assigned to a system should be documented and justified. Where uncertainty exists, the organization should apply its defined escalation process rather than automatically assigning the least demanding tier.

The methodology should also allow a system's documentation tier to change when its intended use, capabilities, deployment scale, or risk profile changes.

### 33. Defining Minimum Documentation Quality Standards

A system card should be assessed for information quality, not merely for the presence of completed fields.

A card can appear complete while containing vague descriptions, unsupported statements, outdated references, or inconsistent information. Such a record may satisfy a superficial completion check without supporting meaningful governance decisions.

A documentation quality standard should define what constitutes acceptable information for each critical field.

For example, a field labeled *“Security controls”* should not be considered adequate merely because it contains the phrase *“security controls implemented.”* The record should identify the relevant safeguards, their scope, their owners, and the evidence needed to verify implementation where appropriate.

A practical quality framework can evaluate the following dimensions:

| Quality dimension  | Assessment question                                                             |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness       | Are all mandatory and applicable fields addressed?                              |
| Accuracy           | Does the information reflect the actual deployed system?                        |
| Specificity        | Is the description sufficiently precise to support review?                      |
| Currency           | Is the information current for the documented system version?                   |
| Traceability       | Can material claims be connected to authoritative records?                      |
| Consistency        | Does the card align with the inventory, risk register, and related assessments? |
| Evidential support | Are important claims supported by appropriate evidence?                         |
| Accountability     | Are responsible owners and approval authorities identified?                     |
| Risk relevance     | Does the card address the system's material risk exposures?                     |
| Accessibility      | Can authorized stakeholders locate and interpret the information?               |

The organization should define which deficiencies prevent approval and which can be accepted temporarily under controlled conditions.

For example, an incorrect model identifier or missing accountable owner may prevent reliable authorization. A minor formatting inconsistency may be corrected without blocking deployment. An unresolved provider-data-retention question may require a more substantive assessment depending on the information being processed.

Quality checks should be designed to identify deficiencies that affect governance outcomes rather than focusing exclusively on formatting or document appearance.

### 34. Implementing System Card Completeness Reviews

A completeness review verifies that the required documentation exists and that applicable sections have been addressed.

It is distinct from a technical validation or substantive risk assessment.

A completeness review may examine whether:

- The system has a unique identifier.
- The intended purpose and scope are defined.
- The business and technical owners are identified.
- The architecture and material dependencies are documented.
- Relevant data categories and flows are described.
- Known limitations are recorded.
- Applicable risk assessments are referenced.
- Required safeguards and controls are identified.
- Evaluation evidence is available where required.
- Approval status and operating restrictions are documented.
- The review date and version information are current.
- Required supporting records can be located.

The reviewer should distinguish between a genuinely inapplicable field and an information gap.

For example, a system that does not process personal data may not require the same privacy documentation as a system that processes customer records. However, the determination that personal data is not processed should have a reasonable basis, particularly where prompts, logs, retrieval sources, or outputs could contain personal information.

Completeness reviews can be performed through standardized checklists or workflow validation. Automated checks may identify missing identifiers, empty mandatory fields, expired review dates, or broken references.

However, automated validation cannot reliably determine whether a complex description is accurate, whether a risk has been adequately evaluated, or whether the evidence supports a particular governance conclusion.

Organizations should therefore combine automated checks with human review appropriate to the system's risk and complexity.

A failed completeness review should generate a documented issue, identify the responsible owner, and define the action required before the card can proceed to the next governance stage.

### 35. Establishing System Card Approval Gates

System cards should support explicit governance decisions at defined lifecycle stages.

Approval gates prevent a system from progressing into a higher-risk stage without the documentation, assessments, and authorization required by organizational policy.

A governance process may establish separate gates for:

1. Initial registration and scoping.
2. Authorization to develop or configure the system.
3. Approval for testing with representative or sensitive data.
4. Approval for production deployment.
5. Approval for a material change.
6. Periodic authorization to continue operating.
7. Reinstatement following suspension.
8. Retirement and closure.

Not every organization needs eight separate approval stages. The appropriate structure depends on system complexity, risk, delivery methodology, and applicable requirements.

Each gate should define its entry criteria, required evidence, decision authority, and possible outcomes.

For example, production approval may require:

- An approved intended-purpose statement.
- Completion of required risk assessments.
- Evaluation against defined acceptance criteria.
- Implementation of mandatory controls.
- Documented ownership and operational responsibilities.
- Defined monitoring and incident-response arrangements.
- Resolution of critical findings.
- Authorized acceptance of residual risks within applicable delegation limits.

Possible outcomes include approval, conditional approval, rejection, or deferral pending additional evidence.

Conditional approval should specify the conditions, owners, deadlines, and consequences of non-compliance. It should not be used to bypass mandatory legal requirements or controls that must be in place before deployment.

The system card should reference the approval decision and record the conditions that constrain the system's permitted operation.

This creates a traceable relationship between documented system characteristics, risk evaluation, and authorization.

### 36. Managing Exceptions and Documentation Deficiencies

Organizations may encounter situations in which a system card cannot be completed fully because information is unavailable, a provider restricts disclosure, or a legacy system lacks adequate documentation.

Such situations should be managed through a formal exception process rather than through informal omissions.

A documentation exception should identify:

- The requirement that cannot be satisfied.
- The reason for the exception.
- The affected system and documentation sections.
- The risk created by the information gap.
- The compensating measures, if any.
- The accountable owner.
- The authorized decision-maker.
- The approval date and expiry or review date.
- The conditions for closure.

The organization should distinguish between a documentation exception and acceptance of the underlying risk.

A documentation exception permits a defined departure from an internal documentation requirement where policy allows it. Risk acceptance is a separate decision concerning the exposure that remains. One does not automatically authorize the other.

For example, a third-party provider may not disclose its complete model architecture. The organization may document that limitation and use alternative assurance evidence, contractual protections, independent evaluations, or restricted deployment conditions.

However, if the unavailable information prevents the organization from satisfying a mandatory regulatory requirement or determining whether the system is suitable for its intended purpose, an exception may not be sufficient to authorize deployment.

Exceptions should be time-bound where appropriate and periodically reviewed. Repeated renewals without meaningful progress may indicate a structural weakness in vendor management, system architecture, or governance design.

### 37. Integrating System Cards with the AI Inventory

The AI inventory and system card should function as connected governance records.

The inventory provides the portfolio-level view of AI systems, while the system card provides a more detailed description of an individual system or defined use case.

The relationship should be established through a stable, unique identifier rather than relying exclusively on names that may change.

For example:

| Inventory field        | Related system card information                       |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| AI system ID           | Primary identifier used to locate the card            |
| System name            | Official system name and documented aliases           |
| Business owner         | Accountable business role and contact reference       |
| Provider               | Model and service provider details                    |
| Business purpose       | Detailed intended-purpose statement                   |
| Lifecycle status       | Current status and relevant approval milestones       |
| Risk classification    | Classification rationale and linked assessment        |
| Deployment environment | Detailed architecture and deployment context          |
| Review date            | Last review, next review, and relevant change history |

The inventory should not attempt to reproduce every field from the system card. Duplicating extensive information across multiple records increases the likelihood of inconsistency.

Instead, the inventory should retain concise portfolio-level attributes and provide a reliable reference to the detailed documentation.

Changes to critical attributes should be governed so that the inventory and system card remain synchronized. For example, a change in system ownership should update both records where ownership is recorded in each.

Organizations should also determine how they represent shared components. One foundation model may support several applications, while a single application may depend on several models and services. The documentation structure should preserve these relationships without treating every shared component as an independent business use case.

### 38. Integrating System Cards with AI Risk Registers

System cards and AI risk registers serve complementary purposes.

The system card provides context about the system, its intended use, architecture, limitations, and principal safeguards. The risk register records the risks identified, their evaluation, ownership, treatment decisions, and residual-risk status.

The relationship should be explicit enough for reviewers to move from a system-level description to the detailed assessment of a specific risk.

A system card may summarize the following for each material risk:

- Risk identifier.
- Short description of the risk.
- Principal affected assets, individuals, or business processes.
- Relevant safeguards.
- Residual-risk status.
- Risk owner or decision authority.
- Reference to the authoritative risk-register entry.

For example, an AI-enabled customer-service system may have a risk involving disclosure of confidential customer information through unauthorized retrieval.

The system card should describe the relevant data-access architecture, identify the safeguards intended to prevent unauthorized retrieval, and reference the detailed risk assessment.

The risk register should contain the complete risk analysis, including the risk statement, evaluation methodology, likelihood and impact where used, treatment actions, control references, residual-risk determination, and risk acceptance record where applicable.

This separation allows the system card to remain readable without losing the traceability needed for risk management.

If the risk register identifies a material risk that is absent from the system card, the documentation should be reviewed for completeness. Conversely, a system card that describes a material limitation or exposure should provide a clear path to the corresponding risk assessment or explain why a formal risk entry is not required under the organization's methodology.

### 39. Integrating System Cards with the AI Control Library

The AI control library defines the control objectives and requirements that the organization expects applicable AI systems to satisfy.

The system card identifies the controls relevant to a particular system and provides context for determining how those controls apply.

The relationship should distinguish among control applicability, implementation status, and operating effectiveness.

These concepts are not interchangeable.

**Control applicability** determines whether a control requirement applies to the system.

**Implementation status** indicates whether the control has been established or implemented.

**Operating effectiveness** concerns whether the control operates as intended over the relevant period and under the conditions examined.

For example, a control requiring human approval before an AI agent performs a sensitive business action may apply to an agent with write access to enterprise systems. The system card should identify the approval boundary and reference the implementation evidence.

If the approval mechanism exists but has not been tested, the card should not imply that the control has demonstrated operating effectiveness.

A useful control mapping may contain:

| Field                    | Purpose                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Control ID               | Identifies the authoritative control-library entry            |
| Applicability            | Records whether and why the control applies                   |
| Implementation reference | Points to the relevant technical or procedural implementation |
| Control owner            | Identifies the person or role accountable for operation       |
| Evidence reference       | Identifies the supporting evidence                            |
| Status                   | Records the documented implementation or assurance state      |
| Exception reference      | Links any approved exception or unresolved deficiency         |

The system card should not become a substitute control library. Control definitions and organization-wide requirements should remain centrally governed, while system-specific implementation details and evidence references should be maintained in the relevant records.

This structure supports consistency across the AI portfolio and enables auditors to evaluate whether controls are applied appropriately to individual systems.

### 40. Integrating System Cards with Third-Party Risk Management

AI systems often depend on external models, hosted inference services, cloud platforms, data providers, software libraries, and specialized AI services.

The system card should identify material third-party dependencies and reference the relevant vendor risk-management records.

Third-party documentation may need to cover:

- Provider identity and service scope.
- Model and service versions where available.
- Data-processing arrangements.
- Data retention and deletion commitments.
- Use of customer information for model training or service improvement.
- Hosting and processing locations.
- Subprocessors and material dependencies.
- Security and privacy assurance evidence.
- Vulnerability and incident-notification arrangements.
- Service availability and continuity commitments.
- Contractual restrictions and permitted uses.
- Provider change-management practices.
- Exit, portability, and replacement considerations.

The system card should distinguish provider assertions from independently reviewed evidence. A provider's statement that a service is secure or compliant does not automatically establish that the organization's specific deployment satisfies its requirements.

For example, a cloud provider may offer a secure managed model service, but the organization remains responsible for configuring access controls, selecting appropriate data sources, restricting tool permissions, monitoring usage, and ensuring that the deployment is suitable for its purpose.

The card should also identify material concentration or dependency risks where a system relies on a single provider, model endpoint, or proprietary interface.

If the provider changes a model or service in a way that could affect the system's behavior, security, data processing, or performance, the organization should determine whether the change triggers evaluation, documentation updates, or renewed approval.

Third-party dependencies should therefore be documented as part of the system's operational context, not treated solely as a procurement concern.

### 41. Maintaining Evidence References and Traceability

System cards should provide reliable references to supporting evidence without duplicating the entire evidence repository.

An evidence reference should enable an authorized reviewer to locate the relevant record and understand which claim it supports.

Useful reference attributes may include:

- Evidence identifier.
- Evidence title or description.
- Evidence owner.
- Record location.
- Relevant system or control version.
- Date of creation or evaluation.
- Review or approval status.
- Classification or access restrictions.
- Relationship to the relevant system card section.

Examples of supporting evidence include architecture diagrams, data-flow assessments, evaluation reports, security testing, privacy assessments, vendor due diligence, monitoring procedures, approval records, and incident reports.

Evidence references should be sufficiently precise to avoid ambiguity. A generic reference such as *“see security documentation”* may be inadequate if the organization maintains multiple security assessments or versions.

Traceability should work in both directions.

A reviewer examining a system card should be able to locate the supporting evidence. A reviewer examining a material test result, risk decision, or approval should also be able to identify the affected system and the applicable system card version.

The organization should establish controls for evidence retention, access, integrity, and version management. If an evidence record is superseded, the historical relationship should remain understandable where needed for audit or regulatory review.

The existence of a link is not proof of evidence quality. Reviewers must still assess whether the referenced record is relevant, current, sufficiently reliable, and appropriate to support the claim.

### 42. Establishing Periodic Review Requirements

System cards should be reviewed periodically to determine whether they continue to describe the system accurately and whether the documented governance arrangements remain appropriate.

The review frequency should reflect the system's risk, complexity, rate of change, and operating context.

A periodic review should consider:

- Whether the intended purpose remains unchanged.
- Whether the system architecture and dependencies remain accurate.
- Whether model or provider versions have changed.
- Whether data sources or processing arrangements have changed.
- Whether evaluation results remain relevant.
- Whether material incidents or failures have occurred.
- Whether safeguards remain implemented and effective.
- Whether risk assessments and acceptance decisions remain valid.
- Whether operating restrictions are still appropriate.
- Whether ownership and approval authority remain correct.
- Whether applicable legal or contractual requirements have changed.
- Whether previous corrective actions have been completed.

The organization should define the required review interval and the events that trigger an earlier review.

A fixed annual review may be appropriate for some systems, but it should not be treated as sufficient where material changes occur between review dates.

Conversely, a low-impact system with stable functionality may require a different review cadence from a rapidly evolving agentic system with access to sensitive enterprise operations.

The system card should record the review outcome, date, reviewer, material findings, and required actions.

Where the review identifies a significant discrepancy, the organization should determine whether the system can continue operating under its current authorization or whether restrictions, reassessment, or suspension are required.

### 43. Managing Material Changes and Reassessment Triggers

A material change is a change that could affect the system's intended purpose, risk profile, performance, security, privacy, regulatory status, or approved operating conditions.

The organization should define materiality criteria so that change-management decisions do not depend solely on individual judgment.

Potential triggers include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- Introduction of a new user population.
- Expansion to a new business process.
- Addition of sensitive data sources.
- Introduction of a new model or provider.
- Significant model-version changes.
- Increased autonomy or additional tool permissions.
- Changes to human-review requirements.
- New external data transfers.
- Material changes in performance.
- Discovery of a previously unknown vulnerability.
- A significant AI-related incident.
- Changes in applicable regulatory requirements.
- Changes in provider terms that affect data use or service conditions.

A material change should initiate an impact assessment. The assessment should determine which parts of the system card require updating and whether additional risk analysis, testing, approval, or control changes are necessary.

Not every change requires the complete governance process to be repeated. The response should be proportionate to the impact of the change.

For example, correcting a minor user-interface defect may require ordinary change control, while enabling an AI agent to modify customer records may materially change its risk profile and require renewed security testing and approval.

The system card should identify the change, its assessment outcome, the updated version, and the relevant authorization decision.

This provides a defensible record of how the organization determined that the changed system remained suitable for its approved purpose.

### 44. Managing System Card Quality Across Multiple Business Units

Large organizations may operate hundreds or thousands of AI systems across different business units, geographic locations, technology platforms, and regulatory environments.

Maintaining consistent documentation across this portfolio requires centralized standards combined with accountable local ownership.

The central AI governance function should establish the system card template, mandatory fields, classification methodology, quality criteria, and minimum approval requirements.

Business units and system owners should remain responsible for providing accurate information about their individual systems.

A federated governance model may include:

- Central ownership of the documentation standard.
- Local ownership of system-specific information.
- Defined review and approval responsibilities.
- Standardized identifiers and controlled terminology.
- Shared evidence repositories and reference conventions.
- Central reporting on completeness and review status.
- Escalation for unresolved deficiencies.
- Periodic quality sampling across business units.

The organization should avoid allowing each business unit to develop unrelated system card templates unless a controlled variation is justified.

Uncontrolled variation can make it difficult to compare systems, identify portfolio-wide risks, or produce reliable management reports.

At the same time, a single template should not force materially different technologies into inappropriate documentation structures. Conditional sections and specialized annexes can accommodate machine learning, generative AI, agentic systems, and other architectures while preserving a consistent governance baseline.

The objective is to standardize the governance requirements without obscuring meaningful differences between systems.

### 45. Monitoring System Card Currency and Completeness

System card currency refers to whether the documentation reflects the current system and its approved operating conditions. Completeness refers to whether required information is present and adequately addressed.

An organization should monitor both dimensions.

Useful indicators include:

- Percentage of in-scope systems with a system card.
- Percentage of system cards with an accountable owner.
- Percentage of cards reviewed within the required interval.
- Percentage of cards linked to current risk assessments.
- Percentage of cards with documented approval status.
- Number of overdue material updates.
- Number of unresolved mandatory-field deficiencies.
- Number of systems with expired or missing approval records.
- Number of material changes not reflected in the card.
- Percentage of high-risk systems with current evaluation evidence.
- Number of documentation exceptions past their review or expiry dates.

These indicators should be interpreted carefully.

A high completion rate does not necessarily indicate high documentation quality. Likewise, a low number of recorded exceptions may indicate either effective governance or inadequate identification and reporting.

Metrics should therefore be supplemented by quality reviews, targeted sampling, and analysis of material deficiencies.

The organization should define the data sources, calculation methods, reporting frequency, and accountable owners for each metric. Where indicators are used for executive reporting, the methodology should be consistent enough to support meaningful comparison over time.

### 46. Using System Cards to Support Internal Audit Planning

System cards can support internal audit planning by providing a structured view of AI systems, their purposes, risk exposures, dependencies, and control arrangements.

An auditor can use this information to understand the system population, identify systems that warrant greater scrutiny, and define an appropriate audit scope.

Potential audit considerations include:

- Whether the system is registered and appropriately classified.
- Whether the documented intended purpose matches actual use.
- Whether material risks have been assessed.
- Whether required controls are implemented and tested.
- Whether approval conditions are being observed.
- Whether third-party dependencies are appropriately governed.
- Whether evaluation evidence supports deployment.
- Whether system cards are updated after material changes.
- Whether access restrictions and human oversight operate as documented.
- Whether exceptions and corrective actions are appropriately managed.

System cards can also help auditors identify inconsistencies across governance records. For example, a system card may describe a human approval requirement while operational records show that the system executes the relevant action automatically.

Such a discrepancy does not by itself establish the full nature or extent of a control failure, but it provides a clear basis for further examination.

Auditors should not rely exclusively on system cards as evidence of control effectiveness. The card is primarily a documentation and governance artifact. Independent testing may require configuration inspection, interviews, transaction sampling, evaluation of logs, or examination of operational evidence.

The system card should therefore inform audit procedures without replacing them.

### 47. Using System Cards to Support Regulatory and Compliance Reviews

System cards can help organizations organize information needed for regulatory, legal, and contractual reviews.

They may provide a starting point for determining which AI systems are in scope, how they are used, what data they process, who is accountable, and which assessments or controls are relevant.

Depending on the applicable framework and use case, reviewers may need to examine:

- Intended purpose and deployment context.
- System classification and applicable obligations.
- Data governance and privacy arrangements.
- Technical documentation.
- Evaluation and validation evidence.
- Human oversight.
- Risk management.
- Logging and traceability.
- Security safeguards.
- Provider and supply-chain dependencies.
- Change management.
- Post-deployment monitoring.
- Incident handling and corrective action.

A system card should reference the authoritative records used to demonstrate compliance rather than claiming that the card itself establishes compliance.

The existence of a completed system card does not automatically satisfy a specific legal documentation obligation. The organization must map applicable requirements to the actual records, processes, and evidence required by the relevant law, regulation, standard, or contract.

Where several obligations apply, the organization should identify common documentation requirements and avoid unnecessary duplication while ensuring that each applicable requirement remains demonstrably satisfied.

The card should also distinguish between obligations that have been assessed as applicable, obligations that are not applicable with a documented rationale, and obligations whose applicability remains unresolved.

This helps prevent broad compliance statements from obscuring important gaps.

### 48. Using System Cards During AI Incidents

An AI-related incident may reveal that a system behaves differently from what its documentation describes.

Examples include unauthorized data retrieval, unsafe agent actions, unexpected output behavior, a material decline in performance, or a provider change that affects system operation.

During incident response, the system card can provide useful context about:

- Intended system behavior.
- Approved operating boundaries.
- Model and service dependencies.
- Data flows and trust boundaries.
- Required safeguards.
- Human intervention points.
- Accountable owners.
- Relevant monitoring arrangements.
- Known limitations and previous findings.
- Approval restrictions.

The incident-response team should compare the documented design and expected behavior with the observed incident.

For example, if an AI agent performs an unauthorized action, the investigation may examine whether the action was outside the documented scope, whether tool permissions were excessive, whether an approval gate failed, or whether the system card itself was outdated.

The card should not be modified in a way that erases the historical state relevant to the incident. Updates should preserve the previous version and record the reason for the change.

Following the incident, the organization should determine whether the card requires updates, whether the risk register must be revised, and whether renewed approval is necessary before the system resumes operation.

Incident findings may also reveal weaknesses in the organization's documentation standards. If several incidents expose undocumented dependencies or unclear accountability, the central governance framework may require improvement.

### 49. Defining System Card Retirement and Archival Requirements

System card governance continues through retirement and closure.

When a system is retired, the organization should update its lifecycle status and preserve the documentation required for accountability, audit, regulatory obligations, contractual commitments, and incident investigation.

Retirement-related information may include:

- Retirement decision and date.
- Approving authority.
- Reason for retirement.
- Final production version.
- Outstanding risks or findings.
- Data retention and deletion status.
- Revocation of credentials and access.
- Disconnection of integrations and external services.
- Contract termination or provider exit arrangements.
- Transfer of relevant records to successor systems.
- Residual obligations and accountable owners.

The organization should distinguish between a system that has been retired and a system that has been suspended temporarily.

A suspended system may remain subject to remediation, reassessment, and possible reinstatement. A retired system should generally no longer be authorized for ordinary production use, although its records and some supporting infrastructure may need to be retained for legitimate purposes.

Archival arrangements should preserve version history and relevant evidence without retaining information longer than permitted by applicable retention requirements.

Where a retired system is replaced by a successor, the new system should not automatically inherit the old system's approval. The organization should assess whether differences in model behavior, architecture, data, purpose, or operational controls require a new or revised system card and a separate authorization decision.

### 50. Part 3 Core Principle

Effective system card governance requires a controlled process that connects documentation to risk assessment, control implementation, approval, change management, monitoring, audit, incident response, and retirement.

A standardized template is only the starting point. The organization must establish accountable ownership, risk-based requirements, evidence traceability, quality reviews, formal approval gates, and defined reassessment triggers.

System cards should remain accurate throughout the AI lifecycle and should be consistent with the inventory, risk register, control library, and supporting evidence. Their purpose is not merely to describe AI systems, but to provide a reliable governance basis for determining whether those systems remain appropriately authorized and controlled.

## Part 4 — System Card Implementation, Governance Metrics, and Continuous Assurance

### 51. Establishing an End-to-End System Card Implementation Process

An effective system card program requires a repeatable implementation process that transforms documentation requirements into operational governance activities. The process should define when a system card is created, how information is collected, how quality is assessed, how approval is obtained, and how the card is maintained throughout the system lifecycle.

The organization should avoid treating system card creation as an isolated administrative task performed immediately before production deployment. System documentation should begin early enough to influence system design, risk assessment, control selection, and deployment decisions.

A practical implementation process consists of the following stages.

**Stage 1 — Identification and registration**

The responsible business or technical team identifies the AI system or use case and creates the corresponding inventory record. A unique system identifier is assigned so that the card can be linked to related governance records.

**Stage 2 — Scope and classification**

The system owner defines the system boundary, intended purpose, users, principal dependencies, and operating context. The organization applies its risk classification methodology to determine the level of documentation and assurance required.

**Stage 3 — Initial documentation**

The system owner creates the system card using the approved template. Initial information includes the system purpose, architecture, data flows, model dependencies, known limitations, ownership, and proposed operating conditions.

**Stage 4 — Assessment and evidence collection**

Relevant stakeholders provide the supporting information required for risk, security, privacy, compliance, technical validation, and third-party assessments. Material information gaps are recorded rather than concealed by generic statements.

**Stage 5 — Quality and consistency review**

The organization verifies that the card is sufficiently complete, accurate, current, and consistent with the AI inventory, risk register, control library, and supporting records.

**Stage 6 — Governance decision**

The designated authority reviews the documentation and supporting assessments. The system may be approved, conditionally approved, returned for remediation, restricted, or rejected according to the organization's decision criteria.

**Stage 7 — Operational maintenance**

After deployment, the system owner maintains the card through periodic reviews, material-change assessments, incident follow-up, and updates to relevant evidence references.

**Stage 8 — Retirement and archival**

When the system is retired, the owner records the retirement decision, closes or transfers relevant responsibilities, and preserves the records required by the organization's retention and accountability requirements.

The implementation process should establish explicit entry and exit criteria for each stage. For example, a system should not progress to production approval if mandatory assessments remain incomplete or a required safeguard has not been implemented.

The process should also allow iterative reassessment. If testing reveals a material weakness, the system may return to an earlier stage for redesign, additional controls, or further evaluation.

### 52. Defining Roles and Responsibilities Through a RACI Model

A responsibility assignment model helps prevent gaps between the people who prepare system documentation and the people who are accountable for governance decisions.

A RACI model distinguishes four responsibility categories:

- **Responsible (R):** Performs the activity or produces the required deliverable.
- **Accountable (A):** Owns the outcome and has the authority to approve or accept responsibility for it.
- **Consulted (C):** Provides expertise or input before the decision.
- **Informed (I):** Receives relevant information about the activity or outcome.

The following example illustrates how responsibilities might be allocated.

| Activity                             | Business owner | System owner | AI governance | Security and privacy | Risk authority | Internal audit |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------- | ------------ | ------------- | -------------------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| Define intended purpose              | A              | R            | C             | C                    | I              | I              |
| Create and maintain system card      | C              | A/R          | C             | C                    | I              | I              |
| Define documentation standard        | I              | C            | A/R           | C                    | I              | C              |
| Assess technical architecture        | C              | A/R          | C             | C                    | I              | I              |
| Conduct security and privacy reviews | C              | R            | C             | A/R                  | I              | I              |
| Identify and assess risks            | C              | R            | C             | C                    | A              | I              |
| Verify documentation quality         | C              | R            | A             | C                    | I              | I              |
| Approve production deployment        | C              | R            | C             | C                    | A              | I              |
| Accept material residual risk        | C              | C            | C             | C                    | A/R            | I              |
| Review documentation independently   | I              | I            | C             | C                    | I              | A/R            |

This table is illustrative. The organization should adapt the assignments to its operating model, segregation-of-duties requirements, and formal delegation of authority.

The model should distinguish the authority to approve a system from the responsibility to maintain its documentation. The system owner may prepare the card and coordinate assessments, but that does not necessarily authorize the owner to accept every associated risk.

Similarly, internal audit should retain appropriate independence. Auditors may provide observations about documentation quality or governance design, but management remains responsible for operating the controls and making management decisions.

Where one individual performs several roles, the organization should document the arrangement and identify any independent review needed to manage conflicts of interest.

### 53. Defining System Card Governance Key Performance Indicators

A system card program should be measured using indicators that reflect coverage, documentation quality, review timeliness, and governance effectiveness.

Metrics should help management determine whether the organization maintains reliable system documentation and whether material deficiencies are being resolved.

Useful indicators include the following.

**53.1 System card coverage**

Measures the proportion of in-scope AI systems with an established system card.

[\
\text{Coverage} =\
\frac{\text{In-scope systems with system cards}}\
{\text{Total in-scope AI systems}}\
\times 100\
]

A low coverage rate may indicate incomplete inventory identification, insufficient documentation capacity, or weaknesses in the process that triggers card creation.

**53.2 Documentation completeness**

Measures the proportion of reviewed system cards that satisfy the organization's mandatory and applicable documentation requirements.

[\
\text{Completeness Rate} =\
\frac{\text{Cards passing completeness review}}\
{\text{Cards reviewed}}\
\times 100\
]

The organization should define the mandatory fields, assessment criteria, and treatment of conditional requirements before calculating this metric.

**53.3 Review timeliness**

Measures the proportion of system cards reviewed within the required review interval.

[\
\text{Review Timeliness} =\
\frac{\text{Cards reviewed on time}}\
{\text{Cards due for review}}\
\times 100\
]

This metric should distinguish overdue cards from cards that are not yet due.

**53.4 Evidence traceability**

Measures the proportion of applicable material claims that have valid references to supporting evidence.

**53.5 Material-change update compliance**

Measures whether system cards are updated within the defined period following changes that require documentation updates.

**53.6 Outstanding documentation deficiencies**

Tracks the number, severity, age, and ownership of unresolved documentation issues.

**53.7 Approval integrity**

Measures whether systems requiring formal approval have an identifiable and current approval record consistent with their actual lifecycle status.

**53.8 Exception management**

Tracks the number of open documentation exceptions, their age, approval status, expiry dates, and remediation progress.

A dashboard should not treat these indicators as interchangeable. A system card may be complete but outdated, current but unsupported by evidence, or well documented but associated with unresolved high-risk findings.

The organization should therefore use a balanced set of measures rather than relying on a single completion percentage.

### 54. Defining Governance Thresholds and Escalation Criteria

Metrics become operationally useful when the organization establishes thresholds that trigger investigation, escalation, or corrective action.

Thresholds should be aligned with internal policy, risk appetite, regulatory obligations, and the significance of the affected systems.

Examples of escalation conditions include:

- An in-scope production system has no system card.
- A high-risk system has an overdue mandatory review.
- A card identifies an unknown owner or unclear approval authority.
- A material change has occurred without the required documentation update.
- An approval record cannot be located.
- A system card contains a critical unsupported claim.
- An unresolved information gap prevents the organization from assessing a material risk.
- A required documentation exception has expired.
- The documented operating restrictions conflict with actual system behavior.

The organization should distinguish between routine documentation defects and conditions that undermine the basis for system authorization.

A minor formatting issue may require ordinary correction. An absent system owner may prevent effective accountability. An outdated card that fails to disclose newly enabled autonomous actions may require immediate escalation because the documented approval may no longer reflect the actual system.

Escalation criteria should identify:

1. The triggering condition.
2. The severity classification.
3. The accountable owner.
4. The required response.
5. The target resolution date.
6. The authority responsible for deciding whether operation may continue.
7. The evidence required to close the issue.

Where the deficiency creates a material governance or safety concern, the organization should determine whether the system requires additional restrictions, renewed approval, suspension, or other proportionate action.

### 55. Conducting Independent System Card Quality Assurance

Independent quality assurance helps determine whether system cards are reliable enough to support governance decisions.

The process should go beyond checking whether mandatory fields contain text. Reviewers should examine whether the documented information is accurate, internally consistent, supported by evidence, and appropriate to the system's risk.

A quality assurance review may assess:

- Whether the documented system matches the deployed implementation.
- Whether the intended purpose defines meaningful operating boundaries.
- Whether material components and dependencies are included.
- Whether data flows and information-processing arrangements are accurately represented.
- Whether known limitations and failure modes are disclosed.
- Whether relevant risks and controls are traceable.
- Whether evaluation claims are supported by appropriate test results.
- Whether ownership and approval arrangements are clear.
- Whether change history reflects material modifications.
- Whether outstanding deficiencies and exceptions are appropriately managed.

The reviewer should distinguish between a documentation defect and an underlying control deficiency.

For example, a system card may fail to reference an existing access-control test report. This is a traceability defect if the test exists and remains applicable. If the access control has never been implemented, the issue is more serious because the underlying safeguard is absent.

Both issues require attention, but they have different causes, risk implications, and closure criteria.

Quality assurance should use defined review procedures and retain evidence of the work performed. Depending on the system's risk, this may include document inspection, interviews with owners, comparison against configuration records, and sampling of operational evidence.

Where the quality assurance function also supports system approval, the organization should consider whether independent review is necessary for high-impact systems or material risk decisions.

### 56. Designing a Risk-Based System Card Sampling Program

Organizations with large AI portfolios may not be able to conduct equally intensive reviews of every system at the same frequency.

A risk-based sampling program can provide additional assurance that system cards are accurate and governance requirements are being applied consistently.

Sampling should be based on a defined population, explicit selection criteria, and a documented review method.

Potential selection factors include:

- Inherent risk classification.
- Potential impact on individuals or business operations.
- Level of autonomy.
- Sensitivity of processed information.
- Recent model or architecture changes.
- Previous documentation deficiencies.
- Significant AI-related incidents.
- Reliance on critical third-party providers.
- New or rapidly evolving technologies.
- Business units with repeated governance exceptions.
- Systems approaching mandatory review deadlines.

The sampling strategy should include both risk-based selection and, where appropriate, some degree of random selection.

Risk-based sampling concentrates effort on systems with elevated exposure. Random selection can help identify deficiencies in parts of the portfolio that have not already attracted management attention.

The organization should document the population, selection method, sample size rationale, review procedures, findings, and limitations.

For example, a review of ten high-risk systems may identify significant weaknesses among those systems, but it cannot automatically establish the prevalence of the same weaknesses across the entire AI portfolio.

Sampling conclusions must reflect the method used. Statistical estimates require an appropriate sampling design and sufficient evidence; judgmental samples should not be presented as statistically representative.

The program should also define how recurring findings affect future sampling. If a business unit repeatedly produces incomplete cards, additional targeted reviews may be justified until the underlying process improves.

### 57. Classifying System Card Findings

System card reviews should use a consistent finding-classification methodology so that deficiencies can be prioritized, escalated, and reported meaningfully.

A finding should describe the observed condition, the requirement or expected state, the supporting evidence, the risk or consequence, and the action required.

A practical classification model may include the following categories.

| Classification | Description                                                                                               | Illustrative example                                                                                                                         |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical       | A deficiency that undermines a fundamental governance requirement or creates an immediate, severe concern | A production AI agent has materially expanded permissions, but its documented approval and operating restrictions do not cover those actions |
| High           | A significant weakness that materially impairs risk oversight or the reliability of authorization         | A high-impact system lacks required evaluation evidence or has no clearly accountable risk owner                                             |
| Moderate       | A meaningful deficiency that weakens documentation reliability or a defined control process               | Material model dependencies are documented incompletely, limiting change-impact assessment                                                   |
| Low            | A limited deficiency with relatively small governance impact                                              | A non-critical reference or review field requires correction without materially affecting the approval basis                                 |
| Observation    | An improvement opportunity that does not currently meet the organization's criteria for a formal finding  | A standardized evidence-reference convention could improve consistency                                                                       |

The organization should define classification criteria rather than relying exclusively on descriptive labels.

Severity should reflect the consequence of the deficiency, the systems affected, the likelihood of resulting harm, the extent of control failure, and the organization's applicable requirements.

A documentation defect should not automatically be classified as low severity simply because it occurs in a document. If the defect conceals a material risk, prevents valid approval, or causes operators to act outside authorized boundaries, its consequences may be significant.

Findings should also distinguish between the underlying condition and its root cause. Several incomplete system cards may result from a single systemic weakness, such as the absence of a defined ownership model or inadequate integration between the AI inventory and change-management process.

Identifying the systemic cause supports more effective corrective action than correcting each card independently.

### 58. Linking Findings to Corrective Actions

A system card finding should be tracked until the organization has sufficient evidence that the required correction has been implemented and the issue has been appropriately resolved.

The corrective-action record should identify:

- Finding identifier.
- Affected system and system card version.
- Finding classification.
- Requirement or control reference.
- Description of the observed condition.
- Risk or consequence.
- Root-cause analysis where required.
- Agreed corrective action.
- Responsible action owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim risk treatment, where needed.
- Closure evidence.
- Independent validation requirements.
- Final closure decision.

The action should address the cause of the deficiency rather than merely improve the appearance of the documentation.

For example, if a system card repeatedly becomes outdated after model changes, the corrective action should not be limited to updating the current card. The organization should also examine whether the model-change process reliably triggers a documentation review.

Corrective actions may require changes to:

- System card content.
- AI governance procedures.
- Inventory or risk-register integration.
- Change-management workflows.
- Technical controls.
- Ownership assignments.
- Approval criteria.
- Evidence-retention arrangements.
- Staff training.
- Monitoring and escalation mechanisms.

A finding should not be closed solely because the owner reports that the action is complete. Closure should be supported by evidence appropriate to the finding's severity and nature.

For material findings, an independent reviewer may need to verify the correction before closure.

### 59. Applying Root-Cause Analysis to Documentation Failures

Repeated system card deficiencies may indicate weaknesses in the governance operating model rather than isolated mistakes by individual owners.

Root-cause analysis should determine why the deficiency occurred and what changes would reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

Potential root causes include:

- Unclear ownership or accountability.
- Inadequate documentation standards.
- Poor integration between governance systems.
- Insufficient staff competence.
- Incomplete AI inventory coverage.
- Weak change-management triggers.
- Unclear approval criteria.
- Excessive reliance on manual processes.
- Inadequate evidence repositories.
- Lack of management oversight.
- Unclear interpretation of regulatory requirements.
- Provider limitations that were not addressed during procurement.

For example, if several system cards omit provider data-retention information, the organization should investigate whether the issue originates in the template, vendor due diligence, contract review, procurement workflow, or assignment of responsibility.

Updating every card may temporarily improve completeness without correcting the process that caused the omission.

Root-cause analysis should therefore lead to corrective actions at the appropriate level: individual system, business unit, governance process, or enterprise policy.

The organization should evaluate whether the corrective action addresses the identified cause and whether subsequent reviews demonstrate a reduction in recurring findings.

### 60. Integrating System Card Assurance into Management Review

Management review should provide oversight of the effectiveness of the system card program and determine whether the organization has sufficient information to govern its AI portfolio.

The review should consider both aggregate indicators and significant individual cases.

Potential inputs include:

- System card coverage and completeness.
- Overdue reviews and material updates.
- Significant quality assurance findings.
- High-risk systems with documentation gaps.
- Unresolved exceptions.
- Recurring deficiencies and root causes.
- Changes in the AI portfolio.
- Material provider or model changes.
- Significant incidents affecting system documentation.
- Corrective-action status.
- Resource and competence requirements.
- Changes in applicable legal or regulatory obligations.

Management should assess whether the documentation program supports effective risk decisions and whether deficiencies indicate a need to change governance arrangements.

For example, a high percentage of completed system cards may appear positive. However, if quality assurance reveals that many cards contain unsupported claims or inaccurate architecture descriptions, management should not conclude that the program is effective based on completion metrics alone.

Management review should produce documented decisions, assigned actions, and appropriate follow-up.

Possible decisions include increasing review capacity, improving tooling, changing mandatory documentation requirements, clarifying ownership, strengthening approval gates, or requiring remediation of material portfolio-wide weaknesses.

The purpose of management review is not simply to receive a dashboard. It is to determine whether the organization can rely on its system documentation when making decisions about AI deployment, risk acceptance, and continued operation.

### 61. Maintaining System Cards in Dynamic AI Environments

AI systems can change more frequently than conventional applications because of model-provider updates, changing data, prompt modifications, evolving retrieval sources, and adjustments to agent behavior or permissions.

A system card governance process must therefore be designed for dynamic environments.

The organization should identify which changes can be detected automatically and which require human assessment.

Potential monitoring mechanisms include:

- Model-version and endpoint monitoring.
- Provider change notifications.
- Configuration and deployment records.
- Data-source change logs.
- Access-control change records.
- Agent-tool permission changes.
- Evaluation and regression-test results.
- Performance and error monitoring.
- Incident-management records.
- Change-management workflow integration.

Where feasible, the organization should link these mechanisms to the system card maintenance process.

For example, a deployment pipeline may identify a model-version change and initiate a review task. The system owner then assesses whether the change affects documented capabilities, evaluation results, risk exposure, or approval conditions.

Automation can help detect changes and initiate workflows, but it should not automatically determine that every change is immaterial. The significance of a model or configuration change depends on the system's purpose, architecture, and risk.

The organization should define how urgent changes are handled when a provider update or security event requires immediate action. In such cases, operational restrictions or suspension may be necessary before the full documentation review is complete.

The system card should be updated through controlled versioning so that the current record reflects the authorized state while historical versions remain available for accountability and investigation.

### 62. Using System Cards to Improve AI Governance Maturity

A mature system card program evolves from basic documentation collection toward a reliable source of structured governance information.

Organizations can assess maturity across several dimensions.

| Maturity dimension    | Initial state                          | Developing state                         | Established state                                            | Advanced state                                                                |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Coverage              | System cards created inconsistently    | Most known systems are documented        | In-scope systems are covered through a defined process       | Coverage is continuously reconciled against multiple discovery sources        |
| Standardization       | Ad hoc templates and terminology       | Common template introduced               | Controlled templates and quality criteria are enforced       | Standards adapt through governed feedback and lessons learned                 |
| Ownership             | Responsibility is unclear              | Owners are assigned to many systems      | Ownership and approval responsibilities are formally defined | Ownership changes and accountability gaps are systematically monitored        |
| Evidence              | Claims are largely self-declared       | Some evidence references exist           | Material claims are traceable to controlled records          | Evidence relationships support efficient assurance and change-impact analysis |
| Lifecycle maintenance | Cards are mainly created at deployment | Periodic reviews are introduced          | Material changes trigger governed updates                    | Change detection and reassessment workflows are integrated with operations    |
| Assurance             | Completeness is checked informally     | Checklists and sampling are used         | Risk-based quality assurance is established                  | Findings and metrics drive continuous improvement                             |
| Reporting             | Basic counts are reported              | Timeliness and completeness are measured | Metrics inform management decisions                          | Portfolio insights support proactive governance prioritization                |

This maturity model is illustrative and should not be interpreted as a mandatory industry scale.

An organization should assess maturity based on demonstrated capabilities rather than the existence of policies or tools alone.

For example, a documented change-management procedure does not establish advanced maturity if material model changes routinely occur without reassessment.

Likewise, automation does not necessarily indicate maturity if the underlying data is incomplete or the automated checks do not evaluate the quality of the information.

Maturity assessment should identify the current state, target state, material gaps, and prioritized improvements. The target should be appropriate to the organization's AI portfolio, risk exposure, regulatory environment, and available resources.

### 63. Evaluating System Card Program Effectiveness

Program effectiveness concerns whether system cards actually support better governance outcomes, rather than merely whether the organization produces and maintains documents.

An effective program should enable authorized stakeholders to understand systems, identify material risks, locate supporting evidence, evaluate approval conditions, and determine whether the documented state matches actual operation.

Evaluation should consider:

- Whether the organization can identify all systems within scope.
- Whether system cards accurately reflect deployed systems.
- Whether material risks and limitations are documented.
- Whether approval decisions are traceable.
- Whether material changes trigger appropriate reassessment.
- Whether evidence references remain valid and accessible.
- Whether recurring deficiencies are reduced.
- Whether auditors can use the cards to scope and perform assurance activities.
- Whether management receives reliable information about documentation weaknesses.
- Whether system owners can maintain cards without disproportionate administrative effort.

The organization should also evaluate unintended effects.

For example, an overly complex template may encourage superficial completion, while an excessively simplified template may omit information needed for high-risk systems. A system card program may also create duplicated records if it is poorly integrated with existing governance platforms.

Effectiveness reviews should therefore examine both governance value and operational efficiency.

The organization may use stakeholder feedback, quality assurance results, audit findings, incident lessons, and trend analysis to identify improvements.

Changes to the system card standard should be governed so that revisions are documented, approved, communicated, and applied consistently.

### 64. Demonstrating Audit Readiness Through System Cards

Audit readiness means that the organization can produce a coherent and supportable account of how an AI system is governed, approved, controlled, and monitored.

System cards contribute to audit readiness by consolidating key information and linking it to authoritative records.

A reviewer should be able to use a system card to identify:

- The system's intended purpose and approved scope.
- The relevant model and deployment context.
- Material data sources and dependencies.
- Known limitations and risk exposures.
- Applicable controls and supporting evidence.
- Evaluation results and acceptance criteria.
- Human oversight and operational restrictions.
- Accountable owners and approval authorities.
- Change history and periodic review records.
- Outstanding findings and corrective actions.

The card should also help the reviewer determine where independent verification is necessary.

For example, if a system card states that access controls restrict users to authorized documents, the auditor should be able to locate the relevant control design and testing evidence. The auditor may then test whether the control operates effectively in the deployed environment.

Audit readiness does not require every piece of evidence to be embedded in the card. It requires evidence to be identifiable, accessible to authorized reviewers, and sufficiently organized to support examination.

The organization should periodically test its ability to retrieve system cards and supporting evidence within the required timeframes. Broken links, inaccessible repositories, inconsistent identifiers, and missing historical versions can undermine assurance even when the underlying information exists.

A reliable system card program therefore supports not only documentation quality but also the practical ability to demonstrate governance.

### 65. System Cards as a Foundation for Enterprise AI Assurance

System cards provide a structured starting point for enterprise AI assurance by connecting individual systems to the organization's wider governance framework.

At the system level, the card describes the intended use, operating context, limitations, risks, safeguards, and accountability arrangements.

At the portfolio level, standardized system cards allow the organization to identify patterns across systems, including shared providers, common dependencies, recurring control weaknesses, and concentrations of risk.

At the enterprise level, this information can support decisions about:

- AI governance priorities.
- Control-library improvements.
- Resource allocation.
- Third-party risk concentration.
- Risk acceptance and escalation.
- Audit planning.
- Compliance evidence.
- Management reporting.
- Governance maturity.
- System retirement or replacement.

However, enterprise conclusions should not be drawn from system card data without evaluating its quality and coverage.

If the inventory excludes unregistered AI systems, a complete set of cards for the known systems does not establish complete enterprise coverage. If cards rely on unverified provider statements, aggregate reporting may overstate assurance.

The organization should therefore assess the reliability of the underlying records before using them to support enterprise-wide conclusions.

System cards should be treated as one component of a broader assurance architecture that includes risk registers, control evidence, operational monitoring, independent testing, audit workpapers, and management review.

Their strategic value comes from connecting these sources into a coherent view of the organization's AI systems and the governance decisions associated with them.

### 66. Part 4 Core Principle

A sustainable system card program requires more than a standardized template. It needs clear ownership, defined approval gates, measurable quality criteria, risk-based assurance, consistent finding classification, effective corrective actions, and continuous lifecycle maintenance.

The organization should evaluate the program by whether it produces accurate, current, traceable, and decision-useful information—not simply by the number of completed cards.

When system cards are integrated with inventories, risk registers, control libraries, change management, incident response, audit, and management review, they become a practical foundation for demonstrating how AI systems are governed throughout their lifecycle.
