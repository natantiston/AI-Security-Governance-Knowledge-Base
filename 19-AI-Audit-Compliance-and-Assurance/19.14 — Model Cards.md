# 19.14 — Model Cards

## Part 1 — Model Card Foundations, Purpose, Structure, and Governance Requirements

### 1. Understanding Model Cards

A model card is a structured document that communicates essential information about an AI model, including its intended purpose, capabilities, evaluation results, limitations, appropriate uses, and relevant risks.

Its primary purpose is to help stakeholders understand what a model is designed to do, how it has been evaluated, the conditions under which its results may be reliable, and the circumstances in which additional controls or human judgment are required.

Model cards provide a standardized way to communicate information that might otherwise be scattered across technical reports, development records, evaluation results, and internal documentation.

A model card may describe:

- The model's intended purpose.
- Its intended and excluded use cases.
- The model architecture or general technical approach.
- Training-data characteristics, where available.
- Evaluation methodology and results.
- Performance across relevant populations or scenarios.
- Known limitations and failure conditions.
- Fairness, safety, privacy, and security considerations.
- Appropriate deployment conditions.
- Required human oversight.
- Relevant model version and provenance information.

The exact content depends on the model, its intended use, the available evidence, and the organization's documentation requirements.

**A model card is not merely a model description. It is a structured communication mechanism for model capabilities, evidence, limitations, and responsible use.**

### 2. Why Model Cards Matter in AI Security Governance

From an AI Security Governance perspective, a model card helps bridge the gap between technical model development and organizational decision-making.

AI engineers may understand a model's architecture and evaluation results, but business owners, risk managers, security teams, compliance officers, and procurement teams may need that information presented in a more consistent and decision-oriented format.

Without a structured model card, stakeholders may misunderstand the model's capabilities or assume that successful testing establishes broader reliability than the evidence supports.

For example, a model may achieve strong accuracy on a particular evaluation dataset but perform poorly on unfamiliar inputs, underrepresented populations, or operating conditions that were not included in testing.

A model card should make the scope of the evidence visible so that decision-makers can distinguish demonstrated capabilities from untested assumptions.

Model cards support several governance objectives:

- **Transparency:** Communicating relevant information about model behavior and intended use.
- **Accountability:** Identifying the responsible model owner and relevant approval authorities.
- **Risk management:** Making known limitations and relevant risks visible.
- **Security governance:** Communicating security-relevant constraints and evaluation findings.
- **Compliance support:** Helping demonstrate that relevant model information has been considered and communicated.
- **Human oversight:** Explaining where human judgment or intervention may be necessary.
- **Lifecycle governance:** Maintaining model-specific information as the model evolves.

A model card does not independently establish that a model is secure, fair, safe, or legally compliant. It supports these objectives by communicating information that must be evaluated alongside other evidence and controls.

### 3. Model Cards Versus General Model Documentation

Model cards and model documentation are closely related, but they serve different purposes.

**Model documentation** is the broader collection of records describing a model's development, evaluation, deployment, operation, governance, and lifecycle history.

**A model card** is a structured summary of selected information from that broader documentation, organized to help defined audiences understand the model and its appropriate use.

The distinction can be represented as follows:

```
MODEL DOCUMENTATION
│
├── Technical specifications
├── Development records
├── Data documentation
├── Evaluation reports
├── Risk assessments
├── Security assessments
├── Validation evidence
├── Deployment records
├── Change history
├── Monitoring records
└── MODEL CARD
      ├── Intended use
      ├── Model characteristics
      ├── Evaluation summary
      ├── Limitations
      ├── Relevant risks
      └── Usage guidance
```

The model card may reference more detailed technical reports rather than reproducing all their contents.

For example, a model card may summarize evaluation performance and identify the evaluation report that contains the full methodology, test results, and limitations.

This approach improves usability while preserving traceability to the underlying evidence.

A model card should not replace detailed technical documentation, risk assessments, validation reports, or mandatory regulatory documentation where those artifacts are separately required.

### 4. Model Cards Versus System Cards

A model card focuses primarily on an individual model. A system card generally describes a broader AI system, including how one or more models are integrated into an application, service, or operational environment.

The distinction is important because an AI system's risk profile is not determined by its model alone.

Consider an enterprise AI assistant that uses a foundation model, a retrieval system, internal documents, access controls, and external APIs.

A model card might describe the foundation model's capabilities, evaluation characteristics, and limitations.

A system card might additionally describe:

- The overall system architecture.
- The application and user interface.
- Retrieval and grounding mechanisms.
- External data sources.
- Authentication and authorization.
- Tool access and system integrations.
- Human-oversight arrangements.
- Operational safeguards.
- System-level evaluation and known failure modes.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
AI SYSTEM
│
├── Application
├── User Interface
├── Identity and Access Controls
├── Data Sources
├── Retrieval Components
├── External APIs and Tools
├── Operational Controls
│
└── AI MODEL
      └── Model Card
```

A model card can provide important evidence for a system card, but it cannot establish the effectiveness of controls implemented outside the model.

For example, a model card describing a model's resistance to certain adversarial inputs does not demonstrate that an enterprise application has correctly enforced access permissions on retrieved documents.

**Model-level transparency and system-level transparency are complementary governance activities.**

### 5. Model Cards as a Governance Artifact

A model card should be treated as a governed artifact rather than an informal technical document.

Its creation, review, approval, publication, and maintenance should follow defined organizational procedures.

The governance process should establish:

- Who is responsible for preparing the model card.
- Who validates technical claims.
- Who reviews relevant risks and limitations.
- Who approves its use or publication.
- Which version of the model the card describes.
- Where the approved card is maintained.
- Which stakeholders may access it.
- When the card must be updated.
- How obsolete versions are retained or withdrawn.
- How deficiencies and exceptions are managed.

These requirements help prevent a model card from becoming disconnected from the model it describes.

For example, an organization may retain a model card describing version 2.1 while production has already moved to version 3.0. Even if the original card was accurate when created, it may no longer provide an adequate basis for current governance decisions.

The organization should therefore maintain explicit links between model cards, model identifiers, version records, evaluation evidence, and approval decisions.

### 6. Establishing the Intended Purpose of a Model

The intended purpose is one of the most important elements of a model card.

It explains what the model is designed or approved to do and provides the context for interpreting its evaluation results, limitations, and risks.

An intended-purpose statement should be sufficiently specific to distinguish approved use from unsupported or prohibited use.

For example, the following statement is too broad:

> The model supports customer service.

A more useful statement might be:

> The model assists authorized customer-service employees by generating draft responses to routine product and account questions using approved reference material. Employees must verify consequential statements before communicating them to customers. The model is not authorized to make independent credit, eligibility, or account-termination decisions.

The more specific statement identifies the model's operational role, intended users, information context, human involvement, and boundaries.

An intended-purpose section should consider:

- The business problem being addressed.
- The intended user population.
- The decisions or activities supported.
- The expected operating environment.
- The information and inputs required.
- The outputs the model produces.
- The degree of automation.
- The expected human involvement.
- The consequences of incorrect outputs.
- The uses that fall outside the approved scope.

The intended purpose should reflect the actual approved deployment, not merely the developer's original research objective.

### 7. Documenting Intended and Excluded Uses

A model may be technically capable of performing activities that the organization has not approved.

Model cards should distinguish between what a model can technically do and what it is intended or authorized to do.

For example, a general-purpose language model may generate legal explanations, medical summaries, financial suggestions, or employment-related recommendations. That capability does not establish that the model has been validated or approved for every such use.

Documentation should therefore identify relevant use boundaries.

| Use Category     | Meaning                                                                      |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Intended use     | The activity for which the model has been designed or approved               |
| Conditional use  | A use permitted only when specified requirements or safeguards are satisfied |
| Unsupported use  | A use for which adequate evidence or validation is unavailable               |
| Prohibited use   | A use that the organization or applicable requirements do not permit         |
| Out-of-scope use | An activity outside the model's defined purpose or governance authorization  |

These categories should be used consistently with the organization's policies and applicable obligations.

The model card should also explain material conditions attached to permitted uses.

For example, a model may be approved to summarize internal documents but not to make autonomous decisions that affect employees. It may be permitted to draft recommendations while requiring human approval before any consequential action is taken.

Where the distinction is material, the model card should identify the controls needed to enforce the boundary.

### 8. Identifying the Model and Its Version

A model card must identify the model it describes.

Without reliable identification, stakeholders may be unable to determine whether the documented capabilities, limitations, and evaluation results apply to the model currently in use.

Core identification fields may include:

- Model name.
- Unique model identifier.
- Model version or release.
- Model provider or development organization.
- Model owner.
- Associated AI system.
- Model type or family.
- Deployment status.
- Documentation version.
- Publication or approval date.
- Last review date.

For internally developed models, identification may include a model registry identifier, artifact reference, or other controlled identifier.

For externally hosted models, identification may include the provider's model identifier, API version, service configuration, and relevant release information.

Some hosted services use dynamic model aliases or provider-managed updates. Where an immutable version cannot be established, the model card should explain the limitation and identify the available mechanisms for tracking changes.

A model name alone is not always sufficient to establish identity.

The organization should be able to determine whether an evaluation result applies to the same model version and configuration as the model used in production.

### 9. Documenting the Model Type and Technical Characteristics

A model card should provide enough technical context for its intended audience to understand the model's general characteristics.

The level of detail should be proportionate to the model's complexity, intended use, and risk.

Relevant information may include:

- Model family or type.
- Learning or inference approach.
- General architecture.
- Input and output formats.
- Supported modalities.
- Important configuration parameters.
- Context or sequence limitations.
- Required dependencies.
- Execution environment.
- Whether the model is internally developed or externally provided.
- Whether it is accessed through an API or deployed within the organization's environment.

For a classification model, the card might explain the classes it predicts and how its scores or thresholds are interpreted.

For a generative model, it might describe the types of content it generates, the supported modalities, and relevant constraints on its context window or output format.

For a model embedded in an agentic system, the card should not imply that the model itself controls all system actions if those actions are actually governed by separate application logic, permissions, or orchestration components.

Technical descriptions should be accurate but audience-appropriate.

A business decision-maker may need to understand the implications of a model's design without requiring every implementation detail. A technical reviewer may need additional information or references to detailed architecture documentation.

### 10. Documenting Training Data and Data Provenance

Training data can materially influence model capabilities, limitations, security exposure, and suitability for a particular use.

Where information is available and appropriate to disclose, model cards should describe relevant training-data characteristics.

These may include:

- Data sources or source categories.
- Data collection period.
- Geographic or linguistic coverage.
- Relevant population characteristics.
- Data preparation and transformation.
- Labeling or annotation processes.
- Known data-quality limitations.
- Known representation gaps.
- Relevant privacy or licensing considerations.
- Data exclusions and their rationale.

The description should be sufficiently informative to support an understanding of the model's evidence base without disclosing confidential or protected information unnecessarily.

For a third-party foundation model, the organization may not have access to the provider's complete training dataset or detailed provenance records.

In such cases, the model card should distinguish between information supplied by the provider, information independently verified by the organization, and information that remains unavailable.

It should not imply that the organization has independently verified training-data properties when it has not.

Where training-data uncertainty creates material risk, the organization should assess whether additional testing, contractual assurances, usage restrictions, or other controls are necessary.

### 11. Documenting Evaluation Methodology

Evaluation results are meaningful only when their methodology and scope can be understood.

A model card should summarize how the model was evaluated and identify the important conditions under which the results were obtained.

Relevant information may include:

- Evaluation objectives.
- Test datasets or evaluation populations.
- Dataset provenance, where available.
- Test scenarios.
- Evaluation metrics.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Relevant model and configuration versions.
- Test environment.
- Baseline comparisons.
- Evaluation dates.
- Important exclusions.
- Known methodological limitations.

For example, a statement that a model achieved 94% accuracy is incomplete without information about what was predicted, which dataset was used, how the test population was selected, and whether the metric is appropriate for the intended use.

Evaluation methodology should also be appropriate to the model's function.

A fraud-detection model may require analysis of false positives, false negatives, and performance under changing fraud patterns. A generative model may require evaluation of factuality, relevance, harmful outputs, robustness, or task-specific quality.

The model card does not necessarily need to reproduce the entire evaluation report. It should summarize the evidence clearly and provide traceable references to the detailed results.

### 12. Presenting Evaluation Results Responsibly

Model cards should communicate evaluation results without overstating what they demonstrate.

A result achieved under a controlled test does not automatically establish performance in every production environment.

For example, a model may perform well on a test dataset but experience reduced performance when user behavior changes, data distributions shift, or the model is integrated into a different application.

Evaluation results should therefore be presented with relevant context.

Where appropriate, the model card should identify:

- The metric and its definition.
- The measured result.
- The applicable acceptance threshold.
- The test population or scenario.
- Relevant subgroup results.
- The conditions under which testing occurred.
- Important uncertainty or variability.
- Known limitations in the evaluation.
- The relationship between test performance and the intended use.

Results should not be selectively presented to create an unjustifiably favorable impression.

If an evaluation identifies significant weaknesses, the model card should communicate those weaknesses and any relevant restrictions or mitigations.

Where evaluation results are unavailable, inconclusive, or not applicable, the documentation should state this clearly rather than substituting unsupported assurances.

### 13. Documenting Model Limitations

Model limitations describe conditions under which a model may produce unreliable, incomplete, inappropriate, or otherwise unsuitable results.

A useful model card makes these limitations understandable to the people who must decide whether and how to use the model.

Limitations may involve:

- Incomplete or unrepresentative training data.
- Poor performance on particular tasks or populations.
- Sensitivity to input quality.
- Difficulty handling unusual or unfamiliar scenarios.
- Limited factual reliability.
- Inability to provide dependable explanations.
- Weaknesses in detecting ambiguity.
- Susceptibility to adversarial manipulation.
- Dependence on external services or data.
- Performance degradation under distribution shift.
- Lack of evaluation in certain operating conditions.

Limitations should be specific enough to guide decisions.

For example, stating that a model “may make mistakes” offers little practical guidance.

A more useful statement might explain that the model has not been evaluated for a particular language, performs less reliably on a defined class of inputs, or requires human verification before its outputs are used in a consequential decision.

Where known, the card should identify the evidence supporting the limitation and the measures intended to reduce its impact.

**A model card should communicate not only what the model can do, but also where confidence in its outputs should be limited.**

### 14. Distinguishing Model Limitations From System Limitations

Not every limitation observed in an AI application originates in the underlying model.

Some limitations arise from the model itself, while others arise from data pipelines, retrieval mechanisms, application logic, access controls, external services, or operational procedures.

Consider an AI assistant that generates incorrect answers because the retrieval system returns outdated documents.

The issue may not be caused solely by the language model. It may arise from stale knowledge sources, indexing delays, inadequate source selection, or weaknesses in the retrieval configuration.

A model card should describe limitations relevant to the model and its evaluated behavior. Where the card is used to communicate information about a particular deployment, it should clearly identify material dependencies and point to system-level documentation for limitations outside the model's direct scope.

A useful distinction is:

| Limitation Type           | Example                                                                            |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model limitation          | The model generates unsupported statements under certain conditions                |
| Data limitation           | The training or reference data does not adequately represent the target population |
| Integration limitation    | The application supplies incomplete or incorrectly formatted inputs                |
| Retrieval limitation      | The system retrieves outdated or irrelevant documents                              |
| Access-control limitation | The application fails to restrict information retrieved for a user                 |
| Operational limitation    | Required human review is not consistently performed                                |
| Provider limitation       | The organization cannot independently verify certain model properties              |

This distinction improves accountability by helping teams identify the correct control owner and remediation path.

### 15. Documenting Fairness and Subgroup Performance

Where a model's use can affect individuals or groups, evaluation may need to examine whether performance differs across relevant populations.

The model card should summarize the relevant evaluation approach and results where such analysis is applicable and supported by evidence.

Potential considerations include:

- The populations represented in evaluation.
- The rationale for selecting relevant subgroups.
- Differences in error rates or other performance measures.
- Known representation gaps.
- Limitations in subgroup sample sizes.
- The suitability of selected fairness metrics.
- Potential consequences of observed differences.
- Relevant mitigations and monitoring requirements.

Fairness cannot be established through a single universal metric for every model and context.

The appropriate assessment depends on the intended purpose, the affected stakeholders, the type of decision, the available data, and applicable legal or organizational requirements.

The model card should avoid making broad claims such as “the model is unbiased” without clearly defined criteria and supporting evidence.

Where subgroup analysis was not performed, the card should not imply that subgroup performance has been established. If the absence of analysis creates material uncertainty, that limitation should be considered in the associated risk assessment and deployment decision.

### 16. Documenting Security Considerations

Model cards can communicate security-relevant characteristics and limitations that affect the model's appropriate use.

The scope should reflect the model type, threat environment, and deployment context.

Potential considerations include:

- Known security weaknesses.
- Susceptibility to adversarial inputs.
- Relevant prompt-injection or jailbreak evaluation findings.
- Potential for sensitive information disclosure.
- Limitations in detecting malicious or manipulative content.
- Model access and usage restrictions.
- Dependencies on external providers.
- Relevant security testing boundaries.
- Required application-level safeguards.

Security information must be communicated carefully.

A model card intended for broad public distribution may not be an appropriate location for detailed exploit instructions, sensitive vulnerability information, or confidential security configurations.

The organization can provide a suitable summary in the model card and maintain more detailed technical evidence in access-controlled security documentation.

The card should also distinguish between model-level security characteristics and system-level controls.

For example, a model's tested resistance to prompt manipulation does not replace authorization checks, tool restrictions, network controls, monitoring, or secure application design.

Security claims should be proportionate to the testing performed and the evidence available.

### 17. Documenting Privacy and Data Protection Considerations

Privacy-related information may be important when a model processes personal data, was trained using datasets containing personal information, or is deployed in a context where its outputs can affect individuals.

Where relevant, the model card may summarize:

- Known privacy-related limitations.
- Relevant training-data considerations.
- Potential memorization or disclosure concerns.
- Evaluation for sensitive-information leakage.
- Restrictions on inputting personal or confidential information.
- Relevant assumptions about data handling.
- Conditions requiring additional privacy controls.
- References to privacy assessments and related documentation.

The model card should not be treated as a substitute for a data protection impact assessment, records of processing activities, data-processing agreements, or other required privacy documentation.

Those records address broader legal, organizational, and processing-specific questions.

The model card should provide information useful for understanding the model's characteristics while referring to the appropriate governance artifacts for the complete privacy assessment.

For third-party models, the organization should distinguish between verified technical behavior and provider statements about data handling or retention.

A provider's privacy commitment does not, by itself, establish that every implementation and configuration satisfies the organization's privacy obligations.

### 18. Documenting Safety and Potential Harm

Where model outputs can contribute to physical, psychological, financial, social, or other consequential harm, the model card should communicate relevant safety considerations.

Potential areas include:

- Known unsafe output patterns.
- Conditions associated with elevated error risk.
- Limitations in recognizing dangerous scenarios.
- Evaluation of harmful or inappropriate outputs.
- Potential impacts on affected stakeholders.
- Required human intervention.
- Use restrictions.
- Known residual safety concerns.

Safety considerations vary significantly by application.

A model used to classify routine internal documents may require a different safety analysis from a model supporting medical workflows, industrial operations, or other consequential activities.

The model card should not present a general safety statement as proof that every possible harmful outcome has been prevented.

Instead, it should communicate the scope of the assessment, the relevant findings, the known limitations, and the safeguards required for the intended use.

Where safety assurance depends on system-level controls, the card should identify those dependencies and refer to the relevant system documentation.

### 19. Documenting Human Oversight Requirements

Some AI applications require human review, approval, intervention, or the ability to override model outputs.

A model card should communicate these requirements when they materially affect appropriate use.

Relevant information may include:

- Which outputs require human review.
- Which decisions must remain with authorized personnel.
- Circumstances requiring escalation.
- Conditions under which the model should not be used.
- Expected reviewer competence.
- Known limitations that reviewers must understand.
- Procedures for rejecting or correcting model outputs.
- Circumstances requiring suspension or intervention.

The phrase “human in the loop” is insufficient when it does not specify what the human is expected to do.

For example, an organization may require employees to review AI-generated customer responses before sending them. The model card should explain the review requirement and relevant limitations, while the operational procedure should establish how review is implemented and evidenced.

The existence of a human approval step does not automatically establish effective oversight. Reviewers must have adequate information, authority, time, and competence to identify and address problematic outputs.

### 20. Documenting Performance Across Operating Conditions

Model performance can vary depending on the inputs, users, environment, and operating conditions.

A model card should describe the conditions under which the model has been evaluated and identify material gaps in coverage.

Relevant conditions may include:

- Input quality.
- Language or geographic context.
- User population.
- Data distribution.
- Workload or operating environment.
- Input complexity.
- Adversarial or unusual scenarios.
- Changes in source data.
- Differences between test and production environments.

For example, a model evaluated using structured, high-quality data may perform less reliably when deployed against incomplete records or noisy inputs.

Similarly, a language model evaluated primarily in English may not have equivalent evidence supporting use in every other language.

The card should avoid implying universal performance from limited test coverage.

Where operating conditions can change over time, the model card should identify relevant assumptions and point to the monitoring requirements used to detect material deterioration.

### 21. Documenting Model Uncertainty

Model uncertainty concerns the limits of confidence that can reasonably be placed in a model's outputs or evaluation results.

The way uncertainty should be represented depends on the model and task.

For some models, uncertainty may be expressed through calibrated probabilities or prediction intervals. For generative AI, apparent confidence in natural-language output may not reliably correspond to factual correctness.

The model card should explain the relevant uncertainty measures and their limitations.

Potential information includes:

- Whether uncertainty is quantified.
- Which method is used.
- Whether the method has been evaluated for the intended context.
- Conditions under which uncertainty estimates may be unreliable.
- How uncertainty affects decision-making.
- When additional verification is required.

A model's confidence score should not automatically be interpreted as the probability that its output is correct unless the measure has been appropriately defined and validated for that interpretation.

For generative models, the card should not imply that fluent or confident language establishes factual accuracy.

Where uncertainty cannot be reliably quantified, the documentation should communicate that limitation and explain the implications for appropriate use.

### 22. Defining the Intended Audience of a Model Card

A model card should be designed for identifiable audiences.

Different stakeholders need different levels of detail, and one document may need to serve several audiences with different information requirements.

Potential audiences include:

- Model developers and engineers.
- Model validators.
- Business owners.
- AI governance teams.
- Cybersecurity professionals.
- Privacy and legal specialists.
- Risk and compliance teams.
- Procurement and third-party risk teams.
- Internal auditors.
- Customers or external users.
- Regulators or other authorized reviewers.

A technical team may need detailed evaluation methods and model configuration references. A business owner may need clear information about intended use, limitations, and approval conditions. An external user may need concise guidance about capabilities and appropriate reliance.

Organizations should determine whether a single model card can meet these needs or whether separate audience-specific versions are necessary.

Where multiple versions are maintained, they should remain consistent with the same authoritative model record. Simplification for a nontechnical audience should not remove a material limitation or change the meaning of a technical finding.

### 23. Establishing Model Card Ownership and Approval

The model card should have an identifiable owner who is responsible for maintaining its accuracy and coordinating required updates.

Ownership does not mean that one individual must personally produce every section.

Technical claims may require input from model developers or validators, while business-purpose statements may require confirmation from the business owner. Security, privacy, and compliance sections may require review by relevant specialists.

A responsibility model may include:

| Activity                                   | Typical Responsibility                      |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Drafting technical content                 | Model development or engineering team       |
| Confirming intended use                    | Business or product owner                   |
| Validating evaluation claims               | Qualified validation or evaluation function |
| Reviewing security information             | Cybersecurity or AI security function       |
| Reviewing privacy implications             | Privacy specialists, where applicable       |
| Reviewing risk and governance implications | AI governance, risk, or compliance function |
| Approving intended deployment              | Authorized business or governance authority |
| Maintaining the approved card              | Designated model documentation owner        |

The exact allocation depends on the organization's operating model and risk classification.

Approval requirements should be proportionate to the model's intended use and potential impact.

A low-risk internal utility may require a streamlined review. A model supporting consequential decisions may require formal validation, specialist review, and explicit approval before deployment.

The model card should make the approval status clear and should not imply that a draft or unreviewed document represents an authorized governance decision.

### 24. Establishing Model Card Review and Update Triggers

Model cards should be maintained throughout the model lifecycle.

A card that was accurate at the time of initial approval may become misleading after changes to the model, data, configuration, provider, intended use, or deployment environment.

The organization should define both periodic review requirements and event-driven update triggers.

Potential triggers include:

- A new model version.
- A material configuration change.
- A change in intended use.
- A change in the target population.
- New evaluation results.
- Discovery of a material limitation.
- A security or privacy incident.
- Significant performance degradation.
- A material change in external dependencies.
- A change in applicable requirements.
- A change to human-oversight arrangements.
- A decision to restrict, suspend, or retire the model.

The update process should determine which parts of the model card are affected and whether the change requires additional evaluation or approval.

For example, changing a model's intended use from internal document summarization to automated customer eligibility assessment is not merely a documentation edit. It may introduce different risks, evaluation requirements, stakeholder impacts, and regulatory considerations.

The model card should reflect the new approved context only after the required governance activities have been completed.

### 25. Model Cards and Regulatory Compliance

Model cards can support regulatory compliance by helping organizations communicate model characteristics, intended use, evaluation evidence, limitations, and relevant governance decisions.

Their value depends on the applicable requirements and the actual information they contain.

A model card may support:

- Internal AI governance reviews.
- Risk and impact assessments.
- Technical transparency activities.
- Documentation required by internal policies.
- Supplier and customer assurance.
- Internal audit evidence collection.
- Regulatory review preparation.
- Communication of model limitations and operating conditions.

However, a model card should not automatically be treated as satisfying every technical documentation, transparency, conformity assessment, or recordkeeping obligation.

Applicable obligations depend on the AI system, its intended purpose, the organization's role, the relevant jurisdiction, and the legal requirements in force.

Where a regulatory framework requires specific information, the organization should map the required information to the model card and other authoritative records.

A gap in the model card may be addressed by a linked artifact if the relevant requirements permit that approach and the evidence remains accessible and sufficiently clear.

The organization should avoid assuming that a document named “Model Card” is compliant simply because it contains a standard set of headings.

### 26. Distinguishing Public Model Cards From Internal Model Cards

Model cards can be intended for public release, internal governance, controlled external disclosure, or a combination of these purposes.

The audience affects the level of technical detail, confidentiality controls, and presentation.

**Public model cards** may provide information about intended use, evaluation results, limitations, and responsible-use guidance without disclosing confidential information.

**Internal model cards** may include additional operational context, risk references, approval details, and information needed for internal decision-making.

**Restricted model cards** may be used when particular details require controlled access because of security, commercial, contractual, or privacy considerations.

These categories are organizational design choices rather than universal legal classifications.

If multiple versions exist, the organization should maintain a clear relationship between them and the authoritative source record.

The public version should not materially misrepresent the model by omitting limitations that are essential to understanding its appropriate use.

Likewise, internal documentation should not assume that every technical detail can be shared externally without reviewing applicable confidentiality, security, intellectual-property, and privacy constraints.

### 27. Model Cards for Third-Party and Foundation Models

Third-party and foundation models create additional challenges because the organization using the model may not control its development or have access to all relevant technical information.

The organization should distinguish between:

- Information published by the provider.
- Information supplied contractually.
- Information independently tested by the organization.
- Information inferred from observed behavior.
- Information that remains unavailable.

A third-party model card should identify the provider and the model or service version as precisely as possible.

It should also communicate the limitations of the available evidence.

For example, a provider may publish performance benchmarks for a foundation model but not disclose complete training-data details. The organization should not treat those benchmarks as independent proof of suitability for its own use case.

Where appropriate, the organization should supplement provider information with application-specific evaluation, security testing, risk assessment, and monitoring.

If the provider updates the model or changes the service, the organization should assess whether the existing model card remains accurate.

Where the provider does not provide sufficient information, the resulting uncertainty should be recorded and considered in the organization's deployment decision.

### 28. Model Cards for Generative AI and Large Language Models

Generative AI and large language models introduce characteristics that should be reflected in their model cards.

Depending on the model and intended use, relevant information may include:

- Supported input and output modalities.
- Supported languages.
- Intended tasks.
- Known hallucination or factuality limitations.
- Sensitivity to prompt formulation.
- Context-window constraints.
- Relevant safety evaluation results.
- Known limitations in refusal behavior.
- Susceptibility to prompt manipulation.
- Potential sensitive-information disclosure risks.
- Limitations of available explainability methods.
- Requirements for grounding or human verification.

The card should distinguish between model-level capabilities and safeguards provided by the surrounding application.

For example, an application may use retrieval-augmented generation to ground answers in approved documents. The presence of retrieval does not establish that every answer is supported by those documents, nor does it guarantee that retrieved content is current or authorized for every user.

Similarly, a system prompt may guide model behavior but should not be represented as an infallible security boundary.

The model card should describe relevant limitations and point to system-level controls where those controls are essential to the intended use.

### 29. Model Cards for AI Agents and Tool-Connected Models

An AI agent may use a model to interpret instructions, plan tasks, call tools, retrieve information, and initiate actions.

In such systems, the model card should explain the model's role in the broader agent architecture without confusing model capabilities with system-level authority.

Relevant model-level information may include:

- The model's intended reasoning or generation tasks.
- Known limitations in instruction following.
- Relevant tool-use evaluation results.
- Failure patterns observed in multi-step tasks.
- Limitations in planning or interpreting context.
- Security-relevant evaluation findings.
- Conditions requiring human review.

The associated system documentation should describe the agent's actual tools, permissions, execution boundaries, approval requirements, and intervention mechanisms.

For example, a model may be capable of generating a command, but the surrounding system determines whether that command can be executed and under which permissions.

The model card should not claim that the model independently enforces least privilege when authorization is actually implemented by an external identity or policy system.

Where model behavior and system permissions interact to create material risk, both the model card and the related system documentation should describe the relevant boundary.

### 30. The Relationship Between Model Cards and AI Risk Management

Model cards contribute to risk management by making model characteristics and limitations available to the people responsible for evaluating and governing the associated risks.

They can inform:

- AI risk identification.
- Risk assessment and prioritization.
- Security assessment.
- Privacy assessment.
- Model validation.
- Deployment approval.
- Human-oversight design.
- Monitoring requirements.
- Change impact assessment.
- Internal audit and assurance.

However, a model card is not itself a complete risk assessment.

A model card may identify that a model performs poorly under certain conditions. The risk assessment must determine the potential consequences of that limitation in the specific business context, the likelihood of exposure, the effectiveness of existing controls, and the acceptability of the remaining risk.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
MODEL CARD
    ↓
Model Capabilities and Limitations
    ↓
AI Risk Identification
    ↓
Context-Specific Risk Assessment
    ↓
Control Selection and Validation
    ↓
Deployment Decision
    ↓
Monitoring and Reassessment
```

The model card provides structured input to risk management. It does not replace the judgment, evidence, and approval required to make a defensible governance decision.

### 31. Common Model Card Deficiencies

Model cards can fail to provide meaningful governance value when they are incomplete, outdated, misleading, or disconnected from supporting evidence.

Common deficiencies include:

- Vague intended-purpose statements.
- Missing model version information.
- Evaluation results without methodology.
- Performance claims without relevant test conditions.
- Generic limitations that do not guide users.
- Unsupported claims of fairness or safety.
- Failure to distinguish provider statements from independent findings.
- Inadequate disclosure of evaluation gaps.
- Confusion between model-level and system-level controls.
- Missing ownership or approval information.
- No defined review or update process.
- Inconsistency with deployed configurations.
- No traceability to supporting technical reports.
- Failure to communicate material use restrictions.

These deficiencies should be evaluated according to their significance.

A missing publication date may be a minor issue in one context, while an incorrect model version or omitted limitation affecting a consequential decision may create a substantial governance concern.

The organization should focus on whether the deficiency impairs understanding, safe use, accountability, risk management, or the ability to support relevant claims with evidence.

### 32. Core Principles for Effective Model Cards

Effective model cards follow several fundamental principles.

**Accuracy:** Claims should reflect the available evidence and actual model characteristics.

**Transparency:** Relevant capabilities, limitations, and evaluation boundaries should be communicated clearly.

**Traceability:** Material statements should link to appropriate supporting records.

**Contextual relevance:** Information should reflect the intended purpose, users, operating conditions, and risk profile.

**Proportionality:** Documentation depth should be appropriate to model complexity and potential impact.

**Accountability:** Ownership, review responsibilities, and approval status should be identifiable.

**Lifecycle maintenance:** Model cards should be updated when material changes affect their accuracy.

**Audience suitability:** Information should be understandable to the stakeholders who rely on it.

**Honest uncertainty:** Missing information and unverified claims should be identified rather than concealed.

**Separation of model and system claims:** The card should distinguish model-level properties from controls and behaviors supplied by the wider AI system.

These principles help ensure that model cards provide substantive governance value rather than serving only as a documentation checklist.

### 33. Part 1 Core Principle

> **A model card is a structured, evidence-informed representation of an AI model's intended purpose, characteristics, evaluation, limitations, and appropriate use. Its value depends on whether it accurately communicates what is known, what remains uncertain, and what conditions are necessary for responsible deployment.**

For AI Security Governance and GRC professionals, the central task is to ensure that model cards support informed decisions rather than merely describe technical components.

A model card should help stakeholders determine whether a model is appropriate for a defined purpose, understand the boundaries of available evidence, identify relevant risks, and recognize when additional controls, testing, or human oversight are necessary.

Model cards are therefore best treated as governed artifacts connected to model documentation, risk management, validation, change management, and assurance processes.

They provide an important communication layer between model development and enterprise governance, but they must remain consistent with the underlying evidence and the actual AI system in which the model is used.
