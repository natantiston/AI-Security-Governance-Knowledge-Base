# 19.14 — Model Cards

## Part 1 — Model Card Foundations, Purpose, Structure, and Governance Requirements

### 1. Understanding Model Cards

A model card is a structured document that communicates essential information about an AI model, including its intended purpose, capabilities, evaluation results, limitations, appropriate uses, and relevant risks.

Its primary purpose is to help stakeholders understand what a model is designed to do, how it has been evaluated, the conditions under which its results may be reliable, and the circumstances in which additional controls or human judgment are required.

Model cards provide a standardized way to communicate information that might otherwise be scattered across technical reports, development records, evaluation results, and internal documentation.

A model card may describe:

- The model's intended purpose.
- Its intended and excluded use cases.
- The model architecture or general technical approach.
- Training-data characteristics, where available.
- Evaluation methodology and results.
- Performance across relevant populations or scenarios.
- Known limitations and failure conditions.
- Fairness, safety, privacy, and security considerations.
- Appropriate deployment conditions.
- Required human oversight.
- Relevant model version and provenance information.

The exact content depends on the model, its intended use, the available evidence, and the organization's documentation requirements.

**A model card is not merely a model description. It is a structured communication mechanism for model capabilities, evidence, limitations, and responsible use.**

### 2. Why Model Cards Matter in AI Security Governance

From an AI Security Governance perspective, a model card helps bridge the gap between technical model development and organizational decision-making.

AI engineers may understand a model's architecture and evaluation results, but business owners, risk managers, security teams, compliance officers, and procurement teams may need that information presented in a more consistent and decision-oriented format.

Without a structured model card, stakeholders may misunderstand the model's capabilities or assume that successful testing establishes broader reliability than the evidence supports.

For example, a model may achieve strong accuracy on a particular evaluation dataset but perform poorly on unfamiliar inputs, underrepresented populations, or operating conditions that were not included in testing.

A model card should make the scope of the evidence visible so that decision-makers can distinguish demonstrated capabilities from untested assumptions.

Model cards support several governance objectives:

- **Transparency:** Communicating relevant information about model behavior and intended use.
- **Accountability:** Identifying the responsible model owner and relevant approval authorities.
- **Risk management:** Making known limitations and relevant risks visible.
- **Security governance:** Communicating security-relevant constraints and evaluation findings.
- **Compliance support:** Helping demonstrate that relevant model information has been considered and communicated.
- **Human oversight:** Explaining where human judgment or intervention may be necessary.
- **Lifecycle governance:** Maintaining model-specific information as the model evolves.

A model card does not independently establish that a model is secure, fair, safe, or legally compliant. It supports these objectives by communicating information that must be evaluated alongside other evidence and controls.

### 3. Model Cards Versus General Model Documentation

Model cards and model documentation are closely related, but they serve different purposes.

**Model documentation** is the broader collection of records describing a model's development, evaluation, deployment, operation, governance, and lifecycle history.

**A model card** is a structured summary of selected information from that broader documentation, organized to help defined audiences understand the model and its appropriate use.

The distinction can be represented as follows:

```
MODEL DOCUMENTATION
│
├── Technical specifications
├── Development records
├── Data documentation
├── Evaluation reports
├── Risk assessments
├── Security assessments
├── Validation evidence
├── Deployment records
├── Change history
├── Monitoring records
└── MODEL CARD
      ├── Intended use
      ├── Model characteristics
      ├── Evaluation summary
      ├── Limitations
      ├── Relevant risks
      └── Usage guidance
```

The model card may reference more detailed technical reports rather than reproducing all their contents.

For example, a model card may summarize evaluation performance and identify the evaluation report that contains the full methodology, test results, and limitations.

This approach improves usability while preserving traceability to the underlying evidence.

A model card should not replace detailed technical documentation, risk assessments, validation reports, or mandatory regulatory documentation where those artifacts are separately required.

### 4. Model Cards Versus System Cards

A model card focuses primarily on an individual model. A system card generally describes a broader AI system, including how one or more models are integrated into an application, service, or operational environment.

The distinction is important because an AI system's risk profile is not determined by its model alone.

Consider an enterprise AI assistant that uses a foundation model, a retrieval system, internal documents, access controls, and external APIs.

A model card might describe the foundation model's capabilities, evaluation characteristics, and limitations.

A system card might additionally describe:

- The overall system architecture.
- The application and user interface.
- Retrieval and grounding mechanisms.
- External data sources.
- Authentication and authorization.
- Tool access and system integrations.
- Human-oversight arrangements.
- Operational safeguards.
- System-level evaluation and known failure modes.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
AI SYSTEM
│
├── Application
├── User Interface
├── Identity and Access Controls
├── Data Sources
├── Retrieval Components
├── External APIs and Tools
├── Operational Controls
│
└── AI MODEL
      └── Model Card
```

A model card can provide important evidence for a system card, but it cannot establish the effectiveness of controls implemented outside the model.

For example, a model card describing a model's resistance to certain adversarial inputs does not demonstrate that an enterprise application has correctly enforced access permissions on retrieved documents.

**Model-level transparency and system-level transparency are complementary governance activities.**

### 5. Model Cards as a Governance Artifact

A model card should be treated as a governed artifact rather than an informal technical document.

Its creation, review, approval, publication, and maintenance should follow defined organizational procedures.

The governance process should establish:

- Who is responsible for preparing the model card.
- Who validates technical claims.
- Who reviews relevant risks and limitations.
- Who approves its use or publication.
- Which version of the model the card describes.
- Where the approved card is maintained.
- Which stakeholders may access it.
- When the card must be updated.
- How obsolete versions are retained or withdrawn.
- How deficiencies and exceptions are managed.

These requirements help prevent a model card from becoming disconnected from the model it describes.

For example, an organization may retain a model card describing version 2.1 while production has already moved to version 3.0. Even if the original card was accurate when created, it may no longer provide an adequate basis for current governance decisions.

The organization should therefore maintain explicit links between model cards, model identifiers, version records, evaluation evidence, and approval decisions.

### 6. Establishing the Intended Purpose of a Model

The intended purpose is one of the most important elements of a model card.

It explains what the model is designed or approved to do and provides the context for interpreting its evaluation results, limitations, and risks.

An intended-purpose statement should be sufficiently specific to distinguish approved use from unsupported or prohibited use.

For example, the following statement is too broad:

> The model supports customer service.

A more useful statement might be:

> The model assists authorized customer-service employees by generating draft responses to routine product and account questions using approved reference material. Employees must verify consequential statements before communicating them to customers. The model is not authorized to make independent credit, eligibility, or account-termination decisions.

The more specific statement identifies the model's operational role, intended users, information context, human involvement, and boundaries.

An intended-purpose section should consider:

- The business problem being addressed.
- The intended user population.
- The decisions or activities supported.
- The expected operating environment.
- The information and inputs required.
- The outputs the model produces.
- The degree of automation.
- The expected human involvement.
- The consequences of incorrect outputs.
- The uses that fall outside the approved scope.

The intended purpose should reflect the actual approved deployment, not merely the developer's original research objective.

### 7. Documenting Intended and Excluded Uses

A model may be technically capable of performing activities that the organization has not approved.

Model cards should distinguish between what a model can technically do and what it is intended or authorized to do.

For example, a general-purpose language model may generate legal explanations, medical summaries, financial suggestions, or employment-related recommendations. That capability does not establish that the model has been validated or approved for every such use.

Documentation should therefore identify relevant use boundaries.

| Use Category     | Meaning                                                                      |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Intended use     | The activity for which the model has been designed or approved               |
| Conditional use  | A use permitted only when specified requirements or safeguards are satisfied |
| Unsupported use  | A use for which adequate evidence or validation is unavailable               |
| Prohibited use   | A use that the organization or applicable requirements do not permit         |
| Out-of-scope use | An activity outside the model's defined purpose or governance authorization  |

These categories should be used consistently with the organization's policies and applicable obligations.

The model card should also explain material conditions attached to permitted uses.

For example, a model may be approved to summarize internal documents but not to make autonomous decisions that affect employees. It may be permitted to draft recommendations while requiring human approval before any consequential action is taken.

Where the distinction is material, the model card should identify the controls needed to enforce the boundary.

### 8. Identifying the Model and Its Version

A model card must identify the model it describes.

Without reliable identification, stakeholders may be unable to determine whether the documented capabilities, limitations, and evaluation results apply to the model currently in use.

Core identification fields may include:

- Model name.
- Unique model identifier.
- Model version or release.
- Model provider or development organization.
- Model owner.
- Associated AI system.
- Model type or family.
- Deployment status.
- Documentation version.
- Publication or approval date.
- Last review date.

For internally developed models, identification may include a model registry identifier, artifact reference, or other controlled identifier.

For externally hosted models, identification may include the provider's model identifier, API version, service configuration, and relevant release information.

Some hosted services use dynamic model aliases or provider-managed updates. Where an immutable version cannot be established, the model card should explain the limitation and identify the available mechanisms for tracking changes.

A model name alone is not always sufficient to establish identity.

The organization should be able to determine whether an evaluation result applies to the same model version and configuration as the model used in production.

### 9. Documenting the Model Type and Technical Characteristics

A model card should provide enough technical context for its intended audience to understand the model's general characteristics.

The level of detail should be proportionate to the model's complexity, intended use, and risk.

Relevant information may include:

- Model family or type.
- Learning or inference approach.
- General architecture.
- Input and output formats.
- Supported modalities.
- Important configuration parameters.
- Context or sequence limitations.
- Required dependencies.
- Execution environment.
- Whether the model is internally developed or externally provided.
- Whether it is accessed through an API or deployed within the organization's environment.

For a classification model, the card might explain the classes it predicts and how its scores or thresholds are interpreted.

For a generative model, it might describe the types of content it generates, the supported modalities, and relevant constraints on its context window or output format.

For a model embedded in an agentic system, the card should not imply that the model itself controls all system actions if those actions are actually governed by separate application logic, permissions, or orchestration components.

Technical descriptions should be accurate but audience-appropriate.

A business decision-maker may need to understand the implications of a model's design without requiring every implementation detail. A technical reviewer may need additional information or references to detailed architecture documentation.

### 10. Documenting Training Data and Data Provenance

Training data can materially influence model capabilities, limitations, security exposure, and suitability for a particular use.

Where information is available and appropriate to disclose, model cards should describe relevant training-data characteristics.

These may include:

- Data sources or source categories.
- Data collection period.
- Geographic or linguistic coverage.
- Relevant population characteristics.
- Data preparation and transformation.
- Labeling or annotation processes.
- Known data-quality limitations.
- Known representation gaps.
- Relevant privacy or licensing considerations.
- Data exclusions and their rationale.

The description should be sufficiently informative to support an understanding of the model's evidence base without disclosing confidential or protected information unnecessarily.

For a third-party foundation model, the organization may not have access to the provider's complete training dataset or detailed provenance records.

In such cases, the model card should distinguish between information supplied by the provider, information independently verified by the organization, and information that remains unavailable.

It should not imply that the organization has independently verified training-data properties when it has not.

Where training-data uncertainty creates material risk, the organization should assess whether additional testing, contractual assurances, usage restrictions, or other controls are necessary.

### 11. Documenting Evaluation Methodology

Evaluation results are meaningful only when their methodology and scope can be understood.

A model card should summarize how the model was evaluated and identify the important conditions under which the results were obtained.

Relevant information may include:

- Evaluation objectives.
- Test datasets or evaluation populations.
- Dataset provenance, where available.
- Test scenarios.
- Evaluation metrics.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Relevant model and configuration versions.
- Test environment.
- Baseline comparisons.
- Evaluation dates.
- Important exclusions.
- Known methodological limitations.

For example, a statement that a model achieved 94% accuracy is incomplete without information about what was predicted, which dataset was used, how the test population was selected, and whether the metric is appropriate for the intended use.

Evaluation methodology should also be appropriate to the model's function.

A fraud-detection model may require analysis of false positives, false negatives, and performance under changing fraud patterns. A generative model may require evaluation of factuality, relevance, harmful outputs, robustness, or task-specific quality.

The model card does not necessarily need to reproduce the entire evaluation report. It should summarize the evidence clearly and provide traceable references to the detailed results.

### 12. Presenting Evaluation Results Responsibly

Model cards should communicate evaluation results without overstating what they demonstrate.

A result achieved under a controlled test does not automatically establish performance in every production environment.

For example, a model may perform well on a test dataset but experience reduced performance when user behavior changes, data distributions shift, or the model is integrated into a different application.

Evaluation results should therefore be presented with relevant context.

Where appropriate, the model card should identify:

- The metric and its definition.
- The measured result.
- The applicable acceptance threshold.
- The test population or scenario.
- Relevant subgroup results.
- The conditions under which testing occurred.
- Important uncertainty or variability.
- Known limitations in the evaluation.
- The relationship between test performance and the intended use.

Results should not be selectively presented to create an unjustifiably favorable impression.

If an evaluation identifies significant weaknesses, the model card should communicate those weaknesses and any relevant restrictions or mitigations.

Where evaluation results are unavailable, inconclusive, or not applicable, the documentation should state this clearly rather than substituting unsupported assurances.

### 13. Documenting Model Limitations

Model limitations describe conditions under which a model may produce unreliable, incomplete, inappropriate, or otherwise unsuitable results.

A useful model card makes these limitations understandable to the people who must decide whether and how to use the model.

Limitations may involve:

- Incomplete or unrepresentative training data.
- Poor performance on particular tasks or populations.
- Sensitivity to input quality.
- Difficulty handling unusual or unfamiliar scenarios.
- Limited factual reliability.
- Inability to provide dependable explanations.
- Weaknesses in detecting ambiguity.
- Susceptibility to adversarial manipulation.
- Dependence on external services or data.
- Performance degradation under distribution shift.
- Lack of evaluation in certain operating conditions.

Limitations should be specific enough to guide decisions.

For example, stating that a model “may make mistakes” offers little practical guidance.

A more useful statement might explain that the model has not been evaluated for a particular language, performs less reliably on a defined class of inputs, or requires human verification before its outputs are used in a consequential decision.

Where known, the card should identify the evidence supporting the limitation and the measures intended to reduce its impact.

**A model card should communicate not only what the model can do, but also where confidence in its outputs should be limited.**

### 14. Distinguishing Model Limitations From System Limitations

Not every limitation observed in an AI application originates in the underlying model.

Some limitations arise from the model itself, while others arise from data pipelines, retrieval mechanisms, application logic, access controls, external services, or operational procedures.

Consider an AI assistant that generates incorrect answers because the retrieval system returns outdated documents.

The issue may not be caused solely by the language model. It may arise from stale knowledge sources, indexing delays, inadequate source selection, or weaknesses in the retrieval configuration.

A model card should describe limitations relevant to the model and its evaluated behavior. Where the card is used to communicate information about a particular deployment, it should clearly identify material dependencies and point to system-level documentation for limitations outside the model's direct scope.

A useful distinction is:

| Limitation Type           | Example                                                                            |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model limitation          | The model generates unsupported statements under certain conditions                |
| Data limitation           | The training or reference data does not adequately represent the target population |
| Integration limitation    | The application supplies incomplete or incorrectly formatted inputs                |
| Retrieval limitation      | The system retrieves outdated or irrelevant documents                              |
| Access-control limitation | The application fails to restrict information retrieved for a user                 |
| Operational limitation    | Required human review is not consistently performed                                |
| Provider limitation       | The organization cannot independently verify certain model properties              |

This distinction improves accountability by helping teams identify the correct control owner and remediation path.

### 15. Documenting Fairness and Subgroup Performance

Where a model's use can affect individuals or groups, evaluation may need to examine whether performance differs across relevant populations.

The model card should summarize the relevant evaluation approach and results where such analysis is applicable and supported by evidence.

Potential considerations include:

- The populations represented in evaluation.
- The rationale for selecting relevant subgroups.
- Differences in error rates or other performance measures.
- Known representation gaps.
- Limitations in subgroup sample sizes.
- The suitability of selected fairness metrics.
- Potential consequences of observed differences.
- Relevant mitigations and monitoring requirements.

Fairness cannot be established through a single universal metric for every model and context.

The appropriate assessment depends on the intended purpose, the affected stakeholders, the type of decision, the available data, and applicable legal or organizational requirements.

The model card should avoid making broad claims such as “the model is unbiased” without clearly defined criteria and supporting evidence.

Where subgroup analysis was not performed, the card should not imply that subgroup performance has been established. If the absence of analysis creates material uncertainty, that limitation should be considered in the associated risk assessment and deployment decision.

### 16. Documenting Security Considerations

Model cards can communicate security-relevant characteristics and limitations that affect the model's appropriate use.

The scope should reflect the model type, threat environment, and deployment context.

Potential considerations include:

- Known security weaknesses.
- Susceptibility to adversarial inputs.
- Relevant prompt-injection or jailbreak evaluation findings.
- Potential for sensitive information disclosure.
- Limitations in detecting malicious or manipulative content.
- Model access and usage restrictions.
- Dependencies on external providers.
- Relevant security testing boundaries.
- Required application-level safeguards.

Security information must be communicated carefully.

A model card intended for broad public distribution may not be an appropriate location for detailed exploit instructions, sensitive vulnerability information, or confidential security configurations.

The organization can provide a suitable summary in the model card and maintain more detailed technical evidence in access-controlled security documentation.

The card should also distinguish between model-level security characteristics and system-level controls.

For example, a model's tested resistance to prompt manipulation does not replace authorization checks, tool restrictions, network controls, monitoring, or secure application design.

Security claims should be proportionate to the testing performed and the evidence available.

### 17. Documenting Privacy and Data Protection Considerations

Privacy-related information may be important when a model processes personal data, was trained using datasets containing personal information, or is deployed in a context where its outputs can affect individuals.

Where relevant, the model card may summarize:

- Known privacy-related limitations.
- Relevant training-data considerations.
- Potential memorization or disclosure concerns.
- Evaluation for sensitive-information leakage.
- Restrictions on inputting personal or confidential information.
- Relevant assumptions about data handling.
- Conditions requiring additional privacy controls.
- References to privacy assessments and related documentation.

The model card should not be treated as a substitute for a data protection impact assessment, records of processing activities, data-processing agreements, or other required privacy documentation.

Those records address broader legal, organizational, and processing-specific questions.

The model card should provide information useful for understanding the model's characteristics while referring to the appropriate governance artifacts for the complete privacy assessment.

For third-party models, the organization should distinguish between verified technical behavior and provider statements about data handling or retention.

A provider's privacy commitment does not, by itself, establish that every implementation and configuration satisfies the organization's privacy obligations.

### 18. Documenting Safety and Potential Harm

Where model outputs can contribute to physical, psychological, financial, social, or other consequential harm, the model card should communicate relevant safety considerations.

Potential areas include:

- Known unsafe output patterns.
- Conditions associated with elevated error risk.
- Limitations in recognizing dangerous scenarios.
- Evaluation of harmful or inappropriate outputs.
- Potential impacts on affected stakeholders.
- Required human intervention.
- Use restrictions.
- Known residual safety concerns.

Safety considerations vary significantly by application.

A model used to classify routine internal documents may require a different safety analysis from a model supporting medical workflows, industrial operations, or other consequential activities.

The model card should not present a general safety statement as proof that every possible harmful outcome has been prevented.

Instead, it should communicate the scope of the assessment, the relevant findings, the known limitations, and the safeguards required for the intended use.

Where safety assurance depends on system-level controls, the card should identify those dependencies and refer to the relevant system documentation.

### 19. Documenting Human Oversight Requirements

Some AI applications require human review, approval, intervention, or the ability to override model outputs.

A model card should communicate these requirements when they materially affect appropriate use.

Relevant information may include:

- Which outputs require human review.
- Which decisions must remain with authorized personnel.
- Circumstances requiring escalation.
- Conditions under which the model should not be used.
- Expected reviewer competence.
- Known limitations that reviewers must understand.
- Procedures for rejecting or correcting model outputs.
- Circumstances requiring suspension or intervention.

The phrase “human in the loop” is insufficient when it does not specify what the human is expected to do.

For example, an organization may require employees to review AI-generated customer responses before sending them. The model card should explain the review requirement and relevant limitations, while the operational procedure should establish how review is implemented and evidenced.

The existence of a human approval step does not automatically establish effective oversight. Reviewers must have adequate information, authority, time, and competence to identify and address problematic outputs.

### 20. Documenting Performance Across Operating Conditions

Model performance can vary depending on the inputs, users, environment, and operating conditions.

A model card should describe the conditions under which the model has been evaluated and identify material gaps in coverage.

Relevant conditions may include:

- Input quality.
- Language or geographic context.
- User population.
- Data distribution.
- Workload or operating environment.
- Input complexity.
- Adversarial or unusual scenarios.
- Changes in source data.
- Differences between test and production environments.

For example, a model evaluated using structured, high-quality data may perform less reliably when deployed against incomplete records or noisy inputs.

Similarly, a language model evaluated primarily in English may not have equivalent evidence supporting use in every other language.

The card should avoid implying universal performance from limited test coverage.

Where operating conditions can change over time, the model card should identify relevant assumptions and point to the monitoring requirements used to detect material deterioration.

### 21. Documenting Model Uncertainty

Model uncertainty concerns the limits of confidence that can reasonably be placed in a model's outputs or evaluation results.

The way uncertainty should be represented depends on the model and task.

For some models, uncertainty may be expressed through calibrated probabilities or prediction intervals. For generative AI, apparent confidence in natural-language output may not reliably correspond to factual correctness.

The model card should explain the relevant uncertainty measures and their limitations.

Potential information includes:

- Whether uncertainty is quantified.
- Which method is used.
- Whether the method has been evaluated for the intended context.
- Conditions under which uncertainty estimates may be unreliable.
- How uncertainty affects decision-making.
- When additional verification is required.

A model's confidence score should not automatically be interpreted as the probability that its output is correct unless the measure has been appropriately defined and validated for that interpretation.

For generative models, the card should not imply that fluent or confident language establishes factual accuracy.

Where uncertainty cannot be reliably quantified, the documentation should communicate that limitation and explain the implications for appropriate use.

### 22. Defining the Intended Audience of a Model Card

A model card should be designed for identifiable audiences.

Different stakeholders need different levels of detail, and one document may need to serve several audiences with different information requirements.

Potential audiences include:

- Model developers and engineers.
- Model validators.
- Business owners.
- AI governance teams.
- Cybersecurity professionals.
- Privacy and legal specialists.
- Risk and compliance teams.
- Procurement and third-party risk teams.
- Internal auditors.
- Customers or external users.
- Regulators or other authorized reviewers.

A technical team may need detailed evaluation methods and model configuration references. A business owner may need clear information about intended use, limitations, and approval conditions. An external user may need concise guidance about capabilities and appropriate reliance.

Organizations should determine whether a single model card can meet these needs or whether separate audience-specific versions are necessary.

Where multiple versions are maintained, they should remain consistent with the same authoritative model record. Simplification for a nontechnical audience should not remove a material limitation or change the meaning of a technical finding.

### 23. Establishing Model Card Ownership and Approval

The model card should have an identifiable owner who is responsible for maintaining its accuracy and coordinating required updates.

Ownership does not mean that one individual must personally produce every section.

Technical claims may require input from model developers or validators, while business-purpose statements may require confirmation from the business owner. Security, privacy, and compliance sections may require review by relevant specialists.

A responsibility model may include:

| Activity                                   | Typical Responsibility                      |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| Drafting technical content                 | Model development or engineering team       |
| Confirming intended use                    | Business or product owner                   |
| Validating evaluation claims               | Qualified validation or evaluation function |
| Reviewing security information             | Cybersecurity or AI security function       |
| Reviewing privacy implications             | Privacy specialists, where applicable       |
| Reviewing risk and governance implications | AI governance, risk, or compliance function |
| Approving intended deployment              | Authorized business or governance authority |
| Maintaining the approved card              | Designated model documentation owner        |

The exact allocation depends on the organization's operating model and risk classification.

Approval requirements should be proportionate to the model's intended use and potential impact.

A low-risk internal utility may require a streamlined review. A model supporting consequential decisions may require formal validation, specialist review, and explicit approval before deployment.

The model card should make the approval status clear and should not imply that a draft or unreviewed document represents an authorized governance decision.

### 24. Establishing Model Card Review and Update Triggers

Model cards should be maintained throughout the model lifecycle.

A card that was accurate at the time of initial approval may become misleading after changes to the model, data, configuration, provider, intended use, or deployment environment.

The organization should define both periodic review requirements and event-driven update triggers.

Potential triggers include:

- A new model version.
- A material configuration change.
- A change in intended use.
- A change in the target population.
- New evaluation results.
- Discovery of a material limitation.
- A security or privacy incident.
- Significant performance degradation.
- A material change in external dependencies.
- A change in applicable requirements.
- A change to human-oversight arrangements.
- A decision to restrict, suspend, or retire the model.

The update process should determine which parts of the model card are affected and whether the change requires additional evaluation or approval.

For example, changing a model's intended use from internal document summarization to automated customer eligibility assessment is not merely a documentation edit. It may introduce different risks, evaluation requirements, stakeholder impacts, and regulatory considerations.

The model card should reflect the new approved context only after the required governance activities have been completed.

### 25. Model Cards and Regulatory Compliance

Model cards can support regulatory compliance by helping organizations communicate model characteristics, intended use, evaluation evidence, limitations, and relevant governance decisions.

Their value depends on the applicable requirements and the actual information they contain.

A model card may support:

- Internal AI governance reviews.
- Risk and impact assessments.
- Technical transparency activities.
- Documentation required by internal policies.
- Supplier and customer assurance.
- Internal audit evidence collection.
- Regulatory review preparation.
- Communication of model limitations and operating conditions.

However, a model card should not automatically be treated as satisfying every technical documentation, transparency, conformity assessment, or recordkeeping obligation.

Applicable obligations depend on the AI system, its intended purpose, the organization's role, the relevant jurisdiction, and the legal requirements in force.

Where a regulatory framework requires specific information, the organization should map the required information to the model card and other authoritative records.

A gap in the model card may be addressed by a linked artifact if the relevant requirements permit that approach and the evidence remains accessible and sufficiently clear.

The organization should avoid assuming that a document named “Model Card” is compliant simply because it contains a standard set of headings.

### 26. Distinguishing Public Model Cards From Internal Model Cards

Model cards can be intended for public release, internal governance, controlled external disclosure, or a combination of these purposes.

The audience affects the level of technical detail, confidentiality controls, and presentation.

**Public model cards** may provide information about intended use, evaluation results, limitations, and responsible-use guidance without disclosing confidential information.

**Internal model cards** may include additional operational context, risk references, approval details, and information needed for internal decision-making.

**Restricted model cards** may be used when particular details require controlled access because of security, commercial, contractual, or privacy considerations.

These categories are organizational design choices rather than universal legal classifications.

If multiple versions exist, the organization should maintain a clear relationship between them and the authoritative source record.

The public version should not materially misrepresent the model by omitting limitations that are essential to understanding its appropriate use.

Likewise, internal documentation should not assume that every technical detail can be shared externally without reviewing applicable confidentiality, security, intellectual-property, and privacy constraints.

### 27. Model Cards for Third-Party and Foundation Models

Third-party and foundation models create additional challenges because the organization using the model may not control its development or have access to all relevant technical information.

The organization should distinguish between:

- Information published by the provider.
- Information supplied contractually.
- Information independently tested by the organization.
- Information inferred from observed behavior.
- Information that remains unavailable.

A third-party model card should identify the provider and the model or service version as precisely as possible.

It should also communicate the limitations of the available evidence.

For example, a provider may publish performance benchmarks for a foundation model but not disclose complete training-data details. The organization should not treat those benchmarks as independent proof of suitability for its own use case.

Where appropriate, the organization should supplement provider information with application-specific evaluation, security testing, risk assessment, and monitoring.

If the provider updates the model or changes the service, the organization should assess whether the existing model card remains accurate.

Where the provider does not provide sufficient information, the resulting uncertainty should be recorded and considered in the organization's deployment decision.

### 28. Model Cards for Generative AI and Large Language Models

Generative AI and large language models introduce characteristics that should be reflected in their model cards.

Depending on the model and intended use, relevant information may include:

- Supported input and output modalities.
- Supported languages.
- Intended tasks.
- Known hallucination or factuality limitations.
- Sensitivity to prompt formulation.
- Context-window constraints.
- Relevant safety evaluation results.
- Known limitations in refusal behavior.
- Susceptibility to prompt manipulation.
- Potential sensitive-information disclosure risks.
- Limitations of available explainability methods.
- Requirements for grounding or human verification.

The card should distinguish between model-level capabilities and safeguards provided by the surrounding application.

For example, an application may use retrieval-augmented generation to ground answers in approved documents. The presence of retrieval does not establish that every answer is supported by those documents, nor does it guarantee that retrieved content is current or authorized for every user.

Similarly, a system prompt may guide model behavior but should not be represented as an infallible security boundary.

The model card should describe relevant limitations and point to system-level controls where those controls are essential to the intended use.

### 29. Model Cards for AI Agents and Tool-Connected Models

An AI agent may use a model to interpret instructions, plan tasks, call tools, retrieve information, and initiate actions.

In such systems, the model card should explain the model's role in the broader agent architecture without confusing model capabilities with system-level authority.

Relevant model-level information may include:

- The model's intended reasoning or generation tasks.
- Known limitations in instruction following.
- Relevant tool-use evaluation results.
- Failure patterns observed in multi-step tasks.
- Limitations in planning or interpreting context.
- Security-relevant evaluation findings.
- Conditions requiring human review.

The associated system documentation should describe the agent's actual tools, permissions, execution boundaries, approval requirements, and intervention mechanisms.

For example, a model may be capable of generating a command, but the surrounding system determines whether that command can be executed and under which permissions.

The model card should not claim that the model independently enforces least privilege when authorization is actually implemented by an external identity or policy system.

Where model behavior and system permissions interact to create material risk, both the model card and the related system documentation should describe the relevant boundary.

### 30. The Relationship Between Model Cards and AI Risk Management

Model cards contribute to risk management by making model characteristics and limitations available to the people responsible for evaluating and governing the associated risks.

They can inform:

- AI risk identification.
- Risk assessment and prioritization.
- Security assessment.
- Privacy assessment.
- Model validation.
- Deployment approval.
- Human-oversight design.
- Monitoring requirements.
- Change impact assessment.
- Internal audit and assurance.

However, a model card is not itself a complete risk assessment.

A model card may identify that a model performs poorly under certain conditions. The risk assessment must determine the potential consequences of that limitation in the specific business context, the likelihood of exposure, the effectiveness of existing controls, and the acceptability of the remaining risk.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
MODEL CARD
    ↓
Model Capabilities and Limitations
    ↓
AI Risk Identification
    ↓
Context-Specific Risk Assessment
    ↓
Control Selection and Validation
    ↓
Deployment Decision
    ↓
Monitoring and Reassessment
```

The model card provides structured input to risk management. It does not replace the judgment, evidence, and approval required to make a defensible governance decision.

### 31. Common Model Card Deficiencies

Model cards can fail to provide meaningful governance value when they are incomplete, outdated, misleading, or disconnected from supporting evidence.

Common deficiencies include:

- Vague intended-purpose statements.
- Missing model version information.
- Evaluation results without methodology.
- Performance claims without relevant test conditions.
- Generic limitations that do not guide users.
- Unsupported claims of fairness or safety.
- Failure to distinguish provider statements from independent findings.
- Inadequate disclosure of evaluation gaps.
- Confusion between model-level and system-level controls.
- Missing ownership or approval information.
- No defined review or update process.
- Inconsistency with deployed configurations.
- No traceability to supporting technical reports.
- Failure to communicate material use restrictions.

These deficiencies should be evaluated according to their significance.

A missing publication date may be a minor issue in one context, while an incorrect model version or omitted limitation affecting a consequential decision may create a substantial governance concern.

The organization should focus on whether the deficiency impairs understanding, safe use, accountability, risk management, or the ability to support relevant claims with evidence.

### 32. Core Principles for Effective Model Cards

Effective model cards follow several fundamental principles.

**Accuracy:** Claims should reflect the available evidence and actual model characteristics.

**Transparency:** Relevant capabilities, limitations, and evaluation boundaries should be communicated clearly.

**Traceability:** Material statements should link to appropriate supporting records.

**Contextual relevance:** Information should reflect the intended purpose, users, operating conditions, and risk profile.

**Proportionality:** Documentation depth should be appropriate to model complexity and potential impact.

**Accountability:** Ownership, review responsibilities, and approval status should be identifiable.

**Lifecycle maintenance:** Model cards should be updated when material changes affect their accuracy.

**Audience suitability:** Information should be understandable to the stakeholders who rely on it.

**Honest uncertainty:** Missing information and unverified claims should be identified rather than concealed.

**Separation of model and system claims:** The card should distinguish model-level properties from controls and behaviors supplied by the wider AI system.

These principles help ensure that model cards provide substantive governance value rather than serving only as a documentation checklist.

### 33. Part 1 Core Principle

> **A model card is a structured, evidence-informed representation of an AI model's intended purpose, characteristics, evaluation, limitations, and appropriate use. Its value depends on whether it accurately communicates what is known, what remains uncertain, and what conditions are necessary for responsible deployment.**

For AI Security Governance and GRC professionals, the central task is to ensure that model cards support informed decisions rather than merely describe technical components.

A model card should help stakeholders determine whether a model is appropriate for a defined purpose, understand the boundaries of available evidence, identify relevant risks, and recognize when additional controls, testing, or human oversight are necessary.

Model cards are therefore best treated as governed artifacts connected to model documentation, risk management, validation, change management, and assurance processes.

They provide an important communication layer between model development and enterprise governance, but they must remain consistent with the underlying evidence and the actual AI system in which the model is used.

# 19.14 — Model Cards

## Part 2 — Model Card Design, Content Requirements, and Technical Implementation

### 34. Establishing a Standardized Model Card Template

An enterprise model card should follow a standardized structure that enables consistent documentation across models, business units, development teams, and deployment environments.

Standardization improves comparability, review efficiency, governance oversight, and the ability to identify missing information.

However, standardization should not force every model into an identical level of technical detail. The template should define mandatory fields, conditional fields, and risk-based extensions.

A practical model card template may contain the following sections:

1. Model identification and version.
2. Ownership and accountability.
3. Intended purpose and approved use cases.
4. Prohibited and restricted uses.
5. Model characteristics and architecture summary.
6. Training-data and provenance information.
7. Evaluation methodology.
8. Performance results.
9. Fairness and subgroup evaluation.
10. Security considerations.
11. Privacy and data protection considerations.
12. Safety considerations.
13. Limitations and uncertainty.
14. Human-oversight requirements.
15. Deployment assumptions and dependencies.
16. Monitoring and reassessment requirements.
17. Approval status and review history.
18. References to supporting documentation.

Each field should have a defined purpose, expected content, validation requirement, and accountable owner.

The organization should also establish rules for fields that are not applicable, unavailable, confidential, or awaiting validation. Leaving a field blank should not be interpreted as evidence that the relevant issue has been assessed and found acceptable.

### 35. Defining Mandatory, Conditional, and Optional Fields

A model card template should distinguish between information that every model must provide and information required only in particular circumstances.

**Mandatory fields** establish the minimum information necessary to identify and govern the model.

Examples include the model identifier, version, owner, intended purpose, approval status, and known limitations.

**Conditional fields** become mandatory when triggered by the model's characteristics, intended use, or risk classification.

Examples include subgroup performance for a model used in consequential decisions, privacy information for models processing personal data, and safety evaluation details for applications where unsafe outputs could cause significant harm.

**Optional fields** provide additional information that improves understanding but may not be necessary for every model.

Examples include detailed architectural diagrams, specialized benchmark comparisons, or advanced uncertainty analyses when they are not relevant to the intended use.

A field classification model can be expressed as follows:

| Field Classification | Requirement                              | Governance Treatment                                                     |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Mandatory            | Required for all in-scope models         | Missing information must be resolved or formally handled as an exception |
| Conditional          | Required when defined criteria apply     | Applicability must be assessed and documented                            |
| Optional             | Added when useful                        | Inclusion depends on relevance and proportionality                       |
| Restricted           | Available only to authorized audiences   | Access and disclosure must be controlled                                 |
| Unavailable          | Information cannot currently be obtained | Limitation, source of uncertainty, and potential impact must be recorded |

A field marked unavailable should not automatically block every deployment. The organization should assess the importance of the missing information, identify compensating measures, and determine whether the residual uncertainty is acceptable.

For high-impact use cases, unavailable information about critical model characteristics may justify restricting deployment or declining to use the model.

### 36. Designing Field-Level Documentation Requirements

A template becomes operationally useful when each field has a clear definition and acceptance criterion.

For example, a field named `Intended Use` should not accept any general statement about the business function. It should require a description of the task, intended users, relevant operating conditions, and important use boundaries.

Field-level requirements should define:

- Field name and purpose.
- Required content.
- Accepted format.
- Responsible contributor.
- Supporting evidence.
- Validation rule.
- Confidentiality classification.
- Applicability conditions.
- Update triggers.

Consider the following design:

| Field              | Acceptance Criterion                                              | Supporting Evidence                      |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Model identifier   | Uniquely identifies the documented model                          | Model registry or provider record        |
| Model version      | Identifies the applicable release or explains version limitations | Release record or provider documentation |
| Intended purpose   | Defines the approved task, users, and relevant boundaries         | Use-case approval                        |
| Evaluation summary | Reports results with relevant methodology and limitations         | Evaluation report                        |
| Known limitations  | Identifies material limitations and their implications            | Testing results and risk assessment      |
| Model owner        | Names the accountable role or authorized owner                    | Governance register                      |
| Approval status    | Shows the current approval state                                  | Approval workflow or decision record     |
| Last review date   | Identifies the latest completed review                            | Documentation history                    |

Acceptance criteria should be sufficiently precise to support consistent review.

For instance, an evaluation summary should not be accepted solely because it contains a numerical score. It should provide enough context to interpret that score and identify the evidence supporting it.

### 37. Establishing a Model Card Data Model

Organizations that manage many AI models should consider representing model card information as structured data rather than relying exclusively on manually maintained documents.

A structured data model allows model card fields to be stored in a model registry, documentation platform, governance system, or controlled metadata repository.

Potential entities include:

- Model.
- Model version.
- Model owner.
- Intended use.
- Evaluation result.
- Limitation.
- Risk.
- Control.
- Approval.
- Supporting evidence.
- Documentation version.
- Review event.

Relationships between these entities support traceability.

For example, an evaluation result should be associated with the model version tested, the test methodology, the evaluation date, and the evidence record. A limitation should be associated with its supporting evidence and any relevant usage restriction.

A simplified relationship model is:

```
MODEL
  |
  +---- MODEL VERSION
  |        |
  |        +---- EVALUATION RESULTS
  |        |
  |        +---- KNOWN LIMITATIONS
  |        |
  |        +---- SECURITY FINDINGS
  |
  +---- INTENDED USE
  |
  +---- MODEL OWNER
  |
  +---- APPROVAL RECORD
  |
  +---- MODEL CARD VERSION
             |
             +---- SUPPORTING EVIDENCE
             |
             +---- REVIEW HISTORY
```

The model card may be generated from structured records, maintained as a controlled document, or produced through a hybrid approach.

The chosen implementation should preserve accuracy, version control, access restrictions, and the ability to trace statements to their sources.

### 38. Establishing Authoritative Sources for Model Card Information

A model card should not become an independent information silo.

Where possible, each field should derive from an authoritative source maintained by the function responsible for that information.

For example:

| Model Card Information        | Potential Authoritative Source                    |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Model identifier and version  | Model registry                                    |
| Business purpose              | Approved AI use-case register                     |
| Model architecture            | Technical architecture documentation              |
| Training-data characteristics | Data governance records or provider documentation |
| Evaluation results            | Controlled evaluation report                      |
| Security findings             | Security assessment or testing repository         |
| Privacy considerations        | Privacy assessment and data governance records    |
| Risk status                   | AI risk register                                  |
| Approval status               | Governance workflow                               |
| Deployment status             | Model registry or deployment management platform  |
| Monitoring requirements       | Model monitoring specification                    |
| Change history                | Model release and change management records       |

The model card should reference these records where practical.

If information is copied manually, the organization should define how discrepancies are identified and resolved.

For example, if the model registry identifies version 4.2 but the model card describes version 4.1, the discrepancy should be investigated before the card is relied upon for approval or deployment decisions.

An authoritative-source approach reduces duplication and helps maintain consistency across the model lifecycle.

### 39. Implementing Model Card Version Control

Model cards should be version-controlled independently of, but linked to, the model versions they describe.

This distinction matters because a model card may change without the underlying model changing. A documentation correction, updated risk assessment, or revised usage restriction may require a new card version.

Conversely, a model may change in a way that requires a new model card version even if the organization retains the same business-facing model name.

A controlled versioning process should record:

- Model version covered.
- Model card version.
- Date of creation.
- Date of approval.
- Author or contributing roles.
- Reviewer and approver.
- Summary of changes.
- Reason for the change.
- Supporting evidence.
- Superseded version.
- Current status.

A version history might look like this:

| Model Version | Model Card Version | Change                     | Governance Action                |
| ------------- | ------------------ | -------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1.0           | 1.0                | Initial documentation      | Initial review and approval      |
| 1.1           | 1.1                | Evaluation results updated | Technical review                 |
| 1.2           | 1.2                | New limitation identified  | Risk reassessment                |
| 2.0           | 2.0                | Material model update      | Revalidation and approval review |

This example illustrates a possible arrangement, not a requirement that model and card versions always follow identical numbering.

The organization should establish its own versioning convention and ensure that stakeholders can identify the currently approved card.

### 40. Defining Model Card Status and Lifecycle States

A model card should have a clearly defined status.

Without lifecycle states, a draft may be mistaken for an approved document, or an obsolete card may remain available without any indication that it no longer describes the current model.

A practical status model includes:

- **Draft:** Content is being prepared.
- **Under review:** Required reviewers are assessing the content.
- **Approved:** Required approval conditions have been satisfied.
- **Published:** The approved card has been released to its intended audience.
- **Update required:** A material change or deficiency requires review.
- **Superseded:** A newer approved version has replaced the card.
- **Withdrawn:** The card is no longer authorized for use or publication.

The organization may use different terminology, but the states should be unambiguous.

Status changes should follow controlled procedures. For example, publication should not automatically occur merely because a document has been saved in a repository.

Where a model is suspended or retired, the card's status should reflect that condition or clearly link to the authoritative lifecycle record.

Historical versions may need to be retained for traceability, legal requirements, internal policy, or investigation purposes. Retaining a historical card should not make it appear to be the current approved version.

### 41. Building a Model Card Approval Workflow

An approval workflow should verify that the card is complete, accurate, consistent with supporting evidence, and appropriate for its intended audience.

A typical workflow may include:

1. Model owner initiates or requests the card.
2. Technical contributors complete the relevant fields.
3. Supporting evidence is linked or attached.
4. The business owner confirms the intended purpose and use boundaries.
5. Relevant specialists review security, privacy, safety, and other applicable concerns.
6. The designated reviewer checks completeness and consistency.
7. The authorized decision-maker approves, rejects, or returns the card for revision.
8. The approved card is published to the intended audience.
9. The card is linked to the model registry and relevant governance records.
10. Review triggers and update responsibilities are activated.

Not every model requires every specialist to approve every card. Review participation should be determined by applicability and risk.

The workflow should also define what happens when reviewers disagree, evidence is incomplete, or a material limitation remains unresolved.

A model card approval must not be used to bypass separate model validation, risk acceptance, or deployment authorization requirements.

### 42. Designing Quality Assurance for Model Cards

Quality assurance should assess more than whether the template fields have been populated.

A card can be complete in appearance but still contain inaccurate, unsupported, ambiguous, or contradictory claims.

Quality checks should therefore evaluate several dimensions:

**Completeness:** Are all mandatory and applicable conditional fields populated?

**Accuracy:** Do the statements reflect the available evidence?

**Consistency:** Does the card agree with the model registry, evaluation reports, risk register, and deployment records?

**Traceability:** Can material claims be linked to supporting records?

**Clarity:** Can the intended audience understand the statements and limitations?

**Currency:** Does the card describe the relevant model version and approved deployment?

**Governance validity:** Are ownership, review, approval, and publication status clear?

**Disclosure appropriateness:** Does the card provide useful information without exposing information that should remain restricted?

Quality assurance may combine automated validation with human review.

Automated checks can identify missing fields, invalid identifiers, broken references, expired review dates, or inconsistent version numbers. Human reviewers are still needed to assess whether claims are meaningful, limitations are adequately explained, and the document supports sound decisions.

### 43. Using Structured Data and Automated Validation

Structured model card data enables automated validation and consistency checks.

For example, a documentation platform may enforce the presence of a model identifier, owner, intended purpose, evaluation reference, and approval status before allowing a card to move to an approved state.

Automated rules may also detect:

- Missing required fields.
- Invalid model identifiers.
- References to unavailable evidence.
- Unsupported status transitions.
- Review dates beyond defined limits.
- Inconsistent model versions.
- Missing approval records.
- Expired exceptions.
- Missing conditional sections.
- Changes to approved fields that require reapproval.

A simplified validation flow is:

```
Model Card Data Submitted
          |
          v
Schema and Required-Field Checks
          |
          v
Reference and Version Validation
          |
          v
Conditional Requirement Checks
          |
          v
Human Review
          |
          v
Approval Decision
          |
          v
Controlled Publication
```

Automation should enforce defined documentation requirements rather than substitute for substantive evaluation.

A system that verifies that a limitation field is populated cannot establish that the limitation has been described adequately.

Similarly, an automated check confirming that an evaluation report exists cannot establish that the evaluation methodology is appropriate for the intended use.

The organization should distinguish between machine-verifiable completeness and judgment-dependent content quality.

### 44. Linking Model Cards to the AI Model Registry

An AI model registry provides a controlled inventory of models and relevant metadata.

Linking model cards to the registry improves discoverability and helps ensure that stakeholders can locate the approved documentation for a particular model version.

The registry may store or reference:

- Model identifier.
- Model version.
- Model owner.
- Business use case.
- Model risk classification.
- Development and deployment status.
- Model card location.
- Evaluation report references.
- Approval status.
- Last review date.
- Known restrictions.
- Related system identifiers.

The model card should identify the model accurately, while the registry should provide a reliable way to find the current card.

Where a model is used across multiple systems, the organization should identify whether one model card adequately describes all relevant uses or whether system-specific supplements are required.

A single model may have consistent underlying characteristics but be deployed in contexts with different data, users, permissions, consequences, and safeguards.

The registry should not imply that approval for one application automatically authorizes every other application using the same model.

### 45. Linking Model Cards to Evaluation and Validation Evidence

A model card should provide a concise account of evaluation results while preserving traceability to the detailed evidence.

Evaluation references should identify the relevant report or record clearly enough that a reviewer can determine which tests were performed and which model version was assessed.

Useful metadata may include:

- Evaluation identifier.
- Evaluated model version.
- Evaluation date.
- Evaluation owner.
- Test methodology.
- Dataset or scenario reference.
- Metrics and results.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Material findings.
- Known limitations.
- Approval or disposition of findings.

Where evaluation evidence is revised, the organization should determine whether the card's summary remains accurate.

If a previously reported result is invalidated or a significant limitation is discovered, the card may require correction even if the model itself has not changed.

The card should not imply that evaluation has been completed when only preliminary testing has occurred.

Likewise, it should distinguish between internal testing, provider-reported results, independent evaluations, and externally published benchmark findings.

### 46. Linking Model Cards to Risk Registers

Model cards and AI risk registers serve complementary purposes.

The model card communicates relevant model properties and limitations. The risk register records identified risks, their assessment, treatment decisions, accountable owners, and residual risk.

A model card may identify that a model performs inconsistently on certain inputs. The risk register should evaluate whether that limitation creates a material risk in the intended context and determine what treatment is necessary.

A controlled relationship may include:

- Model identifier.
- Model card version.
- Limitation or finding identifier.
- Risk identifier.
- Risk owner.
- Risk assessment date.
- Treatment decision.
- Required controls.
- Residual risk status.
- Risk acceptance authority, where applicable.

This relationship enables stakeholders to determine whether important limitations have been considered in risk decisions.

It also supports change impact assessment. If a new limitation is identified, the organization can determine which risks, controls, use restrictions, and approval decisions may be affected.

The model card should not become the sole record of risk acceptance. Formal acceptance should remain within the organization's established risk governance process.

### 47. Linking Model Cards to Security and Privacy Assessments

Security and privacy assessments may contain detailed findings that are too extensive or sensitive to reproduce in a model card.

The card should summarize relevant implications and identify the appropriate supporting records.

Security-related references may include:

- Threat assessments.
- Adversarial testing reports.
- Vulnerability assessments.
- Security architecture reviews.
- Sensitive-information leakage evaluations.
- Third-party security assessments.

Privacy-related references may include:

- Data protection impact assessments.
- Data-flow documentation.
- Data governance records.
- Privacy testing results.
- Data retention and deletion requirements.
- Relevant provider agreements or privacy assessments.

The model card should accurately reflect the status of these assessments.

If a security assessment is incomplete, or a privacy concern remains unresolved, the card should not imply that the relevant risk has been fully addressed.

Where sensitive details are restricted, the card may provide a controlled reference and a suitable summary rather than disclose the full assessment.

Access to the underlying evidence should be managed according to the organization's security, confidentiality, and privacy requirements.

### 48. Model Card Design for Multiple Audiences

A single model card may need to communicate with technical and nontechnical stakeholders.

The organization should design the card so that each audience can locate the information relevant to its responsibilities without losing important context.

A layered presentation can support this requirement.

**Executive and business layer:** Intended purpose, business context, key limitations, principal risks, approval status, and required decisions.

**Governance and assurance layer:** Ownership, risk classification, review status, evidence references, control dependencies, and approval history.

**Technical layer:** Model characteristics, evaluation methodology, performance metrics, configuration constraints, and detailed technical references.

**User-facing layer:** Appropriate use, prohibited use, limitations, human-review requirements, and escalation guidance.

These layers may exist within one document or across linked artifacts.

The design should avoid creating inconsistent versions for different audiences. Material limitations and restrictions must remain consistent even when technical detail is simplified.

Where the card is published externally, the organization should also establish a review process for disclosure, confidentiality, intellectual-property, and security considerations.

### 49. Model Card Design for High-Risk and High-Impact Models

Models used in high-risk or high-impact contexts may require more detailed and carefully controlled model cards than low-impact internal applications.

The additional depth should be driven by the consequences of model error, the complexity of the model, the affected stakeholders, the operating environment, and applicable requirements.

Enhanced documentation may include:

- More detailed evaluation methodology.
- Performance across relevant subgroups.
- Explicit limitations and residual uncertainties.
- Clear use restrictions.
- Human-oversight requirements.
- Security and privacy assessment references.
- Validation status.
- Deployment conditions.
- Monitoring requirements.
- Escalation and suspension criteria.
- Change and reapproval triggers.
- Evidence of independent review.

For example, a model supporting employee screening may require documentation of the intended decision-support role, relevant evaluation coverage, limitations, human decision authority, and safeguards against inappropriate reliance.

A model used in a safety-critical operational context may require evidence about operating boundaries, failure conditions, integration dependencies, and procedures for intervention.

The card should not use a generic “high-risk” label as a substitute for explaining the actual risks and required controls.

Where the organization uses formal risk classifications, the model card should reference the applicable classification and remain consistent with the corresponding risk assessment.

### 50. Part 2 Core Principle

> **A model card becomes operationally effective when its structure, data sources, quality checks, approval workflow, and lifecycle controls ensure that the information presented is accurate, traceable, understandable, and appropriate for the model's intended use.**

The design objective is not to maximize the number of fields. It is to establish a repeatable method for communicating material model information while preserving links to the evidence needed for governance decisions.

Organizations should combine standardized templates with risk-based requirements, structured metadata, controlled versioning, and substantive review. Automation can improve completeness and consistency, but human accountability remains necessary for evaluating the meaning and adequacy of model card content.

## Part 3 — Model Card Governance, Risk Management, and Operational Assurance

### 51. Establishing Model Card Ownership and Accountability

Model card governance requires clearly assigned ownership throughout the model lifecycle. Without explicit accountability, documentation may become outdated, incomplete, or disconnected from operational decisions.

The organization should distinguish between responsibility for producing the model card, accountability for its accuracy, authority to approve its content, and responsibility for acting on the information it communicates.

Typical responsibilities include:

- **Model owner:** Accountable for the model's lifecycle, intended purpose, limitations, and governance status.
- **Model developer:** Provides technical information about model design, training, configuration, and evaluation.
- **Data owner:** Provides relevant information about data provenance, quality, classification, and permitted use.
- **AI risk manager:** Ensures that material model characteristics and limitations are reflected in the risk assessment.
- **Cybersecurity function:** Reviews applicable security risks, safeguards, and testing evidence.
- **Privacy function:** Reviews applicable privacy and data protection considerations.
- **Business owner:** Confirms the intended business purpose, operational context, and acceptable use boundaries.
- **Compliance function:** Determines which regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements are relevant.
- **Model validator:** Performs or coordinates independent validation where required.
- **Documentation owner:** Maintains the controlled record, version history, and publication status.
- **Approving authority:** Makes the formal decision required by the organization's governance process.

One person may perform multiple roles in a smaller organization, but incompatible responsibilities should be separated where the risk and organizational structure justify it.

The model card should identify the accountable owner and the relevant approval authority. Merely listing contributors does not establish who is responsible for keeping the documentation accurate.

### 52. Integrating Model Cards into the AI Governance Operating Model

Model cards should operate as part of the enterprise AI governance model rather than as isolated documentation artifacts.

The governance operating model defines how AI use cases are proposed, assessed, approved, deployed, monitored, changed, and retired. Model cards provide structured information that supports these activities.

Integration points include:

1. AI use-case registration.
2. Initial model classification.
3. Risk assessment and impact assessment.
4. Model selection and procurement.
5. Development and validation.
6. Security and privacy review.
7. Deployment authorization.
8. Operational monitoring.
9. Change management.
10. Periodic reassessment.
11. Incident management.
12. Model retirement.

At each stage, the organization should determine whether a model card is required, which fields must be completed, who reviews the information, and how the documentation affects the relevant decision.

For example, a procurement process should not treat a vendor's model card as automatic evidence that the proposed model is suitable for the organization's use case. The card provides information for due diligence, while the organization remains responsible for evaluating the model against its own requirements and deployment context.

Model cards should also be integrated with the AI inventory and risk register so that governance teams can identify undocumented models, incomplete assessments, and models operating under outdated approvals.

### 53. Defining Model Card Governance Policies

An enterprise model card policy establishes the minimum requirements for creating, reviewing, approving, maintaining, and disclosing model documentation.

The policy should define:

- Scope of models requiring model cards.
- Criteria for determining the required documentation depth.
- Mandatory and conditional content.
- Ownership and contributor responsibilities.
- Required evidence and traceability.
- Review and approval requirements.
- Version control and publication rules.
- Confidentiality and access restrictions.
- Periodic review requirements.
- Event-driven update triggers.
- Exception management.
- Retention and retirement requirements.
- Monitoring and compliance reporting.
- Consequences of material documentation deficiencies.

The policy should explain whether different rules apply to internally developed models, externally supplied models, open-weight models, foundation models, and models embedded in third-party products.

Where a supplier does not disclose particular technical information, the organization should define how the resulting uncertainty is assessed and managed.

A model card policy should be consistent with the broader AI governance framework, risk management policy, secure development standards, data governance policy, third-party risk management procedures, and records management requirements.

Policy compliance should be demonstrable through evidence rather than inferred from the existence of a template.

### 54. Establishing Risk-Based Documentation Requirements

Model card requirements should be proportionate to the risks associated with the model and its intended use.

A low-impact internal model may require a concise card documenting its purpose, owner, data characteristics, evaluation results, limitations, and approval status.

A model used to influence employment, financial eligibility, healthcare, public services, safety, or other consequential decisions may require substantially more detailed documentation and independent review.

Risk-based requirements should consider:

- Severity of potential harm.
- Number and characteristics of affected people.
- Degree of automation.
- Human ability to challenge or override outputs.
- Sensitivity of processed data.
- Model complexity and uncertainty.
- Exposure to adversarial manipulation.
- Dependency on external providers.
- Scale of deployment.
- Reversibility of decisions.
- Applicable legal and contractual obligations.
- Availability of reliable evaluation evidence.

The organization should define how these factors affect required documentation, review depth, and approval authority.

Risk classification should not be determined solely by the model's technical complexity. A relatively simple model can create significant harm when used in a consequential context, while a complex model may have limited impact in a constrained environment.

The model card should reflect the classification established by the organization's approved risk assessment process.

### 55. Using Model Cards in AI Risk Assessments

Model cards provide information that can support risk identification, analysis, evaluation, and treatment.

Relevant information includes intended purpose, limitations, evaluation results, operating assumptions, dependencies, and restrictions on use.

A risk assessment should examine how these characteristics interact with the organization's business processes and deployment environment.

For example, a model card may report reduced performance on a particular category of input. The risk assessment should determine whether that category is relevant to the intended population, whether the reduced performance could cause harm, and which controls are needed.

The process should establish traceability between:

- Model characteristic.
- Documented limitation.
- Potential failure scenario.
- Risk statement.
- Impact and likelihood assessment.
- Existing control.
- Treatment decision.
- Residual risk.
- Accountable risk owner.

A model card does not replace the risk assessment. It provides evidence and context that help the risk owner make an informed decision.

Where the risk assessment identifies a material issue not covered by the card, the organization should determine whether the card requires an update.

### 56. Translating Model Limitations into Enforceable Controls

Documenting a limitation is only the first step. The organization must determine whether the limitation requires an operational control.

A limitation should be evaluated to determine whether it calls for:

- Restricted use.
- Additional validation.
- Human review.
- Input validation.
- Output verification.
- Access restrictions.
- Data quality checks.
- Additional monitoring.
- Escalation procedures.
- Suspension or withdrawal criteria.

Consider a model that performs poorly when presented with incomplete or low-quality input data.

A useful model card should describe the limitation and its operational implications. The corresponding control framework might require input completeness checks, rejection of unsupported inputs, and escalation to a qualified reviewer when minimum quality thresholds are not met.

The organization should record the relationship between the limitation and the control, including the control owner, implementation status, and supporting evidence.

A warning in documentation should not be treated as an effective control if users cannot reasonably understand it, if the workflow ignores it, or if no mechanism exists to enforce the required restriction.

### 57. Managing Model Card Exceptions

Exceptions may be necessary when a model card cannot meet a defined requirement.

Examples include unavailable vendor information, incomplete historical training-data records, missing subgroup evaluation results, or temporary gaps in supporting evidence.

An exception process should require:

1. Identification of the unmet requirement.
2. Explanation of the reason for the gap.
3. Assessment of the potential impact.
4. Identification of affected models and use cases.
5. Definition of compensating controls.
6. Assignment of an accountable owner.
7. Approval by an authorized decision-maker.
8. Establishment of an expiry or review date.
9. Tracking of remediation actions.
10. Closure evidence.

An exception should not silently convert a mandatory requirement into an optional one.

The organization should distinguish between a justified exception, a temporary documentation gap, and a material uncertainty that makes the proposed use unacceptable.

For example, a supplier's refusal to disclose detailed training data may be commercially understandable. However, if the missing information prevents the organization from assessing a critical legal, security, or safety requirement, the organization may need additional independent testing, contractual safeguards, restricted deployment, or rejection of the model.

Risk acceptance should be documented through the formal risk governance process and should not be inferred from approval of the model card itself.

### 58. Integrating Model Cards with Third-Party Risk Management

Third-party model cards can support supplier due diligence, but their completeness and reliability must be assessed.

Supplier-provided documentation may vary significantly in scope, terminology, technical depth, and evidential quality. A supplier may disclose general model characteristics without providing the evidence necessary to evaluate a particular enterprise use case.

Third-party assessment should examine:

- Supplier identity and model provenance.
- Model version and release history.
- Intended purpose and known restrictions.
- Evaluation methodology and reported results.
- Training-data disclosures and limitations.
- Security and privacy documentation.
- Known vulnerabilities or material limitations.
- Update and change notification arrangements.
- Support and incident notification commitments.
- Evidence availability and audit rights.
- Contractual obligations and permitted uses.
- End-of-life and replacement arrangements.

The organization should distinguish supplier claims from independently verified facts.

Where practical, material claims should be supported by evaluation reports, contractual commitments, independent assessments, or other appropriate evidence.

If a supplier updates the model without providing sufficient notice, the organization may lose the ability to determine whether the current card still reflects the deployed model.

Third-party governance should therefore define how model changes are detected, assessed, approved, and documented.

### 59. Governing Foundation Models and Downstream Applications

Foundation models introduce particular governance challenges because a single model may support many downstream applications.

The foundation model provider may publish a model card describing general capabilities, limitations, and evaluation results. A downstream organization must still assess how the model behaves in its own application.

The downstream application may introduce additional components such as:

- System instructions.
- Retrieval-augmented generation.
- Enterprise data sources.
- Fine-tuning.
- Tool integrations.
- Agent workflows.
- Access-control mechanisms.
- Output filters.
- Human approval steps.

These components can materially change the risk profile of the overall system.

A foundation model's card should therefore be treated as one source of evidence within the downstream governance process, not as a complete description of the deployed application.

The organization should document the relationship between the foundation model and the application-specific configuration. Where necessary, it should maintain a model card supplement or system-level documentation covering the integration, configuration, additional controls, and resulting limitations.

The governance process should also distinguish between risks inherited from the foundation model and risks introduced by the downstream implementation.

### 60. Managing Model Cards for Generative AI Systems

Generative AI systems require model card content that addresses risks associated with open-ended outputs and context-dependent behavior.

Depending on the intended use, relevant considerations may include:

- Hallucination and factual unreliability.
- Prompt sensitivity.
- Inconsistent outputs.
- Unsafe or inappropriate content.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Prompt injection and indirect prompt injection.
- Copyright and intellectual-property concerns.
- Bias and harmful stereotypes.
- Retrieval failures.
- Tool-use errors.
- Inadequate refusal behavior.
- Context-window limitations.
- Model update variability.
- Evaluation limitations.

The model card should describe which risks have been assessed, the scope of the evaluation, and the controls or usage restrictions that apply.

For retrieval-augmented generation, the card should not attribute all system behavior to the underlying model. Retrieval quality, source selection, access permissions, document freshness, and grounding mechanisms can materially influence output reliability.

For tool-enabled or agentic systems, the documentation should explain relevant limitations around planning, tool selection, execution, and escalation where these characteristics affect the intended use.

The organization should distinguish model-level characteristics from application-level behavior so that control ownership and responsibility remain clear.

### 61. Model Cards for Fine-Tuned and Customized Models

Fine-tuning, quantization, adapter-based customization, and other modifications may alter model behavior, performance, or risk.

An organization should determine whether the resulting artifact can continue to rely on the original model card or requires a revised card and additional evaluation.

The decision should consider:

- Nature and extent of the modification.
- Data used for customization.
- Changes to model behavior.
- Changes in intended purpose.
- New or altered limitations.
- Security implications.
- Privacy implications.
- Evaluation coverage.
- Compatibility with existing approvals.

A fine-tuned model may inherit some characteristics of its base model, but inherited documentation does not establish that the customized model remains equivalent in every material respect.

The model card should identify the relationship to the base model and document relevant modifications without implying that the original evaluation automatically applies to the new artifact.

Where a change is material, the organization should assess whether additional testing, risk review, and approval are required before deployment.

### 62. Integrating Model Cards into Change Management

Model card maintenance should be linked to the organization's model and system change management processes.

Changes that may require review include:

- Model replacement.
- Model version updates.
- Fine-tuning or retraining.
- Training-data changes.
- Changes to intended use.
- Changes to the target population.
- New data sources.
- Changes to prompts or system instructions.
- New tools or external integrations.
- Changes to access controls.
- New deployment environments.
- Changes to monitoring or safety controls.
- New evaluation findings.
- Security incidents.
- Regulatory or contractual changes.

Not every change requires a complete rewrite of the card. The organization should classify changes according to their potential impact and define proportionate review requirements.

A material change may require re-evaluation, a revised card, renewed approval, or temporary suspension of the affected use case.

A minor administrative correction may require only controlled documentation revision.

The key requirement is that change classification and its rationale should be documented rather than determined informally by the person editing the card.

### 63. Defining Event-Driven Model Card Review Triggers

Periodic reviews are useful, but they cannot address every event that may invalidate model documentation.

Event-driven review triggers should identify circumstances requiring reassessment outside the normal review cycle.

Examples include:

- Discovery of a significant model limitation.
- A material degradation in performance.
- Evidence of bias or unexpected subgroup behavior.
- A security vulnerability affecting the model.
- A privacy incident.
- A material change in provider terms.
- A change in regulatory obligations.
- A new intended use.
- A change to the model's operating environment.
- A serious user complaint.
- A failed control test.
- An incident involving harmful or incorrect outputs.
- Evidence that the deployed version differs from the approved version.

The trigger should initiate a defined workflow that determines the scope and urgency of review.

For urgent issues, the process may require immediate containment or suspension before the documentation review is complete.

The resulting record should capture the event, affected model version, assessment outcome, decisions taken, card changes, and required follow-up actions.

### 64. Establishing Periodic Review and Attestation

Model cards should be reviewed at defined intervals appropriate to the model's risk, stability, and operating context.

The review interval should not be selected solely for administrative convenience. Models that change frequently or support consequential decisions may require more frequent reassessment than stable, low-impact models.

A periodic review should verify:

- The documented model version remains current.
- The intended purpose remains valid.
- The model is being used within approved boundaries.
- Evaluation results remain relevant.
- Known limitations remain accurate.
- Risk and control references remain current.
- Security and privacy assessments remain applicable.
- Monitoring requirements are being followed.
- Approval status remains valid.
- Required changes and exceptions are tracked.

Attestation should be performed by an accountable role with sufficient knowledge to confirm the relevant information.

A checkbox stating that a card has been reviewed is insufficient if the reviewer has not verified the underlying information or considered material changes.

Where the review identifies no change, the organization should retain evidence of the review decision and its scope. Where changes are identified, the card should be updated through the controlled change process.

### 65. Monitoring Model Card Completeness and Currency

Model card governance should include measurable indicators that reveal documentation gaps across the model inventory.

Useful metrics may include:

- Percentage of in-scope models with a model card.
- Percentage of cards with a named accountable owner.
- Percentage of cards approved before deployment.
- Percentage of cards reviewed within the required interval.
- Percentage of cards linked to supporting evaluation evidence.
- Number of overdue documentation updates.
- Number of unresolved mandatory-field deficiencies.
- Number of expired exceptions.
- Number of models deployed with outdated cards.
- Average time to update a card after a material change.
- Percentage of high-risk models with required enhanced documentation.

Metrics should be interpreted in context.

For example, a high percentage of completed cards does not establish that the content is accurate, the evaluations are adequate, or the controls are effective.

Organizations should combine completeness indicators with quality reviews, evidence sampling, control testing, and risk-based assurance.

Thresholds should be established according to the organization's risk appetite, policy requirements, and operational capabilities.

### 66. Using Model Cards in Internal Audit

Internal audit may use model cards to understand the documented characteristics, limitations, ownership, and governance status of selected models.

Audit procedures should determine whether model cards are complete, accurate, approved, current, and consistent with the underlying evidence.

Potential audit tests include:

- Sampling models from the AI inventory.
- Verifying that required cards exist.
- Confirming that the documented version matches the deployed version.
- Checking approval records.
- Tracing evaluation claims to supporting reports.
- Comparing limitations with the risk register.
- Reviewing exception approvals and expiry dates.
- Confirming that material changes triggered reassessment.
- Verifying that cards were reviewed according to policy.
- Checking whether user-facing restrictions are reflected in operational procedures.

Auditors should also assess whether the model card is being used appropriately in governance decisions.

A card may meet documentation requirements while important risks remain inadequately controlled. Audit should therefore distinguish documentation compliance from substantive model governance effectiveness.

Internal audit should maintain appropriate independence and avoid assuming responsibility for management's ownership of the model or approval of the associated risks.

### 67. Managing Model Card Evidence for External Assurance

External auditors, regulators, customers, and other authorized stakeholders may request model documentation to support assurance activities.

The organization should establish a controlled process for producing and disclosing the relevant information.

This process should verify:

- The requested model and version.
- The purpose and scope of the request.
- The approved card version.
- The authenticity and integrity of the record.
- The status of supporting evidence.
- Applicable confidentiality restrictions.
- Personal data and intellectual-property considerations.
- Authorization to disclose.
- Any necessary explanation of limitations.

The organization should avoid presenting a model card as proof of compliance with every requirement that may apply to the model.

The card may support an assurance conclusion, but the conclusion must be based on the relevant requirements, evidence, testing, and assessment criteria.

Where a card summarizes evidence maintained elsewhere, the organization should ensure that the referenced records remain available to authorized reviewers.

External disclosure should be governed so that an outdated draft, incomplete internal document, or unauthorized technical record is not mistakenly presented as the approved statement of the model's characteristics.

### 68. Maintaining Model Card Records and Retention

Model card records should be retained according to the organization's records management policy, legal obligations, contractual commitments, and operational needs.

The record may include the card itself, approval history, supporting evidence references, review outcomes, exceptions, and material change records.

Retention requirements should account for the possibility that the organization may later need to reconstruct which model version was used, what limitations were known, and which governance decisions were made at a particular time.

This can be important when investigating incidents, responding to complaints, reviewing historical decisions, or demonstrating the basis for a prior deployment approval.

The organization should distinguish between retaining a historical record and continuing to authorize its use.

Historical cards should be clearly marked as superseded, withdrawn, or otherwise inactive when appropriate.

Access controls should protect confidential technical details and sensitive supporting information. Retention and disposal procedures should also address applicable privacy and data minimization obligations.

### 69. Handling Incomplete or Unverifiable Model Card Claims

Model card information may be incomplete, ambiguous, or impossible to verify independently.

Examples include broad claims of fairness, security, robustness, safety, or accuracy without a clearly documented methodology or relevant evidence.

The organization should not treat an unsupported statement as a verified property of the model.

Instead, it should classify the statement according to its evidential status, such as:

- Independently verified.
- Internally tested.
- Reported by the provider.
- Supported by limited evidence.
- Based on an unverified assertion.
- Unknown or not assessed.

These labels should be defined consistently and should not create a false impression that every category has the same evidential strength.

Where a material claim cannot be verified, the organization should assess whether additional testing, contractual assurances, independent review, or use restrictions are necessary.

The card should communicate uncertainty clearly rather than converting an absence of evidence into a positive assurance statement.

For example, “No vulnerabilities were identified in the tests performed” is more precise than “The model is secure,” because the former identifies a bounded result rather than making an unrestricted claim.

### 70. Part 3 Core Principle

> **Model card governance must connect documentation ownership, risk assessment, enforceable controls, controlled change, periodic review, and independent assurance so that model information remains reliable throughout the model lifecycle.**

A model card is not a one-time deliverable. Its value depends on whether the organization maintains it as a controlled governance record, updates it when material conditions change, and uses its information to support actual decisions.

Effective governance requires clear accountability, evidence-based claims, risk-proportionate review, traceability to authoritative records, and a defined response when documentation is incomplete or no longer accurate.

The objective is to ensure that stakeholders can understand what a model is intended to do, what is known about its behavior, which limitations matter, what controls apply, and which decisions remain subject to human or organizational accountability.
