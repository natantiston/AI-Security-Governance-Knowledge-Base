# 19.18 — AI Audit Sampling and Finding Classification

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Audit Sampling and Evidence-Based Finding Classification

### 1. Purpose and Scope of AI Audit Sampling

AI audit sampling is the structured process of selecting a subset of AI-related records, controls, systems, transactions, decisions, or operational events for examination to determine whether governance requirements, security controls, regulatory obligations, and organizational policies are implemented effectively.

An AI audit may involve a large population of systems, datasets, model versions, training activities, automated decisions, monitoring events, third-party services, and governance records. Examining every item may be impractical because of population size, technical complexity, cost, time constraints, and the availability of evidence. Sampling provides a defensible method for obtaining sufficient and appropriate audit evidence while managing these constraints.

However, AI audit sampling is not simply a statistical exercise. AI systems introduce risks that conventional information technology audits may not fully address, including model drift, probabilistic outputs, training-data limitations, prompt injection, hallucinations, unauthorized tool execution, bias, and changes in behavior after deployment.

An effective AI audit sampling methodology must therefore consider both conventional control assurance and the distinctive characteristics of AI systems.

The methodology should establish:

- The audit objective and the specific assertion being tested.
- The population from which samples will be selected.
- The unit of analysis, such as an AI system, model version, transaction, dataset, or monitoring event.
- The sampling method and its justification.
- The sample size and associated limitations.
- The evidence required to evaluate each selected item.
- The criteria used to determine whether an item passes or fails.
- The process for classifying findings, escalating significant exceptions, and evaluating their broader implications.

For example, an audit of an organization's AI inventory might test whether identified AI systems have assigned business owners, documented purposes, risk classifications, and approved deployment status. An audit of a customer-facing generative AI application might instead examine access restrictions, prompt-injection defenses, output validation, human oversight, and incident escalation.

Both activities use sampling, but their populations, units of analysis, test procedures, and acceptance criteria differ.

The fundamental objective is to produce an evidence-based conclusion that is proportionate to the audit scope and the risks being examined.

### 2. AI Audit Sampling Versus AI System Testing

AI audit sampling and AI system testing are related but distinct assurance activities.

AI system testing evaluates technical or behavioral properties of an AI solution. AI audit sampling evaluates whether selected evidence demonstrates conformity with defined requirements, control expectations, and governance commitments.

| Dimension           | AI system testing                                                                        | AI audit sampling                                                                                 |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary objective   | Evaluate technical behavior, functionality, or performance                               | Obtain evidence about control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness                 |
| Typical population  | Inputs, prompts, outputs, model responses, test cases, or execution paths                | Controls, systems, records, decisions, changes, approvals, incidents, or transactions             |
| Typical methods     | Adversarial testing, benchmark testing, robustness testing, performance testing          | Random sampling, stratified sampling, risk-based selection, judgmental sampling                   |
| Evaluation criteria | Technical specifications, performance thresholds, safety requirements, expected behavior | Policies, control objectives, regulatory requirements, contractual obligations, audit criteria    |
| Typical evidence    | Test outputs, metrics, logs, benchmark results, model behavior                           | Records, approvals, configuration evidence, logs, interviews, test results, control documentation |
| Primary conclusion  | Whether specified technical or behavioral criteria were met                              | Whether the sampled evidence supports a conclusion about the audited control or process           |

These activities may be combined within a single audit.

For example, an auditor examining an AI model release process may select a sample of production releases and verify that each release has documented approval, completed security testing, an identified model version, and a rollback plan. The auditor may also review the technical test results for selected releases to determine whether required robustness and security criteria were satisfied.

The audit sampling establishes whether the release control operated as required. The technical testing evidence helps establish whether the control's underlying technical activities were adequate.

A critical distinction is that a successful technical test does not automatically demonstrate effective governance. A model may pass a benchmark while lacking documented ownership, appropriate authorization, traceable training-data provenance, or an approved risk assessment.

Similarly, complete documentation does not prove that an AI system behaves safely or that its controls work in practice.

A mature AI assurance program integrates both perspectives without confusing their purposes.

### 3. The AI Audit Population and Unit of Analysis

The audit population is the complete set of items from which an auditor selects samples for a particular test. Defining the population correctly is essential because the validity of a sampling conclusion depends on what was eligible for selection.

In AI governance, the population may consist of substantially different types of objects.

| Population type       | Example audit population                                             | Possible unit of analysis          |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| AI inventory          | All registered AI systems within a business unit                     | One AI system record               |
| Model lifecycle       | All production model releases during a defined period                | One model release                  |
| Access management     | All privileged accounts authorized to operate an AI platform         | One account or entitlement         |
| Human oversight       | All AI-assisted decisions subject to mandatory human review          | One decision record                |
| Data governance       | All datasets approved for training or evaluation                     | One dataset approval               |
| Third-party assurance | All externally provided AI services in scope                         | One service or supplier assessment |
| Incident management   | All AI-related incidents recorded during a defined period            | One incident record                |
| Monitoring            | All alerts generated by an AI monitoring system                      | One alert or alert case            |
| Change management     | All material changes to prompts, models, tools, or AI configurations | One change record                  |
| Output assurance      | All AI-generated outputs subject to a defined review requirement     | One output or review record        |

The auditor must specify the unit of analysis before determining sample size or interpreting results.

For example, selecting ten AI systems is not equivalent to selecting ten model releases. One AI system may have multiple models, several deployments, numerous versions, and multiple integrations. A sample of system records cannot automatically establish whether every release within those systems received the required approval.

Population boundaries should define the relevant organizational scope, time period, lifecycle stage, system status, and inclusion or exclusion criteria.

For example:

**Audit objective:** Determine whether production releases of a customer-facing generative AI service received documented security approval before deployment.

**Population:** All production releases of the service between 1 January and 30 June.

**Unit of analysis:** One production release.

**Required attributes:** Release identifier, model version, deployment date, security assessment, approval timestamp, approver identity, and applicable exception record.

**Exclusion criteria:** None for production releases within the stated period. Development experiments that never entered production are outside the defined population.

This definition allows the auditor to identify the records that should exist, reconcile the population against independent deployment evidence, and select samples using a consistent methodology.

Population completeness is particularly important in AI audits because governance inventories may be incomplete. An organization may maintain an AI inventory that excludes embedded AI functionality, shadow AI applications, externally hosted models, or employee-developed automations.

If the auditor samples only from an incomplete inventory, the sample may accurately represent the inventory while failing to represent the organization's actual AI environment.

Consequently, population validation should precede sample selection whenever population completeness is material to the audit objective.

### 4. Establishing the Audit Objective and Test Criteria

Sampling cannot produce a meaningful assurance conclusion unless the auditor knows what the sample is intended to demonstrate.

The audit objective defines the question being answered. The test criteria define the conditions against which the selected evidence will be evaluated.

A broad objective such as "audit AI governance" is insufficient for a defensible sampling procedure. It does not specify which governance requirements are being examined, which population is relevant, or what constitutes a deviation.

A stronger objective is:

"Determine whether production AI systems classified as high risk have an approved risk assessment, a designated accountable owner, documented human oversight arrangements, and evidence of authorization before deployment."

This objective identifies several testable control requirements. The auditor can determine whether the population includes all relevant high-risk systems, select a sample, and test each required attribute.

Test criteria may originate from:

- Internal AI governance policies and standards.
- Approved risk management procedures.
- Security and privacy control frameworks adopted by the organization.
- Applicable legal and regulatory obligations.
- Customer or supplier contractual requirements.
- Approved system specifications and technical baselines.
- Documented risk treatment decisions.
- Management-approved control objectives.

The auditor should distinguish mandatory criteria from recommended practices. A legal obligation, an internal policy requirement, and an optional maturity enhancement do not necessarily carry the same compliance implications.

For each test, the auditor should define the expected evidence and the failure condition before examining the sample. This reduces the risk of inconsistent decisions, selective interpretation, and criteria being changed to accommodate observed results.

A test specification may include the following fields:

| Field                 | Description                                                          |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Audit objective       | The assurance question the test addresses                            |
| Control objective     | The intended risk-reduction outcome                                  |
| Audit criterion       | The requirement against which evidence is evaluated                  |
| Population            | The complete set of eligible items                                   |
| Sampling unit         | The individual item selected for examination                         |
| Sampling method       | The approach used to select items                                    |
| Sample size           | The number of selected items                                         |
| Evidence requirements | Records, logs, configurations, approvals, or other evidence required |
| Pass condition        | The evidence that demonstrates conformity                            |
| Fail condition        | The deviation that constitutes an exception                          |
| Evaluation period     | The period covered by the test                                       |
| Finding escalation    | The conditions requiring further investigation or reporting          |

Consider a requirement that every production deployment of a high-risk AI system must receive formal approval before release.

The auditor should not treat an approval recorded after deployment as equivalent to approval before deployment. The criterion concerns both authorization and timing.

Likewise, a generic approval covering an earlier model version may not demonstrate authorization for a materially changed release.

Precise criteria allow the auditor to distinguish genuine control failures from documentation gaps, timing differences, scope misunderstandings, and irrelevant evidence.

### 5. Risk-Based Sampling in AI Audits

Risk-based sampling prioritizes audit items according to the likelihood and potential impact of control failure, rather than treating every item as equally important.

This approach is particularly relevant to AI environments because the population may contain systems with substantially different risk profiles.

A low-impact internal summarization tool and an AI system supporting consequential eligibility decisions may operate on the same platform but create very different legal, operational, reputational, and human impacts.

Risk-based sampling may consider:

- AI system risk classification.
- Potential impact on individuals or protected interests.
- Degree of automation and decision-making authority.
- Access to sensitive or confidential information.
- External exposure and attack surface.
- Privileged access to models, tools, or data.
- Use of autonomous or agentic capabilities.
- Reliance on third-party models and services.
- History of incidents, control failures, or audit findings.
- Frequency and materiality of system changes.
- Model drift or evidence of deteriorating performance.
- Regulatory scrutiny and contractual commitments.
- Known weaknesses in monitoring or human oversight.

The auditor should document how these factors influence sample selection. Risk-based sampling should not become an undocumented process in which the auditor selects whichever records appear most interesting.

For example, an organization operates 120 AI applications. Ten are classified as high risk, 30 as moderate risk, and 80 as low risk. An audit of release governance may examine all ten high-risk applications and select additional applications from the moderate- and low-risk populations.

This approach directs greater attention to systems with higher potential consequences while retaining some coverage of the broader environment.

The resulting sample is appropriate for a risk-focused audit objective, but it is not automatically representative of all 120 applications for statistical estimation. Because selection probabilities differ, the auditor must avoid treating the results as if every application had an equal chance of selection.

Risk-based sampling is therefore particularly useful for identifying significant control weaknesses, examining high-impact scenarios, and allocating limited audit resources. It must be distinguished from probability sampling when the intended conclusion depends on statistical representativeness.

### 6. Random Sampling and Statistical Representativeness

Random sampling selects items using a probability-based method in which eligible population items have known selection probabilities. Depending on the design, this may involve simple random sampling, systematic sampling, stratified probability sampling, or other probability-based approaches.

Its principal advantage is that it supports more defensible inferences about a defined population when the sample design, sample size, and statistical assumptions are appropriate.

For example, an auditor may randomly select 60 records from a complete population of 600 AI-assisted customer decisions to examine whether mandatory human review was documented.

If the selection is genuinely random and the population is complete, the sample can provide evidence about the frequency of missing review records in the population. The precision of the estimate depends on factors such as sample size, population characteristics, the observed exception rate, and the confidence level used.

However, statistical representativeness does not mean that every important risk will be detected. A random sample may omit a rare but severe failure, particularly when the affected population is small.

Suppose an AI platform contains 2,000 ordinary user interactions and five privileged tool-execution events. A random sample of ordinary interactions may provide useful information about general output controls while providing little or no evidence about privileged tool execution.

A separate targeted test of privileged events may therefore be necessary.

Auditors should also distinguish between sampling error and non-sampling error.

**Sampling error** arises because a subset of the population is examined rather than the entire population.

**Non-sampling error** arises from other weaknesses, such as incomplete populations, inaccurate records, inappropriate test criteria, unreliable evidence, inconsistent evaluation, or incorrect interpretation of results.

A large sample does not compensate for an incomplete population or a poorly designed test.

Random sampling is especially useful when the audit objective requires an estimate of control deviation across a defined population. It is less suitable as the sole method for investigating rare, severe, emerging, or highly targeted AI risks.

### 7. Stratified Sampling for Heterogeneous AI Environments

Stratified sampling divides a population into meaningful subgroups, called strata, before selecting samples from each subgroup.

AI environments frequently contain heterogeneous populations. Systems may differ by risk classification, business function, model type, deployment environment, data sensitivity, autonomy, supplier, or regulatory treatment.

Sampling across these differences can improve audit coverage and prevent a large, low-risk population from overwhelming smaller, higher-risk groups.

Common AI audit strata include:

| Stratification dimension | Example strata                                              | Assurance purpose                                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk classification      | High, moderate, low                                         | Ensure differentiated coverage by risk                      |
| Deployment model         | Cloud-hosted, on-premises, externally managed               | Examine different operating and control environments        |
| AI capability            | Predictive models, generative AI, AI agents                 | Address different technical risk profiles                   |
| Business function        | Human resources, finance, customer service, operations      | Cover distinct business processes                           |
| Data sensitivity         | Public, internal, confidential, sensitive personal data     | Evaluate controls according to data exposure                |
| Autonomy                 | Advisory, human-approved action, autonomous execution       | Focus on delegated decision and action authority            |
| Lifecycle stage          | Development, validation, production, retirement             | Test lifecycle-specific control requirements                |
| Supplier dependency      | Internally developed, third-party model, managed AI service | Examine different accountability and assurance arrangements |

The choice of strata must be aligned with the audit objective. Excessive stratification can create many small groups that are difficult to sample and analyze. Insufficient stratification may conceal meaningful differences between control environments.

Two common allocation approaches are proportionate and disproportionate allocation.

**Proportionate allocation** selects samples in proportion to each stratum's share of the population. It can support population-wide estimation when combined with an appropriate probability-based design and analysis.

**Disproportionate allocation** deliberately selects more items from particular strata than their population share would imply. This is useful when smaller groups carry greater risk or require minimum audit coverage. Population-wide estimates then require appropriate weighting and analysis where applicable.

For example, an organization has 100 AI systems: 10 high-risk, 30 moderate-risk, and 60 low-risk. A sample of 20 might include all 10 high-risk systems, six moderate-risk systems, and four low-risk systems.

This design provides extensive high-risk coverage, but it does not mirror the population's composition. The auditor should not calculate an unweighted overall exception rate and assume that it represents the entire population.

Instead, the auditor should report the sampling design clearly and distinguish conclusions about each stratum from any overall population estimate.

Stratified sampling is particularly valuable when an organization needs to demonstrate that AI assurance activities cover materially different risk classes and deployment contexts.

### 8. Judgmental and Targeted Sampling for Emerging AI Risks

Judgmental sampling selects items based on the auditor's professional judgment, informed by known risks, unusual characteristics, previous findings, or the need to investigate a specific control concern.

Targeted sampling is often used to examine high-risk or unusual events that may be poorly represented in a random sample.

Examples include:

- Model releases following significant changes to training data or architecture.
- AI systems recently involved in security or privacy incidents.
- Applications using external tools or privileged APIs.
- Systems with repeated model drift alerts.
- AI services processing highly sensitive information.
- Deployments with overdue risk assessments.
- Systems operating under approved control exceptions.
- Agentic workflows capable of making changes or initiating transactions.
- Model versions introduced shortly before a regulatory or contractual deadline.
- Applications with prior findings involving inadequate human oversight.

Targeted sampling can be essential when the objective is to determine whether a known risk is adequately controlled.

For example, an AI agent can access internal documents and create service tickets. A general sample of user interactions may fail to exercise the agent's privileged actions. The auditor may deliberately select execution records involving document retrieval, permission boundaries, external API calls, and attempted actions outside the agent's authorized scope.

The auditor can then examine whether the agent's permissions were appropriately restricted, whether actions were logged, whether approval gates operated correctly, and whether unauthorized attempts were blocked and escalated.

The limitation is that judgmental samples do not ordinarily support statistical claims about the prevalence of deviations throughout the entire population. Selection is driven by risk or professional judgment rather than known probability-based selection.

Auditors should document the rationale for each targeted item and label conclusions accordingly.

Targeted sampling should complement, rather than automatically replace, other sampling methods. A balanced methodology may combine probability-based selection for broader control assurance with targeted testing for high-impact and emerging risks.

### 9. Combining Sampling Methods Within an AI Audit

A single AI audit may require several sampling methods because different audit objectives demand different types of evidence.

For example, an audit of an enterprise generative AI platform may examine:

1. **Inventory completeness:** Reconcile registered applications against deployment records, procurement information, platform logs, and other relevant sources.
2. **Risk classification:** Use stratified sampling to examine systems across risk categories.
3. **Release approval:** Select production releases using probability-based sampling where population-wide operating-effectiveness conclusions are required.
4. **Privileged tool use:** Use targeted sampling to examine high-impact agent actions.
5. **Incident handling:** Examine significant AI incidents and select additional incident records to assess the operation of incident-management controls.
6. **Monitoring:** Sample alerts and independently examine selected periods of telemetry to assess detection and escalation.
7. **Exceptions:** Examine approved deviations and overdue remediation items because these represent known departures from standard control requirements.

These activities should not be combined into one undifferentiated sample. Each test has its own population, selection rationale, criteria, and limitations.

A defensible audit workpaper should identify which conclusions arise from probability-based sampling, which arise from risk-based or judgmental examination, and which arise from full-population procedures such as reconciliations or automated analysis.

The auditor should also avoid double-counting evidence. One record may support multiple control tests, but the fact that the record was examined once does not mean every relevant control passed.

For example, a model release record may contain a risk assessment and security approval. The risk assessment may satisfy a documentation criterion while the approval timestamp reveals that authorization occurred after deployment. Each control must be evaluated against its own requirements.

The combined methodology should maximize useful assurance while preserving traceability between audit objectives, selected evidence, observed exceptions, and reported conclusions.

### 10. Sample Size, Assurance Objectives, and Sampling Limitations

Sample size determines how much of a population is examined, but a larger sample does not automatically produce a stronger audit conclusion. Sample size must be appropriate to the objective, population, sampling design, expected deviation rate, tolerable deviation rate, and desired level of assurance.

For probability-based attribute testing, auditors may consider:

- **Population size:** The number of eligible records or units.
- **Expected deviation rate:** The rate of control failures anticipated before testing.
- **Tolerable deviation rate:** The maximum deviation rate that remains acceptable for the audit objective.
- **Confidence level:** The desired statistical confidence associated with the inference.
- **Selection method:** Whether the sample is random, stratified, systematic, or otherwise probability-based.
- **Population variability:** The extent to which control performance may differ across the population.
- **Risk of reliance:** The risk that the auditor concludes a control is sufficiently effective when it is not.
- **Audit consequences:** The severity of an incorrect conclusion and the need for additional procedures.

The tolerable deviation rate should reflect the nature of the control. For a mandatory pre-deployment approval control covering high-risk AI systems, the expected tolerance for missing approval may be zero for individual in-scope releases, even though statistical sampling can never establish with absolute certainty that no deviation exists elsewhere in the population.

This distinction matters: a control may have a zero-tolerance requirement while the auditor still uses sampling to evaluate operating effectiveness. The audit report must describe the residual uncertainty rather than implying that the sample proves universal compliance.

Where a sample is intended to support a statistical estimate, the auditor should use a defensible sampling plan and, where appropriate, a suitable statistical method or sample-size calculation. Convenience-based rules such as selecting a fixed number of records without considering the objective do not establish statistical adequacy.

Where the objective is to examine every high-risk system or every critical release, full-population testing may be more appropriate than sampling, particularly when the population is small and the consequences of an undetected exception are substantial.

The sample size should also be reconsidered when unexpected deviations occur. A material exception may indicate that the original assumptions about control performance were incorrect or that the affected population is not homogeneous.

The auditor may need to expand the sample, test additional strata, investigate the cause, or perform full-population analysis using available records.

Sample size is therefore part of an assurance design, not an isolated numerical target.

### 11. Defining and Recording Audit Exceptions

An audit exception is a condition in which the evidence for a selected item does not satisfy the applicable test criterion.

Exceptions may involve missing documentation, unauthorized access, late approval, inadequate technical safeguards, incomplete monitoring, ineffective escalation, or evidence that a required control did not operate.

The auditor should record exceptions consistently and preserve enough detail to support independent review.

A well-documented exception generally identifies:

- The sample identifier and population reference.
- The applicable control and audit criterion.
- The evidence examined.
- The observed condition.
- The nature and extent of the deviation.
- The date or period in which the deviation occurred.
- The potential or actual risk exposure.
- The explanation provided by the control owner, where obtained.
- Any compensating control or mitigating factor.
- The need for additional testing or escalation.

For example:

**Control criterion:** Every production release of a high-risk AI system must receive documented security approval before deployment.

**Sample item:** Release R-104, model version 3.2.

**Evidence examined:** Deployment logs, change record, security assessment, and approval workflow.

**Observed condition:** The release entered production at 09:00. The required security approval was recorded at 11:30 on the same day.

**Exception:** The required pre-deployment approval was not obtained before production deployment.

**Potential risk:** The system operated before the prescribed security authorization process was completed.

**Additional investigation:** Determine whether required testing had been completed before deployment, whether the late approval was an administrative recording delay or an actual control bypass, and whether other releases followed the same process.

This exception should not be dismissed simply because approval was obtained later. Nor should the auditor automatically conclude that the system was insecure solely because the approval timestamp was late. The control deviation is established by the evidence; the actual security impact requires separate evaluation.

An exception should also be distinguished from an evidence limitation. If the auditor cannot obtain a record, the correct conclusion depends on the control requirement and the reliability of alternative evidence. Missing evidence may itself constitute a control failure when record retention or documented approval is mandatory. In other cases, the auditor may be unable to determine whether the control operated.

These distinctions prevent audit reports from conflating confirmed nonconformity, insufficient evidence, and proven harm.

### 12. From Audit Exception to Formal Finding

Not every exception necessarily becomes a standalone audit finding. The auditor must evaluate the exception in context, considering its significance, root cause, recurrence, control implications, and potential impact.

A finding is a documented conclusion that a control, process, system, or governance arrangement does not meet an applicable requirement or does not adequately address an identified risk.

The distinction between an exception and a finding is important.

An **exception** is an observed deviation in a particular sample item.

A **finding** is an evaluated issue that warrants formal reporting, corrective action, management attention, or another defined response under the organization's audit methodology.

Several exceptions may be grouped into one finding when they arise from the same underlying control weakness. For example, six releases with late security approvals may indicate a systemic weakness in release governance rather than six unrelated problems.

Conversely, a single exception may warrant a significant finding when it involves a critical control, a severe potential consequence, a regulatory breach, or an unauthorized high-impact AI action.

Finding evaluation should consider at least five dimensions:

1. **Control significance:** How important is the affected control to the AI system's risk treatment?
2. **Impact:** What actual or reasonably foreseeable consequences could result?
3. **Extent:** How many systems, transactions, users, or processes may be affected?
4. **Recurrence:** Is the condition isolated, repeated, or indicative of a systemic weakness?
5. **Governance and compliance implications:** Does the condition affect a legal obligation, contractual commitment, approved risk treatment, or management representation?

The auditor should distinguish observed facts from inferred causes and potential consequences. A finding should not claim that harm occurred when the evidence establishes only exposure to risk.

For example, if an AI system processed personal data without the documented approval required by internal policy, the auditor can report the control deviation. Whether the event also constitutes a breach of a particular legal obligation requires assessment against the applicable legal requirements and the facts of the processing activity.

Formal findings should be traceable to the original evidence and the criteria used to evaluate it. The rationale for combining exceptions, escalating an isolated issue, or determining that an exception does not warrant a formal finding should be documented.

### 13. Core Principle of Part 1

AI audit sampling must begin with a clearly defined audit objective, a validated population, an explicit unit of analysis, and predetermined evaluation criteria. Sampling methods must match the assurance question: probability-based methods support appropriately qualified population inferences, while risk-based and judgmental methods direct attention toward significant or emerging AI risks. Exceptions must be recorded with traceable evidence and evaluated before they become formal findings. The credibility of AI audit assurance depends not on sample size alone, but on the quality of the population definition, selection methodology, evidence, and conclusions drawn from the results.

## Part 2 — AI Audit Sampling Techniques, Test Execution, and Evidence Evaluation

### 14. Designing a Defensible AI Audit Sampling Plan

An AI audit sampling plan translates the audit objective into a repeatable procedure for selecting items, examining evidence, evaluating control performance, and determining whether additional investigation is necessary.

The plan should be designed before substantive testing begins. This reduces the risk of inconsistent sample selection, retrospective changes to acceptance criteria, and selective examination of records that support a preferred conclusion.

A defensible sampling plan should address the following elements:

| Element            | Required specification                                        | AI audit example                                                                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Audit objective    | The assurance question being addressed                        | Determine whether high-risk AI releases received approval before deployment             |
| Scope              | Organizational units, systems, lifecycle stages, and period   | Production AI releases from January through June                                        |
| Population         | Complete set of eligible items                                | All production release records within the audit period                                  |
| Population source  | System or record used to identify eligible items              | Deployment platform, change-management system, and release register                     |
| Sampling unit      | Individual item selected for testing                          | One production release                                                                  |
| Sampling method    | Selection methodology and rationale                           | Stratified random sampling by risk classification                                       |
| Sample size        | Number of items selected and justification                    | Based on the assurance objective and applicable sampling assumptions                    |
| Test attributes    | Specific control requirements evaluated                       | Approval timing, approver authorization, and evidence of completed security testing     |
| Evidence sources   | Records used to evaluate each attribute                       | Deployment logs, approval workflows, test reports, and configuration records            |
| Exception criteria | Conditions constituting deviations                            | Approval recorded after deployment or absence of required testing evidence              |
| Escalation rules   | Conditions requiring expanded testing or immediate escalation | Unauthorized deployment of a high-impact AI system                                      |
| Reporting approach | How results and limitations will be communicated              | Separate reporting of sample deviations, systemic weaknesses, and statistical estimates |

The sampling plan should also identify who is responsible for preparing the population, selecting the sample, conducting the tests, reviewing the results, and approving the audit conclusions.

Where independence is important, the person who operates a control should not be the sole individual responsible for determining whether that control operated effectively.

For complex AI systems, the plan may require input from security engineering, data governance, privacy, model risk management, legal, and business process owners. Their technical expertise can help the auditor identify appropriate evidence and test procedures, but management explanations should not replace independent verification.

The plan must remain sufficiently stable to support consistent testing. Changes may be necessary when the population is found to be incomplete, the original assumptions prove incorrect, or unexpected exceptions indicate a broader problem. Any material change should be documented with its rationale and effect on the audit conclusions.

### 15. Validating Population Completeness and Accuracy

A sampling exercise is only as reliable as the population from which the sample is drawn. If relevant AI systems, model releases, decisions, or events are missing, the auditor may obtain apparently satisfactory results while overlooking the very items most likely to contain control weaknesses.

Population validation is therefore a separate and important audit procedure.

For an AI system inventory, validation may involve comparing the inventory against:

- Cloud AI service deployments and subscriptions.
- Model-serving platforms and model registries.
- Procurement records and third-party service registers.
- Software asset inventories and application catalogs.
- Identity and access management records.
- Enterprise data platforms and approved dataset catalogs.
- API gateways, integration catalogs, and service accounts.
- Security monitoring records and AI platform telemetry.
- Business unit declarations and approved AI use cases.
- Change-management and production deployment records.

No single source is necessarily complete. An organization may use an external generative AI service without deploying a model in its own cloud environment. An employee-built workflow may invoke an AI API without appearing in the formal application inventory.

The auditor should select corroborating sources appropriate to the population being validated and investigate material discrepancies.

For example, suppose the AI inventory contains 75 systems, while the cloud deployment register identifies 82 AI-enabled workloads. The difference does not automatically establish that seven systems are unregistered AI applications. Some workloads may be duplicates, retired resources, or components of existing systems. However, the discrepancy requires reconciliation before the inventory can be treated as a reliable sampling frame.

Population validation should document:

1. The sources used to construct the population.
2. The period covered by each source.
3. The reconciliation procedure performed.
4. Identified duplicates, exclusions, and omissions.
5. The rationale for resolving discrepancies.
6. The residual limitations affecting population completeness.

Auditors should also consider whether the population changes during the audit. New deployments, model releases, supplier changes, and changes to AI functionality may introduce items that were not present when the original population was extracted.

If the audit objective concerns all deployments during a defined period, the population should include every eligible deployment in that period, not merely the versions active on the date of the audit.

Population completeness is especially important when the control being tested is intended to prevent unauthorized AI use. An inventory-based sample cannot establish that unauthorized systems are absent unless the inventory has been reconciled against sufficiently independent evidence.

### 16. Random Sample Selection and Reproducibility

When probability-based sampling is appropriate, the selection procedure should be reproducible and protected against manipulation.

A defensible process begins with a finalized population containing unique identifiers for every eligible item. The auditor should record the population extraction date, relevant filters, record count, source system, and any transformations applied to the data.

The auditor then applies a documented random selection procedure. Depending on the methodology, this may use a statistical sampling tool, a controlled random-number generator, or another suitable method.

The workpaper should preserve enough information for an independent reviewer to understand how the selected items were obtained.

Useful documentation includes:

- The original population or a controlled reference to it.
- A unique identifier for every population item.
- The selection method and relevant parameters.
- The random seed or equivalent reproducibility information, where applicable.
- The selected sample identifiers.
- Any substitutions, exclusions, or replacement selections.
- The reason for each change to the original selection.

Auditors should avoid replacing inconvenient or inaccessible sample items with easier alternatives without documenting and evaluating the consequences. Such substitution can introduce selection bias, particularly when missing records or inaccessible systems are associated with weaker controls.

If a selected record cannot be obtained, the auditor should investigate the reason. The missing record may indicate a record-retention failure, a population-definition issue, an access limitation, or an evidence availability problem. It should not automatically be treated as an ordinary sampling inconvenience.

For example, an auditor randomly selects 40 production releases and discovers that documentation is unavailable for three. The auditor should not simply select three additional releases and ignore the missing records. The missing documentation may itself constitute an exception if the organization was required to retain the relevant evidence.

Additional selections may still be appropriate, but they should supplement rather than erase the original exceptions.

Reproducibility is especially valuable when audit findings are challenged by management, reviewed by an independent assurance function, or examined by a regulator.

### 17. Attribute Sampling for AI Control Testing

Attribute sampling evaluates whether a defined characteristic or control requirement is present or absent for each selected item.

It is particularly useful when testing binary or clearly defined control attributes, such as whether a required approval exists, whether an access review was completed, or whether a model release passed a mandatory security gate.

Common AI audit attributes include:

- An accountable AI system owner is recorded.
- A risk assessment was completed before deployment.
- A model version is uniquely identifiable.
- Training-data provenance is documented where required.
- Security testing was completed before production release.
- Required human oversight is documented and operational.
- Access to model administration functions is restricted.
- Material changes received the required authorization.
- Monitoring alerts are reviewed within defined service levels.
- AI incidents are recorded and escalated according to policy.
- Supplier assurance requirements are satisfied.
- Retirement and deletion activities are documented where applicable.

Attribute sampling requires an explicit pass/fail rule for every tested attribute.

Suppose an auditor selects 30 production releases and evaluates whether each had documented pre-deployment approval. A release passes when reliable evidence demonstrates that the authorized approver granted approval before deployment. It fails when the approval was absent, unauthorized, or obtained after deployment.

The auditor should distinguish a missing approval from an inaccessible approval record. If the control explicitly requires recorded approval, the missing record may establish a deviation. If the evidence is temporarily unavailable because of a technical access issue, the auditor may need further procedures before reaching a conclusion.

A single sample item may contain several attributes. For example, a release may have a valid approval but lack evidence of required adversarial testing. The approval attribute can pass while the testing attribute fails.

These outcomes should be recorded separately rather than assigning one undifferentiated pass or fail to the entire release.

Attribute sampling is useful for evaluating control operation, but the auditor must not interpret a sample's observed deviation rate as a precise estimate of population-wide performance unless the sampling design and analysis support that inference.

### 18. Variables Sampling and Quantitative AI Assurance

Variables sampling examines numerical measurements rather than only the presence or absence of a control attribute.

In conventional audits, variables sampling may be used to estimate monetary values or quantities. In AI assurance, quantitative examination may involve measures such as review delays, processing volumes, error rates, remediation times, or monitoring performance.

Examples include:

- Average time between an AI incident and its escalation.
- Percentage of AI alerts investigated within the required service level.
- Distribution of model inference latency.
- Frequency of human-review overrides.
- Rate of missing or incomplete monitoring records.
- Number of days that high-risk findings remain overdue.
- Measured error rates across defined AI evaluation datasets.
- Frequency of unauthorized or failed tool-execution attempts.
- Percentage of production releases with delayed security approvals.

The auditor should distinguish between sampling used to estimate a population parameter and sampling used to test compliance with a threshold.

For example, an organization requires 95% of high-priority AI security alerts to be investigated within four hours. An audit may select alert records and determine whether each investigation met the four-hour requirement. This is an attribute test if each alert is classified as compliant or noncompliant.

Alternatively, the auditor may estimate the overall percentage of alerts investigated within four hours. This is a quantitative estimation objective, even if the underlying data also supports a binary classification.

AI performance metrics require particular care because their meaning depends on the measurement design.

An observed error rate is not interpretable without understanding the evaluation dataset, sampling process, label quality, class distribution, measurement period, and relevant operating conditions.

Similarly, an average response time may conceal extreme delays affecting a small but important subset of users.

Auditors should therefore consider distributions, outliers, subgroup differences, and measurement limitations rather than relying exclusively on averages.

Where model performance or outcome quality is part of the audit, technical specialists may be needed to assess whether the metric is valid, whether the evaluation data is representative of intended use, and whether the reported results support the claimed conclusion.

Quantitative assurance should preserve the distinction between operational performance, control effectiveness, and regulatory conformity. A favorable metric does not, by itself, establish that the relevant governance requirements have been satisfied.

### 19. Testing Control Design and Operating Effectiveness

An AI control may be documented but poorly designed, or it may be appropriately designed but inconsistently operated. These are different conditions and should be evaluated separately.

**Design effectiveness** concerns whether the control, if implemented as specified, is capable of addressing the identified risk or achieving the intended control objective.

**Operating effectiveness** concerns whether the control actually operated as required during the period under review.

Consider a control requiring human approval before an AI agent executes a high-impact transaction.

A design-effectiveness assessment may examine whether:

- The approval requirement covers all relevant high-impact actions.
- The approval authority is appropriately assigned.
- The workflow prevents execution before approval.
- The approver receives sufficient information to make an informed decision.
- The approval cannot be bypassed through an alternative API or execution path.
- The system records the decision and the identity of the approver.

An operating-effectiveness assessment may select transaction records and test whether approval occurred before each execution, whether the approver was authorized, and whether the recorded action matches the approved action.

A control may fail either assessment independently.

For example, a workflow may consistently require approval for transactions above a specified value, but the threshold may be too high to address the actual risk. The control may operate exactly as configured while remaining inadequately designed.

Conversely, the workflow may have a sound design, but a configuration error may allow transactions to execute without approval.

Sampling is generally more directly suited to examining operating effectiveness across multiple instances. Design effectiveness often requires inspection of control specifications, architecture, configuration, risk analysis, and walkthroughs, although samples of different system configurations may be useful where implementations vary.

For AI systems, the auditor should also examine whether the control design remains appropriate after material changes to the model, prompts, tools, data flows, permissions, or deployment context.

A control that was adequate for a read-only assistant may be inadequate after the system receives permission to modify records or initiate external transactions.

The audit conclusion should state whether the weakness concerns control design, control operation, or both.

### 20. Evidence Reliability and Corroboration

AI audit conclusions depend on the reliability, relevance, and sufficiency of the evidence examined.

Different evidence sources provide different levels of assurance. A policy document may establish what the organization intended to require, but it does not prove that the requirement operated. An interview may explain a process, but it does not independently demonstrate that every relevant transaction followed that process.

Common AI audit evidence sources include:

- Approved policies and standards.
- Risk assessments and treatment plans.
- Model cards, system cards, and model documentation.
- Data lineage and dataset approval records.
- Model registries and version histories.
- Change-management records and release approvals.
- Identity, access, and privilege records.
- Configuration exports and infrastructure-as-code repositories.
- Security testing and red-team reports.
- Monitoring dashboards and machine-generated logs.
- Incident records and investigation reports.
- Human-review records and override logs.
- Supplier assessments and contractual commitments.
- Meeting minutes and management decisions.
- Interviews and process walkthroughs.

The auditor should consider who created the evidence, how it was generated, whether it can be altered, whether it covers the relevant period, and whether it directly addresses the criterion being tested.

System-generated records may provide strong evidence of a recorded event, but their reliability depends on logging configuration, access controls, clock synchronization, retention, and the integrity of the underlying system.

A screenshot of an approval dashboard may be less persuasive than an authoritative workflow record containing the approval identity and timestamp. A model evaluation report may be insufficient if the auditor cannot establish which model version was tested or whether the tested version matches the deployed version.

Corroboration involves comparing evidence from different sources to determine whether the records agree.

For example, the auditor may compare a change-management approval, a model registry entry, a deployment log, and a production configuration record. Agreement among these sources strengthens the conclusion that the approved model version was deployed at the recorded time.

Discrepancies require investigation. A model registry may identify version 4.1 as approved, while deployment logs show that version 4.2 entered production. The auditor should determine whether version 4.2 was separately authorized or whether the deployment bypassed the approved release process.

Evidence quality is particularly important in AI audits because the underlying system may produce outputs that appear authoritative without being independently verified.

The auditor should not treat an AI-generated explanation, summary, or compliance report as conclusive evidence merely because it is detailed or internally consistent. Claims about system behavior and control operation should be corroborated against appropriate source records and independently testable evidence.

### 21. Evaluating AI-Specific Evidence Challenges

AI systems introduce evidence challenges that can complicate conventional audit procedures.

**Model and configuration versioning:** An evaluation result may relate to a different model version, prompt template, retrieval configuration, or tool permission set from the one deployed. The auditor should establish the identity and configuration of the relevant system at the time of the event.

**Probabilistic behavior:** Identical prompts may produce different outputs. A single successful test may not demonstrate that a failure mode is absent. The auditor should examine whether the evaluation method is appropriate to the behavior being assessed and whether repeated trials or broader test coverage are necessary.

**Data provenance:** A dataset approval record may not establish that the deployed model was trained on the approved dataset. Where relevant, the auditor should examine lineage, processing records, training configurations, and links between datasets and model artifacts.

**Human oversight:** A recorded approval may not demonstrate meaningful human review. The auditor may need to examine the information presented to the reviewer, the available decision time, override capabilities, escalation arrangements, and evidence of the actual decision.

**Monitoring coverage:** A monitoring dashboard may show no alerts because no adverse event occurred, because detection rules were ineffective, or because relevant telemetry was not collected. The auditor should examine the monitoring design, event coverage, alert-generation logic, and evidence that the monitoring pipeline operated correctly.

**Third-party opacity:** An external model provider may not disclose all training data, model internals, or evaluation details. The auditor should determine which assurance claims can be supported by contractual evidence, independent assessments, available technical documentation, and testing of the organization's own integration and use controls.

**Non-deterministic and context-dependent behavior:** AI output quality may vary with language, user group, task complexity, context length, or environmental conditions. Evaluation samples should reflect the relevant intended-use conditions rather than relying solely on a convenient or homogeneous test set.

**Evidence retention:** Some AI platforms retain detailed interaction logs only for limited periods. If retention is insufficient to support the organization's audit, incident, or regulatory obligations, the auditor should evaluate the resulting assurance limitation and any underlying retention-control deficiency.

These challenges do not make AI systems unauditable. They require more careful alignment between the assurance question, the evidence source, the system configuration, and the conclusion.

Where the available evidence is insufficient to support a definitive conclusion, the auditor should document the limitation rather than infer successful control operation from the absence of visible failures.

### 22. Expanding Samples After Unexpected Exceptions

Unexpected exceptions may indicate that the original sampling assumptions no longer provide an adequate basis for the audit conclusion.

When a deviation is identified, the auditor should determine whether it is isolated, recurring, concentrated in a specific subgroup, or indicative of a broader control failure.

The appropriate response depends on the nature and severity of the exception.

Possible procedures include:

- Examining additional items from the same population.
- Expanding testing within the affected risk stratum.
- Reviewing all items associated with a particular model version or deployment process.
- Testing related controls that may share the same underlying weakness.
- Examining the relevant configuration or workflow directly.
- Reconstructing the sequence of events using independent logs.
- Investigating whether the exception occurred before or after a material system change.
- Performing full-population analysis where reliable records are available.
- Escalating immediately when the exception indicates a potentially severe or active risk.

For example, an auditor selects 25 production releases and discovers that four lacked documented pre-deployment approval. This result may indicate that the release approval control is not operating consistently.

The auditor should investigate whether the four releases share a common deployment pipeline, business unit, approver, or period. The investigation may reveal that a recent pipeline change allowed deployment without completing the required approval gate.

In that situation, selecting a few additional unrelated releases may not be sufficient. The auditor may need to examine all releases processed through the affected pipeline after the change, establish the period of exposure, and determine whether the same weakness affected other AI applications.

The auditor should distinguish sample expansion from population-wide remediation. Expanding the audit sample helps determine the extent and nature of the problem. Corrective action addresses the underlying weakness and may require changes to systems, procedures, responsibilities, or monitoring.

An unexpected exception does not automatically require a larger sample in every case. A single severe finding may require immediate escalation and investigation regardless of whether additional samples are selected. Conversely, a minor documentation discrepancy may warrant clarification and limited additional testing.

The decision should be risk-based, documented, and consistent with the audit methodology.

### 23. Core Principle of Part 2

A defensible AI audit sampling plan depends on a complete and validated population, reproducible selection procedures, explicit pass/fail criteria, and evidence appropriate to the control being tested. Attribute and quantitative methods must be matched to their intended assurance objectives, while design effectiveness and operating effectiveness must be assessed separately. AI-specific challenges—including versioning, probabilistic behavior, data lineage, human oversight, monitoring coverage, and third-party opacity—require corroboration and careful qualification of conclusions. Unexpected exceptions should trigger proportionate investigation, sample expansion, or broader analysis when warranted by the evidence and risk.

## Part 3 — AI Audit Finding Classification, Severity Assessment, and Root-Cause Analysis

### 24. Establishing a Structured AI Audit Finding Taxonomy

An AI audit finding taxonomy provides a consistent method for categorizing control deficiencies, governance weaknesses, compliance deviations, and assurance limitations identified during an audit.

Without a structured taxonomy, similar issues may be classified differently across business units, audit engagements, or individual auditors. This inconsistency makes it difficult to compare findings, prioritize remediation, identify recurring weaknesses, and report the overall state of AI governance to senior management.

AI audit findings should be classified according to the nature of the deficiency, the affected control objective, the associated risk, and the implications for the organization's governance obligations.

A practical taxonomy may include the following categories:

| Finding category                            | Description                                                                                | Illustrative AI audit finding                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance and accountability            | Deficiencies in ownership, decision rights, oversight, or governance structures            | A high-risk AI system has no formally accountable business owner                         |
| AI risk management                          | Weaknesses in risk identification, assessment, treatment, or acceptance                    | A material model change was deployed without an updated risk assessment                  |
| Data governance and provenance              | Deficiencies in data quality, lineage, authorization, or documented use                    | Training-data provenance cannot be established for a production model                    |
| Model lifecycle management                  | Weaknesses in development, validation, release, change, or retirement controls             | A new model version entered production without required validation                       |
| AI security architecture                    | Inadequate technical safeguards or architectural restrictions                              | An AI agent can invoke administrative tools beyond its approved scope                    |
| Privacy and information protection          | Deficiencies in personal data handling, confidentiality, retention, or disclosure controls | Sensitive information can be returned to unauthorized users through a retrieval workflow |
| Human oversight and decision accountability | Weaknesses in human review, override, escalation, or decision traceability                 | Mandatory human review is bypassed for consequential AI-assisted decisions               |
| AI testing and evaluation                   | Inadequate robustness, safety, performance, or adversarial testing                         | Required prompt-injection testing was not performed before release                       |
| Monitoring and incident management          | Weaknesses in telemetry, alerting, investigation, or response                              | High-priority AI security alerts are not consistently investigated                       |
| Third-party and supply-chain assurance      | Deficiencies in supplier assessment, contractual controls, or dependency oversight         | An externally provided model is used without the required supplier risk assessment       |
| Regulatory and contractual compliance       | Failure to satisfy applicable legal, regulatory, or contractual requirements               | Required records are unavailable for a regulated AI use case                             |
| Auditability and evidence management        | Inadequate documentation, retention, traceability, or evidence reliability                 | The deployed model version cannot be reconciled with its approved release record         |

The taxonomy should be aligned with the organization's AI control library, risk register, audit methodology, and applicable regulatory obligations.

A finding may relate to more than one category. For example, an AI agent that accesses personal information without appropriate authorization may involve AI security architecture, privacy, access management, and human oversight. The auditor should identify a primary category for reporting and record secondary categories where they add meaningful analytical value.

The primary category should reflect the principal control weakness rather than merely the most visible consequence.

A taxonomy is not a substitute for severity assessment. Two findings in the same category can have very different levels of significance. Missing documentation for a low-risk internal experiment is not equivalent to the absence of effective access restrictions for a production AI agent capable of disclosing sensitive information.

Classification establishes what kind of problem exists. Severity assessment determines how urgently and extensively the problem requires attention.

### 25. Distinguishing Control Deficiencies, Compliance Deviations, and Risk Exposures

AI audit reports should distinguish among different types of findings because they support different conclusions and remediation decisions.

A **control deficiency** exists when a control is inadequately designed, incorrectly implemented, inconsistently operated, or unable to achieve its intended objective.

A **compliance deviation** occurs when the organization fails to meet an applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal mandatory requirement.

A **risk exposure** exists when an identified condition creates the possibility of an adverse outcome, even when a specific control violation or actual incident has not been established.

An **assurance limitation** arises when available evidence is insufficient to support a conclusion with the level of confidence required by the audit objective.

These categories can overlap, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.

Consider an AI system that processes customer information.

- If the system lacks an effective access restriction, the auditor may identify a control deficiency.
- If the processing also violates an applicable legal requirement, the issue may constitute a compliance deviation, subject to confirmation of the relevant legal criteria and facts.
- If the access restriction has not failed in observed transactions but permits unauthorized disclosure under foreseeable conditions, the auditor may identify a risk exposure.
- If the auditor cannot obtain sufficient evidence to determine whether the restriction operates effectively, the auditor may report an assurance limitation.

The distinction affects both wording and escalation.

A finding should not claim a regulatory breach merely because a control is weak. Equally, the absence of a confirmed incident does not establish that a control is effective or that a compliance obligation has been met.

For each finding, the auditor should document the applicable criterion, the condition observed, the evidence supporting the conclusion, and the implications that can reasonably be inferred.

Where legal interpretation is necessary, the auditor should coordinate with the appropriate legal or compliance function rather than independently asserting a legal conclusion unsupported by the audit evidence.

This discipline improves the accuracy of audit reporting and helps management distinguish confirmed nonconformity from potential exposure and unresolved uncertainty.

### 26. Root-Cause Classification for AI Audit Findings

Root-cause analysis identifies the underlying conditions that produced a control failure or allowed a weakness to persist.

The immediate symptom of an AI audit finding is not necessarily its root cause. A missing approval record, for example, may result from an employee oversight, a poorly designed workflow, an unclear policy, an inadequate integration, or a governance model that does not assign approval responsibility.

Corrective action is more effective when it addresses the underlying cause rather than merely repairing the visible symptom.

A structured root-cause taxonomy may include:

| Root-cause category              | Description                                                                           | AI-related example                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Governance failure               | Unclear accountability, decision rights, or oversight                                 | No function is responsible for approving changes to an enterprise AI platform                 |
| Policy or standard weakness      | Requirements are missing, ambiguous, inconsistent, or outdated                        | The AI policy does not define approval requirements for agentic capabilities                  |
| Process design failure           | A workflow does not adequately prevent or detect a control failure                    | Production deployment is permitted before risk approval is complete                           |
| Technical implementation failure | A safeguard is incorrectly configured or implemented                                  | The authorization layer does not enforce tool-level permissions                               |
| Technology limitation            | The selected platform cannot support a required control adequately                    | The platform cannot produce the audit records needed for a mandatory review                   |
| Data quality or lineage failure  | Required data characteristics cannot be established or maintained                     | Dataset identifiers are not preserved through model-training pipelines                        |
| Human factors                    | Errors arise from workload, training, unclear instructions, or inappropriate reliance | Reviewers approve AI outputs without understanding the required verification procedure        |
| Resource constraint              | Staffing, budget, expertise, or capacity is insufficient                              | Security testing is repeatedly deferred because no qualified reviewer is available            |
| Supplier dependency              | An external provider's practices or limitations undermine the control                 | The supplier cannot provide evidence needed to assess a critical model dependency             |
| Monitoring weakness              | Controls do not identify or escalate failures effectively                             | Repeated model drift alerts remain unresolved because no owner is assigned                    |
| Change-management weakness       | Changes are not assessed, approved, or communicated appropriately                     | A prompt or retrieval configuration changes without reassessment of its security implications |
| Management oversight failure     | Known weaknesses remain unresolved or risk acceptance is inadequately governed        | Management repeatedly extends a high-risk exception without effective challenge               |

Root-cause categories should be treated as analytical aids rather than mutually exclusive explanations. A single finding may have several contributing causes.

For example, an AI agent may execute an unauthorized tool call because the technical permission boundary is too broad, the development standard does not address tool authorization, the testing process omits authorization-bypass scenarios, and management has not assigned an accountable owner for agent security.

Classifying the finding solely as a technical configuration issue would overlook the process and governance weaknesses that allowed the configuration to reach production.

Auditors should distinguish between:

- **Observed condition:** What the evidence establishes happened or was missing.
- **Immediate cause:** The direct mechanism that produced the condition.
- **Root cause:** The underlying weakness that allowed the condition to occur or recur.
- **Contributing factors:** Additional conditions that increased the likelihood or impact.
- **Consequences:** Actual effects or reasonably foreseeable adverse outcomes.

Root-cause conclusions should be proportionate to the evidence. If the auditor cannot establish why a failure occurred, the report should identify the cause as undetermined or requiring further investigation rather than presenting speculation as fact.

### 27. Severity Classification and Risk-Based Prioritization

Severity classification establishes the relative significance of an AI audit finding and supports decisions about escalation, remediation urgency, management accountability, and risk acceptance.

A finding's severity should reflect the significance of the control failure and its actual or reasonably foreseeable consequences. It should not be determined solely by the number of exceptions in the sample or the effort required to correct the problem.

Organizations commonly use four or five severity levels. A four-level model can be implemented as follows:

| Severity | General definition                                                                                             | Illustrative AI audit finding                                                                                                           | Typical management response                                                                      |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Critical | A severe or potentially catastrophic weakness requiring immediate attention                                    | An AI agent has unrestricted access to highly sensitive data and can execute consequential actions without effective authorization      | Immediate escalation, containment where appropriate, executive oversight, and urgent remediation |
| High     | A significant control failure that creates substantial exposure or affects a critical requirement              | A high-risk AI system is deployed without mandatory security validation or an effective human-oversight mechanism                       | Prompt corrective action, accountable executive ownership, and close progress monitoring         |
| Moderate | A meaningful control weakness that requires remediation but has more limited demonstrated exposure             | AI risk assessments are completed, but material changes are not consistently reflected in the records                                   | Time-bound remediation, designated ownership, and verification of closure                        |
| Low      | A limited deficiency with relatively contained impact and no identified indication of broader systemic failure | A noncritical AI inventory field is inconsistently populated where alternative evidence adequately establishes the relevant information | Routine correction, process improvement, and monitoring for recurrence                           |

These descriptions are illustrative. The organization should define its own severity thresholds, escalation rules, and remediation service levels in the audit methodology.

A severity assessment should consider multiple dimensions:

1. **Impact:** The potential consequences for individuals, customers, operations, finances, security, privacy, and organizational objectives.
2. **Likelihood:** The probability that the weakness will result in an adverse outcome under relevant operating conditions.
3. **Scope:** The number of systems, business units, users, transactions, or processes affected.
4. **Duration:** How long the weakness has existed and whether exposure remains active.
5. **Control criticality:** The importance of the affected control to the organization's risk treatment.
6. **Regulatory significance:** Whether the finding affects a mandatory obligation or a regulated AI use case.
7. **Exploitability or misuse potential:** Whether an attacker, user, or automated component can take advantage of the weakness.
8. **Detectability:** Whether the organization can identify the failure before significant harm occurs.
9. **Recurrence:** Whether the same weakness has appeared repeatedly or remains unresolved after previous findings.
10. **Existing mitigations:** Whether independent, tested safeguards materially reduce the exposure.

A scoring matrix can improve consistency, but it should not replace professional judgment. A mathematically low score must not automatically downgrade a finding involving a critical legal requirement, a potentially severe impact on individuals, or a known active security exposure.

The auditor should document the rationale for the assigned severity, including material mitigating factors and any uncertainty affecting the assessment.

### 28. Evaluating Likelihood and Impact in AI Contexts

Likelihood and impact assessments for AI audit findings require attention to system behavior, intended use, operational context, and the consequences of failure.

Likelihood should consider more than the historical frequency of observed incidents. A failure may be infrequent in the available records yet remain credible under foreseeable conditions.

Relevant considerations include:

- The ease with which the weakness can be triggered or exploited.
- The frequency of system use and exposure.
- The degree of automation.
- The availability of effective preventive and detective controls.
- The reliability of monitoring and incident detection.
- The stability of the model and its operating environment.
- The frequency of relevant changes.
- The extent to which users can bypass safeguards.
- The availability of adversarial inputs or unauthorized access paths.
- The history of related failures and near misses.

Impact should reflect the consequences that could reasonably follow from the weakness.

For AI systems, relevant impact dimensions may include:

- Harm to individuals or groups.
- Unauthorized disclosure or manipulation of information.
- Incorrect or discriminatory decisions.
- Financial loss or operational disruption.
- Compromise of connected systems.
- Loss of decision traceability or accountability.
- Regulatory enforcement or contractual consequences.
- Damage to customer trust or organizational reputation.
- Loss of model integrity or availability.
- Propagation of incorrect outputs into downstream processes.

For example, an AI summarization tool used for internal meeting notes may produce inaccurate summaries with limited consequences when every summary is independently reviewed. The same technical error in an AI system used to support medical triage, employment screening, or financial eligibility decisions may have substantially different implications.

The model's technical performance alone does not determine finding severity. The decision context, user reliance, affected population, safeguards, and consequences are also material.

The auditor should distinguish inherent risk from residual risk.

**Inherent risk** represents the exposure before considering relevant controls.

**Residual risk** represents the exposure remaining after considering controls that are demonstrably effective.

A mitigation should not reduce the assigned severity merely because it exists in documentation. The auditor should establish whether the mitigation is implemented, relevant to the risk, and operating effectively.

For example, an organization may state that human oversight reduces the risk of harmful AI recommendations. If reviewers cannot override recommendations, receive insufficient information, or routinely approve outputs without meaningful examination, the claimed mitigation may not justify a lower severity rating.

### 29. Regulatory and Legal Significance in Finding Classification

Regulatory and legal significance should be assessed separately from general operational severity because some requirements create specific obligations regardless of whether measurable harm has occurred.

AI-related findings may involve requirements concerning risk management, transparency, human oversight, data protection, cybersecurity, record-keeping, documentation, conformity assessment, or post-deployment monitoring.

The auditor should establish which requirement applies to the system, use case, organization, and relevant period before classifying a finding as a compliance deviation.

The analysis may require consideration of:

- The jurisdiction in which the AI system is developed, supplied, or used.
- The organization's role in the AI value chain.
- The intended purpose and actual deployment context.
- The applicable risk classification or regulatory category.
- The specific obligation and its effective date.
- Relevant exemptions, transitional provisions, or scope limitations.
- The evidence demonstrating compliance or noncompliance.
- The potential reporting, notification, or remediation requirements.

An internal AI policy violation is not automatically a statutory violation. Likewise, a system may satisfy an internal control checklist while failing to meet an applicable external obligation.

For example, a missing technical document may represent a documentation-control deficiency. Whether it also constitutes regulatory noncompliance depends on whether the relevant law or applicable conformity process requires that document, whether the requirement applies to the system, and whether the evidence establishes noncompliance.

Regulatory significance can influence severity, but the classification should remain evidence-based. Auditors should avoid making legal conclusions beyond their authority or expertise.

Where the finding may involve a material compliance breach, a reportable incident, or an obligation to notify an authority, escalation to the appropriate legal, privacy, compliance, or regulatory affairs function should occur promptly.

The audit methodology should define how such issues are escalated without waiting for the completion of routine finding-classification procedures.

### 30. Identifying Systemic Findings and Common-Cause Failures

A systemic finding identifies a weakness that affects, or may affect, multiple systems, processes, or business units because they share a common underlying cause.

Systemic findings are particularly important in AI environments where many applications depend on shared platforms, model registries, data pipelines, identity services, supplier relationships, or governance processes.

Examples include:

- A central model registry does not preserve approved model versions.
- The enterprise AI approval workflow permits production deployment before risk acceptance.
- A shared AI platform uses overly broad permissions across multiple applications.
- Supplier assessments omit a critical category of AI security risk.
- The organization lacks a standard method for evaluating material changes to AI systems.
- Central monitoring does not capture relevant tool-execution events.
- The AI inventory process excludes embedded AI features across multiple enterprise applications.

A finding may initially appear isolated because it was identified in one sample item. The auditor should determine whether the same control design, technology, process, or governance arrangement is used elsewhere.

Useful procedures include:

1. Identifying the affected system and control.
2. Determining whether the control is centrally managed or locally implemented.
3. Mapping systems that depend on the same component or process.
4. Reviewing previous findings and related incidents.
5. Examining relevant configuration baselines and process documentation.
6. Expanding testing across affected systems or organizational units.
7. Evaluating whether the same root cause could produce similar failures elsewhere.

For example, an audit identifies an AI application with an ineffective authorization check for external tool calls. Further investigation reveals that the same shared integration framework is used by 18 AI applications.

The original sample establishes the weakness in one application. The shared framework creates a credible possibility of broader exposure, but the auditor should not automatically claim that all 18 applications are affected without appropriate verification.

The audit should test representative configurations, inspect shared enforcement mechanisms, and determine the actual scope of the weakness.

A systemic finding may warrant higher priority than an isolated defect because remediation at the shared-control level can reduce risk across multiple applications. It may also require coordinated remediation across security engineering, platform operations, AI governance, and application owners.

Systemic classification should be supported by evidence of a common underlying weakness, not merely by the fact that several applications use AI.

### 31. Distinguishing Isolated Errors from Recurring Control Failures

An isolated error is a deviation confined to a specific item or event, with no established evidence that the same underlying weakness affects the wider population.

A recurring control failure is a pattern of similar deviations across multiple items, periods, systems, or control executions.

The distinction is important because repeated exceptions may indicate that a control is not operating consistently, even when each individual deviation appears relatively minor.

Consider an audit of AI incident escalation. The control requires high-priority incidents to be escalated to the designated response team within 30 minutes.

The auditor identifies three delayed escalations.

Possible explanations include:

- One incident was delayed because of an unusual operational disruption.
- All three incidents occurred during the same period when the escalation integration was unavailable.
- The incident-management workflow lacks automated escalation, causing delays whenever the assigned analyst is absent.
- Incident severity classifications are inconsistent, causing some high-priority incidents to be treated as routine events.
- The responsible team does not have sufficient capacity to meet the required response time.

These explanations imply different remediation approaches.

The auditor should evaluate the frequency, distribution, timing, and common characteristics of the exceptions. Where possible, the analysis should consider the full population of relevant records, not just the original sample.

A single exception may still represent a serious finding if the control is critical or the consequences are severe. Conversely, several low-impact documentation inconsistencies may have a common administrative cause and require a proportionate response.

Recurrence should be assessed against the nature of the control, the number of opportunities for the control to operate, and the relevant observation period.

A finding should not be labeled systemic solely because similar terminology appears in multiple records. The auditor should establish that the exceptions reflect a common control weakness or materially related causes.

### 32. Finding Confidence, Evidence Strength, and Uncertainty

Finding classification should communicate not only the significance of an issue but also the strength of the evidence supporting the conclusion.

Severity and evidence confidence are separate dimensions.

A potentially critical risk may be supported by incomplete evidence that requires immediate investigation. Conversely, a low-severity documentation issue may be established with high confidence through authoritative records.

The audit methodology should avoid reducing severity merely because an investigation is incomplete. Where credible evidence indicates a potentially severe exposure, the auditor may need to escalate the matter provisionally while continuing to establish the facts.

A practical evidence-confidence assessment may consider:

| Confidence level | Evidence characteristics                                                                               | Appropriate interpretation                                                   |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| High             | Multiple reliable, relevant, and corroborating sources support the conclusion                          | The observed condition and its material characteristics are well established |
| Moderate         | Relevant evidence supports the conclusion, but some corroboration or contextual information is missing | The finding is supported, with specified limitations                         |
| Limited          | Evidence is incomplete, indirect, inconsistent, or dependent on unverified explanations                | The conclusion requires qualification or further investigation               |

These labels are illustrative and should be aligned with the organization's audit methodology.

Evidence confidence should consider the reliability of source records, the completeness of the relevant population, the traceability of model versions and configurations, and the extent to which alternative explanations have been examined.

For example, a deployment log and an authoritative approval workflow may provide strong evidence that deployment occurred before approval. An informal interview alone may be insufficient to establish the precise sequence of events.

Where evidence conflicts, the auditor should investigate the discrepancy and document how it was resolved. The auditor should not select the most convenient source without evaluating its reliability.

The report should distinguish between confirmed facts, reasonable inferences, management representations, and unresolved questions.

This distinction is especially important in AI audits involving opaque third-party systems, incomplete telemetry, probabilistic behavior, or limited access to model internals.

### 33. Core Principle of Part 3

AI audit findings must be classified consistently according to the nature of the deficiency, the applicable requirement, the associated risk, and the evidence supporting the conclusion. Severity assessment should consider impact, likelihood, scope, control criticality, regulatory significance, and the effectiveness of demonstrated mitigations. Root-cause analysis must distinguish symptoms from underlying governance, process, technical, data, supplier, or oversight weaknesses. Auditors should identify systemic and recurring failures where supported by evidence, preserve uncertainty where facts remain incomplete, and escalate potentially severe exposures without waiting for routine classification to be finalized.

## Part 4 — AI Audit Finding Aggregation, Corrective Action Linkage, Reporting, and Assurance Follow-Through

### 34. Aggregating Sample Results Into Audit Conclusions

After individual sample items have been tested and exceptions classified, the auditor must aggregate the results into an overall audit conclusion that accurately reflects the evidence obtained.

Aggregation is not simply a matter of counting the number of failed samples. The auditor must consider:

- The sampling methodology used.
- The population represented by the sample.
- The significance of each exception.
- Whether exceptions are independent or share a common cause.
- Whether exceptions are concentrated in particular risk strata.
- Whether compensating controls exist and operate effectively.
- Whether the observed condition represents a design weakness or an operating failure.
- Whether the sample supports a population-wide inference.
- Whether additional procedures changed the original understanding of the control environment.

For example, an audit samples 40 AI model releases and identifies four releases without documented pre-production approval.

A simple conclusion such as "36 of 40 releases passed" may be misleading.

The four exceptions may represent:

- Four unrelated administrative errors.
- A common failure affecting one business unit.
- A deployment pipeline defect affecting every release processed through a particular workflow.
- A broader control-design deficiency.
- A record-retention failure where approval occurred but evidence was not preserved.

The auditor should therefore analyze the distribution and characteristics of the exceptions before determining the overall conclusion.

Where probability-based sampling was used and the methodology supports statistical inference, the auditor may estimate the likely population deviation rate within the appropriate statistical limitations.

Where risk-based or judgmental sampling was used, the auditor should avoid presenting the observed exception rate as a statistically representative population estimate.

Instead, the conclusion may state that selected high-risk systems or transactions were examined and that identified exceptions indicate specific weaknesses requiring remediation.

The audit conclusion should therefore reflect both the evidence obtained and the limits of the sampling design.

### 35. Evaluating Exception Rates and Patterns

Exception rates can provide useful information about control performance, but the numerical rate alone is insufficient to determine finding significance.

Consider two hypothetical audit populations:

**Population A**

- 1,000 low-risk AI inventory records.
- 20 sampled records.
- 4 missing noncritical metadata fields.
- No security or regulatory impact identified.

**Population B**

- 40 high-risk AI production systems.
- 20 sampled systems.
- 2 systems lack mandatory human-oversight controls.
- Both systems support consequential business decisions.

Population A has a 20% observed exception rate, while Population B has a 10% observed exception rate.

Nevertheless, Population B may represent the more significant audit concern because the affected control is critical and the potential impact is substantially greater.

The auditor should therefore evaluate:

1. **Frequency:** How often did the deviation occur?
2. **Severity:** How consequential is the affected control?
3. **Concentration:** Are deviations clustered within a particular system, business unit, model, supplier, or process?
4. **Recurrence:** Have similar deviations appeared previously?
5. **Common cause:** Do the exceptions share a root cause?
6. **Exposure period:** How long may the weakness have existed?
7. **Population scope:** How many additional items may be affected?
8. **Mitigation:** Are effective compensating controls present?
9. **Detectability:** Would the organization detect a failure before significant harm occurred?
10. **Management response:** Was the issue already known and formally accepted?

This prevents the audit process from reducing complex AI assurance conclusions to a single percentage.

### 36. Compensating Controls and Residual Risk

A compensating control is an alternative control that reduces the risk associated with a weakness in the primary control.

Compensating controls may be relevant when evaluating finding severity, but they should only be credited when their operation is supported by evidence.

For example, an AI application may lack automated enforcement preventing users from accessing a particular sensitive dataset. However, every access request may be subject to an independently enforced manual authorization process.

The manual review may reduce residual risk, but the auditor must determine whether:

- The review covers all relevant access requests.
- Reviewers are authorized and sufficiently independent.
- The review occurs before access is granted.
- Reviewers have enough information to identify inappropriate requests.
- Decisions are recorded.
- Exceptions are escalated.
- The review itself is subject to monitoring or quality assurance.

A documented compensating control that is not operating effectively should not materially reduce the finding's severity.

Similarly, an AI system may have multiple layers of security controls. The failure of one layer does not necessarily establish that the entire security architecture is ineffective.

The auditor should assess the combined control environment and determine whether the remaining controls materially reduce the relevant risk.

Residual risk should be explicitly considered.

For example:

**Primary control:** Automated authorization prevents an AI agent from executing privileged administrative actions.

**Observed deficiency:** The authorization rule does not restrict one administrative API.

**Compensating control:** All administrative actions are independently reviewed by a security operations team.

**Evidence:** Logs demonstrate that all sampled administrative actions were reviewed and unauthorized actions were blocked.

**Conclusion:** The primary preventive control has a design deficiency, but the demonstrated compensating control materially reduces immediate exposure.

This does not mean the primary control passes. The finding remains valid if the original control criterion requires the automated authorization boundary. The compensating control affects the assessment of residual risk and potentially the severity and remediation priority.

### 37. Evaluating Finding Severity Through Control Dependency

AI environments often contain shared controls on which multiple systems depend.

Examples include:

- Central identity and access management.
- Enterprise model registries.
- AI gateways.
- Common logging platforms.
- Shared retrieval infrastructure.
- Centralized model-monitoring services.
- Enterprise AI approval workflows.
- Common third-party AI platforms.

A weakness in a shared control may have a significantly greater impact than an equivalent weakness in one isolated application.

The auditor should therefore consider control dependency when evaluating severity.

For example, an individual application may have a missing monitoring rule affecting one model. A separate issue may involve a central AI gateway that fails to capture tool-execution events for every connected AI application.

The second issue may initially appear as a single technical defect, but its control dependency means that numerous systems may operate without the expected monitoring coverage.

The auditor should determine:

- Which systems depend on the affected control.
- Whether the control is mandatory across those systems.
- Whether alternative controls exist.
- Whether the weakness affects all dependent systems or only a subset.
- Whether the dependent systems have different risk classifications.
- Whether the weakness can be exploited consistently.
- Whether management is aware of the dependency.

Shared-control findings should be reported at the level where corrective action can address the underlying risk.

If the root cause resides in an enterprise platform, creating separate findings for every downstream application may obscure the actual remediation requirement.

However, downstream applications may still require individual corrective actions where their configurations independently contribute to the risk.

### 38. AI Audit Finding Relationships and Parent-Child Findings

Complex AI audit environments may produce several related findings that should be structured hierarchically.

A **parent finding** represents the principal systemic or control-level weakness.

**Child findings** represent specific manifestations of the same underlying weakness within particular systems, processes, or business units.

For example:

**Parent finding:** Enterprise AI change management does not require reassessment of security and privacy risks following material model or configuration changes.

**Child finding A:** Customer-service AI deployment changed retrieval configuration without reassessment.

**Child finding B:** HR AI model version changed without updated validation evidence.

**Child finding C:** Finance AI agent received additional tool permissions without documented risk review.

This structure provides several advantages:

- Prevents duplicate reporting of the same root cause.
- Establishes enterprise-wide visibility.
- Allows business units to address local manifestations.
- Supports centralized remediation tracking.
- Makes systemic trends easier to identify.
- Enables management to distinguish enterprise control weaknesses from local implementation failures.

Parent-child relationships should be established only when the findings share a meaningful common cause or control dependency.

Unrelated issues should not be grouped merely because they concern AI.

For example, a weak model monitoring control and an outdated AI inventory record may both exist within the same application, but they should remain separate findings if they have different causes, risks, and remediation owners.

The audit management system should preserve relationships among findings, controls, risks, systems, business units, and corrective actions.

This creates traceability from individual evidence to enterprise-level assurance conclusions.

### 39. Linking Findings to AI Risk Registers

Audit findings should be linked to the organization's AI risk management process wherever practical.

A finding may:

- Confirm an existing risk.
- Reveal that an existing risk assessment underestimated exposure.
- Identify a previously unknown risk.
- Demonstrate that a risk treatment is ineffective.
- Require modification of a risk owner or treatment plan.
- Trigger a new risk acceptance decision.
- Require reassessment of residual risk.

For example, an AI risk register may contain:

> **Risk:** Unauthorized AI agent actions could modify enterprise records.

> **Inherent risk:** High.

> **Treatment:** Implement least-privilege tool permissions, approval gates, action logging, and continuous monitoring.

An audit identifies that the approval gate can be bypassed through one API endpoint.

The finding should not exist independently from the risk register. The organization should evaluate whether the existing treatment remains effective and whether the residual risk rating should change.

The audit finding may therefore trigger:

1. Risk reassessment.
2. Control reassessment.
3. Treatment-plan modification.
4. New corrective action.
5. Updated risk ownership.
6. Revised residual-risk acceptance, if necessary.

This linkage prevents the audit function from becoming a disconnected reporting mechanism.

It also allows management to understand which enterprise AI risks are actually supported by effective controls and which remain exposed despite documented treatment plans.

### 40. Linking Findings to AI Control Libraries

AI audit findings should also be mapped to the relevant control library entries.

A finding may identify:

- A control that was not implemented.
- A control that was implemented incorrectly.
- A control that operated inconsistently.
- A control whose design is inadequate.
- A control that lacks sufficient evidence.
- A control that has become obsolete because of changes in technology or regulation.

For example:

| Control ID | Control objective | Audit observation | Finding implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| AIC-AC-04 | Restrict privileged AI tool access | Agent can invoke an administrative API without authorization | Control design deficiency |
| AIC-RM-07 | Reassess risk after material changes | Model changes occurred without documented reassessment | Operating effectiveness deficiency |
| AIC-MON-03 | Monitor high-risk AI activity | Tool execution events are not captured centrally | Monitoring control deficiency |
| AIC-IR-05 | Escalate AI incidents within defined time | High-priority incidents were repeatedly escalated late | Operating effectiveness deficiency |

The mapping should identify whether the issue concerns:

- Control design.
- Control implementation.
- Control operation.
- Control evidence.
- Control ownership.
- Control coverage.
- Control applicability.

This distinction is valuable for control-library maintenance.

If multiple audits identify that a particular control is repeatedly difficult to implement, the organization may need to revise the control itself rather than repeatedly issuing findings against individual business units.

A control library should therefore evolve based on audit evidence.

### 41. Corrective Action Requirements Derived From Findings

Corrective actions should directly address the condition and root cause identified by the audit.

A weak corrective action merely restates the finding.

For example:

**Finding:** High-risk AI systems are not consistently subject to documented pre-deployment security approval.

**Weak corrective action:** "Ensure security approvals are completed."

This does not explain how the organization will prevent recurrence.

A stronger corrective action might include:

- Implementing a mandatory deployment gate.
- Integrating security approval status with the production release pipeline.
- Preventing deployment when required approval is absent.
- Assigning an accountable approval authority.
- Establishing evidence-retention requirements.
- Monitoring bypass attempts.
- Testing the control after implementation.

Corrective actions should identify:

- Action description.
- Root cause addressed.
- Responsible owner.
- Supporting stakeholders.
- Target completion date.
- Required resources.
- Dependencies.
- Expected risk reduction.
- Verification method.
- Evidence required for closure.

A corrective action should be proportionate to the finding.

A low-severity documentation deficiency may require a process update and training. A critical authorization weakness may require immediate containment, architectural changes, independent testing, and executive oversight.

Corrective action plans should also distinguish remediation from temporary risk mitigation.

**Remediation** addresses the underlying control weakness.

**Mitigation** reduces exposure while remediation is pending.

For example, disabling an AI agent's external tool access may temporarily reduce risk while a permanent authorization architecture is developed.

The mitigation should not be treated as equivalent to completing the permanent corrective action unless the organization formally determines that the revised control environment adequately addresses the original risk.

### 42. Finding Remediation Priority and Due Dates

Finding priority should be determined by risk rather than convenience.

A mature AI assurance program should establish remediation expectations according to severity and applicable regulatory or contractual obligations.

An illustrative model is:

| Severity | Illustrative remediation expectation |
|---|---|
| Critical | Immediate containment and executive escalation; permanent remediation initiated urgently |
| High | Accelerated remediation with senior management ownership and frequent progress monitoring |
| Moderate | Defined corrective-action plan with a controlled target date |
| Low | Routine remediation through normal governance and process-improvement activities |

These timelines should be adjusted according to the organization's formal methodology.

A finding's due date should also account for:

- Active exploitation or exposure.
- Regulatory deadlines.
- Contractual commitments.
- Dependence on major technology changes.
- Availability of interim mitigations.
- Complexity of architectural remediation.
- Potential impact on customers or employees.
- Whether the affected AI system remains in production.

A technically complex remediation does not automatically justify a long delay when the risk remains severe.

Where permanent remediation cannot be completed within an appropriate period, management should implement effective interim controls and formally assess whether residual risk requires acceptance or additional escalation.

Repeated extensions should trigger additional scrutiny.

A control weakness that remains open through multiple reporting cycles may indicate inadequate management commitment, unrealistic remediation planning, insufficient resources, or an incorrect assessment of the underlying root cause.

### 43. Management Response and Risk Acceptance

Management should have an opportunity to respond to audit findings, but management responses should not alter established facts.

A management response normally addresses:

- Whether management agrees with the finding.
- The business context.
- Root-cause interpretation.
- Corrective action.
- Responsible owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim mitigation.
- Residual-risk position.
- Any disagreement with the audit conclusion.

Management disagreement should be documented when material.

For example, management may state that a control deviation was caused by a temporary system outage and that all affected transactions were subsequently reviewed.

The auditor should consider this information and determine whether it changes the factual conclusion. If the evidence confirms that the control was unavailable during the relevant period, the finding may remain valid while the explanation and compensating review are reflected in the final assessment.

Management may also request formal risk acceptance instead of remediation.

Risk acceptance should not automatically close an audit finding.

Acceptance should be governed by the organization's risk-management authority, based on:

- The nature of the risk.
- Residual-risk level.
- Duration of the acceptance.
- Business justification.
- Applicable legal and regulatory requirements.
- Available alternatives.
- Compensating controls.
- Approval authority.
- Review or expiration date.

Some risks may not be legally or contractually acceptable merely because management is willing to accept them.

Where risk acceptance is valid, the audit finding may be tracked separately from the accepted risk, depending on the organization's methodology. The audit record should preserve the relationship between the finding, the risk, the acceptance decision, and the expiration or review date.

### 44. Verification of Corrective Action Effectiveness

Closing a corrective action requires more than receiving a statement that the action has been completed.

Audit closure should establish whether the corrective action was implemented and whether it adequately addresses the original deficiency.

Verification procedures may include:

- Inspecting updated policies or procedures.
- Reviewing system configurations.
- Testing access restrictions.
- Reperforming the original control test.
- Examining a new sample of transactions or events.
- Reviewing post-implementation monitoring.
- Confirming that the relevant risk register and control library were updated.
- Evaluating whether the root cause was actually addressed.
- Checking whether similar findings have recurred.

For example, an organization claims that it has implemented mandatory security approval for AI deployments.

The auditor should not close the finding merely because a new policy exists.

The auditor may instead:

1. Inspect the deployment workflow.
2. Confirm that the approval gate is technically enforced.
3. Attempt to identify bypass paths.
4. Review configuration and authorization settings.
5. Select post-remediation production releases.
6. Verify that approval occurred before deployment.
7. Confirm that evidence is retained.
8. Determine whether unauthorized deployment attempts are detected and escalated.

If the control operates effectively after remediation, the finding can be considered for closure according to the organization's audit methodology.

If the corrective action only addresses documentation while the technical weakness remains, the finding should remain open or be reclassified according to the evidence.

Corrective-action verification is therefore a new assurance procedure, not an administrative confirmation.

### 45. Recurrent Findings and Management Escalation

Repeated findings indicate that the organization may not be effectively learning from assurance activities.

A recurring AI audit finding may arise because:

- Previous corrective action addressed only the symptom.
- Root cause was incorrectly identified.
- Remediation was implemented but not sustained.
- Ownership was unclear.
- Monitoring did not detect recurrence.
- Risk acceptance expired without reassessment.
- New technology reintroduced the same weakness.
- The control design itself remains inadequate.

Recurring findings should be analyzed across audit cycles.

For example, three consecutive audits identify weaknesses in AI model change management. Each business unit implemented local procedures, but the enterprise continues to experience undocumented model changes.

This pattern suggests that local remediation may not address the enterprise-level cause.

Management may need to establish a centralized change-management control, integrate AI changes with enterprise release processes, and introduce monitoring capable of detecting unregistered model or configuration changes.

Repeated findings should influence management reporting and potentially the organization's AI governance maturity assessment.

Where a previously identified high-severity finding remains unresolved beyond its approved remediation period, escalation should occur according to the organization's governance structure.

This may involve:

- AI governance committees.
- Chief Information Security Officer.
- Chief Risk Officer.
- Chief Data or AI Officer.
- Internal audit leadership.
- Executive risk committees.
- Board-level risk oversight, where appropriate.

The escalation level should correspond to the severity and governance significance of the unresolved issue.

### 46. AI Audit Finding Trend Analysis

Finding trend analysis allows organizations to identify changes in the control environment over time.

Useful dimensions include:

- Number of findings by severity.
- Findings by control domain.
- Findings by AI lifecycle stage.
- Findings by business unit.
- Findings by technology platform.
- Findings by AI risk classification.
- Average remediation duration.
- Number of overdue corrective actions.
- Number of recurring findings.
- Number of findings caused by shared controls.
- Number of findings associated with third-party AI services.
- Percentage of findings closed after independent verification.
- Number of findings escalated to executive management.

Trend analysis can identify structural weaknesses that individual audits may not reveal.

For example, the organization may observe:

- Declining findings related to AI inventory accuracy.
- Increasing findings related to third-party AI assurance.
- Persistent weaknesses in model change management.
- Growing numbers of monitoring deficiencies as AI agent deployments increase.

These patterns can inform the annual AI assurance plan and control-library priorities.

Trend analysis should be interpreted carefully. A decline in findings does not necessarily mean that the control environment improved. It may also reflect:

- Reduced audit coverage.
- Changes in sampling methodology.
- Incomplete populations.
- Fewer audits.
- Changes in classification criteria.
- Reduced reporting of minor findings.

Trend metrics should therefore be accompanied by contextual information about audit coverage and methodology.

### 47. Audit Finding Reporting to AI Governance Committees

AI audit findings should be reported to the governance forums responsible for overseeing AI risk.

The reporting format should allow decision-makers to understand:

- What failed.
- Why it matters.
- Which systems or processes are affected.
- How severe the issue is.
- Whether exposure is active.
- Whether the issue is systemic.
- Who owns remediation.
- When remediation is expected.
- Whether interim controls exist.
- Whether management has accepted residual risk.
- Whether regulatory or contractual escalation may be required.

A governance-level report should avoid overwhelming decision-makers with technical audit working papers while retaining sufficient detail to support informed decisions.

A useful executive finding summary may contain:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | AI-2026-014 |
| Domain | AI Security Architecture |
| Severity | High |
| Control | Privileged AI tool authorization |
| Condition | Agent can invoke an administrative API without required authorization |
| Scope | Three production AI agents using the shared integration service |
| Root cause | Shared authorization framework does not enforce tool-level privilege boundaries |
| Risk | Unauthorized modification of enterprise records |
| Interim mitigation | Administrative tool access disabled pending remediation |
| Owner | AI Platform Security |
| Target date | Defined according to approved remediation SLA |
| Verification | Independent retesting of authorization controls |
| Risk status | Reduced by interim control; permanent remediation open |

The governance committee should be able to distinguish findings requiring immediate intervention from findings that can be managed through routine corrective-action processes.

Critical findings should not be buried in aggregated metrics.

### 48. Audit Evidence Retention and Finding Traceability

AI audit findings must remain traceable to the evidence from which they were derived.

Traceability should connect:

**Audit objective → control criterion → population → sample → evidence → exception → finding → root cause → risk → corrective action → verification → closure.**

This chain allows another qualified reviewer to understand how the audit conclusion was reached.

Evidence-retention requirements should address:

- Audit workpapers.
- Population extracts.
- Sample-selection records.
- Test results.
- System logs.
- Screenshots or configuration evidence where appropriate.
- Management responses.
- Finding approvals.
- Corrective-action records.
- Closure evidence.
- Risk-acceptance decisions.
- Relevant communications and escalation records.

AI systems may change rapidly, making historical evidence particularly important.

A model version may be replaced, a prompt may change, a platform configuration may be updated, or a third-party provider may alter its service. Without preserved evidence, an organization may be unable to reconstruct the state of the system at the time of the audit.

Evidence retention should therefore preserve sufficient context to establish:

- What system was examined.
- Which model and configuration were active.
- What period was covered.
- Which controls were applicable.
- What evidence was available.
- What conclusion was reached.
- Which remediation was subsequently implemented.

Sensitive AI audit evidence must also be protected appropriately. Audit records may contain personal data, confidential business information, security configurations, model prompts, proprietary model information, or details of exploitable weaknesses.

Retention must therefore balance auditability with data minimization, confidentiality, access control, and applicable retention requirements.

### 49. Quality Assurance and Independent Review of AI Audit Findings

AI audit findings should undergo appropriate quality assurance before final issuance.

Independent review can evaluate whether:

- The audit objective was clearly defined.
- The population was appropriately constructed.
- The sampling methodology was suitable.
- Sample selection was performed correctly.
- Evidence supports the reported condition.
- Findings are classified consistently.
- Severity is adequately justified.
- Root-cause analysis is evidence-based.
- Regulatory claims are appropriately qualified.
- Management responses are accurately represented.
- Corrective actions address the identified root cause.
- Conclusions reflect the limitations of the audit.

Complex AI findings may require specialist review.

For example, a finding concerning model robustness may require an AI security specialist to evaluate whether the technical evidence supports the stated conclusion. A finding involving privacy obligations may require privacy or legal review.

Specialist review should complement, not replace, audit judgment.

The reviewer should also challenge findings that appear overly broad.

For example, evidence showing that one model failed a particular robustness test does not automatically establish that every model in the organization has the same weakness.

Conversely, evidence showing a weakness in a centralized AI platform may justify broader reporting if the platform is shared across many systems.

Quality assurance therefore helps maintain proportionality between evidence and conclusion.

### 50. Core Principle of Part 4

AI audit findings must progress through a controlled assurance lifecycle: aggregate sample results appropriately, evaluate exception patterns and compensating controls, classify systemic and recurring weaknesses, link findings to AI risks and controls, assign proportionate corrective actions, obtain governed management responses, and independently verify remediation effectiveness. Every conclusion should remain traceable from audit objective through evidence, finding, root cause, risk, corrective action, and closure. The final objective is not merely to document deficiencies, but to establish whether AI governance controls are actually reducing risk and whether identified weaknesses are sustainably resolved.
