# 19.08 — AI Control Testing

## Part 1 — AI Control Testing Foundations, Control Objectives, Test Design and Assessment Methodology

### 1. Introduction to AI Control Testing

AI control testing is the structured evaluation of controls designed to manage risks associated with artificial intelligence systems throughout their lifecycle.

It determines whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively to address defined risks, governance objectives, security requirements, regulatory obligations, and organizational policies.

AI control testing differs from general AI system evaluation. Model evaluation typically examines characteristics such as accuracy, robustness, fairness, or performance. Control testing examines whether the processes, technical safeguards, governance mechanisms, and operational activities intended to manage those characteristics are appropriately established and functioning.

For example, testing a model for discriminatory outcomes evaluates model behavior. Testing whether the organization has an approved fairness-testing procedure, performs required assessments, documents exceptions, and remediates identified disparities evaluates the associated control environment.

AI control testing may cover:

- AI governance and accountability
- AI inventory and classification
- AI risk assessment and treatment
- Data governance and quality management
- Model development and validation
- AI security architecture
- Privacy and data protection
- Human oversight
- Transparency and disclosure
- Third-party and supply-chain security
- AI incident management
- Regulatory compliance
- Monitoring and continuous improvement

The testing methodology should reflect the actual control objective, system context, applicable requirements, and risk exposure.

**Core principle:** An AI control is not demonstrated to be effective merely because a policy exists or a technical feature is available. Its design, implementation, operation, and supporting evidence must be evaluated against defined criteria.

### 2. Objectives of AI Control Testing

AI control testing provides evidence about whether the organization's AI governance and risk-management arrangements operate as intended.

The primary objectives are to:

1. **Verify control design:** Determine whether a control is appropriately designed to address its intended risk or requirement.
2. **Verify implementation:** Establish whether the control has been deployed or incorporated into the relevant process.
3. **Evaluate operating effectiveness:** Determine whether the control operates consistently and as intended during the assessment period.
4. **Identify control deficiencies:** Detect missing, ineffective, inconsistent, or inadequately evidenced controls.
5. **Validate compliance implementation:** Evaluate whether controls support the applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements.
6. **Assess risk mitigation:** Determine whether the control reduces the relevant risk to the intended extent.
7. **Support assurance conclusions:** Provide documented evidence for internal audit, compliance, risk management, and management reporting.
8. **Validate remediation:** Establish whether corrective actions have addressed previously identified deficiencies.
9. **Improve control maturity:** Identify opportunities to improve control design, ownership, monitoring, and evidence quality.
10. **Support lifecycle governance:** Confirm that relevant controls remain appropriate as AI systems, models, data, suppliers, and deployment contexts change.

The objective should be specific enough to determine the testing procedure, evidence requirements, acceptance criteria, and reporting approach.

A control test that merely confirms the existence of an AI governance policy does not establish whether the organization applies that policy to its actual AI systems.

### 3. AI Control Testing Versus AI Model Testing

AI control testing and AI model testing are related but distinct disciplines.

| Dimension             | AI control testing                                           | AI model testing                                                          |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary focus         | Controls, processes, and governance mechanisms               | Model behavior and technical characteristics                              |
| Main question         | Does the control operate as intended?                        | Does the model satisfy defined performance or safety criteria?            |
| Typical subject       | Policies, workflows, approvals, monitoring, safeguards       | Models, datasets, outputs, metrics                                        |
| Evidence              | Records, configurations, logs, approvals, test results       | Benchmark results, evaluation datasets, outputs, metrics                  |
| Typical methods       | Inspection, inquiry, observation, reperformance, sampling    | Benchmarking, statistical evaluation, adversarial testing                 |
| Main output           | Control conclusion and identified deficiencies               | Model evaluation results and identified limitations                       |
| Responsible functions | GRC, Internal Audit, Compliance, control owners, specialists | Data Science, ML Engineering, AI Safety, Security, independent evaluators |

The two disciplines should be integrated when model behavior is an input to a governance or technical control.

For example, an organization may test model robustness and separately test whether robustness testing is required before deployment, independently reviewed, documented, and repeated after material changes.

A successful model evaluation does not automatically establish that the surrounding governance controls are effective.

Likewise, an effective governance process does not automatically establish that the model itself satisfies every relevant technical criterion.

### 4. AI Control Testing Versus AI Risk Assessment

AI risk assessment identifies and analyzes risks associated with an AI system, use case, or organizational activity.

AI control testing evaluates whether specific controls intended to manage those risks are appropriately designed and operating.

The relationship is sequential but iterative:

```
AI SYSTEM AND USE CASE
          |
          v
     RISK ASSESSMENT
          |
          v
      RISK TREATMENT
          |
          v
     CONTROL DESIGN
          |
          v
   CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
          |
          v
      CONTROL TESTING
          |
          v
  DEFICIENCY IDENTIFICATION
          |
          v
    CORRECTIVE ACTION
          |
          v
      RETESTING
          |
          v
  RESIDUAL RISK REASSESSMENT
```

For example, a risk assessment may identify the possibility that employees enter confidential information into an unauthorized generative AI service.

Potential controls may include approved-tool restrictions, data-loss prevention, user guidance, access controls, and monitoring.

Control testing then evaluates whether these controls exist, whether they are configured appropriately, whether users and systems follow the required process, and whether the controls detect or prevent the relevant activity.

Risk assessment identifies the risk; control testing evaluates the response to that risk.

### 5. AI Control Testing Within the Three Lines Model

AI control testing may be performed by several organizational functions, each with different responsibilities.

**First line — Business and technology operations**

Responsibilities may include:

- Operating assigned controls
- Maintaining required records
- Performing operational checks
- Monitoring control failures
- Correcting deficiencies
- Providing evidence to assurance functions

**Second line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy, and AI Governance**

Responsibilities may include:

- Establishing control requirements
- Maintaining assessment methodologies
- Monitoring control implementation
- Conducting compliance reviews
- Challenging control-owner assertions
- Coordinating remediation oversight
- Reporting material control deficiencies

**Third line — Internal Audit**

Responsibilities may include:

- Independently evaluating control design and effectiveness
- Testing the adequacy of governance arrangements
- Reviewing risk-management processes
- Assessing the reliability of management reporting
- Reporting significant deficiencies
- Following up on agreed management actions

External auditors, independent assessors, and specialist testing providers may also perform defined assurance activities.

The organizational model should preserve appropriate independence and clarify who designs, operates, tests, approves, and independently evaluates each control.

A control owner should not be treated as an independent tester merely because the owner has performed a self-assessment.

### 6. AI Control Testing Principles

A defensible AI control-testing program should follow these principles.

**Risk-based testing:** Allocate testing effort according to the significance of the control, the risks addressed, and the consequences of failure.

**Defined criteria:** Establish what the control must achieve before evaluating whether it has achieved it.

**Evidence-based conclusions:** Support findings and conclusions with relevant, reliable, and sufficiently complete evidence.

**Reproducibility:** Document test procedures and results sufficiently for another qualified reviewer to understand the work and, where practicable, reproduce it.

**Independence and objectivity:** Manage conflicts of interest and ensure that testing conclusions are not improperly influenced by control owners.

**Lifecycle awareness:** Consider development, validation, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement where relevant.

**Technical and organizational coverage:** Evaluate technical safeguards alongside governance processes, accountability, training, and operational procedures.

**Proportionality:** Match testing depth to the risk, complexity, criticality, and applicable requirements of the AI system.

**Traceability:** Link test procedures and results to controls, risks, requirements, evidence, and remediation records.

**Continuous improvement:** Use findings, incidents, changes, and testing outcomes to improve the control environment.

These principles support consistent testing without assuming that every AI system requires the same tests or assurance depth.

### 7. AI Control Taxonomy and Unique Identifiers

A control taxonomy organizes controls into a consistent structure and assigns identifiers that support testing, reporting, and traceability.

An organization may group AI controls into domains such as:

- AI governance and accountability
- AI inventory and classification
- AI risk management
- Data governance
- Model lifecycle management
- AI security
- Privacy and fundamental rights
- Transparency and human oversight
- Third-party and supply-chain governance
- Incident management and resilience
- Regulatory compliance
- Monitoring and assurance

Each control should have a unique identifier, a clear title, and a defined objective.

Example internal identifiers:

| Control ID | Control domain      | Illustrative control                    |
| ---------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| AI-CT-001  | Governance          | AI system ownership and accountability  |
| AI-CT-002  | Inventory           | AI system inventory completeness        |
| AI-CT-003  | Classification      | Documented AI risk classification       |
| AI-CT-004  | Risk management     | AI risk assessment approval             |
| AI-CT-005  | Data governance     | Dataset quality and suitability review  |
| AI-CT-006  | Model lifecycle     | Independent model validation            |
| AI-CT-007  | Security            | AI access control enforcement           |
| AI-CT-008  | Transparency        | Required user disclosures               |
| AI-CT-009  | Human oversight     | Defined human intervention mechanisms   |
| AI-CT-010  | Monitoring          | Production monitoring and escalation    |

The taxonomy should be version-controlled. If a control is materially changed, the organization should preserve its history and document how the change affects previous test results.

### 8. AI Control Objectives

A control objective describes the outcome that a control is intended to achieve.

Control objectives should be specific, testable, and connected to a defined risk or requirement.

For example:

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI systems are identified, recorded, and reviewed before being introduced into production.

Potential control requirements may include:

- Defined inventory fields
- Assigned system ownership
- Identification of intended purpose
- Recording of provider and deployer roles where relevant
- Documented classification decisions
- Approval before deployment
- Periodic reconciliation with procurement and technology records
- Updates after material changes

The control objective describes the desired outcome, while the control activities describe how the organization achieves it.

The testing procedure then evaluates whether those activities are suitably designed and operating.

A control objective should not be written so broadly that almost any activity could be claimed to satisfy it.

### 9. AI Control Attributes

Control attributes are the characteristics that the assessor examines when testing a control.

Common attributes include:

**Ownership:** Is a responsible owner assigned?

**Authorization:** Are decisions approved by the appropriate authority?

**Frequency:** Is the control performed at the required or defined interval?

**Completeness:** Does the control cover the relevant population?

**Accuracy:** Are recorded decisions and results correct?

**Timeliness:** Is the control performed at the appropriate point in the lifecycle?

**Consistency:** Is the control applied according to defined procedures?

**Evidence:** Are records sufficient to demonstrate execution?

**Exception handling:** Are failures and deviations identified, recorded, and escalated?

**Change responsiveness:** Does the control respond to material system or regulatory changes?

**Segregation of duties:** Are conflicting responsibilities appropriately separated where necessary?

**Monitoring:** Is the control's performance reviewed?

The relevant attributes depend on the control objective. An access-control test, for example, may emphasize authorization, completeness, configuration, and exception handling. A legal classification test may emphasize accuracy, evidence, approval, and reassessment after material changes.

### 10. AI Control Design Assessment

Control design assessment evaluates whether the control, as specified, is capable of addressing the intended risk or requirement.

The assessor should determine:

1. What risk or requirement the control addresses.
2. Whether the control objective is sufficiently precise.
3. Whether the control activity is appropriate.
4. Whether responsibility is clearly assigned.
5. Whether timing and frequency are suitable.
6. Whether required inputs are available.
7. Whether the control produces usable evidence.
8. Whether exceptions are handled.
9. Whether the control addresses relevant changes.
10. Whether the control depends on other controls that may be missing or ineffective.

**Example: AI deployment approval**

A documented approval process may require the AI system owner to confirm that relevant assessments are complete before production release.

The design assessment should examine whether the process:

- Defines the required approvals
- Identifies the applicable assessments
- Prevents unauthorized release where necessary
- Records the approval decision
- Handles incomplete assessments
- Escalates exceptions
- Responds to material changes

If the process permits deployment without required assessments and has no effective exception mechanism, the control may be inadequately designed even if the approval form is completed consistently.

### 11. AI Control Implementation Assessment

Implementation assessment determines whether the control has been put into operation in the relevant environment.

A control may be documented but not implemented. Conversely, a technical safeguard may exist without an approved procedure or clearly assigned owner.

Implementation evidence may include:

- Approved procedures
- Configured system settings
- Workflow rules
- Access-control assignments
- Assigned control owners
- Operational logs
- Training records
- Completed approvals
- Monitoring dashboards
- Implemented technical safeguards

The assessor should verify that the evidence corresponds to the actual system, environment, and relevant period.

For example, a data-loss prevention rule documented in a design specification does not establish that the rule is enabled in the production environment.

Implementation testing establishes that the control exists and is deployed; it does not necessarily establish that it operates effectively over time.

### 12. AI Control Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness evaluates whether the control operated consistently with its design during the relevant period.

The assessment should establish:

- The period under review
- The relevant control population
- The control frequency
- The expected execution criteria
- The evidence required
- The testing procedure
- The exception criteria
- The conclusion methodology

For a monthly AI governance review, the assessor might inspect records for selected months, verify the review occurred, examine the evidence considered, and confirm that identified issues were appropriately handled.

For an automated access restriction, the assessor might inspect configuration settings, review relevant logs, and test whether unauthorized access attempts are blocked.

A control can be implemented but ineffective in operation because personnel fail to perform it, system configurations drift, evidence is incomplete, or exceptions remain unresolved.

### 13. AI Control Testing Methodology

A standardized methodology improves consistency across systems, control domains, and assessment teams.

A typical testing lifecycle includes:

**Phase 1 — Planning**

- Define objectives and scope.
- Identify risks and applicable criteria.
- Select controls for testing.
- Identify owners and evidence sources.
- Establish timing and resources.

**Phase 2 — Control understanding**

- Review the control description.
- Confirm the control objective.
- Understand the process and dependencies.
- Identify relevant systems and populations.
- Establish expected outcomes.

**Phase 3 — Test design**

- Select the test procedure.
- Define the sample or testing population.
- Establish acceptance criteria.
- Identify required evidence.
- Determine how exceptions will be evaluated.

**Phase 4 — Test execution**

- Collect evidence.
- Inspect records and configurations.
- Perform inquiry and observation.
- Reperform selected activities where appropriate.
- Record results and exceptions.

**Phase 5 — Evaluation**

- Compare actual results with criteria.
- Determine whether deficiencies exist.
- Assess the significance of exceptions.
- Identify evidence limitations.
- Document conclusions.

**Phase 6 — Reporting and remediation**

- Communicate findings.
- Assign responsible owners.
- Agree corrective actions.
- Establish target dates.
- Define validation criteria.

**Phase 7 — Retesting and closure**

- Obtain remediation evidence.
- Retest affected controls.
- Evaluate residual issues.
- Document the closure decision.
- Update the control register.

This methodology should be tailored to the control type, assessment objective, and applicable requirements.

### 14. AI Control Testing Procedures

The assessor selects testing procedures according to the nature of the control and the evidence required.

**Inspection**

Examine documents, records, configurations, approvals, or other evidence.

Example: Inspect the AI inventory and verify that required fields are populated for selected systems.

**Inquiry**

Ask relevant personnel to explain the control and its execution.

Example: Ask the AI governance owner how unregistered AI applications are identified.

**Observation**

Observe a control activity being performed.

Example: Observe a system owner completing the prescribed pre-deployment review.

**Reperformance**

Independently repeat a control activity or calculation.

Example: Reperform a documented classification analysis using the recorded facts and applicable criteria.

**Configuration testing**

Inspect or test technical settings.

Example: Verify that an approved access restriction is enabled in the production environment.

**Data analysis**

Analyze records or logs to identify exceptions, anomalies, or patterns.

Example: Reconcile AI inventory records against procurement and cloud-application records.

**Sampling**

Select items from a defined population for detailed testing.

Example: Select approved AI deployments from the assessment period and inspect their associated records.

A robust test may combine several procedures. Inquiry alone is generally insufficient when the objective requires evidence that a control was actually performed.

### 15. AI Control Testing Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria define the conditions that must be satisfied for a control test to pass.

Criteria should be established before the test result is evaluated.

Examples include:

- Every in-scope AI system has an assigned owner.
- Required approvals exist before deployment.
- Selected access restrictions match approved configurations.
- Required reviews were completed within the defined period.
- Identified exceptions were recorded and escalated.
- Required evidence is retrievable.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Corrective actions meet documented closure criteria.

Acceptance criteria may derive from law, applicable standards, contracts, approved policies, or documented internal control requirements.

The assessor should clearly identify the source of each criterion.

For example, a quarterly review frequency may be an internal policy requirement rather than a universal statutory requirement.

Where a criterion is ambiguous or lacks an objective measurement, the assessor should resolve the issue before relying on the test result.

### 16. AI Control Testing Population Definition

A testing population is the complete set of items to which the test procedure is intended to apply.

Examples include:

- AI systems deployed during a period
- All approved model changes
- All monthly monitoring reviews
- All AI incidents recorded during a defined period
- All supplier assessments completed during a period
- All users with access to a particular AI service
- All exceptions approved by a designated authority

The population must be sufficiently complete and relevant to the test objective.

For example, sampling only systems already listed in an AI inventory cannot independently establish that the inventory includes every AI system in use.

Population completeness may require reconciliation against independent sources such as procurement records, cloud application inventories, development repositories, or business-unit declarations.

Where the population cannot be established reliably, the assessor should document the limitation and evaluate its effect on the conclusion.

### 17. AI Control Testing Sampling Strategy

Sampling allows the assessor to examine selected items from a defined population when testing every item is not practicable or necessary.

Sampling strategies may include:

**Random sampling:** Select items using a random method to reduce selection bias.

**Stratified sampling:** Divide the population into relevant groups, such as high-risk and lower-risk systems, and select samples from each group.

**Risk-based sampling:** Prioritize items with higher risk, greater complexity, or a history of control failure.

**Judgmental sampling:** Select items using documented professional judgment.

**Targeted sampling:** Select items associated with known issues, recent changes, or specific control concerns.

The sampling method should be appropriate to the objective.

A targeted sample can help investigate a known problem but may not support a statistically representative conclusion about the entire population.

Where statistical sampling is used, the assessor should define the relevant population, sample design, confidence assumptions, tolerable deviation, and evaluation method.

Sampling limitations should be considered when drawing conclusions.

### 18. AI Control Testing Sample Size Considerations

Sample size should be determined by the assessment objective and the characteristics of the control.

Relevant factors include:

- Population size
- Control frequency
- Expected deviation rate
- Tolerable deviation rate
- Risk significance
- Reliability of available evidence
- Required assurance level
- Sampling methodology
- Nature of the control
- Consequences of an undetected exception

A control performed daily may require a different sampling approach from a control performed annually.

For an automated control, testing the relevant configuration and change-management process may provide stronger evidence than simply sampling a large number of routine transactions. However, automated controls may still require testing of configuration accuracy, access, change management, and relevant exception handling.

No universal sample size is appropriate for every AI control.

The assessor should document why the chosen sample is sufficient for the intended conclusion and expand testing when results indicate a higher-than-expected deviation rate or a material deficiency.

### 19. AI Control Testing Evidence Standards

Evidence standards establish the quality and sufficiency expected of information used to support test conclusions.

Evidence should be evaluated for:

- **Relevance:** Does it address the control objective?
- **Reliability:** Is the source trustworthy?
- **Completeness:** Does it cover the required information?
- **Accuracy:** Are the records correct?
- **Currency:** Does it relate to the relevant period and system version?
- **Authenticity:** Can its origin be established?
- **Traceability:** Can it be linked to the relevant control and test?
- **Integrity:** Is it protected against unauthorized alteration?
- **Sufficiency:** Is enough appropriate evidence available to support the conclusion?

Evidence sources may include system logs, configuration exports, workflow records, approved documents, monitoring outputs, test results, and direct observations.

A screenshot may be useful evidence, but it may not establish historical operation, population completeness, or the authenticity of the underlying configuration.

Where evidence is incomplete or contradictory, the assessor should investigate the issue and document its effect on the test result.

### 20. AI Control Testing Workpapers

Workpapers record the testing performed and preserve the basis for the conclusion.

A standard workpaper should contain:

| Field               | Description                                          |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Test ID             | Unique test identifier                               |
| Control ID          | Identifier of the control tested                     |
| Control objective   | Intended control outcome                             |
| Risk addressed      | Relevant risk or requirement                         |
| Test objective      | Question the test is designed to answer              |
| Control owner       | Person or function responsible for operation         |
| Testing period      | Period covered by the test                           |
| Population          | Complete set of items considered                     |
| Sampling method     | Selection method and rationale                       |
| Sample size         | Number of items selected                             |
| Evidence references | Records, logs, documents, or configurations examined |
| Test procedure      | Steps performed by the assessor                      |
| Acceptance criteria | Expected result                                      |
| Actual result       | Observed outcome                                     |
| Exceptions          | Deviations and supporting evidence                   |
| Conclusion          | Pass, fail, qualified, or other defined result       |
| Reviewer            | Person responsible for review                        |
| Review date         | Date of review                                       |
| Finding reference   | Associated deficiency, if applicable                 |
| Limitations         | Constraints affecting the conclusion                 |

Workpapers should be sufficiently clear for a qualified reviewer to understand the procedure, evaluate the evidence, and determine whether the conclusion is justified.

Evidence and workpapers should be retained according to applicable legal obligations and organizational policies.

### 21. AI Control Testing Exception Identification

An exception occurs when the observed result differs from the defined acceptance criteria.

Examples include:

- Missing approval
- Incomplete inventory record
- Incorrect access configuration
- Overdue review
- Missing evidence
- Unresolved monitoring alert
- Incorrect classification record
- Unapproved model change
- Incomplete incident documentation
- Corrective action not completed as required

Not every exception necessarily represents a legal breach. Its significance depends on the applicable criterion, the facts, the control objective, and the surrounding circumstances.

The assessor should document the exception, determine whether it is isolated or systemic, and evaluate whether further testing is necessary.

Where an exception may indicate a material deficiency, the assessor should escalate it according to the approved methodology.

### 22. AI Control Testing Preliminary Conclusions

Preliminary conclusions summarize the results of a test before the findings are finalized and reported.

Common internal results may include:

- **Pass:** Evidence supports satisfaction of the defined criteria.
- **Fail:** Evidence establishes that one or more required criteria were not satisfied.
- **Partially effective:** Some criteria were satisfied while others were not.
- **Unable to conclude:** Evidence or scope limitations prevent a reliable conclusion.
- **Not applicable:** The control or criterion does not apply to the assessed circumstances.

These labels are illustrative internal conventions and should be defined consistently by the organization.

A test should not be marked as passed merely because the assessor did not find an exception. The evidence must be sufficient to support the conclusion.

Similarly, an inability to obtain evidence should not automatically be treated as proof that the control failed in operation. It may constitute an evidence deficiency, a control deficiency, or both, depending on the applicable criteria and circumstances.

### 23. AI Control Testing Finding Documentation

When a test identifies a deficiency, the assessor should record enough information to support investigation, remediation, and independent review.

A finding should include:

1. Finding identifier.
2. Affected control and system.
3. Applicable criterion.
4. Expected condition.
5. Actual condition.
6. Supporting evidence.
7. Potential cause, where established.
8. Potential consequence.
9. Significance or internal severity.
10. Required corrective action.
11. Responsible owner.
12. Target date.
13. Validation method.
14. Closure criteria.

For example:

**Control:** AI inventory completeness.

**Criterion:** All in-scope AI applications must be recorded in the approved inventory before production use, according to the organization's defined procedure.

**Observed condition:** Testing identified an AI application in production that was not present in the inventory.

**Potential consequence:** The application may not have been subjected to the required classification, risk assessment, or governance review.

**Corrective action:** Register the application, complete the applicable assessments, investigate how the omission occurred, and strengthen inventory reconciliation.

**Validation:** Verify the updated record, confirm completion of required assessments, and test whether the revised reconciliation control identifies comparable omissions.

The example illustrates an internal control-testing finding. Any conclusion about a statutory breach would require separate evaluation of the applicable legal requirements and facts.

### 24. AI Control Testing Remediation and Retesting

Remediation addresses a deficiency; retesting evaluates whether the corrective action has resolved it.

A complete remediation process should establish:

- The deficiency and underlying cause
- The corrective action
- The responsible owner
- The target date
- Any interim safeguards
- The evidence required for closure
- The retesting procedure
- The closure decision
- Any residual issue

The assessor should distinguish implementation of the action from effectiveness of the revised control.

For example, publishing a new AI inventory procedure demonstrates that a procedure was updated. It does not establish that the organization now identifies all relevant AI applications.

Retesting may require inspecting the updated process, reviewing subsequent records, reconciling independent data sources, and verifying that the control detects the type of omission previously identified.

A finding should be closed only when the defined closure criteria are met or when the organization's approved process explicitly permits another documented disposition.

### 25. AI Control Testing Reporting and Metrics

Control-testing results should be reported in a way that helps management understand coverage, effectiveness, deficiencies, and remediation progress.

Useful internal metrics may include:

- Percentage of planned control tests completed
- Percentage of controls passing defined criteria
- Number of deficiencies by control domain
- Number of high-priority findings
- Percentage of findings overdue
- Average time to remediate findings
- Repeat-finding rate
- Percentage of remediation actions independently validated
- Percentage of material systems with completed testing
- Number of tests with insufficient evidence
- Number of reassessments triggered by material changes

Each metric should have a defined calculation, source, reporting frequency, and accountable owner.

A high pass rate does not necessarily mean that the AI control environment is effective if the testing scope is narrow, the population is incomplete, or the acceptance criteria are weak.

Management reporting should therefore combine quantitative indicators with qualitative analysis of significant findings, systemic causes, coverage gaps, and residual risks.

### 26. AI Control Testing Quality Assurance

Quality assurance evaluates whether control testing has been performed consistently and whether the results are adequately supported.

Quality-review procedures may examine:

- Whether the control objective is clear
- Whether the acceptance criteria are appropriate
- Whether the population is complete
- Whether the sampling method is suitable
- Whether evidence is sufficient and relevant
- Whether test steps were performed as documented
- Whether exceptions were evaluated consistently
- Whether conclusions follow from the evidence
- Whether findings are substantiated
- Whether limitations are disclosed
- Whether remediation closure is justified

Review depth should reflect the significance of the control and the consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

Where a test involves complex AI technical behavior, legal interpretation, or high-impact decisions, specialist review may be appropriate.

Quality assurance should be documented, and material reviewer comments should be resolved before the final result is issued.

### 27. AI Control Testing Common Failure Modes

Common weaknesses in AI control testing include:

- Testing documentation without verifying actual implementation
- Relying exclusively on interviews
- Using an incomplete testing population
- Selecting samples without a documented rationale
- Failing to define acceptance criteria before testing
- Treating a model evaluation as a substitute for governance control testing
- Confusing control design with operating effectiveness
- Ignoring technical configuration changes
- Failing to investigate contradictory evidence
- Treating missing evidence as proof of a specific operational failure without further analysis
- Closing findings based only on management declarations
- Reporting internal severity ratings as formal legal classifications
- Applying outdated control criteria
- Failing to retest after remediation
- Making broad assurance claims from narrow testing
- Ignoring changes to systems, suppliers, or intended purposes

A mature testing program addresses these weaknesses through standardized methodology, qualified personnel, independent review, evidence standards, and disciplined remediation.

### 28. AI Control Testing Program Governance

The testing program should define how controls are selected, tested, reviewed, reported, and reassessed.

Program governance should establish:

- Control taxonomy and ownership
- Risk-based test planning
- Testing methodology
- Competence requirements
- Independence and objectivity expectations
- Evidence-handling procedures
- Workpaper standards
- Finding classification
- Escalation requirements
- Reporting arrangements
- Remediation tracking
- Quality assurance
- Methodology review
- Regulatory and system change monitoring

The program should coordinate with AI governance, Enterprise Risk Management, Information Security, Privacy, Compliance, Legal, Engineering, and Internal Audit.

Coordination reduces duplicated testing and improves coverage, but each function should retain clarity about its mandate and the conclusions it is authorized to make.

### 29. AI Control Testing Lifecycle Integration

AI control testing should be integrated into the AI lifecycle rather than performed only after a system has entered production.

Relevant testing points include:

**Planning and procurement:** Verify that ownership, classification, supplier requirements, and assessment responsibilities are addressed.

**Design and development:** Evaluate governance workflows, data controls, model-development safeguards, and required reviews.

**Validation and pre-deployment:** Test that required assessments, approvals, and technical safeguards are complete before release.

**Production operation:** Evaluate monitoring, access controls, incident processes, human oversight, and evidence retention.

**Modification:** Verify that relevant changes trigger review, approval, and reassessment.

**Retirement:** Evaluate whether access, data, model assets, dependencies, and records are handled according to applicable requirements.

The exact controls and testing frequency depend on the system, its use, applicable requirements, and the organization's risk methodology.

Lifecycle integration allows control deficiencies to be identified before they create avoidable operational or compliance exposure.

### 30. Core AI Control Testing Principle

AI control testing provides structured, evidence-based assurance that controls designed to govern AI risks are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating as intended.

Its effectiveness depends on clearly defined control objectives, suitable acceptance criteria, complete populations, defensible sampling, reliable evidence, reproducible testing procedures, independent review, and effective remediation.

The central testing sequence is:

**Risk or Requirement → Control Objective → Control Activity → Test Procedure → Evidence → Evaluation → Finding → Remediation → Retesting → Assurance Conclusion**

A successful control test establishes only what the defined scope, criteria, procedures, and evidence support. It does not automatically establish that an AI system is safe, that every relevant legal obligation has been met, or that future control performance is guaranteed.
