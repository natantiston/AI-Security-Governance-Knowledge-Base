# 19.08 — AI Control Testing

## Part 1 — AI Control Testing Foundations, Control Objectives, Test Design and Assessment Methodology

### 1. Introduction to AI Control Testing

AI control testing is the structured evaluation of controls designed to manage risks associated with artificial intelligence systems throughout their lifecycle.

It determines whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively to address defined risks, governance objectives, security requirements, regulatory obligations, and organizational policies.

AI control testing differs from general AI system evaluation. Model evaluation typically examines characteristics such as accuracy, robustness, fairness, or performance. Control testing examines whether the processes, technical safeguards, governance mechanisms, and operational activities intended to manage those characteristics are appropriately established and functioning.

For example, testing a model for discriminatory outcomes evaluates model behavior. Testing whether the organization has an approved fairness-testing procedure, performs required assessments, documents exceptions, and remediates identified disparities evaluates the associated control environment.

AI control testing may cover:

- AI governance and accountability
- AI inventory and classification
- AI risk assessment and treatment
- Data governance and quality management
- Model development and validation
- AI security architecture
- Privacy and data protection
- Human oversight
- Transparency and disclosure
- Third-party and supply-chain security
- AI incident management
- Regulatory compliance
- Monitoring and continuous improvement

The testing methodology should reflect the actual control objective, system context, applicable requirements, and risk exposure.

**Core principle:** An AI control is not demonstrated to be effective merely because a policy exists or a technical feature is available. Its design, implementation, operation, and supporting evidence must be evaluated against defined criteria.

### 2. Objectives of AI Control Testing

AI control testing provides evidence about whether the organization's AI governance and risk-management arrangements operate as intended.

The primary objectives are to:

1. **Verify control design:** Determine whether a control is appropriately designed to address its intended risk or requirement.
2. **Verify implementation:** Establish whether the control has been deployed or incorporated into the relevant process.
3. **Evaluate operating effectiveness:** Determine whether the control operates consistently and as intended during the assessment period.
4. **Identify control deficiencies:** Detect missing, ineffective, inconsistent, or inadequately evidenced controls.
5. **Validate compliance implementation:** Evaluate whether controls support the applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements.
6. **Assess risk mitigation:** Determine whether the control reduces the relevant risk to the intended extent.
7. **Support assurance conclusions:** Provide documented evidence for internal audit, compliance, risk management, and management reporting.
8. **Validate remediation:** Establish whether corrective actions have addressed previously identified deficiencies.
9. **Improve control maturity:** Identify opportunities to improve control design, ownership, monitoring, and evidence quality.
10. **Support lifecycle governance:** Confirm that relevant controls remain appropriate as AI systems, models, data, suppliers, and deployment contexts change.

The objective should be specific enough to determine the testing procedure, evidence requirements, acceptance criteria, and reporting approach.

A control test that merely confirms the existence of an AI governance policy does not establish whether the organization applies that policy to its actual AI systems.

### 3. AI Control Testing Versus AI Model Testing

AI control testing and AI model testing are related but distinct disciplines.

| Dimension             | AI control testing                                           | AI model testing                                                          |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary focus         | Controls, processes, and governance mechanisms               | Model behavior and technical characteristics                              |
| Main question         | Does the control operate as intended?                        | Does the model satisfy defined performance or safety criteria?            |
| Typical subject       | Policies, workflows, approvals, monitoring, safeguards       | Models, datasets, outputs, metrics                                        |
| Evidence              | Records, configurations, logs, approvals, test results       | Benchmark results, evaluation datasets, outputs, metrics                  |
| Typical methods       | Inspection, inquiry, observation, reperformance, sampling    | Benchmarking, statistical evaluation, adversarial testing                 |
| Main output           | Control conclusion and identified deficiencies               | Model evaluation results and identified limitations                       |
| Responsible functions | GRC, Internal Audit, Compliance, control owners, specialists | Data Science, ML Engineering, AI Safety, Security, independent evaluators |

The two disciplines should be integrated when model behavior is an input to a governance or technical control.

For example, an organization may test model robustness and separately test whether robustness testing is required before deployment, independently reviewed, documented, and repeated after material changes.

A successful model evaluation does not automatically establish that the surrounding governance controls are effective.

Likewise, an effective governance process does not automatically establish that the model itself satisfies every relevant technical criterion.

### 4. AI Control Testing Versus AI Risk Assessment

AI risk assessment identifies and analyzes risks associated with an AI system, use case, or organizational activity.

AI control testing evaluates whether specific controls intended to manage those risks are appropriately designed and operating.

The relationship is sequential but iterative:

```
AI SYSTEM AND USE CASE
          |
          v
     RISK ASSESSMENT
          |
          v
      RISK TREATMENT
          |
          v
     CONTROL DESIGN
          |
          v
   CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
          |
          v
      CONTROL TESTING
          |
          v
  DEFICIENCY IDENTIFICATION
          |
          v
    CORRECTIVE ACTION
          |
          v
      RETESTING
          |
          v
  RESIDUAL RISK REASSESSMENT
```

For example, a risk assessment may identify the possibility that employees enter confidential information into an unauthorized generative AI service.

Potential controls may include approved-tool restrictions, data-loss prevention, user guidance, access controls, and monitoring.

Control testing then evaluates whether these controls exist, whether they are configured appropriately, whether users and systems follow the required process, and whether the controls detect or prevent the relevant activity.

Risk assessment identifies the risk; control testing evaluates the response to that risk.

### 5. AI Control Testing Within the Three Lines Model

AI control testing may be performed by several organizational functions, each with different responsibilities.

**First line — Business and technology operations**

Responsibilities may include:

- Operating assigned controls
- Maintaining required records
- Performing operational checks
- Monitoring control failures
- Correcting deficiencies
- Providing evidence to assurance functions

**Second line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy, and AI Governance**

Responsibilities may include:

- Establishing control requirements
- Maintaining assessment methodologies
- Monitoring control implementation
- Conducting compliance reviews
- Challenging control-owner assertions
- Coordinating remediation oversight
- Reporting material control deficiencies

**Third line — Internal Audit**

Responsibilities may include:

- Independently evaluating control design and effectiveness
- Testing the adequacy of governance arrangements
- Reviewing risk-management processes
- Assessing the reliability of management reporting
- Reporting significant deficiencies
- Following up on agreed management actions

External auditors, independent assessors, and specialist testing providers may also perform defined assurance activities.

The organizational model should preserve appropriate independence and clarify who designs, operates, tests, approves, and independently evaluates each control.

A control owner should not be treated as an independent tester merely because the owner has performed a self-assessment.

### 6. AI Control Testing Principles

A defensible AI control-testing program should follow these principles.

**Risk-based testing:** Allocate testing effort according to the significance of the control, the risks addressed, and the consequences of failure.

**Defined criteria:** Establish what the control must achieve before evaluating whether it has achieved it.

**Evidence-based conclusions:** Support findings and conclusions with relevant, reliable, and sufficiently complete evidence.

**Reproducibility:** Document test procedures and results sufficiently for another qualified reviewer to understand the work and, where practicable, reproduce it.

**Independence and objectivity:** Manage conflicts of interest and ensure that testing conclusions are not improperly influenced by control owners.

**Lifecycle awareness:** Consider development, validation, deployment, operation, modification, and retirement where relevant.

**Technical and organizational coverage:** Evaluate technical safeguards alongside governance processes, accountability, training, and operational procedures.

**Proportionality:** Match testing depth to the risk, complexity, criticality, and applicable requirements of the AI system.

**Traceability:** Link test procedures and results to controls, risks, requirements, evidence, and remediation records.

**Continuous improvement:** Use findings, incidents, changes, and testing outcomes to improve the control environment.

These principles support consistent testing without assuming that every AI system requires the same tests or assurance depth.

### 7. AI Control Taxonomy and Unique Identifiers

A control taxonomy organizes controls into a consistent structure and assigns identifiers that support testing, reporting, and traceability.

An organization may group AI controls into domains such as:

- AI governance and accountability
- AI inventory and classification
- AI risk management
- Data governance
- Model lifecycle management
- AI security
- Privacy and fundamental rights
- Transparency and human oversight
- Third-party and supply-chain governance
- Incident management and resilience
- Regulatory compliance
- Monitoring and assurance

Each control should have a unique identifier, a clear title, and a defined objective.

Example internal identifiers:

| Control ID | Control domain      | Illustrative control                    |
| ---------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| AI-CT-001  | Governance          | AI system ownership and accountability  |
| AI-CT-002  | Inventory           | AI system inventory completeness        |
| AI-CT-003  | Classification      | Documented AI risk classification       |
| AI-CT-004  | Risk management     | AI risk assessment approval             |
| AI-CT-005  | Data governance     | Dataset quality and suitability review  |
| AI-CT-006  | Model lifecycle     | Independent model validation            |
| AI-CT-007  | Security            | AI access control enforcement           |
| AI-CT-008  | Transparency        | Required user disclosures               |
| AI-CT-009  | Human oversight     | Defined human intervention mechanisms   |
| AI-CT-010  | Monitoring          | Production monitoring and escalation    |

The taxonomy should be version-controlled. If a control is materially changed, the organization should preserve its history and document how the change affects previous test results.

### 8. AI Control Objectives

A control objective describes the outcome that a control is intended to achieve.

Control objectives should be specific, testable, and connected to a defined risk or requirement.

For example:

**Control objective:** Ensure that AI systems are identified, recorded, and reviewed before being introduced into production.

Potential control requirements may include:

- Defined inventory fields
- Assigned system ownership
- Identification of intended purpose
- Recording of provider and deployer roles where relevant
- Documented classification decisions
- Approval before deployment
- Periodic reconciliation with procurement and technology records
- Updates after material changes

The control objective describes the desired outcome, while the control activities describe how the organization achieves it.

The testing procedure then evaluates whether those activities are suitably designed and operating.

A control objective should not be written so broadly that almost any activity could be claimed to satisfy it.

### 9. AI Control Attributes

Control attributes are the characteristics that the assessor examines when testing a control.

Common attributes include:

**Ownership:** Is a responsible owner assigned?

**Authorization:** Are decisions approved by the appropriate authority?

**Frequency:** Is the control performed at the required or defined interval?

**Completeness:** Does the control cover the relevant population?

**Accuracy:** Are recorded decisions and results correct?

**Timeliness:** Is the control performed at the appropriate point in the lifecycle?

**Consistency:** Is the control applied according to defined procedures?

**Evidence:** Are records sufficient to demonstrate execution?

**Exception handling:** Are failures and deviations identified, recorded, and escalated?

**Change responsiveness:** Does the control respond to material system or regulatory changes?

**Segregation of duties:** Are conflicting responsibilities appropriately separated where necessary?

**Monitoring:** Is the control's performance reviewed?

The relevant attributes depend on the control objective. An access-control test, for example, may emphasize authorization, completeness, configuration, and exception handling. A legal classification test may emphasize accuracy, evidence, approval, and reassessment after material changes.

### 10. AI Control Design Assessment

Control design assessment evaluates whether the control, as specified, is capable of addressing the intended risk or requirement.

The assessor should determine:

1. What risk or requirement the control addresses.
2. Whether the control objective is sufficiently precise.
3. Whether the control activity is appropriate.
4. Whether responsibility is clearly assigned.
5. Whether timing and frequency are suitable.
6. Whether required inputs are available.
7. Whether the control produces usable evidence.
8. Whether exceptions are handled.
9. Whether the control addresses relevant changes.
10. Whether the control depends on other controls that may be missing or ineffective.

**Example: AI deployment approval**

A documented approval process may require the AI system owner to confirm that relevant assessments are complete before production release.

The design assessment should examine whether the process:

- Defines the required approvals
- Identifies the applicable assessments
- Prevents unauthorized release where necessary
- Records the approval decision
- Handles incomplete assessments
- Escalates exceptions
- Responds to material changes

If the process permits deployment without required assessments and has no effective exception mechanism, the control may be inadequately designed even if the approval form is completed consistently.

### 11. AI Control Implementation Assessment

Implementation assessment determines whether the control has been put into operation in the relevant environment.

A control may be documented but not implemented. Conversely, a technical safeguard may exist without an approved procedure or clearly assigned owner.

Implementation evidence may include:

- Approved procedures
- Configured system settings
- Workflow rules
- Access-control assignments
- Assigned control owners
- Operational logs
- Training records
- Completed approvals
- Monitoring dashboards
- Implemented technical safeguards

The assessor should verify that the evidence corresponds to the actual system, environment, and relevant period.

For example, a data-loss prevention rule documented in a design specification does not establish that the rule is enabled in the production environment.

Implementation testing establishes that the control exists and is deployed; it does not necessarily establish that it operates effectively over time.

### 12. AI Control Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness evaluates whether the control operated consistently with its design during the relevant period.

The assessment should establish:

- The period under review
- The relevant control population
- The control frequency
- The expected execution criteria
- The evidence required
- The testing procedure
- The exception criteria
- The conclusion methodology

For a monthly AI governance review, the assessor might inspect records for selected months, verify the review occurred, examine the evidence considered, and confirm that identified issues were appropriately handled.

For an automated access restriction, the assessor might inspect configuration settings, review relevant logs, and test whether unauthorized access attempts are blocked.

A control can be implemented but ineffective in operation because personnel fail to perform it, system configurations drift, evidence is incomplete, or exceptions remain unresolved.

### 13. AI Control Testing Methodology

A standardized methodology improves consistency across systems, control domains, and assessment teams.

A typical testing lifecycle includes:

**Phase 1 — Planning**

- Define objectives and scope.
- Identify risks and applicable criteria.
- Select controls for testing.
- Identify owners and evidence sources.
- Establish timing and resources.

**Phase 2 — Control understanding**

- Review the control description.
- Confirm the control objective.
- Understand the process and dependencies.
- Identify relevant systems and populations.
- Establish expected outcomes.

**Phase 3 — Test design**

- Select the test procedure.
- Define the sample or testing population.
- Establish acceptance criteria.
- Identify required evidence.
- Determine how exceptions will be evaluated.

**Phase 4 — Test execution**

- Collect evidence.
- Inspect records and configurations.
- Perform inquiry and observation.
- Reperform selected activities where appropriate.
- Record results and exceptions.

**Phase 5 — Evaluation**

- Compare actual results with criteria.
- Determine whether deficiencies exist.
- Assess the significance of exceptions.
- Identify evidence limitations.
- Document conclusions.

**Phase 6 — Reporting and remediation**

- Communicate findings.
- Assign responsible owners.
- Agree corrective actions.
- Establish target dates.
- Define validation criteria.

**Phase 7 — Retesting and closure**

- Obtain remediation evidence.
- Retest affected controls.
- Evaluate residual issues.
- Document the closure decision.
- Update the control register.

This methodology should be tailored to the control type, assessment objective, and applicable requirements.

### 14. AI Control Testing Procedures

The assessor selects testing procedures according to the nature of the control and the evidence required.

**Inspection**

Examine documents, records, configurations, approvals, or other evidence.

Example: Inspect the AI inventory and verify that required fields are populated for selected systems.

**Inquiry**

Ask relevant personnel to explain the control and its execution.

Example: Ask the AI governance owner how unregistered AI applications are identified.

**Observation**

Observe a control activity being performed.

Example: Observe a system owner completing the prescribed pre-deployment review.

**Reperformance**

Independently repeat a control activity or calculation.

Example: Reperform a documented classification analysis using the recorded facts and applicable criteria.

**Configuration testing**

Inspect or test technical settings.

Example: Verify that an approved access restriction is enabled in the production environment.

**Data analysis**

Analyze records or logs to identify exceptions, anomalies, or patterns.

Example: Reconcile AI inventory records against procurement and cloud-application records.

**Sampling**

Select items from a defined population for detailed testing.

Example: Select approved AI deployments from the assessment period and inspect their associated records.

A robust test may combine several procedures. Inquiry alone is generally insufficient when the objective requires evidence that a control was actually performed.

### 15. AI Control Testing Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria define the conditions that must be satisfied for a control test to pass.

Criteria should be established before the test result is evaluated.

Examples include:

- Every in-scope AI system has an assigned owner.
- Required approvals exist before deployment.
- Selected access restrictions match approved configurations.
- Required reviews were completed within the defined period.
- Identified exceptions were recorded and escalated.
- Required evidence is retrievable.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Corrective actions meet documented closure criteria.

Acceptance criteria may derive from law, applicable standards, contracts, approved policies, or documented internal control requirements.

The assessor should clearly identify the source of each criterion.

For example, a quarterly review frequency may be an internal policy requirement rather than a universal statutory requirement.

Where a criterion is ambiguous or lacks an objective measurement, the assessor should resolve the issue before relying on the test result.

### 16. AI Control Testing Population Definition

A testing population is the complete set of items to which the test procedure is intended to apply.

Examples include:

- AI systems deployed during a period
- All approved model changes
- All monthly monitoring reviews
- All AI incidents recorded during a defined period
- All supplier assessments completed during a period
- All users with access to a particular AI service
- All exceptions approved by a designated authority

The population must be sufficiently complete and relevant to the test objective.

For example, sampling only systems already listed in an AI inventory cannot independently establish that the inventory includes every AI system in use.

Population completeness may require reconciliation against independent sources such as procurement records, cloud application inventories, development repositories, or business-unit declarations.

Where the population cannot be established reliably, the assessor should document the limitation and evaluate its effect on the conclusion.

### 17. AI Control Testing Sampling Strategy

Sampling allows the assessor to examine selected items from a defined population when testing every item is not practicable or necessary.

Sampling strategies may include:

**Random sampling:** Select items using a random method to reduce selection bias.

**Stratified sampling:** Divide the population into relevant groups, such as high-risk and lower-risk systems, and select samples from each group.

**Risk-based sampling:** Prioritize items with higher risk, greater complexity, or a history of control failure.

**Judgmental sampling:** Select items using documented professional judgment.

**Targeted sampling:** Select items associated with known issues, recent changes, or specific control concerns.

The sampling method should be appropriate to the objective.

A targeted sample can help investigate a known problem but may not support a statistically representative conclusion about the entire population.

Where statistical sampling is used, the assessor should define the relevant population, sample design, confidence assumptions, tolerable deviation, and evaluation method.

Sampling limitations should be considered when drawing conclusions.

### 18. AI Control Testing Sample Size Considerations

Sample size should be determined by the assessment objective and the characteristics of the control.

Relevant factors include:

- Population size
- Control frequency
- Expected deviation rate
- Tolerable deviation rate
- Risk significance
- Reliability of available evidence
- Required assurance level
- Sampling methodology
- Nature of the control
- Consequences of an undetected exception

A control performed daily may require a different sampling approach from a control performed annually.

For an automated control, testing the relevant configuration and change-management process may provide stronger evidence than simply sampling a large number of routine transactions. However, automated controls may still require testing of configuration accuracy, access, change management, and relevant exception handling.

No universal sample size is appropriate for every AI control.

The assessor should document why the chosen sample is sufficient for the intended conclusion and expand testing when results indicate a higher-than-expected deviation rate or a material deficiency.

### 19. AI Control Testing Evidence Standards

Evidence standards establish the quality and sufficiency expected of information used to support test conclusions.

Evidence should be evaluated for:

- **Relevance:** Does it address the control objective?
- **Reliability:** Is the source trustworthy?
- **Completeness:** Does it cover the required information?
- **Accuracy:** Are the records correct?
- **Currency:** Does it relate to the relevant period and system version?
- **Authenticity:** Can its origin be established?
- **Traceability:** Can it be linked to the relevant control and test?
- **Integrity:** Is it protected against unauthorized alteration?
- **Sufficiency:** Is enough appropriate evidence available to support the conclusion?

Evidence sources may include system logs, configuration exports, workflow records, approved documents, monitoring outputs, test results, and direct observations.

A screenshot may be useful evidence, but it may not establish historical operation, population completeness, or the authenticity of the underlying configuration.

Where evidence is incomplete or contradictory, the assessor should investigate the issue and document its effect on the test result.

### 20. AI Control Testing Workpapers

Workpapers record the testing performed and preserve the basis for the conclusion.

A standard workpaper should contain:

| Field               | Description                                          |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Test ID             | Unique test identifier                               |
| Control ID          | Identifier of the control tested                     |
| Control objective   | Intended control outcome                             |
| Risk addressed      | Relevant risk or requirement                         |
| Test objective      | Question the test is designed to answer              |
| Control owner       | Person or function responsible for operation         |
| Testing period      | Period covered by the test                           |
| Population          | Complete set of items considered                     |
| Sampling method     | Selection method and rationale                       |
| Sample size         | Number of items selected                             |
| Evidence references | Records, logs, documents, or configurations examined |
| Test procedure      | Steps performed by the assessor                      |
| Acceptance criteria | Expected result                                      |
| Actual result       | Observed outcome                                     |
| Exceptions          | Deviations and supporting evidence                   |
| Conclusion          | Pass, fail, qualified, or other defined result       |
| Reviewer            | Person responsible for review                        |
| Review date         | Date of review                                       |
| Finding reference   | Associated deficiency, if applicable                 |
| Limitations         | Constraints affecting the conclusion                 |

Workpapers should be sufficiently clear for a qualified reviewer to understand the procedure, evaluate the evidence, and determine whether the conclusion is justified.

Evidence and workpapers should be retained according to applicable legal obligations and organizational policies.

### 21. AI Control Testing Exception Identification

An exception occurs when the observed result differs from the defined acceptance criteria.

Examples include:

- Missing approval
- Incomplete inventory record
- Incorrect access configuration
- Overdue review
- Missing evidence
- Unresolved monitoring alert
- Incorrect classification record
- Unapproved model change
- Incomplete incident documentation
- Corrective action not completed as required

Not every exception necessarily represents a legal breach. Its significance depends on the applicable criterion, the facts, the control objective, and the surrounding circumstances.

The assessor should document the exception, determine whether it is isolated or systemic, and evaluate whether further testing is necessary.

Where an exception may indicate a material deficiency, the assessor should escalate it according to the approved methodology.

### 22. AI Control Testing Preliminary Conclusions

Preliminary conclusions summarize the results of a test before the findings are finalized and reported.

Common internal results may include:

- **Pass:** Evidence supports satisfaction of the defined criteria.
- **Fail:** Evidence establishes that one or more required criteria were not satisfied.
- **Partially effective:** Some criteria were satisfied while others were not.
- **Unable to conclude:** Evidence or scope limitations prevent a reliable conclusion.
- **Not applicable:** The control or criterion does not apply to the assessed circumstances.

These labels are illustrative internal conventions and should be defined consistently by the organization.

A test should not be marked as passed merely because the assessor did not find an exception. The evidence must be sufficient to support the conclusion.

Similarly, an inability to obtain evidence should not automatically be treated as proof that the control failed in operation. It may constitute an evidence deficiency, a control deficiency, or both, depending on the applicable criteria and circumstances.

### 23. AI Control Testing Finding Documentation

When a test identifies a deficiency, the assessor should record enough information to support investigation, remediation, and independent review.

A finding should include:

1. Finding identifier.
2. Affected control and system.
3. Applicable criterion.
4. Expected condition.
5. Actual condition.
6. Supporting evidence.
7. Potential cause, where established.
8. Potential consequence.
9. Significance or internal severity.
10. Required corrective action.
11. Responsible owner.
12. Target date.
13. Validation method.
14. Closure criteria.

For example:

**Control:** AI inventory completeness.

**Criterion:** All in-scope AI applications must be recorded in the approved inventory before production use, according to the organization's defined procedure.

**Observed condition:** Testing identified an AI application in production that was not present in the inventory.

**Potential consequence:** The application may not have been subjected to the required classification, risk assessment, or governance review.

**Corrective action:** Register the application, complete the applicable assessments, investigate how the omission occurred, and strengthen inventory reconciliation.

**Validation:** Verify the updated record, confirm completion of required assessments, and test whether the revised reconciliation control identifies comparable omissions.

The example illustrates an internal control-testing finding. Any conclusion about a statutory breach would require separate evaluation of the applicable legal requirements and facts.

### 24. AI Control Testing Remediation and Retesting

Remediation addresses a deficiency; retesting evaluates whether the corrective action has resolved it.

A complete remediation process should establish:

- The deficiency and underlying cause
- The corrective action
- The responsible owner
- The target date
- Any interim safeguards
- The evidence required for closure
- The retesting procedure
- The closure decision
- Any residual issue

The assessor should distinguish implementation of the action from effectiveness of the revised control.

For example, publishing a new AI inventory procedure demonstrates that a procedure was updated. It does not establish that the organization now identifies all relevant AI applications.

Retesting may require inspecting the updated process, reviewing subsequent records, reconciling independent data sources, and verifying that the control detects the type of omission previously identified.

A finding should be closed only when the defined closure criteria are met or when the organization's approved process explicitly permits another documented disposition.

### 25. AI Control Testing Reporting and Metrics

Control-testing results should be reported in a way that helps management understand coverage, effectiveness, deficiencies, and remediation progress.

Useful internal metrics may include:

- Percentage of planned control tests completed
- Percentage of controls passing defined criteria
- Number of deficiencies by control domain
- Number of high-priority findings
- Percentage of findings overdue
- Average time to remediate findings
- Repeat-finding rate
- Percentage of remediation actions independently validated
- Percentage of material systems with completed testing
- Number of tests with insufficient evidence
- Number of reassessments triggered by material changes

Each metric should have a defined calculation, source, reporting frequency, and accountable owner.

A high pass rate does not necessarily mean that the AI control environment is effective if the testing scope is narrow, the population is incomplete, or the acceptance criteria are weak.

Management reporting should therefore combine quantitative indicators with qualitative analysis of significant findings, systemic causes, coverage gaps, and residual risks.

### 26. AI Control Testing Quality Assurance

Quality assurance evaluates whether control testing has been performed consistently and whether the results are adequately supported.

Quality-review procedures may examine:

- Whether the control objective is clear
- Whether the acceptance criteria are appropriate
- Whether the population is complete
- Whether the sampling method is suitable
- Whether evidence is sufficient and relevant
- Whether test steps were performed as documented
- Whether exceptions were evaluated consistently
- Whether conclusions follow from the evidence
- Whether findings are substantiated
- Whether limitations are disclosed
- Whether remediation closure is justified

Review depth should reflect the significance of the control and the consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

Where a test involves complex AI technical behavior, legal interpretation, or high-impact decisions, specialist review may be appropriate.

Quality assurance should be documented, and material reviewer comments should be resolved before the final result is issued.

### 27. AI Control Testing Common Failure Modes

Common weaknesses in AI control testing include:

- Testing documentation without verifying actual implementation
- Relying exclusively on interviews
- Using an incomplete testing population
- Selecting samples without a documented rationale
- Failing to define acceptance criteria before testing
- Treating a model evaluation as a substitute for governance control testing
- Confusing control design with operating effectiveness
- Ignoring technical configuration changes
- Failing to investigate contradictory evidence
- Treating missing evidence as proof of a specific operational failure without further analysis
- Closing findings based only on management declarations
- Reporting internal severity ratings as formal legal classifications
- Applying outdated control criteria
- Failing to retest after remediation
- Making broad assurance claims from narrow testing
- Ignoring changes to systems, suppliers, or intended purposes

A mature testing program addresses these weaknesses through standardized methodology, qualified personnel, independent review, evidence standards, and disciplined remediation.

### 28. AI Control Testing Program Governance

The testing program should define how controls are selected, tested, reviewed, reported, and reassessed.

Program governance should establish:

- Control taxonomy and ownership
- Risk-based test planning
- Testing methodology
- Competence requirements
- Independence and objectivity expectations
- Evidence-handling procedures
- Workpaper standards
- Finding classification
- Escalation requirements
- Reporting arrangements
- Remediation tracking
- Quality assurance
- Methodology review
- Regulatory and system change monitoring

The program should coordinate with AI governance, Enterprise Risk Management, Information Security, Privacy, Compliance, Legal, Engineering, and Internal Audit.

Coordination reduces duplicated testing and improves coverage, but each function should retain clarity about its mandate and the conclusions it is authorized to make.

### 29. AI Control Testing Lifecycle Integration

AI control testing should be integrated into the AI lifecycle rather than performed only after a system has entered production.

Relevant testing points include:

**Planning and procurement:** Verify that ownership, classification, supplier requirements, and assessment responsibilities are addressed.

**Design and development:** Evaluate governance workflows, data controls, model-development safeguards, and required reviews.

**Validation and pre-deployment:** Test that required assessments, approvals, and technical safeguards are complete before release.

**Production operation:** Evaluate monitoring, access controls, incident processes, human oversight, and evidence retention.

**Modification:** Verify that relevant changes trigger review, approval, and reassessment.

**Retirement:** Evaluate whether access, data, model assets, dependencies, and records are handled according to applicable requirements.

The exact controls and testing frequency depend on the system, its use, applicable requirements, and the organization's risk methodology.

Lifecycle integration allows control deficiencies to be identified before they create avoidable operational or compliance exposure.

### 30. Core AI Control Testing Principle

AI control testing provides structured, evidence-based assurance that controls designed to govern AI risks are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating as intended.

Its effectiveness depends on clearly defined control objectives, suitable acceptance criteria, complete populations, defensible sampling, reliable evidence, reproducible testing procedures, independent review, and effective remediation.

The central testing sequence is:

**Risk or Requirement → Control Objective → Control Activity → Test Procedure → Evidence → Evaluation → Finding → Remediation → Retesting → Assurance Conclusion**

A successful control test establishes only what the defined scope, criteria, procedures, and evidence support. It does not automatically establish that an AI system is safe, that every relevant legal obligation has been met, or that future control performance is guaranteed.

## Part 2 — AI Control Testing by Domain, Technical Validation, Security Controls and Regulatory Requirements

### 31. AI Governance Control Testing

AI governance control testing evaluates whether the organization has established and operates the governance mechanisms needed to direct, oversee, and manage its AI portfolio.

The assessment should examine whether governance responsibilities, decision rights, escalation paths, and oversight mechanisms are documented and implemented.

Key testing areas include:

- AI governance policies and standards
- Executive and board-level oversight
- AI governance committee responsibilities
- System ownership and accountability
- Risk acceptance authority
- AI system approval processes
- Exception management
- Governance reporting
- Policy review and maintenance
- Escalation of material AI risks

**Example test:** Select AI systems introduced during the assessment period and verify that the required governance approvals were obtained before deployment.

The assessor should confirm that the approvals were performed by authorized individuals, covered the relevant system and use case, and were supported by appropriate evidence.

A governance committee's existence does not establish that it exercises effective oversight. Testing should examine its actual decisions, actions, escalations, and follow-up activities.

### 32. AI Inventory Completeness and Accuracy Testing

AI inventory testing determines whether the organization can identify and maintain reliable records of its AI systems.

An incomplete inventory can undermine risk assessment, classification, regulatory applicability analysis, security monitoring, and assurance coverage.

Testing should examine whether the inventory includes relevant attributes, such as:

- System name and unique identifier
- Business owner and technical owner
- Provider and deployer roles, where applicable
- Intended purpose
- Deployment environment
- Model and application version
- Data categories and sources
- Business process supported
- Risk classification and rationale
- Relevant supplier dependencies
- Applicable assessments and approvals
- Current operational status
- Review date and reassessment triggers

**Testing procedure:**

1. Obtain the current AI inventory.
2. Identify independent sources of AI-system information.
3. Reconcile inventory records against selected sources.
4. Investigate systems found outside the inventory.
5. Verify the accuracy of selected inventory fields.
6. Inspect whether material changes are reflected in the inventory.
7. Document omissions, inaccurate records, and unresolved limitations.

Potential independent sources include procurement records, approved software catalogues, cloud application inventories, model registries, development repositories, and business-unit declarations.

Sampling only existing inventory entries may test record accuracy but will not independently establish inventory completeness.

### 33. AI Risk Classification Control Testing

Classification control testing evaluates whether AI systems are classified using appropriate criteria and whether classification decisions are documented, reviewed, and updated when necessary.

The applicable classification method depends on the organization's framework and the purpose of the assessment.

Testing may cover:

- Identification of intended purpose
- Identification of affected persons and processes
- Assessment of relevant legal criteria
- Documented classification rationale
- Review and approval
- Escalation of ambiguous cases
- Reassessment after material changes
- Traceability to authoritative requirements

For an EU AI Act assessment, the assessor should evaluate the applicable statutory definitions, high-risk criteria, prohibited-practice provisions, and relevant exceptions rather than relying solely on an internal risk score.

**Example test:** Select a sample of systems classified as not high-risk and independently review whether the recorded facts and rationale support that conclusion.

The assessor should also consider whether systems with materially changed purposes or deployment contexts were reassessed.

An internal classification label is not a substitute for the legal analysis required by the applicable framework.

### 34. AI Risk Assessment Control Testing

AI risk assessment controls are intended to ensure that relevant risks are identified, analyzed, evaluated, documented, and addressed.

Testing should determine whether the organization's process:

- Defines assessment criteria
- Identifies relevant risk scenarios
- Considers affected stakeholders
- Evaluates likelihood and impact where appropriate
- Records assumptions and limitations
- Assigns risk owners
- Establishes risk-treatment actions
- Obtains required approvals
- Tracks residual risk
- Triggers reassessment when relevant circumstances change

**Example test:** Select completed AI risk assessments and verify that identified risks have corresponding treatment decisions, assigned owners, and documented approval where required.

For a generative AI system, relevant risks might include confidential-data disclosure, prompt injection, inaccurate outputs, unauthorized tool use, and overreliance by users.

The assessor should verify that risk scenarios reflect the actual system architecture and intended use rather than relying exclusively on a generic AI risk checklist.

### 35. AI Risk Treatment Control Testing

Risk treatment controls establish how identified risks are addressed through mitigation, avoidance, transfer, or other approved responses.

Testing should examine whether:

- Treatment options are documented.
- Selected controls address the identified risks.
- Implementation responsibilities are assigned.
- Target dates are defined.
- Dependencies are understood.
- Residual risk is assessed.
- Required approvals are recorded.
- Overdue actions are escalated.
- Treatment effectiveness is evaluated.

**Example test:** Select high-priority AI risks and trace each risk from the risk register to the approved treatment plan, implemented controls, supporting evidence, and residual-risk decision.

Where management accepts residual risk, the assessor should verify that the decision was made by an authorized person and complies with applicable internal and legal requirements.

Risk acceptance does not authorize the organization to disregard a mandatory legal obligation.

### 36. AI Data Governance Control Testing

Data governance testing evaluates controls governing the selection, acquisition, preparation, use, protection, and management of data used by AI systems.

Relevant controls may cover:

- Data ownership
- Data provenance
- Data quality
- Data suitability
- Data access authorization
- Data classification
- Data lineage
- Data retention
- Data deletion
- Dataset versioning
- Data-quality issue management
- Restrictions on sensitive information

The exact requirements depend on the system, data-processing context, applicable legal provisions, and organizational policies.

**Example test:** Select a dataset used in an AI development or operational workflow and trace its source, approval, transformation history, quality checks, access restrictions, and version information.

The assessor should distinguish data-governance requirements from model-performance requirements. A dataset may have documented provenance while still being unsuitable for the intended purpose.

### 37. AI Data Quality Control Testing

Data-quality controls are intended to ensure that data is suitable for its defined use.

Testing may examine:

- Completeness

- Representativeness
- Duplicate records
- Missing values
- Label quality
- Data drift
- Documented acceptance thresholds
- Exception handling

**Example test:** Inspect the data-quality procedure and reperform selected validation checks on a sample of the relevant dataset.

For systems where data representativeness or bias is relevant, the assessor should evaluate whether the organization has defined suitable evaluation criteria and whether the checks are appropriate for the system's intended purpose.

A high aggregate data-quality score does not necessarily establish suitability for every use case or affected population.

### 38. AI Data Lineage Control Testing

Data lineage controls support traceability from data sources through transformations to datasets used in model development or production.

- Dataset versions
- Model-development use
- Relevant approvals
- Known limitations
- Retention and deletion events

**Example test:** Select a dataset version associated with a deployed model and trace it backward to its source and forward to the model or system that uses it.

The assessor should determine whether lineage records are sufficiently detailed to support investigations, reproducibility, and relevant legal or internal obligations.

Where lineage depends on automated pipelines, the assessor may inspect pipeline configurations, logs, version-control records, and access restrictions.

### 39. AI Model Development Control Testing

Model-development controls govern how models are designed, built, trained, configured, and prepared for evaluation.

- Review of material changes
- Release authorization

**Example test:** Select a model release and trace it to the approved development record, dataset version, relevant evaluation results, review evidence, and release decision.

The assessor should verify that the records relate to the actual model artifact deployed rather than a different development version.

Development documentation alone does not establish that the model satisfies the applicable technical or legal requirements.

### 40. AI Model Validation Control Testing

Model-validation controls are intended to ensure that models are evaluated against defined acceptance criteria before deployment or material use changes.

- Independent review where required
- Failed-test handling
- Approval of exceptions
- Validation after material changes

**Example test:** Select a model release and verify that the required validation activities were completed, failures were resolved or appropriately handled, and the release decision was supported by evidence.

Where independence is required by the organization's methodology, the assessor should verify that the reviewer was sufficiently independent of the development activity.

A validation report is not sufficient if it evaluates a different model version or omits material aspects of the intended deployment.

### 41. AI Model Change Management Control Testing

- Application code
- Inference settings
- External model providers
- Intended purpose
- Deployment context

The organization should define which changes require routine approval, enhanced assessment, or formal reassessment.

**Example test:** Select material changes made during the assessment period and verify that each change was recorded, risk-assessed, approved, tested, and released according to the applicable process.

For generative AI applications, a change to system instructions or connected tools may materially alter behavior even when the underlying model version remains unchanged.

The testing approach should therefore consider the entire AI application and its dependencies, not only the foundation model.

### 42. AI Model Registry and Version Control Testing

Model registry controls maintain reliable records of model artifacts, versions, status, ownership, and deployment relationships.

Testing should examine whether:

- Changes are traceable.
- Release approvals are recorded.
- Rollback arrangements are documented where appropriate.
- Access to registry functions is controlled.

**Example test:** Select a production AI service and verify that its deployed model version matches the approved release record and corresponding validation evidence.

Where model versions are managed by an external provider, the organization should determine what version information is available and whether its monitoring and change-management controls adequately address that limitation.

### 43. AI Access Control Testing

Access testing should consider both conventional application permissions and AI-specific capabilities, such as retrieval access, model administration, and permission to invoke connected tools.

### 47. Generative AI Prompt Injection Control Testing

The test should assess the surrounding technical controls, not merely whether the model refuses one specific prompt.

A single successful prompt-injection test does not establish comprehensive resistance to prompt injection.

### 60. AI Control Testing by Domain — Core Principle

AI control testing should evaluate controls in the context of the actual system, intended purpose, deployment architecture, legal role, and risk exposure.

The assessor should map each control to its objective, applicable criteria, expected evidence, test procedure, and acceptance criteria. Testing should then establish whether the control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

The principal domains covered in this part include governance, inventory, classification, risk management, data governance, model lifecycle, security, human oversight, transparency, monitoring, incident management, resilience, supplier governance, regulatory compliance, privacy, and fairness.

**Control-domain coverage does not automatically equal complete AI assurance.** The testing program must still determine which controls are applicable, whether the testing population is complete, whether the evidence is sufficient, and whether conclusions are limited to what the assessment actually establishes.

## Part 3 — AI Control Testing Execution, Advanced Test Techniques, Evidence Validation and Technical Assurance

### 61. AI Control Testing Planning and Scoping

Effective AI control testing begins with a clearly defined scope that identifies what will be tested, why it will be tested, and what conclusions the assessment is intended to support.

The testing scope should identify:

- AI systems and business processes under review
- Applicable control domains
- Relevant risks and requirements
- Assessment period
- Organizational and technical boundaries
- Responsible control owners
- Testing objectives
- Required evidence
- Testing resources and specialist skills
- Known limitations and dependencies

Scope decisions should be documented before execution. Changes to the scope should be approved and recorded where they materially affect the planned assurance conclusion.

**Example:** An assessment of an AI-powered customer-service application may include identity and access management, prompt-injection safeguards, data protection, output validation, incident monitoring, and human escalation. It may exclude the underlying foundation model's internal training controls if those are operated by an external provider and cannot be directly assessed.

The exclusion should be documented, and the assessor should determine whether alternative evidence or compensating controls are available.

A clearly defined scope prevents the final report from implying that controls were tested when they were not.

### 62. Risk-Based Control Test Planning

Risk-based test planning prioritizes controls according to the significance of the risks they address and the consequences of control failure.

Factors may include:

- Potential impact on individuals
- Business criticality
- Legal or regulatory significance
- Security exposure
- Data sensitivity
- System autonomy
- Scale of deployment
- External dependencies
- History of incidents or control failures
- Recent changes
- Reliability of existing evidence

A high-risk AI application may require deeper technical testing, more frequent reassessment, and greater independent scrutiny than a low-impact internal productivity tool.

Risk-based planning does not justify omitting mandatory controls or disregarding applicable legal obligations. It determines the appropriate testing approach within the permitted scope.

**Illustrative prioritization model**

| Priority | Characteristics                                                                      | Potential testing approach                                                       |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Material potential harm, highly consequential decisions, or severe security exposure | Enhanced technical validation, independent review, targeted and expanded testing |
| High     | Significant business, privacy, security, or compliance exposure                      | Detailed evidence inspection, risk-based sampling, validation of key safeguards  |
| Moderate | Manageable exposure with established controls                                        | Standard control testing and periodic reassessment                               |
| Low      | Limited exposure and relatively simple control environment                           | Proportionate testing with documented rationale                                  |

These categories are illustrative internal conventions, not universal statutory classifications.

### 63. AI Control Test Script Development

A control test script converts a control objective into repeatable testing instructions.

A well-designed script should identify the control, its objective, the relevant population, required evidence, testing steps, acceptance criteria, and the expected documentation.

**Example test script — AI deployment approval**

| Field               | Test specification                                                                                                          |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Test ID             | AI-TST-DEP-001                                                                                                              |
| Control ID          | AI-CT-006                                                                                                                   |
| Objective           | Verify that required approval occurs before production deployment                                                           |
| Population          | AI systems deployed during the assessment period                                                                            |
| Evidence            | Deployment records, approval workflow, assessment records, release logs                                                     |
| Procedure           | Select deployments and trace each release to its required approval and supporting assessments                               |
| Acceptance criteria | Required approvals and assessments are completed before deployment, subject to formally approved and permissible exceptions |
| Exception condition | Missing approval, incomplete required assessment, or deployment before authorization                                        |
| Workpaper output    | Sample details, evidence references, deviations, and conclusion                                                             |

The test script should be reviewed before execution to confirm that it addresses the intended control objective and uses appropriate criteria.

Scripts should be version-controlled when changes affect the test procedure or interpretation.

### 64. Walkthrough Testing for AI Controls

A walkthrough follows a process from initiation to completion to establish how a control operates in practice.

Walkthroughs are useful for understanding workflows, identifying control dependencies, and determining where evidence is generated.

For an AI model-release process, a walkthrough might trace:

1. A proposed model or application change.
2. The associated change request.
3. Required risk and security assessments.
4. Validation results.
5. Approval decisions.
6. Production deployment.
7. Post-deployment monitoring.

The assessor should observe the actual process where possible and compare it with documented procedures.

A walkthrough may provide evidence of process understanding and initial implementation. It does not necessarily establish operating effectiveness across the full assessment period.

Additional inspection, sampling, configuration testing, or reperformance may be required.

### 65. Inquiry and Corroboration

Inquiry obtains explanations from control owners, technical personnel, users, or other relevant stakeholders.

For AI controls, inquiry can help explain complex system behavior, identify dependencies, and clarify why particular procedures are performed.

However, inquiry should be corroborated when the test objective requires evidence of actual control operation.

For example, an AI platform owner may state that only approved models can be deployed. The assessor should seek supporting evidence such as deployment restrictions, approval records, access configurations, or logs.

Corroboration helps distinguish management representations from independently verifiable control evidence.

Where explanations conflict with technical records or documented procedures, the inconsistency should be investigated and documented.

### 66. Observation-Based AI Control Testing

Observation allows an assessor to see a control being performed.

Potential examples include:

- An operator reviewing an AI monitoring alert
- A reviewer completing a model validation checklist
- A security analyst investigating a suspicious tool invocation
- A data steward approving a dataset
- A business user completing a required human-review step

The assessor should document the activity observed, the personnel involved, the relevant system or process, and any deviations from the expected procedure.

Observation has limitations. Personnel may behave differently while being observed, and a single observed event does not demonstrate that the control operated consistently throughout the period.

Where appropriate, observation should be combined with historical records and sample-based testing.

### 67. Reperformance of AI Governance Controls

Reperformance independently repeats a control activity to determine whether the original result can be reproduced.

It is particularly useful when controls involve classification, calculations, eligibility decisions, risk scoring, or review of documented criteria.

**Example:** An organization classifies an AI application as not subject to a particular high-risk requirement. The assessor independently reviews the intended purpose, system characteristics, relevant statutory criteria, and documented rationale.

The assessor compares the independent result with the original classification decision and investigates any differences.

Reperformance should use the criteria applicable to the relevant assessment date and preserve a record of the inputs, methodology, and results.

For legal assessments, a difference between the assessor's interpretation and management's interpretation may require qualified legal review rather than an automatic conclusion that one party has violated the law.

### 68. Automated Control Testing

Automated control testing evaluates safeguards implemented through software, platform configurations, policy engines, workflows, or technical enforcement mechanisms.

Examples include:

- Automated access restrictions
- Mandatory approval gates
- Model-release policy checks
- Data-loss prevention rules
- API authorization
- Logging configuration
- Policy-based workload restrictions
- Automated detection and escalation

Testing should establish whether the relevant control is correctly configured, active, appropriately scoped, and protected against unauthorized modification.

The assessor should also consider whether the control depends on accurate input data, trusted identities, reliable policy evaluation, or other underlying services.

An automated control can operate consistently but still produce incorrect results if its rules or assumptions are defective.

### 69. AI Configuration Review and Drift Testing

Configuration review examines whether an AI system's deployed settings comply with approved security and operational requirements.

Configuration drift occurs when the actual state of a system diverges from its approved or expected state.

Relevant configuration items may include:

- Model version
- System instructions
- Tool permissions
- Retrieval-source access
- API authorization
- Logging settings
- Data-retention settings
- Network restrictions
- Safety filters
- Monitoring thresholds
- Deployment parameters

**Example test:** Compare the production configuration of an AI agent against its approved baseline and investigate unauthorized or unexplained differences.

Testing may use configuration exports, change records, infrastructure-as-code definitions, platform audit logs, or direct inspection.

The assessor should distinguish an authorized change from an uncontrolled deviation. A configuration difference is not automatically a deficiency if it is approved, documented, and consistent with applicable criteria.

### 70. AI Access Review Reperformance

Access review controls evaluate whether user and service permissions remain appropriate.

Reperformance can determine whether the review was based on a complete and accurate population and whether decisions were applied correctly.

A test may involve:

1. Obtaining the relevant access population.
2. Confirming its completeness against the system or identity platform.
3. Inspecting reviewer decisions.
4. Verifying whether inappropriate access was identified.
5. Tracing removal or modification requests to completion.
6. Reviewing unresolved exceptions.

For AI systems, the assessment should include permissions to access datasets, administer models, configure prompts, invoke tools, and perform consequential actions where relevant.

A completed access-review form does not establish effectiveness if the reviewer lacked sufficient information or identified access was never removed.

### 71. AI Logging Control Reperformance

Logging control reperformance evaluates whether defined events are generated, retained, and made available as required.

A suitable test may begin with a known event and trace it through the logging architecture.

For example, the assessor may initiate an authorized test action, verify the associated log entry, confirm the recorded identity and timestamp, and determine whether the event reaches the designated monitoring system.

Testing should consider:

- Event coverage
- Identity attribution
- Timestamp consistency
- Record completeness
- Log integrity
- Access restrictions
- Retention
- Alerting, where applicable
- Evidence retrieval

The test should be performed only within approved scope and authorization.

The absence of a log entry may indicate a logging failure, an incorrect event definition, an incomplete test setup, or a limitation in the evidence source. The cause should be investigated before assigning a final conclusion.

### 72. AI Monitoring Rule Testing

Monitoring rule testing determines whether configured detection rules identify the events or conditions they are intended to detect.

Potential test scenarios include:

- Unauthorized access attempts
- Unexpected model or configuration changes
- Abnormal API usage
- Excessive tool invocation
- Suspicious data access
- Repeated policy violations
- Unexpected error rates
- Monitoring threshold breaches

Testing should establish the expected trigger condition, whether the event is detected, whether an alert is generated, and whether escalation follows the defined procedure.

Where practical, the assessor should also evaluate false positives and false negatives.

A monitoring rule that generates an alert is not necessarily effective if alerts are not reviewed, if important events are missed, or if the rule is configured incorrectly.

### 73. AI Vulnerability Management Control Testing

Vulnerability-management controls identify, prioritize, remediate, and track technical weaknesses affecting AI systems and their dependencies.

Testing may examine:

- Asset coverage
- Vulnerability scanning
- Dependency and package identification
- Model-serving infrastructure
- Container images
- API endpoints
- External libraries
- Prioritization criteria
- Remediation deadlines
- Exception approval
- Retesting
- Reporting

For AI applications, testing may include vulnerabilities in the surrounding software, model-serving infrastructure, retrieval systems, plugins, connectors, and external dependencies.

**Example test:** Select vulnerabilities identified during the assessment period and verify that they were classified, assigned, remediated, or formally managed according to the applicable process.

The assessor should determine whether the vulnerability population includes relevant AI components rather than relying solely on traditional infrastructure inventories.

### 74. AI Security Test Evidence Validation

Security test evidence should be evaluated for relevance, authenticity, completeness, and traceability.

Evidence may include penetration-test reports, red-team results, vulnerability scans, configuration exports, and security-test logs.

The assessor should determine:

- Which system and version were tested
- What scope was authorized
- Which test methods were used
- What limitations applied
- Which findings were identified
- Whether findings were independently reviewed where appropriate
- Whether remediation was completed
- Whether retesting verified the correction

A report may be technically valid but insufficient for a particular control objective if it excludes relevant components or was performed before material system changes.

The assessor should avoid treating the presence of a security report as proof that every security control is effective.

### 75. AI Data Access and Leakage Control Testing

Data-access and leakage controls aim to prevent unauthorized disclosure of information through AI applications and their integrations.

Testing may examine:

- User and service permissions
- Retrieval access boundaries
- Data-loss prevention
- Prompt and response handling
- Logging of sensitive information
- External service restrictions
- Output filtering
- Data-retention settings
- Authorization of connected tools

**Example test:** Use approved test accounts with different access permissions to verify whether the AI application retrieves and discloses only information those accounts are authorized to access.

The assessor should include relevant retrieval sources and downstream integrations in the test scope.

A model's refusal to reveal information in one prompt does not establish that access boundaries are consistently enforced. Authorization should be enforced by reliable system controls rather than relying solely on model-generated behavior.

### 76. AI Agent Tool-Use Control Testing

Agentic AI systems may invoke tools, access resources, execute code, or initiate actions on behalf of users or other systems.

Tool-use controls should constrain these actions according to defined authorization and risk boundaries.

Testing may examine:

- Tool allowlists
- Identity and permission assignments
- Input validation
- Action authorization
- Transaction limits
- Human approval gates
- Execution logging
- Error handling
- Rate limits
- Separation between planning and execution
- Emergency shutdown or suspension mechanisms

**Example test:** Select a defined set of authorized and unauthorized tool actions and verify that the agent can perform permitted actions while prohibited actions are blocked by the relevant enforcement mechanisms.

Testing should consider indirect instruction sources, malicious retrieved content, and unexpected tool arguments where relevant.

A tool-use policy described in a prompt is not equivalent to a technically enforced authorization boundary.

### 77. AI Output Accuracy and Reliability Control Testing

Output-reliability controls address the risk that AI outputs are inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent, or unsuitable for their intended use.

Testing may examine:

- Defined quality criteria
- Evaluation datasets
- Grounding requirements
- Source attribution
- Confidence or uncertainty handling
- Human verification
- Error thresholds
- Escalation rules
- Version-specific evaluation
- Monitoring of degraded performance

**Example test:** Select a sample of outputs from a defined use case and evaluate them against a pre-established reference standard or documented acceptance criteria.

The testing method should reflect the nature of the output. A factual retrieval system, a classification model, and a creative-generation application may require different evaluation procedures.

The assessor should document the evaluation dataset, scoring method, limitations, and results.

A small sample of accurate outputs cannot establish universal accuracy or guarantee future performance.

### 78. AI Hallucination Management Control Testing

Hallucination-management controls aim to reduce the likelihood or impact of unsupported or fabricated outputs in contexts where factual reliability matters.

Potential controls include:

- Retrieval from approved sources
- Citation or provenance requirements
- Output validation
- Confidence thresholds
- Human verification
- Restrictions on high-consequence use
- Escalation of uncertain answers
- User guidance
- Monitoring of factual errors

**Example test:** Construct an approved test set containing answerable questions, ambiguous questions, and questions for which the approved sources provide insufficient information.

Evaluate whether the application follows its defined behavior for each category, including whether it identifies uncertainty or refrains from unsupported assertions where required.

Testing should use predefined acceptance criteria and representative scenarios.

No single hallucination test proves that the system will never generate incorrect information.

### 79. AI Fairness Control Reperformance

Fairness-control reperformance independently evaluates selected fairness analyses or checks.

The assessor should establish:

- The intended purpose of the system
- The populations considered
- The relevant outcome or decision
- The selected fairness criteria
- The data and evaluation period
- The metrics and assumptions
- The acceptance thresholds
- The interpretation of results
- The treatment of identified disparities

**Example test:** Recalculate selected fairness metrics using the documented dataset and methodology, then compare the results with the original report.

If the original assessment identifies material differences between groups, the assessor should verify whether the organization investigated the results and documented its decision.

The appropriate fairness analysis is context-dependent. Reperformance should evaluate the suitability of the chosen method rather than assuming that one metric is universally correct.

### 80. AI Privacy Control Reperformance

Privacy-control reperformance independently repeats selected checks associated with personal-data processing.

Potential procedures include:

- Verifying recorded processing purposes
- Inspecting access permissions
- Comparing retention settings with approved requirements
- Checking selected deletion records
- Tracing data-subject requests
- Reviewing data-sharing arrangements
- Inspecting relevant privacy assessment records

**Example test:** Select a sample of personal-data records subject to a documented deletion requirement and verify whether the relevant deletion process was completed and supported by appropriate evidence.

The assessor should consider whether deletion from one system is sufficient or whether relevant replicas, caches, backups, derived datasets, or downstream recipients require separate treatment under the applicable rules.

The test must reflect the actual technical architecture and applicable retention obligations.

### 81. AI Human Oversight Effectiveness Testing

Human-oversight testing evaluates whether human intervention mechanisms function as intended.

Testing may examine:

- Whether reviewers receive sufficient information
- Whether outputs are presented clearly
- Whether review deadlines are appropriate
- Whether reviewers have authority to intervene
- Whether override mechanisms function
- Whether decisions are recorded
- Whether escalation works
- Whether workload or automation bias undermines meaningful review

**Example test:** Select AI-assisted decisions that require human review and verify that the reviewer had the necessary information, performed the required assessment, and had a functional mechanism to reject or modify the proposed outcome.

Where the control depends on human judgment, testing should consider both procedural compliance and whether the process enables meaningful oversight.

A recorded click or approval is not necessarily evidence of effective human review.

### 82. AI Incident Response Simulation Testing

Incident-response simulations evaluate whether the organization can execute its documented AI incident procedures under defined scenarios.

Potential scenarios include:

- Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information
- Prompt injection leading to unintended tool use
- Compromise of an AI service credential
- Unexpected model behavior
- Malicious modification of a model or dataset
- Provider outage
- Failure of a critical AI-supported process
- Detection of a potentially reportable event

Testing should examine whether personnel identify the event, apply the correct escalation path, preserve relevant evidence, assess impact, implement containment, and document follow-up actions.

The assessor should review the exercise objectives, participants, scenario assumptions, observed performance, identified gaps, and corrective actions.

A tabletop exercise can test decision-making and coordination, but it does not by itself prove that technical containment mechanisms work. Technical controls may require separate validation.

### 83. AI Business Continuity and Recovery Testing

Recovery testing evaluates whether the organization can restore or continue critical AI-supported processes after disruption.

Testing may include:

- Restoration of configuration and application components
- Recovery of required data
- Validation of restored model artifacts
- Re-establishment of external dependencies
- Alternative operating procedures
- Recovery-time measurement
- Recovery-point validation
- Integrity checks
- User communication
- Post-recovery monitoring

**Example test:** Review a recovery exercise for an AI service and compare actual recovery results against approved recovery objectives.

Where an external model provider is essential, the assessment should consider provider outages, loss of connectivity, service discontinuation, and available fallback options.

A documented recovery plan does not establish that recovery will succeed within the intended timeframe.

### 84. AI Supplier Assurance Evidence Testing

Supplier assurance testing evaluates whether evidence supplied by an external AI provider is suitable for the organization's assurance needs.

The assessor should examine:

- Scope of the assurance report
- Systems and services covered
- Assessment period
- Exclusions and limitations
- Independent assessor qualifications
- Control exceptions
- Remediation status
- Subservice organizations
- Relevance to the organization's deployment
- Changes since the report date

**Example test:** Select a supplier assurance report relied upon by the organization and compare its scope with the AI service and controls actually used.

If the report excludes a critical service component, the organization should identify the resulting assurance gap and determine whether supplementary evidence or controls are necessary.

Supplier assurance is an input to the organization's risk assessment; it does not automatically demonstrate the organization's own compliance.

### 85. AI Regulatory Obligation-to-Control Testing

This procedure verifies that selected legal or regulatory obligations are translated into appropriate organizational controls.

The assessor should trace:

1. The authoritative legal source.
2. The applicable provision.
3. The applicability analysis.
4. The responsible organizational role.
5. The mapped control objective.
6. The implemented control activity.
7. The supporting evidence.
8. The test procedure and result.
9. Any identified deficiency.
10. The remediation or compliance conclusion.

For EU AI Act testing, the assessor should use the applicable consolidated legal text and verify the provisions and dates relevant to the specific system.

An internal mapping table may facilitate traceability, but the mapping must not misrepresent internal control identifiers as statutory article numbers.

Where legal applicability or interpretation remains uncertain, the uncertainty should be documented and escalated to appropriately qualified personnel.

### 86. AI Control Dependency Testing

Control dependency testing examines whether a control relies on other controls, services, or processes that are necessary for it to operate effectively.

Examples include:

- AI access restrictions relying on accurate identity records
- Monitoring relying on complete event logging
- Human oversight relying on timely and understandable output
- Deployment approval relying on complete validation evidence
- Data-loss prevention relying on correct data classification
- Supplier assurance relying on accurate service boundaries

**Example test:** An AI application is configured to restrict retrieval by user identity. The assessor tests whether the identity is propagated correctly through the application, retrieval service, and underlying data source.

A control may appear effective in isolation while failing because an upstream dependency is missing or unreliable.

The assessment should identify material dependencies, test them where appropriate, and document any limitations in the resulting conclusion.

### 87. AI Control Failure Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis seeks to identify why a control deficiency occurred rather than simply documenting the immediate failure.

Potential causes include:

- Unclear control ownership
- Inadequate control design
- Incomplete system inventory
- Insufficient training
- Excessive permissions
- Inadequate automation
- Weak change management
- Incomplete monitoring
- Supplier limitations
- Conflicting procedures
- Resource constraints
- Poor evidence retention
- Ineffective management oversight

The assessor should distinguish an established cause from a hypothesis.

For example, an AI application missing from the inventory may result from an ineffective procurement integration, unclear ownership, an unauthorized deployment, or an inadequate discovery process.

Corrective actions should address the identified cause where possible. Merely adding the omitted application may correct the individual record without preventing recurrence.

### 88. AI Control Exception Evaluation

Exceptions should be evaluated in relation to the control objective, applicable criteria, and potential consequences.

The assessor should determine:

- What criterion was not satisfied
- Whether the exception is isolated or recurring
- Which systems or processes are affected
- Whether compensating controls exist
- Whether the exception indicates a wider control failure
- Whether additional testing is needed
- Whether escalation is required
- Whether the issue may affect compliance or safety

The assessment should distinguish between a documented exception permitted by the control design and an unauthorized deviation.

A compensating control may reduce risk, but its existence should be verified rather than assumed.

The final conclusion should reflect the evidence and the approved assessment methodology.

### 89. AI Control Test Result Aggregation

Test results may be aggregated to support reporting across systems, domains, business units, and the enterprise.

Aggregation should preserve the meaning of the underlying results.

The assessor should consider:

- The number and significance of controls tested
- Differences in control applicability
- System risk profiles
- Testing coverage
- Evidence limitations
- Repeated deficiencies
- Dependencies between controls
- The period covered
- The effect of unresolved findings

A single enterprise pass rate can obscure material deficiencies in a critical system or control domain.

For example, a high proportion of passing low-risk controls should not automatically offset a serious failure in an essential access-control or incident-response mechanism.

Reports should combine quantitative summaries with analysis of significant exceptions and limitations.

### 90. AI Control Testing Advanced Execution Principle

Advanced AI control testing combines repeatable procedures with technical understanding, risk-based judgment, and disciplined evidence evaluation.

The assessor should select methods that are appropriate to the control objective and should not assume that one testing method is sufficient for every control.

The core execution model is:

**Scope Definition → Test Design → Evidence Collection → Independent Verification → Exception Evaluation → Root-Cause Analysis → Remediation → Retesting → Documented Conclusion**

Technical tests should be authorized, reproducible where practicable, and bounded by a defined scope. Legal and regulatory conclusions should be based on applicable authoritative requirements, while technical conclusions should reflect the actual architecture, configuration, and behavior tested.

The objective is not merely to demonstrate that testing occurred. It is to produce a defensible conclusion supported by appropriate evidence and clearly stated limitations.
