# 19.05 — ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration

## Part 1 — Foundations, ISMS Integration, and AI Security Governance

### 1. Introduction to ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration

ISO/IEC 27001 is an international standard specifying requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

When an organization develops, deploys, purchases, or operates artificial intelligence systems, its information security risks may extend beyond conventional IT infrastructure to include AI models, training data, inference data, prompts, embeddings, model interfaces, AI agents, and third-party AI services.

Integrating AI into an ISMS allows an organization to manage relevant AI-related information security risks through established governance, risk assessment, risk treatment, control implementation, monitoring, and continual improvement processes.

The objective is not to replace ISO/IEC 27001 with a separate AI security framework. Instead, the organization determines how its existing ISMS should address AI-related risks and whether additional controls or specialized assessment methods are required.

### 2. Purpose of ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration

The primary purpose of integration is to extend an organization's information security management practices to cover relevant AI assets, systems, services, and dependencies.

An organization may already operate an ISO/IEC 27001-certified ISMS while introducing generative AI, machine learning, predictive analytics, or AI-enabled business applications. Without an appropriate integration process, these new technologies may introduce risks that existing inventories, risk assessments, and controls do not adequately address.

Integration helps ensure that AI-related information security risks are identified, evaluated, treated, monitored, and reviewed within the organization's established management system.

It also helps avoid creating disconnected governance processes that duplicate existing responsibilities without improving risk visibility or control effectiveness.

### 3. Understanding ISO/IEC 27001:2022

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 specifies requirements for an ISMS. Its structure establishes management-system requirements covering organizational context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, and improvement.

For AI integration, these requirements provide the organizational foundation for managing information security risks associated with AI systems.

The organization must understand its context, establish the ISMS scope, assign responsibilities, assess risks, implement risk treatment, monitor performance, conduct internal audits, perform management reviews, and address nonconformities.

The standard does not provide a complete AI-specific security engineering methodology. Organizations must therefore determine how their existing ISMS processes apply to AI and supplement them where necessary.

### 4. ISO/IEC 27001 Versus ISO/IEC 42001

ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 42001 address related but distinct management objectives.

ISO/IEC 27001 focuses on information security management, including protecting information confidentiality, integrity, and availability through a risk-based ISMS.

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS), addressing organizational management of AI-related considerations and risks.

An organization may use ISO/IEC 27001 to manage AI-related information security risks while using ISO/IEC 42001 to establish broader AI management governance.

The two standards can complement one another, but conformity with one does not automatically establish conformity with the other.

### 5. AI as an Information Security Risk Domain

AI systems introduce information security considerations that may not be adequately represented by conventional infrastructure-focused risk assessments.

Examples include unauthorized disclosure through model outputs, manipulation of model behavior, poisoning of training data, compromise of model artifacts, unauthorized use of AI tools, and exposure through third-party AI services.

These risks may affect confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

For example, an attacker who manipulates an AI model's training data may undermine the integrity of its outputs. An attacker who extracts confidential information through a generative AI interface may compromise confidentiality. An attack against an AI inference service may affect availability.

The ISMS should identify these risks according to the organization's actual AI use cases, architecture, threat environment, and business context.

### 6. The CIA Triad in AI Security

The confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA) triad remains a fundamental information security model when assessing AI systems.

**Confidentiality** concerns preventing unauthorized access to information, including prompts, training datasets, model inputs, generated outputs, embeddings, and connected knowledge sources.

**Integrity** concerns protecting information and system components against unauthorized or inappropriate modification. This includes datasets, model artifacts, model configurations, inference pipelines, and AI-generated information used in business processes.

**Availability** concerns ensuring that authorized users can access AI services and supporting information when required.

The assessment should identify which CIA objectives are relevant to each AI asset and determine whether existing controls adequately address the associated risks.

### 7. AI Assets Within the ISMS

An ISMS asset inventory should account for AI-related assets that are relevant to the organization's information security objectives and risk exposure.

Potential assets include:

- Training, validation, and inference datasets.
- Machine learning models and model weights.
- Generative AI models and model endpoints.
- Prompts, system instructions, and configuration files.
- Embedding databases and vector stores.
- Retrieval-augmented generation knowledge sources.
- AI agents and connected tools.
- Model registries and development pipelines.
- AI application programming interfaces.
- AI infrastructure, cloud platforms, and supporting services.
- Logs, evaluation results, and monitoring data.
- Third-party AI services and contractual dependencies.

The organization should determine how these assets are identified, classified, owned, protected, and monitored under its ISMS.

### 8. AI Asset Classification

Asset classification determines the appropriate level of protection based on an asset's characteristics, business value, sensitivity, and potential impact if compromised.

For AI systems, classification may consider the sensitivity of training data, confidentiality of model artifacts, business criticality of inference services, and consequences of manipulated outputs.

A model trained on public information may still be sensitive if it incorporates proprietary business logic or supports a critical business process.

Likewise, embeddings should not automatically be considered harmless simply because they are not stored as ordinary readable documents. Their sensitivity depends on the information represented and the possibility of unauthorized disclosure or reconstruction.

Classification should inform access restrictions, encryption, retention, monitoring, and other protective measures.

### 9. AI Asset Ownership and Accountability

Every material AI asset or system should have clearly assigned ownership and accountability.

The business owner is responsible for the business purpose and operational decisions associated with the AI system. Technical owners may be responsible for development, configuration, deployment, and maintenance. Information security personnel support risk assessment and security control design.

Data owners may define acceptable data use and access requirements, while privacy, legal, procurement, and compliance functions contribute according to the system's context.

The ISMS should define responsibilities for risk acceptance, control operation, incident escalation, and change approval.

Where an AI service is supplied by a third party, internal accountability for the organization's use of that service must remain clear.

### 10. Determining the ISMS Scope for AI

The ISMS scope defines the organizational boundaries and activities covered by the information security management system.

When integrating AI, the organization should determine which AI systems, business processes, locations, information assets, technology platforms, and external dependencies fall within the scope.

For example, an ISMS covering a customer-service platform may need to include its AI chatbot, model provider, retrieval database, customer data, integration APIs, and supporting cloud infrastructure where these are relevant to the defined scope.

Excluding an AI component without considering its dependencies may create an incomplete view of information security risks.

The scope should be documented and reviewed when material changes to the organization's technology or operating environment occur.

### 11. AI Use-Case Discovery

AI integration should begin by identifying where AI is used, developed, procured, or embedded within the organization.

AI may be introduced through formal development projects, enterprise software, cloud services, productivity applications, customer-facing products, and employee use of public AI tools.

The organization should establish a process for discovering AI use cases across business and technical functions.

Potential sources include procurement records, software inventories, cloud-service records, architecture documentation, development repositories, business interviews, and employee declarations.

Incomplete discovery can undermine the ISMS because unregistered AI usage may bypass security review, data protection requirements, access controls, and incident-response procedures.

### 12. Shadow AI and ISMS Governance

Shadow AI refers to AI tools or services used without appropriate organizational authorization, visibility, or governance.

Examples include employees submitting confidential information to public AI platforms, connecting unapproved AI extensions to enterprise applications, or deploying personal AI agents against internal resources.

Shadow AI may introduce data leakage, unauthorized processing, unclear retention, third-party dependency, and access-control risks.

The ISMS should establish appropriate acceptable-use policies, approved-service processes, user awareness, monitoring where lawful and proportionate, and exception procedures.

The objective is not necessarily to prohibit every AI tool. It is to ensure that AI usage is visible, appropriately authorized, and subject to risk-based controls.

### 13. AI System Boundaries and Trust Boundaries

System boundaries define the components and interfaces included in an AI system. Trust boundaries identify where information or commands cross between components with different security assumptions or levels of trust.

For a generative AI application, boundaries may exist between the user interface, model endpoint, retrieval database, enterprise identity service, external model provider, and connected tools.

Each boundary may introduce authentication, authorization, data-handling, validation, and monitoring requirements.

An ISMS assessment should determine whether relevant boundaries are documented and whether controls are designed around the actual information flows.

Poorly defined boundaries can result in excessive permissions, inadequate input validation, unauthorized data retrieval, or insufficient oversight of third-party services.

### 14. AI Data Flows and Information Security

AI data-flow analysis documents how information enters, moves through, and leaves an AI system.

A typical workflow may include source data collection, preprocessing, model training or configuration, inference, output generation, logging, and storage.

For retrieval-augmented generation, the workflow may additionally include document ingestion, indexing, embedding generation, retrieval, context assembly, and response generation.

The ISMS should consider where information is stored, which components process it, who can access it, and whether it crosses organizational or geographic boundaries.

Data-flow diagrams help identify exposure points and support decisions concerning encryption, access control, retention, monitoring, and third-party risk management.

### 15. AI Information Security Risk Assessment

ISO/IEC 27001 requires the organization to establish and apply an information security risk assessment process.

AI integration requires that this process adequately covers relevant AI-related assets, threats, vulnerabilities, consequences, and existing controls.

The organization should define its risk assessment criteria, including how risk is identified, analyzed, evaluated, and prioritized.

AI-related scenarios may include model theft, training-data poisoning, unauthorized disclosure through outputs, prompt injection, compromised model dependencies, and disruption of AI services.

The assessment method should remain consistent with the organization's documented ISMS approach while accommodating the specific characteristics of AI systems.

### 16. AI Risk Identification

Risk identification establishes what could happen, how it could happen, and which assets or business objectives could be affected.

For AI systems, the organization should consider threats to models, data, infrastructure, interfaces, connected tools, and downstream business processes.

Examples include unauthorized access to model artifacts, malicious modification of training data, leakage of sensitive prompts, abuse of an AI agent's permissions, and compromise of a third-party model endpoint.

Risk identification should also consider relevant operational conditions, such as model updates, changing datasets, new integrations, and changes in how users rely on outputs.

The resulting scenarios should be specific enough to support meaningful analysis and risk treatment.

### 17. AI Risk Analysis

Risk analysis estimates the likelihood and consequences of identified information security risk scenarios.

The organization should apply its defined methodology, using qualitative or quantitative analysis as appropriate.

For example, the risk of sensitive-data disclosure through a generative AI service may depend on the sensitivity of the information, access restrictions, model and application behavior, provider data-handling practices, and effectiveness of monitoring.

AI uncertainty can make precise probability estimates difficult. The organization should document material assumptions and avoid presenting uncertain estimates as exact measurements.

Risk analysis should provide enough information for decision-makers to compare risks and determine appropriate treatment priorities.

### 18. AI Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares analyzed risks against the organization's established risk criteria to determine which risks require treatment and which may be accepted.

The evaluation should consider business impact, information sensitivity, threat exposure, control effectiveness, and applicable obligations.

For example, an AI tool processing public information for an internal brainstorming exercise may present a different risk profile from an AI system processing confidential customer records.

Risk evaluation should also consider dependencies and potential cascading consequences.

The organization should document decisions and ensure that risk acceptance is performed by authorized personnel within established boundaries.

### 19. AI Risk Treatment

Risk treatment determines how the organization will modify, avoid, share, or retain identified risks in accordance with its risk management approach.

AI risk treatment may involve restricting data access, selecting a different AI provider, introducing secure gateways, reducing agent permissions, validating outputs, improving monitoring, or prohibiting a particular use case.

The selected treatment should address the actual risk scenario rather than merely adding a generic security control.

For example, if a retrieval system can expose documents across access boundaries, remediation should address authorization enforcement and retrieval behavior, not only encrypt the database.

Treatment decisions should be documented, assigned to accountable owners, and tracked through implementation.

### 20. Statement of Applicability and AI Controls

The Statement of Applicability (SoA) is a key ISMS document identifying necessary controls, explaining their inclusion, recording implementation status, and justifying exclusions from the reference set of controls.

In ISO/IEC 27001:2022, Annex A provides a reference set of information security controls aligned with the risk treatment process.

When AI-related risks are identified, the organization should determine which Annex A controls are necessary to address them.

Relevant controls may include asset management, access control, information classification, secure development, supplier security, logging, incident management, and change management.

The organization should not assume that every Annex A control is automatically applicable to every AI system, nor should it exclude relevant controls without a defensible rationale.

### 21. ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 4 — Context of the Organization

Clause 4 addresses understanding the organization and its context, the needs and expectations of interested parties, the scope of the ISMS, and the ISMS itself.

For AI integration, the organization should consider how AI adoption affects its business objectives, information security risks, stakeholder expectations, and operational dependencies.

Relevant interested parties may include customers, employees, regulators, business partners, cloud providers, model providers, and other parties whose information or services are involved.

The organization should determine which AI-related requirements are relevant to the ISMS.

The resulting context analysis should inform the ISMS scope, risk assessment, and governance arrangements rather than treating AI as an isolated technology issue.

### 22. Clause 4.1 — Understanding the Organization and Its Context

Clause 4.1 requires the organization to determine relevant internal and external issues that affect its ability to achieve the intended outcomes of the ISMS.

AI-related issues may include adoption of generative AI, dependence on external model providers, changes to data processing, new threat patterns, evolving technology, and increased automation of business processes.

Internal issues may include skills, governance maturity, legacy architecture, data quality, and existing security capabilities.

External issues may include supplier concentration, contractual conditions, applicable legislation, threat activity, and changes in the AI services market.

The organization should identify issues that are relevant to information security and incorporate them into its ongoing ISMS planning and review.

### 23. Clause 4.2 — Needs and Expectations of Interested Parties

Clause 4.2 requires the organization to determine relevant interested parties and their information security-related requirements.

For AI systems, interested parties may have expectations concerning confidentiality, integrity, availability, transparency of data handling, service reliability, and protection of sensitive information.

Customers may require assurances that confidential business information is not disclosed to unauthorized parties. Suppliers may impose restrictions on how their data can be processed by AI systems.

Regulators and contractual partners may impose additional requirements, depending on the organization's circumstances.

The ISMS should identify which requirements are relevant and determine how they will be addressed through policies, risk treatment, controls, and monitoring.

### 24. Clause 4.3 — Determining the ISMS Scope

Clause 4.3 requires the organization to determine the boundaries and applicability of the ISMS.

AI integration should consider organizational units, locations, interfaces, technologies, information assets, and dependencies that affect information security within the defined scope.

For example, an organization may need to include an AI service even when the model is hosted by a third party, because the organization still uses the service to process information within its business activities.

The scope should accurately describe the relevant boundaries and interfaces.

The organization should review scope changes when AI adoption, outsourcing, business processes, or system architecture materially changes.

### 25. Clause 4.4 — Information Security Management System

Clause 4.4 requires the organization to establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve the ISMS, including the processes needed and their interactions.

AI integration should therefore be incorporated into relevant ISMS processes rather than managed solely through isolated project approvals.

Processes may include AI asset discovery, security risk assessment, procurement review, secure development, access management, incident response, change management, and internal audit.

The organization should establish how these processes interact and who is responsible for them.

For example, a new AI procurement process should feed relevant information into the asset inventory, risk assessment, supplier review, and control implementation activities.

### 26. Clause 5 — Leadership

Clause 5 addresses leadership and commitment, information security policy, and organizational roles, responsibilities, and authorities.

For AI integration, leadership should ensure that information security objectives and responsibilities account for relevant AI use cases.

Management should establish appropriate accountability, provide resources, communicate the importance of information security, and ensure that the ISMS achieves its intended outcomes.

AI-related responsibilities may span business leadership, technology, information security, data governance, privacy, procurement, and risk management.

The organization should avoid assigning AI security responsibility exclusively to technical teams when decisions about data use, business risk, procurement, and acceptable risk require management involvement.

### 27. Clause 5.1 — Leadership and Commitment

Clause 5.1 addresses top management's commitment to the ISMS.

AI integration requires management to support the identification and treatment of relevant information security risks arising from AI adoption.

Leadership may need to approve governance arrangements, allocate resources, resolve competing priorities, and ensure that AI projects comply with the organization's security requirements.

For example, a business unit may wish to deploy an AI service quickly, while the security review identifies unresolved data leakage risks. Leadership should ensure that the organization follows its risk acceptance and deployment approval processes.

Management commitment should be demonstrated through decisions, resource allocation, oversight, and corrective action—not merely through policy statements.

### 28. Clause 5.2 — Information Security Policy

Clause 5.2 requires an information security policy appropriate to the organization, including commitments relevant to the ISMS.

The policy may be supported by AI-specific policies, standards, or procedures addressing approved AI use, sensitive-data handling, model development, AI supplier security, and security incident reporting.

The organization should ensure that these documents are consistent with the overall information security policy.

For example, an AI acceptable-use policy should not permit employees to submit information to external AI services when doing so would violate established data classification or confidentiality requirements.

Policy expectations should be communicated to relevant personnel and reviewed when significant AI-related changes occur.

### 29. Clause 5.3 — Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities

Clause 5.3 requires relevant information security roles, responsibilities, and authorities to be assigned and communicated.

For AI integration, responsibilities may include AI system ownership, risk assessment, security architecture, data protection, supplier review, monitoring, incident handling, and risk acceptance.

The organization should determine who is accountable for each material activity and how responsibilities are coordinated across functions.

A responsibility matrix may help identify gaps and prevent conflicting decisions.

For example, an AI development team may own secure implementation, while the business owner remains accountable for the use case and an authorized manager accepts residual business risk.

The precise allocation should reflect the organization's governance model and documented authority structure.

### 30. Clause 6 — Planning

Clause 6 addresses actions to address risks and opportunities, information security objectives, and planning to achieve those objectives.

AI integration should be reflected in risk assessment and treatment planning, including decisions about controls, responsibilities, resources, and implementation timelines.

The organization should also determine whether AI adoption creates opportunities to improve information security processes or introduces changes that require new objectives.

For example, an AI system used for security monitoring may improve detection capabilities but also create risks involving false positives, sensitive log processing, and over-reliance on automated conclusions.

Planning should consider both the potential benefits and the security risks associated with the intended use.

### 31. Clause 6.1.1 — General Risk and Opportunity Planning

Clause 6.1.1 establishes the general requirement to plan actions addressing relevant risks and opportunities in the ISMS context.

AI integration may require changes to governance processes, technical safeguards, supplier oversight, workforce competence, and monitoring arrangements.

The organization should determine which actions are necessary and how their effectiveness will be evaluated.

Actions should be proportionate to the issues identified and should be integrated into the relevant ISMS processes.

For example, the discovery of unauthorized AI usage may lead to an approved-service process, updated employee guidance, improved inventory coverage, and additional monitoring.

### 32. Clause 6.1.2 — Information Security Risk Assessment

Clause 6.1.2 requires the organization to define and apply an information security risk assessment process.

For AI systems, the process should identify relevant risks to information confidentiality, integrity, and availability while considering AI-specific technical and operational characteristics.

The organization should define risk criteria, ensure consistent and valid assessment results, identify risks associated with loss of confidentiality, integrity, and availability, assign risk owners, and analyze and evaluate risks according to the standard's requirements.

AI-related assessment scenarios should be incorporated into the established methodology.

The assessment process should produce documented results that support risk treatment and management decisions.

### 33. Clause 6.1.3 — Information Security Risk Treatment

Clause 6.1.3 addresses the process for treating information security risks.

For AI integration, the organization should select appropriate treatment options, determine necessary controls, compare those controls with Annex A, produce the Statement of Applicability, formulate a risk treatment plan, and obtain the required risk owner approvals.

The control selection should be driven by the risks and treatment decisions rather than by an assumption that every possible control must be implemented.

AI-related treatment measures may include access restrictions, secure development, data protection, supplier requirements, monitoring, and incident-response capabilities.

The organization should maintain evidence of treatment decisions, implementation progress, and residual-risk acceptance.

### 34. Clause 6.2 — Information Security Objectives

Clause 6.2 requires the organization to establish information security objectives at relevant functions and levels.

AI-related objectives may address coverage of the AI inventory, timely completion of risk assessments, remediation of critical vulnerabilities, completion of security testing, or implementation of approved AI security controls.

Objectives should be measurable where practicable, monitored, communicated, and updated as appropriate.

For example, an organization may establish an objective that all AI systems processing confidential information undergo documented security risk assessment before production deployment.

The objective should have an accountable owner, a defined measurement method, and a process for addressing missed targets.

### 35. Clause 6.3 — Planning of Changes

Clause 6.3 addresses planning changes to the ISMS.

AI adoption can introduce material changes to systems, data flows, service providers, business processes, and control responsibilities.

The organization should evaluate whether changes affect the ISMS scope, risk assessment, risk treatment, documentation, or control operation.

For example, replacing an internally hosted model with an external AI service may introduce new supplier, data-transfer, access, and contractual risks.

Changes should be planned and implemented in a controlled manner so that the integrity of the ISMS is maintained.

The organization should retain evidence of relevant approvals and updates to affected processes.

### 36. Clause 7 — Support

Clause 7 addresses resources, competence, awareness, communication, and documented information.

AI integration requires appropriate resources and skills to identify, assess, and manage AI-related information security risks.

Personnel may need training on secure AI use, data handling, model-related threats, incident reporting, and the limitations of AI-assisted security decisions.

The organization should also ensure that relevant documentation is available, controlled, and maintained.

Support arrangements should reflect the complexity of the AI systems and the organization's ability to operate the necessary controls.

### 37. Clause 7.1 — Resources

Clause 7.1 requires the organization to determine and provide the resources needed to establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve the ISMS.

AI integration may require investment in specialized security expertise, assessment tools, secure infrastructure, monitoring, testing, supplier assurance, and workforce training.

The organization should determine whether existing resources are sufficient for the additional risks and operational demands created by AI.

For example, introducing AI agents with access to enterprise systems may require stronger authorization controls, security testing, and monitoring capabilities.

Resource decisions should be linked to identified risks, ISMS objectives, and management priorities.

### 38. Clause 7.2 — Competence

Clause 7.2 addresses the competence of persons performing work under the organization's control that affects information security performance.

AI-related responsibilities may require knowledge of machine learning workflows, model deployment, data pipelines, generative AI threats, cloud security, and third-party AI services.

The organization should determine necessary competence, ensure personnel are competent through appropriate education or experience, take action where competence gaps exist, and retain appropriate evidence.

Not every employee needs advanced AI engineering knowledge. Competence requirements should be proportionate to the person's role and the risks associated with their activities.

### 39. Clause 7.3 — Awareness

Clause 7.3 requires relevant persons to be aware of the information security policy, their contribution to ISMS effectiveness, and the implications of failing to conform to ISMS requirements.

AI awareness activities may explain approved AI services, restrictions on sensitive information, risks of prompt injection, limitations of generated outputs, and procedures for reporting security incidents.

Awareness should be tailored to different user groups.

For example, developers may require guidance on model supply-chain risks, while business users may need clear instructions about confidential data and approved AI tools.

The organization should retain appropriate evidence of awareness activities and evaluate whether communications support expected behavior.

### 40. Clause 7.4 — Communication

Clause 7.4 requires the organization to determine relevant internal and external ISMS communications, including what, when, with whom, and how to communicate.

AI integration may require communication between security teams, developers, business owners, data owners, procurement, privacy, legal, and external service providers.

The organization should define escalation paths for material AI security risks, supplier incidents, failed security tests, and unauthorized AI usage.

Communication procedures should ensure that relevant information reaches people authorized to make decisions or initiate corrective action.

Where external communication is necessary, the organization should consider confidentiality, contractual obligations, and applicable legal requirements.

### 41. Clause 7.5 — Documented Information

Clause 7.5 addresses documented information required by the standard and documented information determined by the organization as necessary for ISMS effectiveness.

AI integration may require updates to asset inventories, risk assessments, treatment plans, the Statement of Applicability, policies, procedures, system descriptions, supplier records, and incident documentation.

Documentation should be controlled so that relevant versions are available where needed and protected against inappropriate access or modification.

The organization should determine which AI-related records are necessary to demonstrate that security risks are managed.

Documentation should reflect actual operating practices. A comprehensive AI security procedure is of limited value if personnel do not follow it or the underlying process is not implemented.

### 42. Clause 8 — Operation

Clause 8 addresses operational planning and control, information security risk assessment, and information security risk treatment.

For AI integration, the organization should ensure that AI-related security processes operate according to established criteria.

This may include pre-deployment assessments, secure development requirements, supplier reviews, access approvals, security testing, change management, and ongoing monitoring.

Operational arrangements should define how the organization responds when required activities are missed or when material risks emerge.

For example, an AI system should not bypass established deployment approval requirements simply because a business unit uses a managed model service instead of developing a model internally.

### 43. Clause 8.1 — Operational Planning and Control

Clause 8.1 requires the organization to plan, implement, and control processes needed to meet ISMS requirements and implement the actions determined under Clause 6.

AI integration should ensure that security requirements are incorporated into relevant operational processes.

These may include AI procurement, model development, data preparation, testing, deployment, access management, maintenance, and retirement.

The organization should establish criteria for process execution and retain sufficient documented information to demonstrate that processes were carried out as planned.

Changes should be controlled, and unintended consequences should be reviewed where appropriate.

### 44. Clause 8.2 — Information Security Risk Assessment During Operation

Clause 8.2 requires information security risk assessments to be performed at planned intervals or when significant changes are proposed or occur, according to the organization's defined criteria.

For AI systems, significant changes may include adopting a new model provider, connecting an AI agent to sensitive enterprise systems, introducing new data sources, or changing the intended purpose.

The organization should determine whether such changes affect existing risk assumptions or control effectiveness.

Risk reassessment should be triggered by relevant changes rather than relying exclusively on a fixed annual review.

The organization should retain documented information about assessment results and the decisions arising from them.

### 45. Clause 8.3 — Information Security Risk Treatment During Operation

Clause 8.3 requires the organization to implement its information security risk treatment plan and retain documented information about the results.

For AI integration, treatment implementation may include configuring safeguards, enforcing access restrictions, completing security testing, establishing monitoring, or implementing contractual protections.

The organization should track whether planned actions are completed and whether controls operate as intended.

If a treatment action cannot be implemented as planned, the resulting risk should be reassessed and escalated through the established process.

The objective is to ensure that documented risk treatment translates into operational controls and authorized decisions about residual risk.

### 46. Clause 9 — Performance Evaluation

Clause 9 addresses monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation, internal audit, and management review.

AI integration should establish how the organization evaluates whether its AI-related information security controls and processes are effective.

Relevant indicators may include security assessment coverage, overdue remediation actions, incidents involving AI systems, access-control exceptions, supplier assurance gaps, and completion of required reviews.

Metrics should be interpreted in context and should not be treated as conclusive evidence of effectiveness merely because targets are met.

Performance evaluation should support management decisions and continual improvement of the ISMS.

### 47. Clause 9.1 — Monitoring, Measurement, Analysis, and Evaluation

Clause 9.1 requires the organization to determine what needs to be monitored and measured, the methods to be used, when monitoring and measurement occur, and when results are analyzed and evaluated.

For AI systems, monitoring may include access events, API usage, security alerts, data exposure indicators, model or service changes, and relevant operational anomalies.

The organization should define metrics and thresholds that correspond to identified risks and control objectives.

For example, monitoring excessive AI API usage may help identify credential compromise or unauthorized automation, but the organization should investigate alerts rather than assuming that every anomaly represents an attack.

Results should be recorded and used to evaluate ISMS performance.

### 48. Clause 9.2 — Internal Audit

Clause 9.2 requires the organization to conduct internal audits at planned intervals to provide information about whether the ISMS conforms to applicable requirements and is effectively implemented and maintained.

An internal audit may examine how AI-related risks are incorporated into the ISMS.

Audit procedures may include reviewing the AI inventory, sampling risk assessments, examining supplier reviews, testing access controls, inspecting security testing evidence, and verifying that material findings are remediated.

The audit program should consider the importance of the processes being audited and the results of previous audits.

The organization should maintain audit independence and objectivity in accordance with its audit arrangements and applicable requirements.

### 49. Clause 9.3 — Management Review

Clause 9.3 requires top management to review the ISMS at planned intervals to ensure its continuing suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness.

AI-related inputs may include significant changes in AI usage, risk assessment results, incidents, monitoring performance, audit findings, nonconformities, and remediation status.

Management may need to decide whether additional resources, policy changes, revised controls, or changes to the ISMS scope are necessary.

For example, widespread adoption of AI agents may require changes to governance, access management, monitoring, and security training.

The review should produce documented outputs and decisions that support continued ISMS improvement.

### 50. Clause 10 — Improvement

Clause 10 addresses continual improvement and the treatment of nonconformities and corrective actions.

AI integration should provide mechanisms for identifying weaknesses, investigating causes, implementing corrections, and evaluating whether corrective actions are effective.

Improvement inputs may include audit findings, incidents, control testing, supplier changes, new threat information, and lessons learned from AI deployments.

The organization should avoid treating each AI-related issue as an isolated technical defect when the underlying cause may involve governance, competence, procurement, or risk management.

Continual improvement helps ensure that the ISMS remains appropriate as AI systems, threats, and organizational dependencies evolve.

### 51. Clause 10.1 — Continual Improvement

Clause 10.1 requires the organization to continually improve the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the ISMS.

AI-related improvement may involve refining risk assessment methods, improving inventory completeness, strengthening supplier assurance, enhancing security monitoring, or updating employee guidance.

The organization should use evidence from operational performance, incidents, audits, and management reviews to identify opportunities for improvement.

Improvements should be prioritized according to their expected contribution to ISMS effectiveness and risk reduction.

For example, repeated findings involving AI service authorization may indicate that the organization needs a standardized control pattern rather than repeated project-specific fixes.

### 52. Clause 10.2 — Nonconformity and Corrective Action

Clause 10.2 addresses how the organization responds to nonconformities, evaluates the need for action to eliminate their causes, implements corrective action, and reviews effectiveness.

AI-related nonconformities may include failure to perform required security assessments, incomplete risk treatment, unauthorized AI use contrary to policy, or missing evidence of required control operation.

The organization should record the nonconformity, take appropriate action to control or correct it, investigate the cause, and determine whether similar issues exist elsewhere.

Corrective action should address the cause rather than only the immediate symptom.

The organization should retain appropriate evidence of the nonconformity, actions taken, and results of effectiveness review.

### 53. Annex A Overview

Annex A of ISO/IEC 27001:2022 provides a reference set of information security controls.

The controls are organized into four groups:

- Organizational controls.
- People controls.
- Physical controls.
- Technological controls.

When integrating AI, the organization should assess which controls are necessary to address the identified information security risks.

The Annex A reference set should be considered as part of the risk treatment process, but the organization should not assume that it is a complete list of every AI-specific technical safeguard that might be needed.

Additional controls may be necessary where existing controls do not adequately address the organization's AI-related risks.

### 54. Organizational Controls Relevant to AI

Organizational controls address governance, policies, roles, information management, supplier relationships, incident management, business continuity, and other organization-level security requirements.

AI integration may require reviewing policies, responsibilities, asset inventories, threat intelligence, secure development governance, supplier assurance, and incident processes.

For example, an organization may need to update its supplier security process to evaluate model providers' data-handling practices, service dependencies, and change notification arrangements.

The organization should map these controls to specific AI risks and determine whether existing implementations remain adequate.

A control's general relevance to information security does not automatically prove that it addresses every AI-specific scenario.

### 55. People Controls Relevant to AI

People controls address security-related responsibilities, competence, awareness, acceptable behavior, and personnel-related risks.

AI integration may require employees and contractors to understand approved AI services, data classification, restrictions on information disclosure, and reporting procedures.

Specialist personnel may also require competence in AI security testing, model deployment, data governance, and the management of AI-related incidents.

The organization should determine which personnel activities create relevant risks and apply proportionate controls.

For example, employees using public AI services should understand whether the information they enter may be processed by an external provider and which categories of information are prohibited from disclosure.

### 56. Physical Controls Relevant to AI

Physical controls protect facilities, equipment, and supporting infrastructure against unauthorized physical access, damage, and disruption.

Their relevance depends on how the AI system is developed and hosted.

An organization operating its own model-training infrastructure may need to consider physical access to servers, storage, networking equipment, and media containing sensitive datasets or model artifacts.

An organization using cloud-hosted AI services may rely partly on provider-operated physical controls while retaining responsibility for its own facilities and endpoints.

The assessment should determine which physical controls are relevant to the AI system's architecture and whether sufficient evidence exists for controls operated by external providers.

### 57. Technological Controls Relevant to AI

Technological controls address technical protection of systems, networks, applications, data, and information-processing facilities.

AI integration may require evaluating identity management, access restrictions, secure configuration, logging, monitoring, cryptography, vulnerability management, network security, secure development, and change management.

AI-specific technical safeguards may supplement these controls by addressing model interfaces, prompt injection, tool permissions, training-data integrity, model artifact protection, and output handling.

The organization should assess the effectiveness of the complete control environment rather than treating AI security as a separate technical layer disconnected from existing security architecture.

### 58. Access Control for AI Systems

Access control ensures that users and services receive only the permissions needed for their authorized activities.

AI systems may involve access to models, datasets, vector stores, prompt templates, APIs, model registries, and connected enterprise applications.

The organization should determine how identities are authenticated, permissions are granted, privileged access is controlled, and access is reviewed.

For AI agents, access control must also consider the actions the agent can perform through connected tools.

A chatbot that can only generate text has a different access profile from an agent that can send emails, modify records, execute code, or initiate transactions.

Permissions should reflect the actual capabilities and risks of the deployed system.

### 59. Least Privilege for AI Agents

The principle of least privilege limits an identity or process to the minimum permissions required for its intended function.

AI agents can create additional risk because they may select tools and initiate actions based on model-generated reasoning or instructions.

The organization should restrict each agent's access to the systems, data, operations, and execution environments required for its approved purpose.

Where possible, read-only permissions, narrowly scoped credentials, transaction limits, approval gates, and isolated execution environments should be considered.

The assessor should examine whether permissions are technically enforced rather than relying solely on system prompts or instructions telling the agent not to perform unauthorized actions.

### 60. Identity and Authentication for AI Services

AI applications often rely on human identities, service identities, API keys, workload identities, and provider-specific authentication mechanisms.

The organization should determine how these identities are issued, protected, monitored, rotated, and revoked.

Credentials should not be embedded insecurely in prompts, source code, notebooks, configuration files, or model artifacts.

The assessment should also consider whether model endpoints and connected tools authenticate the calling service and verify authorization for requested operations.

Weak identity controls can allow unauthorized parties to access AI services, consume resources, extract information, or execute actions through connected systems.

### 61. Data Protection for AI Workloads

AI workloads may process sensitive information during training, inference, retrieval, evaluation, and monitoring.

The organization should apply appropriate data protection controls based on information classification, risk assessment, and the system's operating environment.

Measures may include access restrictions, encryption, data minimization, secure transfer, retention controls, and protection of logs and backups.

The assessment should examine whether sensitive information can enter unintended processing paths, including external model providers or third-party plugins.

Data protection requirements should be applied throughout the relevant data lifecycle rather than only to the primary database.

### 62. Encryption and Key Management

Encryption can protect AI-related information in transit and at rest.

Relevant information may include datasets, model artifacts, inference requests, generated outputs, logs, backups, and stored embeddings.

The organization should determine where encryption is required and how cryptographic keys are managed, including access restrictions, rotation, storage, and revocation.

Encryption does not eliminate every AI security risk. It does not, by itself, prevent authorized applications from exposing sensitive information through outputs or ensure that a model behaves securely.

The assessment should therefore evaluate encryption alongside access management, application security, data handling, and monitoring.

### 63. Secure AI Development

Secure development practices help prevent vulnerabilities from being introduced into AI applications and supporting components.

Relevant activities may include requirements analysis, threat modeling, secure coding, dependency management, code review, testing, approval, and controlled deployment.

AI development may additionally require protection of datasets, model artifacts, training pipelines, evaluation results, and model-serving configurations.

The organization should define which development controls apply to internally developed AI systems and how those controls are adapted when the model itself is supplied by a third party.

Security requirements should be established early enough to influence architecture and design rather than being added only after implementation.

### 64. Secure AI Procurement

Procurement can introduce AI systems without the organization directly developing the underlying model.

The organization should establish security requirements for evaluating AI products, platforms, model providers, and managed services.

Relevant considerations include data handling, identity integration, access controls, logging, incident notification, vulnerability management, service availability, model updates, and subcontractor dependencies.

The assessment should determine whether procurement processes capture these requirements before a service is approved.

For example, a product that sends confidential information to an external model provider should undergo appropriate security and contractual review before production use.

### 65. Supplier Security for AI Providers

AI providers may process organizational data, operate critical model infrastructure, or supply components used in business decisions.

The organization should assess supplier risks according to the service's importance, data sensitivity, architecture, and contractual dependencies.

Due diligence may include security documentation, independent assurance reports, incident processes, data-processing terms, access restrictions, and service continuity arrangements.

The organization should also consider how provider changes affect risk.

Where provider assurance is incomplete, the organization should document the uncertainty and determine whether additional safeguards or restrictions are necessary.

### 66. Cloud Security for AI Services

Cloud-based AI services introduce shared-responsibility considerations.

Providers may manage physical infrastructure and certain platform controls, while customers remain responsible for aspects such as identities, data, application configuration, access permissions, and service usage.

The precise allocation depends on the service model and contract.

The organization should document relevant responsibilities and verify that required controls are implemented on the customer side.

For example, using a managed model endpoint does not automatically ensure that the organization's retrieval database enforces document-level authorization or that its application prevents sensitive information from appearing in logs.

### 67. API Security for AI Systems

AI applications commonly expose or consume APIs for model inference, retrieval, orchestration, monitoring, and integration with enterprise systems.

API security should address authentication, authorization, input validation, rate limiting, secure transport, error handling, logging, and protection against abuse.

The assessment should determine whether endpoints are exposed only to authorized users and services and whether permissions are enforced for the data and actions they support.

For AI APIs, the organization should also consider prompt injection, excessive resource consumption, unauthorized model access, and sensitive output disclosure.

Security controls should be appropriate to the endpoint's purpose and the consequences of misuse.

### 68. Logging and Monitoring for AI Security

Logging and monitoring support detection, investigation, and accountability for AI-related security events.

Relevant records may include authentication events, API requests, administrative changes, model deployments, access to sensitive data, tool invocations, and security alerts.

The organization should determine what information must be logged, who can access logs, how long they are retained, and how sensitive content is protected.

Logging all prompts and outputs may create privacy or confidentiality risks, while logging too little may impair investigations.

The assessment should evaluate whether logging decisions balance investigative needs with data protection, legal requirements, and information security objectives.

### 69. AI Security Incident Management

AI-related incidents should be handled through the organization's established information security incident management process, with appropriate procedures for AI-specific scenarios.

Examples include sensitive-data disclosure through model outputs, unauthorized tool execution, compromised model artifacts, malicious dataset modification, and exploitation of an AI application.

Incident procedures should identify reporting channels, escalation criteria, investigation responsibilities, containment actions, and recovery requirements.

Depending on the system, containment may involve disabling a tool, revoking credentials, restricting model access, rolling back a model version, or suspending an AI workflow.

The organization should ensure that incident lessons feed into risk assessment, control improvement, and relevant management reviews.

### 70. Business Continuity and AI Availability

AI services may support important business processes and therefore create availability dependencies.

The organization should evaluate the impact of AI service outages, provider disruptions, API failures, resource exhaustion, and loss of supporting data or infrastructure.

Continuity measures may include backup procedures, alternative workflows, fallback services, recovery arrangements, and defined manual operating modes.

The appropriate measures depend on the system's criticality and recovery requirements.

The assessment should determine whether AI availability risks are included in relevant business continuity and disaster recovery planning rather than assuming that the model provider alone is responsible for operational resilience.

### 71. Change Management for AI Systems

Changes to AI systems can alter their security properties, dependencies, and operating assumptions.

Relevant changes include model upgrades, prompt modifications, new datasets, changes to retrieval sources, altered permissions, new tools, and provider configuration changes.

The organization should define how changes are requested, assessed, approved, tested, implemented, and recorded.

Material changes may require reassessment of information security risks and verification that existing controls remain effective.

For example, adding email-sending capabilities to an AI agent materially changes its potential impact and should trigger a review of permissions, approvals, monitoring, and incident procedures.

### 72. Configuration Management for AI

Configuration management maintains visibility and control over system settings and approved states.

AI configurations may include model versions, inference parameters, system prompts, tool permissions, retrieval settings, safety filters, API configurations, and deployment policies.

The organization should establish how relevant configurations are documented, protected, reviewed, and changed.

Unauthorized configuration changes can alter model behavior, expose information, weaken safeguards, or permit unapproved actions.

The assessment should determine whether configuration records support traceability and whether material changes are linked to the change management and risk assessment processes.

### 73. Vulnerability Management for AI Systems

Vulnerability management identifies, evaluates, prioritizes, and addresses weaknesses in systems and components.

For AI environments, this includes conventional software and infrastructure vulnerabilities as well as relevant weaknesses in model-serving applications, orchestration layers, plugins, dependencies, and AI integrations.

The organization should determine how vulnerabilities are discovered, assessed, remediated, and verified.

AI-specific testing may also examine insecure output handling, prompt injection, excessive agent permissions, and unsafe integration patterns.

The assessment should establish whether findings are prioritized according to realistic exploitability, business impact, exposure, and available compensating controls.

### 74. Model and Dataset Integrity

Integrity controls help protect models and datasets against unauthorized or inappropriate modification.

Relevant measures may include access restrictions, provenance records, version control, cryptographic hashes, signed artifacts, approval workflows, and controlled release processes.

The organization should establish how approved model and dataset versions are identified and how changes are authorized.

For internally trained models, the assessment may examine the protection of training pipelines and the provenance of training data.

For third-party models, it may examine how the organization verifies provider identity, tracks versions, and evaluates the security implications of updates.

### 75. Model Supply-Chain Security

AI systems may depend on pretrained models, datasets, libraries, plugins, orchestration frameworks, container images, and external services.

Compromise or weakness in any component may affect the security of the overall system.

The organization should identify material dependencies and determine how they are evaluated, approved, updated, and monitored.

Relevant practices may include trusted-source verification, dependency scanning, artifact integrity checks, software bills of materials where appropriate, and supplier risk review.

The assessment should consider whether changes to upstream components trigger appropriate testing and risk reassessment.

Supply-chain security is especially important where an AI system incorporates components that the organization cannot inspect fully.

### 76. Generative AI Security Considerations

Generative AI systems create and process natural-language, code, image, audio, or other content.

Their security risks may include prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, malicious generated code, unsafe tool use, output manipulation, and over-reliance on inaccurate content.

The ISMS should determine which risks are relevant to the organization's use cases and how they will be controlled.

Controls may include access restrictions, input and output validation, data filtering, constrained tool permissions, security testing, monitoring, and human approval for consequential actions.

The organization should not rely exclusively on instructions embedded in a system prompt to enforce security requirements.

### 77. Prompt Injection and Information Security

Prompt injection occurs when malicious or untrusted content influences an AI system to disregard intended instructions or perform unintended actions.

The risk may arise from direct user inputs or indirect content retrieved from documents, websites, emails, or other sources.

Potential consequences include unauthorized information disclosure, inappropriate tool execution, or manipulation of downstream workflows.

The organization should assess how prompts and retrieved content interact with access controls and tool permissions.

Defenses may include separation of trusted instructions from untrusted data, authorization checks outside the model, restricted tool access, output validation, and monitoring.

The assessment should verify that security boundaries are enforced by the surrounding system rather than relying only on model behavior.

### 78. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Security

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines a generative model with external information retrieved from a knowledge source.

The security of a RAG system depends on the model, retrieval pipeline, source documents, indexing process, permissions, and application logic.

The organization should evaluate whether users can retrieve only information they are authorized to access.

It should also consider document ingestion controls, index integrity, sensitive-data handling, source provenance, prompt injection through retrieved content, and logging.

A correctly secured vector database does not automatically ensure secure retrieval. Authorization must be enforced across the full retrieval workflow and reflected in the information provided to the model.

### 79. AI Agent Security

AI agents may combine model-generated decisions with tool execution, memory, external APIs, and access to business systems.

Their permissions and potential impact may therefore exceed those of systems that only generate responses.

The organization should identify which actions agents can perform and establish suitable restrictions, approvals, monitoring, and recovery mechanisms.

Controls may include scoped identities, allowlisted tools, transaction limits, execution isolation, approval gates, and logging.

The assessment should consider whether untrusted instructions can influence agent actions and whether the agent can access or modify information beyond its authorized purpose.

### 80. AI Output Validation

AI outputs may contain inaccurate information, unsafe instructions, malicious content, or information that should not be disclosed.

Output validation evaluates whether generated content meets the requirements of its intended use before it is consumed or acted upon.

Depending on the use case, validation may include schema checks, authorization checks, content filtering, factual verification, code testing, or human review.

For AI agents, the organization should verify proposed actions against independently enforced authorization and business rules.

The assessment should not assume that a model's confidence or fluent response establishes correctness, security, or suitability.

### 81. AI Security Testing Before Deployment

Pre-deployment security testing evaluates whether an AI system and its supporting controls meet defined security requirements before production use.

Testing may include vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, API testing, access-control verification, adversarial testing, data leakage tests, and validation of logging and monitoring.

The organization should define testing requirements according to the system's risk and intended use.

Testing should cover relevant components and interfaces, including external services and connected tools where they are within scope.

Material findings should be evaluated and resolved, mitigated, or formally accepted by authorized decision-makers before deployment in accordance with the organization's approval criteria.

### 82. AI Security Testing After Deployment

Security testing should continue after deployment where appropriate because systems, threats, dependencies, and configurations can change.

Post-deployment activities may include periodic testing, monitoring-driven investigation, regression testing after updates, and reassessment after material changes.

The organization should determine the required frequency and triggers based on risk, system criticality, and applicable requirements.

For example, changing an AI model or connecting a new enterprise data source may require additional testing even if the application code remains unchanged.

Testing results should feed into the ISMS risk assessment, risk treatment, and continual improvement processes.

### 83. AI Security Metrics and Key Risk Indicators

Security metrics help the organization monitor whether controls and processes are operating as intended.

Potential metrics include the percentage of AI systems inventoried, completion of pre-deployment security assessments, remediation time for significant findings, frequency of unauthorized access attempts, and the number of overdue risk treatment actions.

Key risk indicators may identify increasing exposure, such as growth in unapproved AI services or repeated security exceptions.

Metrics should have defined calculation methods, data sources, owners, and review intervals.

A low incident count does not necessarily demonstrate effective security if monitoring is weak or incidents are underreported. Metrics should therefore be interpreted alongside other evidence.

### 84. Risk-Based Control Selection

Control selection should be driven by identified risks and the organization's risk treatment decisions.

For an AI system processing public information, basic access and availability controls may be central. For a system handling sensitive customer information, data protection, authorization, supplier assurance, and logging may require greater attention.

An AI agent capable of changing business records may require stronger transaction controls and human approval than a read-only assistant.

The organization should document why selected controls are necessary and how they address identified risk scenarios.

The selection process should also consider dependencies between controls and whether additional measures are needed beyond the Annex A reference set.

### 85. Mapping AI Risks to Annex A Controls

Risk-to-control mapping links an identified risk scenario to controls selected to address it.

For example, unauthorized disclosure through an AI service may require controls related to information classification, access management, supplier security, secure development, logging, and data protection.

The mapping should identify the control objective, responsible owner, implementation evidence, and relevant testing procedures.

A single risk may require multiple controls, and one control may support several risks.

The organization should avoid assuming that naming an Annex A control proves that the associated risk is adequately managed. Effectiveness must be evaluated using evidence appropriate to the control and risk.

### 86. AI Controls Beyond Annex A

Annex A provides a reference set of information security controls, but an organization's risk assessment may identify needs that require more specialized or additional safeguards.

For AI systems, these may include model-specific evaluation, prompt-injection testing, agent permission constraints, output validation, dataset provenance checks, or specialized model monitoring.

The organization should document the rationale for additional controls and integrate them into its risk treatment and operational processes.

Additional controls should not be treated as substitutes for applicable Annex A controls that remain necessary.

The objective is to create a coherent control environment that addresses the actual risks rather than limiting the organization to a fixed checklist.

### 87. ISO/IEC 27002 as Implementation Guidance

ISO/IEC 27002 provides guidance on information security controls and can support the interpretation and implementation of controls selected within an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.

An organization integrating AI may use relevant guidance to develop control objectives, procedures, implementation practices, and evidence requirements.

The organization should distinguish between ISO/IEC 27001's management-system requirements and ISO/IEC 27002's control guidance.

Neither the use of guidance nor a control mapping automatically demonstrates that the ISMS is effective.

The assessment should examine how the organization has implemented the selected controls and whether their operation addresses the identified AI-related risks.

### 88. AI Security Policies and Standards

AI integration may require supplementary policies, standards, and procedures to clarify how existing information security requirements apply to AI.

Examples include an AI acceptable-use policy, secure AI development standard, AI supplier assessment procedure, AI agent authorization standard, and AI security testing procedure.

The documents should be aligned with the organization's information security policy and risk-management methodology.

Requirements should be clear enough to guide implementation and support objective assessment.

The organization should also establish document ownership, approval, review frequency, exception handling, and communication arrangements.

### 89. AI Security Risk Treatment Plans

An AI security risk treatment plan identifies actions required to address selected risks.

The plan should specify the risk, treatment approach, controls to be implemented, accountable owners, target dates, dependencies, and expected evidence.

Where the risk cannot be reduced immediately, interim measures may be necessary to limit exposure.

For example, an organization may temporarily disable an AI agent's write access while implementing a more granular authorization model.

The plan should connect to the risk register and Statement of Applicability where appropriate.

Completion should be verified, and remaining risk should be evaluated and accepted through the established process when necessary.

### 90. Evidence of AI Control Implementation

Evidence demonstrates whether an AI security control has been designed and implemented.

Examples include approved policies, access-control configurations, supplier assessments, security test results, model release records, risk assessments, and monitoring procedures.

Evidence should correspond to the relevant system, environment, period, and control objective.

A statement that a model provider offers enterprise security features is not sufficient evidence that the organization has configured those features correctly.

Similarly, an approved procedure does not demonstrate that personnel consistently perform the required activity.

The assessor should gather evidence appropriate to the conclusion being reached and document material limitations.

### 91. AI Control Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness concerns whether a control functions as intended in actual operation.

For AI systems, this may involve examining access reviews, completed security tests, model release approvals, incident handling, monitoring records, or supplier review activities.

A control may be correctly designed but inconsistently performed.

For example, an organization may require security approval before deploying an AI system, but sample testing may reveal deployments that bypassed the approval process.

The assessment should identify the extent and cause of any failure and determine whether additional evidence or remediation is required.

### 92. Integrating AI Into the Internal Audit Program

The internal audit program should consider AI-related risks when determining the audit universe, audit frequency, scope, and resource requirements.

Relevant audit subjects may include AI governance, information security risk assessment, supplier management, data protection, access control, secure development, and incident response.

Audit prioritization should reflect risk significance, changes in the AI environment, prior findings, and the importance of the processes being examined.

Where specialist AI expertise is required, the organization should ensure that the audit team has appropriate competence or obtains suitable support.

The audit program should preserve objectivity and follow the organization's established internal audit procedures.

### 93. AI Risk in Internal Audit Planning

AI can affect both the risks being audited and the methods used by auditors.

For example, an organization may use AI to process sensitive information, automate business decisions, or support cybersecurity operations. These uses can introduce risks that warrant audit attention.

The internal audit team should understand the organization's AI footprint and identify where AI changes existing control assumptions.

Audit planning may consider system criticality, information sensitivity, third-party dependence, incident history, and the degree of automation.

AI risk should be incorporated into audit planning based on evidence and risk significance rather than being selected merely because AI is a current technology trend.

### 94. Combining ISO/IEC 27001 With Other Frameworks

Organizations may use several frameworks and standards to manage related risks.

ISO/IEC 27001 may provide the ISMS foundation, while NIST AI RMF can help structure broader AI risk-management activities and ISO/IEC 42001 can support an AI management system.

Other security, privacy, sector-specific, and regulatory requirements may also apply.

The organization should establish clear mappings while preserving the distinct objectives and criteria of each framework.

A control that supports multiple frameworks may reduce duplicated work, but the organization must still verify that it meets each relevant requirement.

Framework integration should improve traceability and efficiency without overstating conformity or assurance.

### 95. Integrated AI Governance and ISMS Reporting

Integrated reporting provides management with a coherent view of AI-related information security risks, control effectiveness, and remediation progress.

Reporting may include AI inventory coverage, material risk scenarios, security assessment results, significant incidents, supplier concerns, unresolved findings, and changes in exposure.

The organization should distinguish between technical indicators, risk-management outcomes, and formal conformity conclusions.

Reports should identify the decisions required from management, including resource allocation, remediation priorities, risk acceptance, and changes to the ISMS.

The goal is to make AI-related information security risk visible within existing governance structures while retaining sufficient detail for effective oversight.

### 96. Common Integration Mistakes

Several mistakes can weaken the integration of AI into an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.

**Treating AI as out of scope by default:** This can omit material information security risks.

**Relying only on existing controls:** Traditional controls may not fully address model manipulation, prompt injection, or agent behavior.

**Assuming provider security removes organizational responsibility:** Third-party services still require appropriate risk assessment and governance.

**Creating a separate AI policy without operational integration:** A policy may have little effect if it is disconnected from asset management, procurement, risk assessment, and monitoring.

**Confusing documentation with effectiveness:** Written procedures do not prove that controls operate as intended.

**Overlooking changes:** Model updates, new data sources, and additional tool permissions can invalidate previous assumptions.

**Treating Annex A as an exhaustive AI security checklist:** Additional safeguards may be needed based on the risk assessment.

### 97. AI Integration Readiness Assessment

An AI integration readiness assessment evaluates whether the organization's ISMS is prepared to manage relevant AI-related information security risks.

Readiness dimensions may include AI discovery, asset ownership, risk assessment, policy coverage, supplier management, secure development, access control, monitoring, incident response, and internal audit competence.

The assessment should identify existing capabilities, gaps, dependencies, and improvement priorities.

A readiness assessment is not equivalent to a certification audit and does not establish conformity with ISO/IEC 27001 by itself.

Its value lies in helping management determine what needs to change before the organization expands AI usage or relies on AI systems for more consequential activities.

### 98. Building an AI Integration Roadmap

An AI integration roadmap translates readiness findings into a sequenced improvement plan.

A typical roadmap may begin with AI use-case discovery and scope confirmation, followed by risk assessment, governance updates, control mapping, technical safeguards, monitoring, and assurance activities.

The sequence should reflect the organization's risk profile and existing ISMS maturity.

High-risk AI systems may require immediate safeguards, while broader governance improvements can be implemented through a structured program.

Each initiative should have an accountable owner, target date, required resources, measurable outcome, and evidence of completion.

Progress should be reviewed through established governance and ISMS performance processes.

### 99. Measuring Successful ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration

Successful integration is demonstrated by effective risk management, not simply by adding AI references to existing documentation.

Indicators may include complete and current AI asset records, consistent risk assessments, clear ownership, appropriate control implementation, effective monitoring, timely remediation, and meaningful management oversight.

The organization should also verify that material AI changes trigger the required reviews and that third-party dependencies are included where relevant.

Internal audit results, incident trends, control testing, and management reviews can provide evidence of effectiveness.

Measures should be selected according to the organization's objectives and risks rather than treated as a universal maturity score.

### 100. Core Principles of ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration

Effective integration rests on several principles:

- **Risk-based governance:** Identify and treat AI-related information security risks according to their significance.
- **ISMS integration:** Incorporate AI into existing processes rather than relying on disconnected governance documents.
- **Asset visibility:** Identify relevant AI systems, data, models, interfaces, and dependencies.
- **Clear accountability:** Assign owners for systems, controls, and risk decisions.
- **Control traceability:** Connect identified risks to treatment decisions, selected controls, and supporting evidence.
- **Lifecycle awareness:** Reassess risks when systems, data, providers, or intended uses change.
- **Evidence-based assurance:** Evaluate actual implementation and operating effectiveness.
- **Continual improvement:** Use monitoring, incidents, audits, and management reviews to strengthen the ISMS.

The central principle is that **ISO/IEC 27001 integration should make AI-related information security risks part of the organization's operational ISMS—not merely add AI terminology to existing policies.**

This foundation prepares the organization to examine the practical mapping of ISO/IEC 27001 controls to AI security risks, the Statement of Applicability, implementation evidence, and integrated audit procedures in the next part.

## Part 2 — ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A Control Mapping, AI Security Implementation, and Audit Evidence

### 101. Mapping AI Security Risks to ISO/IEC 27001 Controls

Mapping AI security risks to ISO/IEC 27001 controls establishes traceability between identified risk scenarios and the safeguards selected to address them.

The organization should begin with a documented AI-related risk scenario, identify the affected assets and business processes, evaluate existing safeguards, and determine which additional controls are necessary.

For example, the risk of unauthorized disclosure through a generative AI application may involve information classification, access management, supplier security, secure development, logging, and incident management.

The mapping should identify the control objective, implementation approach, accountable owner, and evidence required to assess effectiveness.

A control mapping is not proof that a risk has been adequately treated. The organization must verify that selected controls are implemented and operate effectively.

### 102. Establishing an AI Risk-to-Control Matrix

An AI risk-to-control matrix provides a structured view of how identified AI risks are addressed through the ISMS.

A typical matrix includes:

- Risk identification number and description.
- Affected AI system and information assets.
- Inherent risk rating.
- Existing controls.
- Applicable ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A controls.
- Additional AI-specific safeguards.
- Control owner.
- Implementation status.
- Residual risk rating.
- Evidence references.
- Risk acceptance or remediation status.

The matrix should be linked to the organization's risk register, Statement of Applicability, and risk treatment plan.

For audit purposes, the matrix should enable an auditor to trace a risk from identification through treatment and testing without relying on undocumented assumptions.

### 103. AI Security Risk Scenario: Confidential Data Leakage

Confidential data leakage occurs when sensitive information becomes accessible to unauthorized users, systems, or external service providers.

In an AI environment, leakage may occur through prompts, model outputs, retrieval systems, logs, training pipelines, plugins, or external model APIs.

Relevant safeguards may include data classification, access restrictions, secure configuration, supplier security, data loss prevention, logging, and incident management.

The organization should determine which controls are necessary based on the data involved, the AI architecture, and the consequences of disclosure.

Audit testing may examine whether employees can submit prohibited information to external AI services, whether retrieval permissions are enforced, and whether sensitive information is unnecessarily retained in logs.

### 104. AI Security Risk Scenario: Model and Dataset Manipulation

Model and dataset manipulation involves unauthorized or malicious changes to training data, model artifacts, evaluation datasets, or deployment configurations.

Such changes may compromise model integrity, introduce harmful behavior, undermine business decisions, or create security weaknesses.

Relevant safeguards may include access management, secure development, change management, supplier security, version control, and protection of information-processing environments.

The organization should establish how approved model and dataset versions are identified, who can modify them, and how changes are reviewed and authorized.

Audit evidence may include repository permissions, version histories, approval records, integrity verification results, and model release documentation.

### 105. AI Security Risk Scenario: Unauthorized AI Agent Actions

An AI agent may interact with enterprise applications, databases, communication platforms, or external services through connected tools.

If the agent has excessive permissions or insufficient authorization controls, it may perform actions beyond its approved purpose.

Relevant safeguards include least privilege, identity management, secure application development, change management, logging, and incident response.

The organization should distinguish between instructions given to the model and security controls enforced by the surrounding application.

Audit testing should determine whether unauthorized actions are technically prevented, whether sensitive operations require appropriate approval, and whether agent activity can be traced to an accountable identity.

### 106. AI Security Risk Scenario: Third-Party AI Provider Failure

An AI provider may experience a security incident, service outage, unauthorized data access, or material change to its service.

These events may affect the organization's information security, business continuity, contractual obligations, and ability to deliver services.

Relevant safeguards include supplier relationship management, contractual security requirements, incident management, business continuity, and ongoing supplier monitoring.

The organization should assess the provider's importance, the information processed, available assurance evidence, and the feasibility of alternative arrangements.

Audit evidence may include supplier assessments, contracts, service-level agreements, incident notification terms, assurance reports, and documented contingency plans.

### 107. Control Design Versus Control Implementation

Control design determines whether a control, if implemented as intended, is capable of addressing the relevant risk.

Control implementation establishes whether the organization has actually put the control into operation.

For example, a policy requiring approval before deploying an AI system may be appropriately designed. However, if the organization has no workflow to enforce or record the approval, implementation may be inadequate.

An auditor should evaluate both dimensions.

Evidence of design may include policies, standards, procedures, architecture specifications, and responsibility matrices. Evidence of implementation may include system configurations, completed approvals, access records, test results, and operational logs.

Neither dimension alone establishes operating effectiveness.

### 108. Control Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness concerns whether a control functions consistently and as intended over the period being assessed.

For an AI security control, the auditor should determine whether required activities actually occurred, whether they were performed by authorized personnel, and whether exceptions were handled appropriately.

For example, an organization may require quarterly access reviews for AI model repositories. The auditor should inspect evidence that reviews occurred, assess whether relevant privileged accounts were included, and verify whether identified excessive permissions were corrected.

A written procedure or a single successful demonstration may not be sufficient to establish consistent operation.

The required evidence depends on the control's nature, frequency, risk significance, and assessment objective.

### 109. Mapping ISO/IEC 27001 to AI Security Requirements

An organization may maintain a mapping between its ISO/IEC 27001 controls and AI-specific security requirements.

Such a mapping can support consistent implementation, reduce duplicated effort, and identify areas where existing controls do not adequately address AI risks.

For example, a secure development control may support requirements for protecting AI application code, model-serving components, and orchestration logic. Additional testing may still be necessary to assess prompt injection, model-specific vulnerabilities, or unsafe agent behavior.

The mapping should identify both direct and partial coverage.

A control should not be described as fully satisfying an AI security requirement merely because its general subject appears relevant.

### 110. Organizational Control: Policies for Information Security

The ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Annex A reference set includes control A.5.1, Policies for information security.

For AI integration, the organization should determine whether its existing information security policies adequately address AI-related use cases and risks.

Supplementary policies may cover approved AI services, sensitive-data handling, secure AI development, and the use of AI agents.

The organization should establish ownership, approval, communication, review, and exception-handling arrangements.

Audit procedures may include reviewing policy coverage, checking consistency between related documents, interviewing relevant personnel, and sampling AI use cases to determine whether policy requirements are followed.

### 111. Organizational Control: Information Security Roles and Responsibilities

Control A.5.2 addresses information security roles and responsibilities.

AI systems may introduce responsibilities across business ownership, security architecture, model development, data management, procurement, operations, and risk acceptance.

The organization should define responsibilities clearly enough to avoid gaps in accountability.

For example, a technical team may be responsible for implementing an AI gateway, while a business owner is accountable for the approved use case and an authorized risk owner decides whether residual risk is acceptable.

Audit testing may compare the documented responsibility model with actual approvals, operational records, and staff understanding.

### 112. Organizational Control: Segregation of Duties

Control A.5.3 addresses segregation of duties.

AI development and deployment processes may create conflicts if one person can change a model, approve the change, deploy it, and modify the related audit records without independent oversight.

The organization should identify material conflicting responsibilities and establish proportionate separation or compensating controls.

Possible measures include independent release approval, restricted administrative access, peer review, and protected logging.

Audit testing should determine whether the separation exists in practice and whether exceptions are appropriately authorized.

Segregation requirements should be proportionate to the system's criticality, risk exposure, and operational context.

### 113. Organizational Control: Contact With Authorities

Control A.5.5 addresses contact with relevant authorities.

AI-related security incidents may create notification, reporting, or coordination requirements depending on the incident, the organization's sector, and applicable law.

The organization should identify relevant authorities and establish procedures for determining when contact is required.

The procedure should specify responsible personnel, escalation criteria, required information, and approval arrangements.

An auditor may inspect the incident-response procedure, verify that responsibilities are assigned, and review relevant exercises or historical incident records.

The existence of a contact list alone does not demonstrate that the organization can determine and meet applicable reporting obligations.

### 114. Organizational Control: Contact With Special Interest Groups

Control A.5.6 addresses contact with special interest groups, professional associations, or other specialist security forums.

For AI security, relevant sources may include trusted security communities, industry groups, vulnerability disclosure programs, and specialist information-sharing arrangements.

These relationships can help the organization identify emerging threats, weaknesses, and defensive practices.

The organization should determine whether such information sources are relevant to its risk profile and how information received is assessed and incorporated into security processes.

Audit evidence may include documented subscriptions, threat intelligence reviews, internal advisories, and records showing that relevant information informed risk assessments or control improvements.

### 115. Organizational Control: Threat Intelligence

Control A.5.7 addresses threat intelligence.

AI-related threat intelligence may concern prompt injection, model theft, dataset poisoning, malicious packages, compromised AI dependencies, and attacks against AI-enabled applications.

The organization should establish how relevant threat information is collected, analyzed, prioritized, and communicated to responsible teams.

The process should distinguish between general threat reporting and intelligence relevant to the organization's actual architecture and exposure.

Audit testing may examine whether relevant intelligence is reviewed, whether material findings trigger risk reassessment, and whether resulting actions are tracked.

A threat intelligence subscription alone does not establish that the organization uses the information effectively.

### 116. Organizational Control: Information Security in Project Management

Control A.5.8 addresses information security in project management.

AI projects should incorporate security requirements throughout planning, design, procurement, development, testing, deployment, and closure.

The organization should determine which project stages require risk assessments, security reviews, approvals, and evidence retention.

For example, a project introducing an AI assistant connected to internal documents may require architecture review, retrieval authorization testing, supplier assessment, and approval before production release.

Audit sampling should examine whether the required security activities were completed at the appropriate stages and whether unresolved issues were addressed before deployment.

### 117. Organizational Control: Inventory of Information and Other Associated Assets

Control A.5.9 addresses the inventory of information and other associated assets.

An AI-related inventory may need to identify models, datasets, AI applications, model endpoints, vector databases, connected tools, and external AI services.

The organization should define the inventory's scope, ownership, update process, and required attributes.

Audit testing may compare inventory records with procurement data, cloud-service records, architecture diagrams, development repositories, and interviews with business units.

The auditor should investigate discrepancies to determine whether material AI assets are missing or incorrectly classified.

Inventory completeness is important because risks and controls cannot be managed reliably when relevant assets remain unknown.

### 118. Organizational Control: Acceptable Use of Information and Other Associated Assets

Control A.5.10 addresses acceptable use of information and associated assets.

AI acceptable-use requirements should define permitted and prohibited uses of AI services and establish rules for handling organizational information.

Requirements may address confidential data, source code, personal information, external AI tools, generated code, and AI-assisted decisions.

The organization should communicate requirements and provide a process for questions, exceptions, and incident reporting.

Audit testing may include policy review, employee interviews, training records, and sampled investigations of unauthorized AI use.

Where technical enforcement is possible and proportionate, the organization should consider controls that supplement user awareness.

### 119. Organizational Control: Return of Assets

Control A.5.11 addresses the return of assets when employment, contracts, or other relationships change or terminate, as applicable.

AI environments may include access to model repositories, datasets, cloud AI services, API credentials, evaluation platforms, and administrative consoles.

The organization should ensure that relevant assets and access rights are addressed during personnel and supplier offboarding.

Audit testing may examine termination records, account revocation evidence, credential rotation, asset-return records, and access to AI development environments.

The auditor should verify that the process covers relevant digital access and information assets, not only physical equipment.

### 120. Organizational Control: Classification of Information

Control A.5.12 addresses classification of information according to the organization's information security needs.

AI workflows may process information of different sensitivity levels, including public content, internal documentation, confidential records, and restricted business information.

The organization should define how classification requirements apply to prompts, datasets, outputs, embeddings, model artifacts, and stored conversation records where relevant.

Audit testing may examine whether information classification is reflected in AI usage restrictions, access permissions, retention practices, and approved service configurations.

Classification should guide protection decisions across the information lifecycle rather than being applied only to original source documents.

### 121. Organizational Control: Labelling of Information

Control A.5.13 addresses labelling of information in accordance with the organization's classification scheme.

AI-related information may require labels or metadata that preserve classification when information is transferred, stored, processed, or reused.

Examples include labelled documents ingested into a retrieval system, classified evaluation datasets, and exported AI-generated reports containing confidential material.

The organization should determine whether existing labelling mechanisms remain effective when information is transformed or incorporated into AI workflows.

Audit testing may inspect representative data flows and verify whether classification metadata is retained or otherwise enforced.

Where automatic labelling is used, the organization should assess its reliability and define how errors are handled.

### 122. Organizational Control: Information Transfer

Control A.5.14 addresses information transfer.

AI applications frequently transfer information between users, enterprise systems, model providers, plugins, and external APIs.

The organization should establish requirements for secure transfer, authorized destinations, data protection, and the handling of third-party services.

Audit testing may examine API configurations, network controls, supplier agreements, data-flow diagrams, and records of approved transfers.

The auditor should verify that information transfers follow documented requirements and that sensitive data is not sent to unapproved destinations.

The presence of encryption does not, by itself, establish that a transfer is authorized or appropriate.

### 123. Organizational Control: Access Control

Control A.5.15 addresses access control.

AI integration should apply the organization's access-control rules to models, datasets, applications, administrative functions, and supporting services.

The organization should define access requirements according to business and information security needs.

Audit procedures may include reviewing access policies, sampling user permissions, examining privileged accounts, and testing authorization at relevant interfaces.

For AI systems using retrieval or connected tools, the assessment should determine whether access decisions are enforced for the underlying information and operations.

Access control should not depend exclusively on model-generated decisions or natural-language instructions.

### 124. Organizational Control: Identity Management

Control A.5.16 addresses identity management.

AI environments may use human identities, service accounts, workload identities, and identities associated with automated agents.

The organization should establish processes for identity creation, modification, review, and removal.

Audit testing may inspect identity inventories, account lifecycle records, privileged identity configurations, and evidence of access reviews.

Where AI agents use service identities, the auditor should verify that identities are attributable, appropriately scoped, and managed through approved processes.

Shared or untraceable credentials can weaken accountability and make incident investigations more difficult.

### 125. Organizational Control: Authentication Information

Control A.5.17 addresses authentication information.

AI systems may rely on API keys, tokens, passwords, certificates, and other authentication mechanisms.

The organization should establish requirements for issuing, protecting, storing, transmitting, rotating, and revoking authentication information.

Audit testing may examine secrets-management configurations, repository scanning results, credential rotation records, and access to secret stores.

The auditor should investigate whether credentials are exposed through source code, logs, prompts, notebooks, or configuration files.

Where credentials are compromised, the organization should have a defined process for revocation, replacement, investigation, and remediation.

### 126. Organizational Control: Access Rights

Control A.5.18 addresses access rights.

The organization should provision, review, modify, and remove access rights in accordance with its access-control policy and applicable business requirements.

For AI systems, access rights may cover model registries, training datasets, vector stores, cloud platforms, production endpoints, and administrative interfaces.

Audit sampling may compare approved access requests with current permissions and examine whether access reviews identify and remediate inappropriate privileges.

The auditor should also consider whether personnel changing roles retain access that is no longer required.

For high-impact AI systems, excessive permissions may increase the consequences of compromised identities or unsafe automation.

### 127. Organizational Control: Information Security in Supplier Relationships

Control A.5.19 addresses information security in supplier relationships.

AI integration may create dependencies on model providers, data providers, cloud platforms, managed AI services, and software suppliers.

The organization should determine which supplier relationships create information security risks and establish appropriate requirements.

Audit testing may examine supplier inventories, risk assessments, security requirements, approvals, and ongoing review records.

The auditor should verify that AI providers are considered within the supplier management process and that relevant information-processing dependencies are not overlooked.

### 128. Organizational Control: Addressing Information Security Within Supplier Agreements

Control A.5.20 addresses information security within supplier agreements.

Agreements with AI providers may need to address confidentiality, permitted data use, access, security responsibilities, incident notification, subcontracting, retention, deletion, and service continuity, depending on the service and applicable obligations.

The organization should identify which requirements are necessary for each material supplier relationship.

Audit testing may compare risk assessment results with contractual provisions and investigate gaps.

The existence of a standard supplier contract does not prove that it adequately addresses the risks of a particular AI service.

### 129. Organizational Control: Managing Information Security in the ICT Supply Chain

Control A.5.21 addresses managing information security in the information and communication technology supply chain.

AI systems may depend on upstream model providers, open-source packages, datasets, orchestration frameworks, cloud services, and other technical components.

The organization should assess material dependencies and determine how relevant supply-chain risks are identified and managed.

Audit evidence may include dependency inventories, supplier reviews, component verification, vulnerability monitoring, and change records.

The assessment should consider whether the organization can identify critical dependencies and respond when a component becomes compromised, unsupported, or unsuitable.

### 130. Organizational Control: Monitoring, Review, and Change Management of Supplier Services

Control A.5.22 addresses monitoring, review, and change management of supplier services.

AI providers may change models, service configurations, processing locations, dependencies, or service capabilities over time.

The organization should determine how material changes are identified, assessed, communicated, and approved where required.

Audit testing may examine provider notifications, periodic supplier reviews, service performance reports, and records of risk reassessment.

The auditor should verify whether changes that affect data handling or security assumptions trigger appropriate action.

### 131. Organizational Control: Information Security for Use of Cloud Services

Control A.5.23 addresses information security for the use of cloud services.

Cloud AI platforms may provide model hosting, inference APIs, managed data stores, training environments, and AI development tools.

The organization should establish processes for acquisition, use, management, and exit from cloud services in accordance with its security requirements.

Audit testing may examine cloud service approvals, responsibility assignments, identity configurations, data-handling arrangements, monitoring, and exit plans.

The assessment should account for the actual service model and shared-responsibility arrangement.

### 132. Organizational Control: Information Security Incident Management Planning and Preparation

Control A.5.24 addresses planning and preparation for information security incident management.

AI-related preparation should identify relevant incident scenarios, escalation routes, responsibilities, communication requirements, and available response capabilities.

Scenarios may include unauthorized data disclosure, model compromise, agent misuse, supplier incidents, and disruption of AI-dependent business processes.

Audit evidence may include incident-response plans, contact arrangements, playbooks, exercises, and records of lessons learned.

The organization should verify that its incident-management arrangements address AI systems where they are relevant to the ISMS scope.

### 133. Organizational Control: Assessment and Decision on Information Security Events

Control A.5.25 addresses the assessment of information security events and decisions about whether to classify them as incidents.

AI monitoring may generate unusual outputs, suspicious API activity, unexpected tool calls, access anomalies, or reports of sensitive information disclosure.

The organization should define how such events are assessed, prioritized, escalated, and classified.

Audit testing may examine event records, triage decisions, escalation times, and evidence supporting incident classification.

The assessment should determine whether personnel can distinguish ordinary model errors from events that indicate a potential information security incident, while allowing uncertain cases to be investigated appropriately.

### 134. Organizational Control: Response to Information Security Incidents

Control A.5.26 addresses response to information security incidents in accordance with documented procedures.

AI incident response may require disabling a model endpoint, restricting an agent's tool access, revoking credentials, isolating affected systems, or suspending an integration.

The organization should define response responsibilities and ensure that actions are proportionate to the incident and business impact.

Audit testing may examine incident records, response timelines, containment decisions, communications, and remediation evidence.

The auditor should verify that AI-related incidents are managed through the established incident process and that lessons learned inform subsequent improvements.

### 135. Organizational Control: Learning From Information Security Incidents

Control A.5.27 addresses learning from information security incidents.

AI incidents may reveal weaknesses in access control, model configuration, data governance, supplier management, monitoring, or employee awareness.

The organization should determine how lessons are identified, recorded, prioritized, and incorporated into risk treatment and control improvements.

Audit evidence may include root-cause analyses, corrective action records, updated risk assessments, revised procedures, and follow-up testing.

Repeated incidents with similar causes may indicate that previous corrective actions were incomplete or ineffective.

### 136. Organizational Control: Collection of Evidence

Control A.5.28 addresses the collection of evidence.

AI-related investigations may require logs, access records, model versions, prompts, tool-call histories, dataset records, configuration snapshots, and provider communications.

The organization should establish procedures for collecting and preserving relevant evidence in a manner appropriate to the incident and applicable requirements.

Audit testing may examine whether evidence sources are identified, access is controlled, timestamps and versions are retained, and relevant records are protected against unauthorized alteration.

The organization should also consider privacy, confidentiality, and retention requirements when collecting AI-related information.

### 137. Organizational Control: Information Security During Disruption

Control A.5.29 addresses information security during disruption.

If an AI service becomes unavailable or compromised, the organization should maintain appropriate information security during the disruption and recovery process.

Relevant arrangements may include fallback procedures, restricted emergency access, alternative processing, and continued protection of sensitive information.

Audit testing may examine continuity plans, disruption exercises, emergency access arrangements, and records of actual incidents.

The organization should verify that emergency procedures do not introduce uncontrolled access or bypass essential security safeguards without appropriate authorization and oversight.

### 138. Organizational Control: ICT Readiness for Business Continuity

Control A.5.30 addresses ICT readiness for business continuity.

Where AI services support critical processes, the organization should determine the required availability and recovery capabilities of the supporting technology.

Relevant considerations may include model endpoint availability, dependent cloud services, data stores, network connectivity, credentials, and alternative workflows.

Audit evidence may include recovery objectives, continuity plans, test results, provider arrangements, and documented recovery responsibilities.

The organization should verify that recovery planning reflects the business importance of the AI service rather than relying only on a provider's general availability commitment.

### 139. Organizational Control: Legal, Statutory, Regulatory, and Contractual Requirements

Control A.5.31 addresses identifying, documenting, and keeping relevant legal, statutory, regulatory, and contractual requirements up to date.

AI-related information security requirements may arise from data protection laws, sector regulations, contractual commitments, confidentiality obligations, and customer requirements.

The organization should identify which requirements apply to the AI use case and determine how they affect security controls and evidence.

Audit testing may examine requirements registers, legal assessments, contracts, and records demonstrating that relevant obligations have been translated into operational requirements.

The organization should avoid assuming that every AI system is subject to the same legal requirements; applicability depends on context and use.

### 140. Organizational Control: Intellectual Property Rights

Control A.5.32 addresses intellectual property rights.

AI systems may use proprietary datasets, software, model artifacts, generated code, licensed materials, and third-party components.

The organization should establish processes for identifying and meeting relevant intellectual property requirements.

Audit testing may examine licensing records, approved data sources, software dependency reviews, model usage terms, and restrictions on copying or redistribution.

The assessment should determine whether relevant rights and restrictions are considered in procurement, development, and deployment activities.

### 141. Organizational Control: Protection of Records

Control A.5.33 addresses protection of records.

AI-related records may include risk assessments, approvals, audit logs, model release records, security test results, incident evidence, and management review decisions.

The organization should determine which records must be retained and protect them against loss, unauthorized access, alteration, and inappropriate disclosure.

Audit testing may examine retention schedules, access restrictions, backup arrangements, and record integrity controls.

The organization should balance evidentiary needs with confidentiality, privacy, and applicable retention requirements.

### 142. Organizational Control: Privacy and Protection of Personally Identifiable Information

Control A.5.34 addresses privacy and protection of personally identifiable information (PII).

AI systems may process personal information through prompts, training data, retrieval sources, generated outputs, logs, and connected applications.

The organization should determine applicable privacy and information security requirements and establish appropriate protections.

Audit testing may examine data inventories, access restrictions, retention settings, provider terms, and controls preventing unauthorized disclosure.

The assessment should consider the entire processing workflow rather than focusing exclusively on the original dataset.

### 143. Organizational Control: Independent Review of Information Security

Control A.5.35 addresses independent review of information security.

AI-related security arrangements may require independent review where the risk profile, governance model, or applicable requirements make such review appropriate.

The review may examine policies, risk assessments, control implementation, supplier arrangements, and the effectiveness of AI security governance.

The organization should define the review's scope, competence requirements, independence, reporting arrangements, and follow-up process.

Audit evidence may include review plans, findings, management responses, and corrective action records.

### 144. Organizational Control: Compliance With Information Security Policies, Rules, and Standards

Control A.5.36 addresses compliance with the organization's information security policies, rules, and standards.

For AI integration, compliance activities may verify adherence to approved AI use, access control, secure development, supplier review, and data-handling requirements.

Audit sampling may compare actual AI deployments with documented approval requirements and inspect evidence of required assessments.

The organization should define how deviations are identified, recorded, escalated, and corrected.

The assessment should distinguish between an isolated exception and a recurring process failure that may indicate a broader governance weakness.

### 145. Organizational Control: Documented Operating Procedures

Control A.5.37 addresses documented operating procedures.

AI-related operating procedures may cover model deployment, data ingestion, access reviews, service configuration, security monitoring, incident handling, and recovery.

The organization should determine which procedures are necessary for consistent and secure operation.

Audit testing may compare documented procedures with actual operational records and interviews with personnel performing the work.

Procedures should be sufficiently clear and current to support reliable execution, particularly where AI systems are changed frequently or depend on external services.

### 146. People Control: Screening

Control A.6.1 addresses screening, subject to applicable laws, regulations, ethics, and proportionality requirements.

Where personnel have access to sensitive AI assets, privileged environments, or confidential datasets, the organization should determine appropriate screening requirements for relevant roles.

The assessment should consider role sensitivity, legal restrictions, and the organization's personnel security process.

Audit evidence may include role-based screening requirements, documented completion records, and exception handling.

Screening should be proportionate and must not be treated as a substitute for technical access control, supervision, or ongoing security management.

### 147. People Control: Terms and Conditions of Employment

Control A.6.2 addresses information security responsibilities included in employment terms and conditions, as applicable.

AI-related responsibilities may include confidentiality, acceptable use, protection of credentials, reporting of incidents, and compliance with organizational security policies.

The assessment should determine whether responsibilities are communicated consistently to employees and other relevant personnel.

### 148. People Control: Information Security Awareness, Education, and Training

Control A.6.3 addresses information security awareness, education, and training.

AI security training may cover approved AI services, sensitive-data handling, prompt injection, generated-code risks, secure tool use, and incident reporting.

Audit evidence may include training plans, completion records, learning materials, and assessments of understanding.

### 149. People Control: Disciplinary Process

Control A.6.4 addresses a formal disciplinary process for personnel who commit information security policy violations, where appropriate and lawful.

AI-related violations may include unauthorized disclosure of confidential information, misuse of AI services, or deliberate circumvention of security controls.

Audit testing may examine whether policies define consequences, whether responsibilities are assigned, and whether relevant cases are managed through authorized processes.

Disciplinary measures should be proportionate, documented appropriately, and consistent with applicable employment and legal requirements.

### 150. People Control: Responsibilities After Termination or Change of Employment

Control A.6.5 addresses information security responsibilities that remain valid after termination or change of employment.

For AI environments, continuing responsibilities may include confidentiality, protection of proprietary model artifacts, restrictions on disclosure of sensitive datasets, and compliance with applicable intellectual property obligations.

The organization should ensure that relevant responsibilities are communicated and reflected in appropriate agreements or procedures.

Audit evidence may include contractual terms, offboarding procedures, and records of communication where applicable.

The organization should also remove or modify access rights when responsibilities change or employment ends.

### 151. People Control: Confidentiality or Non-Disclosure Agreements

Control A.6.6 addresses confidentiality or non-disclosure agreements.

AI projects may involve confidential prompts, training datasets, proprietary model configurations, customer information, and commercially sensitive outputs.

Audit testing may examine agreement coverage, approval processes, and alignment with data classification and supplier requirements.

### 152. People Control: Remote Working

Control A.6.7 addresses information security for remote working.

### 153. People Control: Information Security Event Reporting

Control A.8.6 addresses capacity management.

AI services may consume substantial compute, memory, storage, network, and API resources.

The organization should monitor capacity and establish processes to manage expected demand and prevent avoidable service disruption.

Audit evidence may include capacity plans, usage monitoring, alert configurations, and scaling procedures.

The assessment should consider resource exhaustion, unexpected usage spikes, and dependencies on external service limits.

### 174. Technological Control: Protection Against Malware

Control A.8.7 addresses protection against malware.

AI development and deployment environments may be exposed to malicious code through dependencies, datasets, notebooks, plugins, container images, and user endpoints.

The organization should implement appropriate malware protection and related safeguards.

Audit testing may examine endpoint protection, scanning procedures, dependency controls, and incident records.

### 175. Technological Control: Management of Technical Vulnerabilities

Control A.8.8 addresses management of technical vulnerabilities.

AI environments may contain vulnerable operating systems, libraries, frameworks, model-serving components, APIs, and orchestration software.

The organization should identify relevant vulnerabilities, evaluate their significance, and implement timely treatment.

Audit testing may examine scanning coverage, remediation records, risk acceptance, and verification of fixes.

### 176. Technological Control: Configuration Management

Control A.8.9 addresses configuration management.

AI system configurations may include model versions, endpoints, access permissions, prompt templates, inference parameters, tool permissions, and deployment settings.

The organization should establish secure baselines, authorize changes, and maintain configuration records appropriate to the system's risk.

Audit testing may compare approved configurations with deployed settings and examine change records.

### 177. Technological Control: Information Deletion

Control A.8.10 addresses information deletion.

AI environments may retain information in source datasets, model-serving systems, logs, caches, vector stores, backups, and third-party services.

The organization should establish deletion requirements according to business needs, contractual obligations, and applicable legal requirements.

Audit testing may examine deletion procedures, retention configurations, provider terms, and evidence of completed deletion requests.

The assessment should distinguish between deleting source data, removing indexed content, deleting stored outputs, and addressing information that may have been incorporated into a trained model.

### 178. Technological Control: Data Masking

Control A.8.11 addresses data masking.

Data masking may reduce exposure when AI systems use sensitive information for development, testing, evaluation, or analytics.

The organization should determine whether masking is appropriate and whether the method sufficiently reduces the risk of identifying individuals or exposing confidential values.

Audit testing may examine masking rules, test datasets, access restrictions, and validation results.

### 179. Technological Control: Data Leakage Prevention

Control A.8.12 addresses data leakage prevention.

AI use can create additional channels through which sensitive information may be disclosed, including prompts, generated outputs, file uploads, external APIs, and connected applications.

The organization should determine whether data leakage prevention controls are appropriate to the AI architecture and risk profile.

Audit testing may include reviewing policy enforcement, testing prohibited data flows, and examining alerts and exceptions.

The auditor should verify whether controls address relevant AI pathways rather than assuming that conventional email or endpoint controls cover every AI interaction.

### 180. Technological Control: Information Backup

Control A.8.13 addresses information backup.

AI systems may depend on datasets, model artifacts, configuration files, application code, indexes, and supporting records.

The organization should determine what requires backup, how frequently backups occur, how they are protected, and whether restoration is tested.

Audit evidence may include backup schedules, completion records, access controls, retention settings, and restoration test results.

### 181. Technological Control: Redundancy of Information Processing Facilities

Control A.8.14 addresses redundancy of information processing facilities.

Where AI services support critical processes, the organization should determine whether redundancy is necessary to meet availability requirements.

Redundancy may involve infrastructure, network connectivity, service instances, or alternative processing arrangements.

Audit testing may examine architecture documentation, failover tests, and recovery records.

### 182. Technological Control: Logging

Control A.8.15 addresses logging.

AI-related logging may cover authentication, administrative actions, model deployments, API access, data retrieval, and agent tool execution.

The organization should determine which events are logged, how records are protected, and how they support investigation and monitoring.

Audit testing may verify log generation, retention, access restrictions, and the ability to reconstruct relevant activities.

### 183. Technological Control: Monitoring Activities

Control A.8.16 addresses monitoring activities.

AI security monitoring may detect unusual API usage, unauthorized access, unexpected tool calls, configuration changes, or anomalous activity involving sensitive data.

The organization should define monitoring objectives, alert criteria, investigation procedures, and responsibilities.

Audit testing may examine alert records, incident escalation, monitoring coverage, and evidence that material alerts are investigated.

### 184. Technological Control: Clock Synchronization

Control A.8.17 addresses clock synchronization.

Reliable timestamps help correlate events across AI applications, identity systems, cloud platforms, APIs, and monitoring tools.

The organization should establish appropriate synchronization arrangements for relevant systems.

Audit testing may examine time synchronization configuration and determine whether timestamps support accurate event reconstruction.

### 185. Technological Control: Use of Privileged Utility Programs

Control A.8.18 addresses the use of utility programs capable of overriding system or application controls.

AI development and operations may involve administrative utilities, debugging tools, scripts, and platform functions with elevated capabilities.

The organization should restrict and monitor use of tools that could bypass normal safeguards.

Audit testing may examine authorization, privileged access, activity logging, and controls over administrative utilities.

### 186. Technological Control: Installation of Software on Operational Systems

Control A.8.19 addresses installation of software on operational systems.

AI systems may depend on model-serving software, libraries, agents, plugins, and supporting components.

The organization should control software installation to reduce the risk of unauthorized or vulnerable components entering production.

Audit testing may examine approved software lists, deployment pipelines, change approvals, and vulnerability checks.

### 187. Technological Control: Network Security

Control A.8.20 addresses network security.

AI architectures may include model endpoints, data stores, orchestration services, management interfaces, and external providers.

The organization should establish network controls appropriate to the information flows and trust boundaries.

Audit testing may examine segmentation, access rules, exposed endpoints, and network monitoring.

### 188. Technological Control: Security of Network Services

Control A.8.21 addresses security of network services.

AI services may depend on network connectivity, managed endpoints, gateways, and external APIs.

The organization should determine and monitor relevant network service security requirements.

Audit evidence may include service agreements, technical configurations, monitoring records, and reviews of service performance and security.

### 189. Technological Control: Segregation of Networks

Control A.8.22 addresses segregation of networks.

AI development, testing, and production environments may require separation to reduce the risk of unauthorized access or unintended changes.

The organization should determine appropriate network boundaries based on system architecture and risk.

Audit testing may examine network rules, environment separation, and controls governing movement between environments.

### 190. Technological Control: Web Filtering

Control A.8.23 addresses web filtering.

Web filtering may help manage access to malicious or inappropriate online resources used during AI development or operation.

The organization should determine whether web filtering is relevant to the systems and users within scope.

Audit testing may examine policy configuration, coverage, exception handling, and monitoring records.

### 191. Technological Control: Use of Cryptography

Control A.8.24 addresses the use of cryptography.

AI systems may require cryptographic protection for data, model artifacts, credentials, communications, and backups.

The organization should establish requirements for cryptographic use and key management appropriate to its risks and obligations.

Audit testing may examine encryption settings, key access, rotation procedures, and protection of sensitive artifacts.

### 192. Technological Control: Secure Development Life Cycle

Control A.8.25 addresses secure development life cycle requirements.

AI application development should incorporate security considerations throughout requirements definition, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance.

The organization should determine how its secure development process applies to AI applications and supporting services.

Audit testing may examine development standards, security gates, testing results, approvals, and evidence of remediation.

### 193. Technological Control: Application Security Requirements

Control A.8.26 addresses application security requirements.

AI applications should have security requirements appropriate to their intended purpose, information sensitivity, interfaces, and connected capabilities.

Requirements may cover authentication, authorization, data handling, secure integration, logging, error handling, and restrictions on tool execution.

Audit testing may compare documented requirements with implementation and test results.

### 194. Technological Control: Secure System Architecture and Engineering Principles

Control A.8.27 addresses secure system architecture and engineering principles.

AI architecture should account for trust boundaries, information flows, component dependencies, failure modes, and the consequences of compromised components.

The organization should establish appropriate architecture review practices.

Audit evidence may include architecture diagrams, threat models, design decisions, and security review records.

### 195. Technological Control: Secure Coding

Control A.8.28 addresses secure coding.

AI applications may include APIs, orchestration logic, retrieval pipelines, plugin integrations, and code that processes model-generated content.

The organization should apply secure coding practices appropriate to these components.

Audit testing may examine coding standards, code reviews, automated analysis, and remediation of identified weaknesses.

### 196. Technological Control: Security Testing in Development and Acceptance

Control A.8.29 addresses security testing in development and acceptance.

AI applications should undergo relevant security testing before acceptance or production deployment.

Testing may include access-control tests, API testing, vulnerability assessment, prompt-injection testing, and verification of connected-tool permissions.

The organization should define acceptance criteria and ensure that material findings are addressed or handled through authorized risk decisions.

### 197. Technological Control: Outsourced Development

Control A.8.30 addresses outsourced development.

Where external parties develop AI applications or supporting components, the organization should establish appropriate security requirements, oversight, and acceptance criteria.

Audit testing may examine contracts, development requirements, supplier reviews, testing evidence, and delivery approvals.

The organization should retain sufficient oversight to understand material risks in externally developed components.

### 198. Technological Control: Separation of Development, Test, and Production Environments

Control A.8.31 addresses separation of development, test, and production environments.

AI environments may contain sensitive datasets, model artifacts, credentials, and production integrations.

The organization should establish appropriate separation and controls for movement between environments.

Audit testing may examine permissions, environment configurations, deployment workflows, and the use of production information in testing.

### 199. Technological Control: Change Management

Control A.8.32 addresses change management.

AI-related changes may include model upgrades, prompt modifications, new data sources, configuration changes, and integration with additional tools.

The organization should apply appropriate change approval, testing, documentation, and risk assessment procedures.

Audit testing may sample changes and verify that they were authorized, evaluated, tested, and recorded according to requirements.

### 200. Technological Control: Test Information

Control A.8.33 addresses test information.

AI testing may require representative datasets, prompts, model outputs, and evaluation records.

The organization should protect test information and control the use of sensitive production data in development and testing.

Audit testing may examine dataset selection, masking, access restrictions, retention, and disposal procedures.

### 201. Technological Control: Protection of Information Systems During Audit Testing

Control A.8.34 addresses protection of information systems during audit testing.

Security assessments and audit tests involving AI systems should be planned to avoid unacceptable disruption or exposure.

The organization should define authorization, scope, test conditions, and safeguards for testing activities.

For example, testing an AI agent with write access to production systems may require a controlled environment or narrowly scoped test account.

Audit evidence may include test plans, approvals, execution records, and incident handling for unexpected effects.

### 202. Integrating the Control Mapping Into the Statement of Applicability

The Statement of Applicability should reflect the controls determined to be necessary through the organization's risk treatment process, including the status and rationale required by the standard.

AI-related risks may result in the selection of existing Annex A controls, additional controls, or both.

The organization should ensure that the SoA is consistent with the risk assessment, risk treatment plan, and actual control environment.

Audit testing may compare these records to determine whether selected controls have been implemented and whether exclusions are justified.

The SoA should not be used as a substitute for detailed implementation evidence or operating-effectiveness testing.

### 203. Identifying Control Gaps in AI Environments

A control gap exists when the organization's existing safeguards do not adequately meet a relevant control objective or address an identified risk.

AI control gaps may involve incomplete asset discovery, weak access restrictions, missing security testing, inadequate supplier oversight, or insufficient monitoring.

The organization should document each material gap, evaluate its risk significance, identify a treatment action, and assign accountability.

Audit evidence may include gap assessments, risk register entries, remediation plans, and follow-up testing.

The organization should distinguish between a missing control, an ineffective control, and insufficient evidence to establish whether a control is operating.

### 204. Compensating Controls for AI Security Risks

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard used when the preferred control cannot be implemented or is not appropriate in the circumstances.

For example, if a legacy AI integration cannot support fine-grained authorization, temporary restrictions on access, network isolation, enhanced monitoring, and manual approval may reduce exposure.

The organization should document why the primary control is unavailable, how the compensating control addresses the risk, and whether the residual risk is acceptable.

Audit testing should assess the compensating control's actual effectiveness and whether it remains appropriate over time.

A compensating control should not be accepted solely because it is easier to implement.

### 205. Residual Risk Acceptance for AI Systems

Residual risk is the risk remaining after selected controls and treatment actions have been applied.

The organization should evaluate residual AI security risk against its established criteria and ensure that acceptance is performed by an authorized risk owner.

For example, a business owner may accept a limited residual risk associated with an AI assistant that processes internal information, provided required safeguards are operating and the risk remains within approved boundaries.

The decision should document the risk, rationale, existing controls, remaining exposure, approval, and any conditions or review date.

Risk acceptance should be reconsidered when the system, threat environment, or business context changes materially.

### 206. AI Security Exceptions and Waivers

An exception or waiver permits a defined deviation from an established security requirement under specified conditions.

AI-related exceptions may arise when a legacy system cannot meet a technical requirement, a supplier cannot provide particular evidence, or a business use case requires a temporary alternative arrangement.

The organization should require documented justification, risk assessment, approval by an authorized role, compensating measures where appropriate, and a defined validity period.

Audit testing may examine the exception register, approval records, expiry dates, and evidence that expired exceptions are resolved or formally renewed.

Repeated exceptions may indicate that the standard, architecture, or implementation approach requires review.

### 207. AI Security Control Ownership

Each material AI security control should have an accountable owner responsible for ensuring that the control is implemented, maintained, and supported by appropriate evidence.

Control ownership may reside with information security, infrastructure, application development, data management, procurement, or a business function.

The organization should distinguish control ownership from risk ownership and system ownership where these responsibilities differ.

Audit testing may compare documented ownership with actual operating responsibilities, approvals, and evidence production.

Unclear ownership can result in missed reviews, unresolved findings, and inconsistent implementation.

### 208. AI Control Evidence Requirements

Evidence requirements define what records or observations are needed to demonstrate that a control has been implemented and operates as intended.

For AI systems, evidence may include system configurations, access logs, test results, supplier assessments, approval records, monitoring reports, and incident records.

The organization should determine evidence requirements according to the control objective, risk significance, and assurance purpose.

Evidence should be relevant, reliable, sufficiently detailed, and traceable to the system and period under review.

The organization should also consider how evidence is protected, retained, and made available to authorized auditors.

### 209. AI Control Testing Methods

AI security control testing may combine document inspection, interviews, observation, configuration review, technical testing, and sampling of operational records.

For example, an auditor assessing access control may review the policy, inspect identity configurations, interview the system owner, and test whether an unauthorized user can retrieve restricted information.

Different methods provide different levels of assurance.

Interviews help explain how a control is intended to operate, but should be corroborated where practical. Configuration inspection shows the state of a system at a point in time, while operating records may provide evidence of activity over a period.

The testing method should align with the control objective and the conclusion being evaluated.

### 210. Assessing AI Security Control Maturity

Control maturity describes the degree to which a control process is defined, implemented, repeatable, measured, and improved.

An organization may use a maturity model to understand the development of AI security capabilities across different domains.

For example, AI asset discovery may progress from informal identification to a maintained inventory integrated with procurement and change management.

Maturity scoring should use defined criteria and evidence rather than subjective impressions alone.

Maturity is not identical to conformity or effectiveness: a highly documented process may still fail to prevent a material risk, while a simple control may be effective for a narrowly defined use case.

### 211. Prioritizing AI Security Control Remediation

AI control remediation should be prioritized according to risk significance, exploitability, potential business impact, regulatory or contractual obligations, and the effectiveness of existing safeguards.

A critical access-control weakness affecting a sensitive AI system may require immediate action, while a documentation improvement for a low-risk system may be scheduled later.

The organization should define escalation thresholds and ensure that high-priority findings receive appropriate management attention.

Audit evidence may include risk-based remediation plans, issue tracking records, approved deadlines, and verification results.

Prioritization should be transparent and consistent with the organization's risk appetite and treatment criteria.

### 212. Validating Remediation Effectiveness

Remediation is complete only when the organization has sufficient evidence that the identified issue has been addressed and the intended outcome achieved.

For an AI access-control weakness, this may require repeating authorization tests. For a supplier oversight gap, it may require verifying updated contractual terms or completed assurance activities.

The auditor should determine whether the corrective action addresses the underlying cause and whether similar weaknesses exist elsewhere.

A closed ticket or management statement alone may not provide sufficient evidence of effectiveness.

The organization should retain test results and update relevant risk and control records.

### 213. Control Dependencies in AI Security

AI security controls often depend on one another.

For example, effective retrieval authorization may depend on accurate identity information, reliable classification metadata, correct document permissions, and enforcement within the retrieval application.

If one dependency fails, the overall control may be ineffective even when individual components appear correctly configured.

The organization should identify critical dependencies and consider them during architecture review, risk assessment, and control testing.

Audit procedures may trace a representative information flow from the user identity through retrieval, model processing, and output generation to determine whether the intended restrictions remain effective throughout the workflow.

### 214. Evaluating Third-Party Control Evidence

Organizations may rely on provider documentation, independent assurance reports, certifications, contractual commitments, and service descriptions to evaluate third-party AI security.

The organization should determine whether the evidence covers the relevant service, environment, period, and control objectives.

A provider's certification may cover only certain organizational boundaries or services. It does not automatically establish that the customer's own configuration is secure.

Audit testing should examine the applicability of provider evidence, identify exclusions or limitations, and assess customer responsibilities.

Where evidence is insufficient, the organization should determine whether additional information, safeguards, or restrictions are required.

### 215. Shared Responsibility in AI Services

Shared responsibility defines how security obligations are divided between the service provider and the customer.

For managed AI services, the provider may operate the underlying infrastructure and model-serving platform, while the customer remains responsible for data selection, user permissions, application configuration, and business use.

The exact division depends on the service, contractual terms, and architecture.

The organization should document the relevant responsibilities and establish how each is verified.

Audit testing may compare provider commitments with customer-side configurations and procedures to identify unassigned or misunderstood responsibilities.

### 216. AI Security Assurance Across the Lifecycle

AI security assurance should cover the relevant lifecycle stages, including procurement, development, testing, deployment, operation, change, and retirement.

Security risks can change as datasets evolve, models are updated, new tools are connected, or systems are repurposed.

The organization should establish which reviews and controls apply at each stage and how lifecycle transitions are approved.

Audit testing may examine whether required security assessments occur before deployment and whether material operational changes trigger reassessment.

Lifecycle coverage should reflect the system's risk profile and the organization's documented processes.

### 217. Secure AI Decommissioning

AI system retirement may require removing access, terminating provider services, revoking credentials, deleting stored information, retaining required records, and disposing of relevant assets securely.

The organization should define decommissioning requirements for AI applications, model endpoints, datasets, vector stores, and associated integrations.

Audit evidence may include retirement approvals, account revocation records, deletion confirmations, asset inventory updates, and closure of supplier arrangements.

The organization should verify that decommissioning does not leave unmanaged endpoints, forgotten credentials, exposed data, or unnecessary access rights.

### 218. Integrated AI Security Control Dashboard

An integrated control dashboard provides management with a consolidated view of the implementation and effectiveness of AI-related information security controls.

Possible indicators include control implementation status, overdue reviews, open findings, risk treatment progress, supplier assurance gaps, and the proportion of AI systems assessed.

The dashboard should identify material exceptions and areas requiring management decisions.

Metrics should have defined owners, sources, calculation methods, and reporting intervals.

A dashboard should summarize reliable underlying evidence rather than replace the detailed records needed for audit and investigation.

### 219. Auditor's End-to-End Traceability Test

An end-to-end traceability test evaluates whether an identified AI security risk can be followed through the organization's management and control processes.

The auditor may select one material risk and trace it through:

1. AI system and asset identification.
2. Risk scenario documentation.
3. Risk analysis and evaluation.
4. Risk owner assignment.
5. Risk treatment decision.
6. Applicable control selection.
7. Statement of Applicability alignment.
8. Control implementation evidence.
9. Operating-effectiveness testing.
10. Residual-risk acceptance or remediation.
11. Monitoring and periodic review.

Gaps in this chain may indicate weaknesses in governance, documentation, control implementation, or assurance.

The auditor should record the evidence examined and distinguish verified facts from management representations.

### 220. Core Principles of AI Security Control Mapping

Effective mapping of ISO/IEC 27001 controls to AI security risks requires disciplined traceability, risk-based control selection, clear ownership, and evidence of actual implementation.

The organization should use Annex A as a reference set within its risk treatment process, not as a substitute for identifying AI-specific threats and vulnerabilities.

It should also distinguish control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness; assess third-party and shared-responsibility arrangements; and verify that remediation actions reduce the intended risks.

The central principle is that **AI security assurance depends on demonstrating how controls address real risks and how their effectiveness is verified—not merely documenting that a control exists.**

This control-mapping foundation prepares the organization to develop AI-specific audit procedures, evaluate evidence quality, and test the operating effectiveness of AI-related information security controls.

## Part 3 — AI Security Audit Procedures, Control Testing, Technical Assurance, and Compliance Evidence

### 221. Establishing an AI Security Audit Program

An AI security audit program defines how the organization evaluates the design, implementation, and effectiveness of information security controls associated with AI systems.

The program should align with the organization's ISMS scope, risk assessment results, audit methodology, and applicable compliance obligations.

The audit universe may include AI applications, model development environments, training and inference pipelines, datasets, cloud AI services, third-party providers, and AI-enabled business processes.

The organization should determine audit frequency according to risk, business criticality, previous findings, system changes, and relevant requirements.

The program should also define audit responsibilities, competence requirements, reporting arrangements, and follow-up procedures.

### 222. Defining the Scope of an AI Security Audit

Audit scope establishes the boundaries and subject matter of an assessment.

For AI integration with ISO/IEC 27001, scope may include the ISMS processes supporting AI systems, selected Annex A controls, technical infrastructure, supplier relationships, and relevant information flows.

The auditor should identify the systems, locations, organizational units, interfaces, and time period included in the assessment.

Scope exclusions should be documented and justified. Excluding an AI component that materially affects the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an in-scope information asset may undermine the audit's conclusions.

The audit scope should distinguish between evaluating the organization's ISMS and performing a specialized technical assessment of an AI model.

### 223. Developing Risk-Based AI Audit Objectives

Audit objectives specify what the assessment is intended to establish.

Examples include determining whether:

- AI-related information security risks are identified and evaluated.
- Selected controls are appropriately designed and implemented.
- Access restrictions protect sensitive AI information.
- AI service providers meet relevant security requirements.
- Changes to models and AI applications follow approved procedures.
- Security incidents involving AI can be detected and managed.
- Risk treatment and corrective actions are effective.

Objectives should be specific enough to guide evidence collection and support clear conclusions.

An audit should not claim to assess model safety, legal conformity, or overall AI trustworthiness if its objectives and procedures address only information security controls.

### 224. Preparing an AI Security Audit Criteria Set

Audit criteria provide the requirements against which evidence is evaluated.

For an ISO/IEC 27001 audit, criteria may include applicable clauses of ISO/IEC 27001:2022, selected Annex A controls, organizational policies, approved procedures, contractual requirements, and relevant legal obligations.

AI-specific technical standards or internal security requirements may supplement the criteria when appropriate to the audit objectives.

The auditor should record the applicable version of each criterion and explain how it relates to the scope.

Where a requirement is not applicable, the auditor should document the rationale rather than silently omitting it.

### 225. Distinguishing ISO/IEC 27001 Audit From AI Technical Testing

An ISO/IEC 27001 audit evaluates conformity and effectiveness of the relevant information security management system and its controls within the defined audit scope.

AI technical testing evaluates particular technical properties or weaknesses of an AI system.

Examples of technical testing include prompt-injection testing, retrieval authorization tests, model endpoint penetration testing, and testing whether an agent can perform unauthorized actions.

These activities can provide useful evidence for an ISMS audit, but they are not interchangeable.

The auditor should clearly distinguish between a management-system finding, a control implementation weakness, and a technical vulnerability discovered during testing.

### 226. Preparing an AI System Audit Profile

An AI system audit profile summarizes the characteristics of the system being assessed.

It may include the system owner, business purpose, model provider, hosting environment, information classification, data sources, integrations, user groups, external dependencies, and production status.

The profile should also identify whether the system uses retrieval-augmented generation, autonomous tool execution, fine-tuning, or other capabilities relevant to its security risk.

The auditor can use the profile to identify likely risk scenarios and determine which controls require testing.

The profile should be validated against technical records and system-owner evidence rather than accepted solely as a management description.

### 227. AI Architecture Review as an Audit Procedure

Architecture review examines how system components interact and where security boundaries exist.

For AI systems, the review may cover user interfaces, identity services, application logic, model endpoints, retrieval stores, data pipelines, plugins, monitoring systems, and external providers.

The auditor should determine whether the architecture reflects actual deployed components and whether relevant trust boundaries are documented.

Potential concerns include direct exposure of model endpoints, excessive permissions, unprotected administrative interfaces, and uncontrolled information transfers.

Architecture review should be supported by appropriate technical evidence, such as configuration records, network diagrams, API specifications, and deployment documentation.

### 228. Auditing AI Information Flows

An information-flow audit traces how information enters, moves through, and leaves an AI system.

The flow may include user prompts, uploaded files, retrieved documents, model processing, generated outputs, logs, and downstream integrations.

The auditor should identify the information involved, the systems that process it, and the security controls applied at each transfer or processing stage.

Testing may include comparing documented data flows with observed application behavior and reviewing the configuration of relevant interfaces.

The objective is to identify unauthorized disclosure, inadequate access restrictions, undocumented processing, and other information security weaknesses.

### 229. Auditing AI Asset Inventory Completeness

The auditor should assess whether the AI asset inventory captures the assets necessary for effective information security management.

Relevant assets may include AI applications, models, datasets, cloud services, API endpoints, vector databases, service accounts, and third-party integrations.

A practical procedure is to compare the inventory with independent sources such as procurement records, cloud service listings, application repositories, architecture diagrams, and interviews with business units.

Discrepancies should be investigated to determine whether they represent missing assets, outdated records, or legitimate scope differences.

The auditor should document the implications of material omissions for risk assessment and control coverage.

### 230. Auditing AI System Ownership

AI system ownership establishes accountability for the system's business purpose, operation, and relevant risks.

The auditor should verify that each material AI system has an identifiable owner and that ownership responsibilities are understood.

Evidence may include asset registers, governance records, approval workflows, role descriptions, and risk acceptance decisions.

The auditor should also determine whether technical control owners and risk owners are identified where their responsibilities differ from those of the business owner.

Unclear ownership may result in unreviewed changes, incomplete risk treatment, and delayed incident response.

### 231. Auditing AI Risk Assessment Methodology

The auditor should determine whether the organization's risk assessment methodology is consistently applied to AI-related information security risks.

The review may examine risk criteria, likelihood and impact definitions, risk acceptance thresholds, assessment records, and treatment decisions.

Sampling should include AI systems with different risk profiles, business purposes, and deployment arrangements.

The auditor should assess whether material threats and vulnerabilities have been considered and whether ratings are supported by documented reasoning.

A completed risk assessment template does not, by itself, demonstrate that the assessment is adequate.

### 232. Auditing AI Risk Treatment Plans

Risk treatment plans document how selected risks will be addressed.

For AI-related risks, treatment actions may include restricting access, changing system architecture, implementing data protection controls, improving monitoring, changing providers, or discontinuing an unsafe integration.

The auditor should verify that treatment actions have accountable owners, realistic deadlines, and measurable completion criteria.

Evidence may include project plans, change records, configuration results, testing reports, and approvals.

Where actions remain open, the auditor should assess whether interim safeguards and risk acceptance arrangements are appropriate.

### 233. Auditing the Statement of Applicability for AI Coverage

The Statement of Applicability should be consistent with the organization's risk treatment decisions and the controls determined to be necessary.

The auditor should evaluate whether relevant AI risks have been considered when selecting controls and whether exclusions have an adequate rationale.

The assessment should also consider whether the SoA reflects the current ISMS and its actual control environment.

A control may be relevant to AI even if it was originally introduced for conventional IT systems.

Conversely, the presence of an AI-related risk does not automatically mean every Annex A control must be selected. Selection should follow the organization's risk treatment process and applicable requirements.

### 234. Auditing AI Security Policies and Standards

The auditor should determine whether relevant security policies and standards address the AI use cases within scope.

Possible documents include acceptable-use policies, data-handling requirements, secure development standards, supplier security requirements, access-control standards, and incident-response procedures.

Testing should evaluate document approval, ownership, communication, review frequency, and consistency with operational practice.

The auditor may compare policy requirements with sampled AI deployments to identify gaps between documented expectations and actual behavior.

Policies should be assessed for applicability and effectiveness, not merely for existence.

### 235. Auditing AI Governance Responsibilities

AI governance may involve security, legal, privacy, procurement, data management, engineering, risk management, and business leadership.

The auditor should evaluate whether relevant responsibilities and decision rights are defined and whether escalation paths are understood.

Evidence may include governance charters, responsibility matrices, committee records, approval workflows, and risk decisions.

The auditor should identify conflicting responsibilities or areas where material decisions lack accountable approval.

Governance arrangements should be proportionate to the organization's size, the complexity of its AI use, and the associated risks.

### 236. Auditing Shadow AI Usage

Shadow AI refers to AI services or capabilities used without appropriate organizational authorization or oversight.

Such usage may introduce information security risks when employees submit confidential information to unapproved services or connect unreviewed tools to enterprise systems.

The auditor should determine how the organization identifies, assesses, and manages unauthorized AI use.

Evidence may include approved-service inventories, network or endpoint monitoring, procurement reviews, awareness activities, and exception records.

The auditor should consider whether detection methods are sufficiently broad and proportionate while respecting privacy and applicable monitoring requirements.

### 237. Auditing AI Acceptable-Use Enforcement

An acceptable-use policy establishes permitted and prohibited uses of AI services.

The auditor should determine whether the policy is communicated and whether relevant restrictions are enforced where practical.

For example, if employees are prohibited from uploading restricted information to public AI services, the auditor may examine technical restrictions, data loss prevention rules, awareness records, and exception procedures.

The audit should distinguish between policy awareness and actual compliance.

Where enforcement relies primarily on user behavior, the auditor should evaluate whether that approach is appropriate for the sensitivity of the information and the consequences of misuse.

### 238. Auditing AI Data Classification and Handling

The auditor should determine whether information used by AI systems is classified and handled according to organizational requirements.

The assessment may include source documents, prompts, uploaded files, retrieved content, generated outputs, logs, and datasets.

Testing may involve selecting representative information items and tracing their classification through relevant processing stages.

The auditor should evaluate whether classification affects access, storage, transfer, retention, and permitted use.

If transformations such as embeddings or summaries are created, the organization should determine how associated information security requirements are maintained.

### 239. Auditing AI Data Minimization Controls

Data minimization reduces the information collected or processed to what is necessary for an approved purpose.

In an information security audit, the focus is on whether unnecessary information creates avoidable security exposure or conflicts with established handling requirements.

The auditor may examine data-flow diagrams, dataset specifications, prompt construction, logging configuration, and retrieval rules.

Evidence should establish whether unnecessary sensitive information is excluded or appropriately protected.

The assessment should distinguish security-related data minimization from a broader legal or privacy compliance determination unless those matters are explicitly included in the audit scope.

### 240. Auditing AI Data Retention and Deletion

The auditor should determine whether AI-related information is retained and deleted according to approved requirements.

Relevant information may include prompts, generated outputs, uploaded documents, vector indexes, training datasets, logs, and provider-held records.

Testing may examine retention settings, deletion workflows, backup handling, provider terms, and completed deletion requests.

The auditor should verify that the organization understands relevant retention limitations, including cases where deletion from one system does not automatically remove information from connected stores or backups.

Unclear retention responsibility between the organization and its AI provider should be treated as a potential control weakness.

### 241. Auditing AI Provider Data Handling

The auditor should assess whether the organization understands how an external AI provider handles organizational information.

Relevant issues may include processing purposes, retention, access, service improvement, model training, subprocessors, security incidents, and data deletion.

Evidence may include contracts, provider documentation, security assessments, configuration settings, and approved service terms.

The auditor should verify that actual service settings and contractual commitments align with the organization's requirements.

Marketing statements alone may not provide sufficient assurance about the specific service and configuration used.

### 242. Auditing AI Identity and Access Management

The auditor should evaluate whether access to AI systems is restricted to authorized users and service identities.

Testing may include examining identity lifecycle procedures, multi-factor authentication where required, role assignments, privileged accounts, and periodic access reviews.

For AI agents, the auditor should identify the identities used for tool execution and determine whether their permissions are appropriately limited.

The auditor should also test whether authorization is enforced by the underlying application or service rather than being left solely to model instructions.

### 243. Auditing Least-Privilege Enforcement for AI Agents

Least privilege requires permissions to be limited to what is necessary for an approved function.

An AI agent may have access to email, document repositories, databases, ticketing systems, or administrative functions.

The auditor should identify the agent's permissions and compare them with its documented purpose and approved operating boundaries.

Testing may include attempting unauthorized actions within a controlled environment, reviewing role assignments, and inspecting approval requirements for sensitive operations.

Excessive permissions should be evaluated according to potential impact, available safeguards, and the feasibility of reducing access.

### 244. Auditing Service Accounts and Workload Identities

AI applications commonly use service accounts or workload identities to access data and invoke external services.

The auditor should determine whether each identity has an accountable owner, a defined purpose, appropriately scoped permissions, and suitable lifecycle management.

Evidence may include identity inventories, credential configurations, access logs, and periodic review records.

Testing should identify unused identities, shared credentials, excessive permissions, and secrets stored in inappropriate locations.

The assessment should establish whether service activity can be attributed sufficiently for monitoring and investigation.

### 245. Auditing Secrets Management in AI Applications

Secrets management protects API keys, tokens, passwords, certificates, and other authentication information.

The auditor should determine whether AI applications store and access secrets through approved mechanisms.

Testing may include reviewing secret-store configurations, repository scanning results, access permissions, rotation records, and incident procedures for compromised credentials.

The auditor should examine whether secrets can appear in prompts, logs, source code, notebooks, or generated output.

Where a secret has been exposed, evidence should demonstrate appropriate revocation, replacement, investigation, and remediation.

### 246. Auditing AI API Security

AI APIs provide interfaces through which applications and users interact with models and supporting services.

The auditor should evaluate authentication, authorization, input validation, rate limiting, logging, error handling, and exposure of administrative functions where relevant.

Testing may include reviewing API gateway policies, endpoint configurations, access records, and security test results.

For external model APIs, the auditor should examine how requests are authenticated and whether data transfers comply with approved requirements.

The audit should distinguish API security weaknesses from model-behavior weaknesses, although both may affect the overall risk.

### 247. Auditing AI Network Segmentation

Network segmentation limits communication between systems and security zones.

AI environments may require separation between development, testing, production, data stores, management interfaces, and external services.

The auditor should compare network architecture documentation with deployed configurations and relevant traffic rules.

Testing may verify whether prohibited communication paths are blocked and whether administrative interfaces are exposed only to authorized sources.

Exceptions should be documented and justified according to risk.

### 248. Auditing AI Cloud Configuration

Cloud AI environments may include model endpoints, data stores, identity services, logging systems, virtual networks, and managed development platforms.

The auditor should assess whether the organization's secure configuration requirements are applied to relevant resources.

Testing may include configuration reviews, access policy inspection, exposure checks, logging verification, and examination of configuration drift.

The assessment should consider the shared-responsibility model and identify which controls are operated by the provider and which remain the customer's responsibility.

### 249. Auditing AI Model Endpoint Exposure

A model endpoint may be accessible internally, publicly, or through a provider-managed interface.

The auditor should determine whether endpoint exposure is consistent with the approved architecture and business requirements.

Testing may examine authentication, network restrictions, authorization, rate limits, monitoring, and protection against unauthorized inference requests.

The auditor should verify that unused or obsolete endpoints are disabled and that changes to exposure are controlled.

An endpoint that is technically reachable is not necessarily vulnerable, but unnecessary exposure may increase attack surface and should be evaluated.

### 250. Auditing AI Encryption and Key Management

Encryption can protect information in transit and at rest, while key management controls govern access to the cryptographic material.

The auditor should determine whether encryption requirements cover relevant AI data flows, storage locations, backups, and provider integrations.

Testing may inspect encryption settings, certificate configurations, key access permissions, rotation procedures, and relevant exceptions.

The assessment should consider whether keys are accessible to unauthorized identities or stored alongside the information they protect.

Encryption should be evaluated as one element of a broader information security control set.

### 251. Auditing AI Logging Coverage

The auditor should determine whether logs capture the events necessary to support security monitoring, accountability, and investigation.

Relevant events may include authentication, administrative changes, model deployment, configuration changes, retrieval access, API requests, and agent tool execution.

The organization should define which events must be recorded and how logging requirements vary by system criticality.

Testing may compare the required event list with actual log output and examine whether events can be associated with the relevant user, service identity, system, and time.

The audit should identify material gaps in event visibility.

### 252. Auditing AI Log Integrity and Retention

Security logs must be protected against unauthorized modification and retained according to approved requirements.

The auditor should inspect access permissions, storage arrangements, retention settings, backup practices, and relevant monitoring.

Where feasible, testing should establish whether privileged users can alter or delete logs without detection.

The auditor should also verify that log retention supports the organization's investigation and assurance objectives.

AI logs may contain sensitive prompts or personal information, so retention and access must be balanced with confidentiality and applicable requirements.

### 253. Auditing AI Security Monitoring and Alerting

AI security monitoring should identify relevant events and route them to personnel capable of investigating them.

The auditor should review monitoring use cases, alert thresholds, escalation rules, and responsibility assignments.

Testing may include tracing selected alerts from generation through triage, investigation, and closure.

The auditor should examine whether important events are missed because of incomplete telemetry, excessive noise, or unclear ownership.

A monitoring platform's presence does not demonstrate that AI-related risks are effectively monitored.

### 254. Auditing Prompt-Injection Defenses

Prompt injection occurs when malicious or untrusted instructions influence an AI system in ways that conflict with its intended purpose or security requirements.

For an ISO/IEC 27001 audit, prompt-injection testing may provide evidence about secure development, access control, application security, and risk treatment.

The auditor should review the organization's threat assessment, defensive design, test cases, and remediation process.

Testing should be authorized and conducted in a controlled environment appropriate to the risk.

The auditor should not conclude that a system is secure merely because a limited set of prompt-injection tests did not produce an adverse result.

### 255. Auditing Retrieval-Augmented Generation Security

Retrieval-augmented generation systems retrieve information from external or internal sources and provide it to a model for response generation.

The auditor should examine document permissions, indexing processes, source validation, data freshness, retrieval configuration, and access enforcement.

Testing may verify whether a user can retrieve documents that the same user cannot access through the source application.

The auditor should also assess whether removed or reclassified information is reflected appropriately in the retrieval system.

The presence of a secure source repository does not guarantee that permissions remain effective after content is indexed or transformed.

### 256. Auditing AI Output Handling

AI-generated outputs may contain incorrect, sensitive, malicious, or otherwise unsuitable content.

From an information security perspective, the auditor should evaluate controls governing how outputs are validated, displayed, stored, and transferred to other systems.

Relevant safeguards may include output encoding, destination restrictions, approval workflows, data leakage prevention, and controls over automated execution.

Testing may examine whether generated content can trigger unsafe application behavior or unauthorized information disclosure.

The scope should distinguish information security concerns from broader content quality or model safety assessments.

### 257. Auditing AI-Generated Code Security

AI-generated code may introduce vulnerabilities, insecure dependencies, exposed secrets, or logic errors.

The auditor should assess whether generated code is subject to the organization's secure development and code review requirements.

Evidence may include developer guidance, code review records, static analysis results, dependency scans, and testing reports.

Testing should determine whether AI-assisted development follows the same relevant security gates as other development methods.

The organization should not assume that generated code is secure because it compiles, passes basic tests, or appears plausible.

### 258. Auditing AI Agent Tool Permissions

AI agents may call tools that read data, modify records, send communications, or initiate business transactions.

The auditor should identify the available tools and determine whether each capability is authorized for the agent's purpose.

Testing may examine permission scopes, approval requirements, execution constraints, and logs of tool invocation.

For high-impact operations, the organization may require independent confirmation or human approval before execution.

The auditor should verify that restrictions are enforced by the tool or application rather than relying exclusively on instructions provided to the model.

### 259. Auditing Human Approval Controls for AI Actions

Human approval controls require designated personnel to review or authorize specified AI-related actions.

The auditor should determine which actions require approval, who may approve them, and how approvals are recorded.

Testing may include sampling transactions, reviewing approval workflows, and verifying that the system cannot bypass required authorization.

The organization should define how urgent exceptions are handled and how unauthorized or failed actions are investigated.

A nominal approval step is insufficient if the system can execute the action before approval or if approvals are routinely granted without the required review.

### 260. Auditing AI Model and Configuration Changes

AI systems may change through model upgrades, prompt changes, data-source updates, configuration modifications, or changes to tool permissions.

The auditor should determine whether these changes are recorded, risk-assessed, authorized, tested, and approved according to established procedures.

Sampling may include model releases, prompt-template revisions, endpoint changes, and updates to retrieval or orchestration components.

The auditor should compare approved records with deployed configurations and investigate unauthorized or undocumented changes.

### 261. Auditing AI Model Version Control

Version control enables the organization to identify which model or artifact was approved and deployed at a particular time.

The auditor should determine whether relevant model versions are uniquely identified and whether their provenance and deployment status are documented.

Evidence may include model registry records, artifact hashes, release approvals, deployment logs, and rollback records.

Testing may trace a production deployment to the corresponding approved model artifact and associated release documentation.

Where the provider controls the underlying model version, the organization should evaluate the available versioning and change-notification capabilities.

### 262. Auditing AI Artifact Integrity

AI artifacts may include model files, datasets, container images, packages, configuration files, and supporting code.

The auditor should assess whether the organization protects these artifacts against unauthorized modification or substitution.

Relevant evidence may include integrity verification, access controls, repository protections, signed artifacts, and controlled release pipelines.

Testing may verify that deployed artifacts correspond to approved versions and that unexpected changes can be detected.

The control approach should reflect the technical capabilities of the environment and the significance of the affected asset.

### 263. Auditing AI Development and Testing Separation

Development and testing environments may contain experimental code, unapproved models, temporary credentials, or less restrictive configurations.

The auditor should determine whether these environments are appropriately separated from production and whether transitions between them are controlled.

Testing may inspect environment permissions, network boundaries, deployment pipelines, and the use of production data.

The auditor should assess whether development tools or test credentials can be used to alter production systems without authorization.

### 264. Auditing AI Security Testing Coverage

The organization should establish which security tests are required for AI systems and when those tests must be performed.

Coverage may include conventional application security testing and AI-specific tests relevant to the architecture and risk profile.

The auditor should examine testing plans, test cases, results, identified findings, and remediation evidence.

The assessment should consider whether testing addresses material interfaces and dependencies rather than only the model's conversational behavior.

### 265. Auditing AI Vulnerability Management

AI environments may be affected by vulnerabilities in operating systems, frameworks, APIs, model-serving software, libraries, and third-party components.

The auditor should evaluate how vulnerabilities are identified, prioritized, remediated, and verified.

Evidence may include scanning results, vulnerability records, remediation deadlines, exception approvals, and retest results.

The auditor should determine whether AI-related assets are included in vulnerability management coverage and whether ownership is clear.

### 266. Auditing AI Software Dependencies

AI applications may rely on open-source packages, model libraries, orchestration frameworks, plugins, and container images.

The auditor should determine whether dependencies are identified, assessed, updated, and monitored for relevant security risks.

Testing may include examining dependency inventories, software composition analysis, package approval procedures, and vulnerability remediation records.

The organization should assess whether dependencies are obtained from trusted sources and whether unauthorized packages can enter production.

### 267. Auditing AI Supply-Chain Provenance

Provenance records help establish where a model, dataset, package, or other artifact originated and how it was transformed or approved.

The auditor should assess whether material artifacts have sufficient provenance information to support integrity, accountability, and investigation.

Evidence may include supplier records, repository histories, dataset lineage, model registry metadata, and release documentation.

Where provenance cannot be established, the organization should evaluate the risk and determine whether additional validation or restrictions are required.

### 268. Auditing AI Dataset Integrity

Dataset integrity concerns whether data remains accurate, complete, and protected against unauthorized changes relative to its intended use.

The auditor should examine dataset acquisition, validation, transformation, access control, versioning, and change management.

Testing may compare approved dataset versions with deployed or training inputs and review records of modifications.

The assessment should determine whether unauthorized or unexplained changes can be detected and investigated.

Dataset integrity should be evaluated according to the dataset's role in the AI system and its information security implications.

### 269. Auditing Training Data Access Restrictions

Training datasets may contain sensitive information, proprietary content, or material whose modification could affect system integrity.

The auditor should verify that dataset access is restricted to authorized identities and that changes are governed by appropriate procedures.

Evidence may include storage permissions, repository settings, access logs, approval records, and data-processing workflows.

The auditor should investigate whether training data can be copied or exported without appropriate authorization and protection.

### 270. Auditing Data Poisoning Risk Treatment

Data poisoning involves manipulating data in ways that may compromise a model's behavior or integrity.

The auditor should determine whether the organization has assessed the risk where training, fine-tuning, or other data-dependent processes are in scope.

Potential safeguards include trusted data sources, access restrictions, dataset validation, change tracking, anomaly detection, and controlled training procedures.

Audit evidence may include threat assessments, validation rules, data lineage, test results, and documented responses to suspicious changes.

The audit should assess whether the selected controls are appropriate to the system and its threat model rather than requiring one universal technical defense.

### 271. Auditing Model Integrity Monitoring

Model integrity monitoring aims to identify unauthorized or unexpected changes to model artifacts, configurations, or deployment states.

The auditor should examine whether integrity checks are defined, whether relevant changes generate alerts, and whether exceptions are investigated.

Evidence may include artifact verification results, model registry records, deployment monitoring, and incident records.

The organization should determine how integrity is assessed when the underlying model is managed by an external provider.

### 272. Auditing AI Supplier Due Diligence

Supplier due diligence evaluates whether a provider can meet the organization's relevant information security requirements.

For AI providers, assessment may include service architecture, data handling, access control, incident management, resilience, subcontracting, and assurance evidence.

The auditor should examine whether the assessment is proportionate to the supplier's importance and the sensitivity of the information processed.

Evidence may include completed questionnaires, risk assessments, independent reports, contract reviews, and approval records.

The auditor should determine whether material gaps were resolved, accepted, or addressed through additional safeguards.

### 273. Auditing AI Supplier Change Notifications

AI service providers may change models, APIs, data processing arrangements, hosting, or security features.

The auditor should determine whether the organization receives and evaluates material change information.

Testing may include reviewing provider notifications, change assessments, internal approvals, and related risk register updates.

The auditor should verify whether significant changes trigger appropriate testing or reassessment before continued use.

Where the provider offers limited transparency, the organization should evaluate the resulting assurance limitation and decide whether additional controls or restrictions are needed.

### 274. Auditing AI Supplier Incident Notifications

The organization should determine whether its supplier arrangements provide appropriate mechanisms for receiving and managing security incident notifications.

The auditor should examine contractual terms, escalation procedures, provider contacts, and internal incident-management processes.

Where historical incidents exist, testing may trace notification receipt through assessment, containment, communication, and closure.

Where no incidents have occurred, the auditor may assess preparedness through documented procedures, tabletop exercises, and review of contractual arrangements.

The audit should not infer that a supplier is secure simply because no incidents have been reported.

### 275. Auditing AI Business Continuity Dependencies

An AI system may depend on external model providers, cloud infrastructure, identity services, data stores, and network connectivity.

The auditor should determine whether critical dependencies are identified and reflected in business continuity planning.

Evidence may include dependency maps, business impact assessments, recovery plans, service agreements, and test results.

The organization should assess whether alternative processes or providers are available when an AI dependency becomes unavailable.

Continuity arrangements should also preserve appropriate security controls during disruption.

### 276. Auditing AI Recovery and Rollback Capabilities

Recovery and rollback procedures help restore service or return to a previously approved state following a failure or harmful change.

The auditor should determine whether relevant recovery requirements are defined and whether procedures have been tested.

Testing may examine model version rollback, configuration restoration, data recovery, credential replacement, and restoration of dependent services.

Evidence should demonstrate that recovery actions are authorized, technically feasible, and compatible with business requirements.

The organization should also consider whether rollback could restore a vulnerable or otherwise unsuitable configuration.

### 277. Auditing AI Incident Response Playbooks

AI incident playbooks provide operational guidance for responding to defined incident scenarios.

Examples may include suspected data leakage, compromised credentials, malicious tool execution, unauthorized model changes, and supplier incidents.

The auditor should examine whether playbooks define triggers, responsibilities, containment actions, communication, evidence collection, and recovery requirements.

Exercises or incident records can help demonstrate whether the playbooks are usable in practice.

The organization should update playbooks when system architecture, threat exposure, or operational responsibilities change materially.

### 278. Auditing AI Incident Classification and Escalation

The organization should establish criteria for determining the severity and escalation path of AI-related information security events.

The auditor should assess whether classification considers potential information exposure, business impact, affected systems, and the scope of unauthorized activity.

Testing may include reviewing sampled event records and evaluating whether escalation decisions were supported by documented criteria.

The auditor should investigate delays, inconsistent classifications, and incidents closed without adequate evidence.

### 279. Auditing AI Incident Evidence Preservation

Incident investigations may require preserving prompts, logs, model versions, tool-call histories, API records, and configuration snapshots.

The auditor should determine whether the organization can identify relevant evidence sources and preserve records appropriately.

Evidence may include incident procedures, access restrictions, collection records, timestamps, and documented custody or handling arrangements where required.

The assessment should consider whether privacy and confidentiality requirements are addressed during evidence collection.

### 280. Auditing AI Incident Lessons Learned

Lessons learned should lead to proportionate improvements in risk management and control effectiveness.

The auditor should examine whether incident reviews identify contributing causes and whether resulting actions are tracked to completion.

Evidence may include root-cause analyses, corrective action plans, revised risk assessments, updated playbooks, and follow-up testing.

Recurring incidents may indicate that the organization has addressed symptoms rather than underlying causes.

### 281. Auditing AI Backup and Restoration

The auditor should determine whether important AI information and supporting components are backed up according to defined requirements.

Relevant assets may include datasets, model artifacts, configuration records, source code, indexes, and critical documentation.

Testing should examine backup coverage, protection, completion records, retention, and restoration results.

The auditor should determine whether restoration has been tested for the system's actual dependencies and whether restored information remains protected.

### 282. Auditing AI Availability Controls

Availability controls should support the required level of service for AI systems and their dependent business processes.

The auditor may examine capacity monitoring, redundancy, failure handling, provider commitments, and continuity arrangements.

Evidence may include service monitoring, availability reports, capacity assessments, incident records, and resilience tests.

The assessment should determine whether the selected safeguards are consistent with business requirements and whether known single points of failure have been evaluated.

### 283. Auditing AI Resource Exhaustion Risks

AI workloads may consume significant compute resources or incur substantial API usage costs.

Resource exhaustion may result from unexpected demand, automated loops, malicious requests, or poorly controlled agent behavior.

The auditor should determine whether the organization has assessed relevant availability and operational risks.

Potential safeguards include quotas, rate limits, budgets, anomaly detection, concurrency limits, and emergency shutdown mechanisms.

Testing may inspect configuration settings and review whether unusual usage triggers appropriate alerts and response.

### 284. Auditing AI Configuration Drift

Configuration drift occurs when a deployed system diverges from its approved configuration.

For AI systems, drift may affect model versions, endpoint exposure, access permissions, retrieval settings, logging, or tool capabilities.

The auditor should assess whether approved baselines are defined and whether deviations can be detected and resolved.

Evidence may include configuration monitoring, deployment records, change approvals, and exception logs.

The auditor should investigate material differences between approved and deployed states and determine whether they are authorized and adequately controlled.

### 285. Auditing AI Production Release Approval

Production release approval establishes that required checks have been completed before an AI system or material change is deployed.

The auditor should identify the organization's release criteria and determine whether they address relevant security requirements.

Testing may sample releases and inspect risk assessments, security test results, change approvals, unresolved findings, and deployment records.

The auditor should verify that approval was provided by authorized personnel and that material conditions were satisfied.

### 286. Auditing AI Emergency Changes

Emergency changes may be necessary to respond to incidents or restore service, but they can bypass ordinary approval or testing steps.

The organization should define when emergency procedures may be used and how changes are documented, reviewed, and validated afterward.

The auditor should sample emergency changes and verify the justification, authorization, safeguards, and retrospective review.

Repeated emergency changes may indicate weaknesses in planning, change management, or operational stability.

### 287. Auditing AI Decommissioning and Access Revocation

When an AI system is retired, its endpoints, credentials, integrations, and access permissions should be addressed.

The auditor should inspect decommissioning records and verify that relevant services are disabled or removed according to approved procedures.

Testing may include checking asset inventories, cloud resources, identity systems, API credentials, and supplier arrangements.

The auditor should investigate whether dormant endpoints or unused service accounts remain active after retirement.

### 288. Auditing AI Security Awareness Effectiveness

The auditor should determine whether personnel receive security awareness and training appropriate to their AI-related responsibilities.

Evidence may include training plans, completion records, assessments, communications, and targeted exercises.

Where appropriate, the auditor may examine whether employees understand approved AI usage, sensitive-data handling, and incident reporting requirements.

Training completion percentages alone may not establish that personnel understand or follow the relevant controls.

### 289. Auditing AI Developer Security Competence

AI developers may need security competence covering application security, API protection, data handling, model integration, and relevant AI-specific threats.

The auditor should determine whether the organization has identified role-specific competence requirements and provides suitable training or support.

Evidence may include role profiles, training records, code review practices, and technical assessment results.

The objective is to evaluate whether the organization has appropriate arrangements to support secure development, not to require every developer to possess identical qualifications.

### 290. Auditing AI Security Responsibilities of Contractors

Contractors and external developers may have access to AI source code, datasets, infrastructure, or production services.

The auditor should determine whether contractual requirements, access restrictions, onboarding, monitoring, and offboarding procedures cover relevant external personnel.

Testing may examine contractor access records, confidentiality agreements, project approvals, and termination records.

The assessment should identify whether external personnel retain access beyond the period or scope required for their work.

### 291. Auditing AI Security Exceptions Register

The exceptions register records approved deviations from established security requirements.

The auditor should determine whether AI-related exceptions are documented, risk-assessed, approved, time-bound where appropriate, and reviewed.

Testing may examine the rationale, accountable owner, compensating controls, expiry date, and current status of selected exceptions.

Expired or repeatedly renewed exceptions should be investigated to determine whether they remain justified.

### 292. Auditing AI Risk Acceptance Records

Risk acceptance records demonstrate that an authorized person has considered and accepted a defined residual risk.

The auditor should verify that acceptance decisions identify the risk, relevant controls, remaining exposure, rationale, approver, and any conditions or review requirements.

The auditor should determine whether the decision falls within the person's delegated authority and the organization's risk acceptance criteria.

Acceptance should not be treated as permanent where the underlying system, threat environment, or business context changes materially.

### 293. Auditing AI Security Metrics

AI security metrics can help management assess control performance and identify trends.

Examples include overdue access reviews, unresolved high-risk vulnerabilities, security testing coverage, supplier assessment completion, and corrective action aging.

The auditor should examine metric definitions, data sources, ownership, reporting frequency, and validation procedures.

The assessment should determine whether reported figures are accurate and whether thresholds trigger appropriate action.

A metric should support a meaningful management decision rather than merely demonstrate activity.

### 294. Auditing AI Security Management Review Inputs

Management review should consider relevant information needed to evaluate the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the ISMS.

For AI-related information security, inputs may include risk trends, audit findings, incidents, supplier issues, changes affecting the ISMS, control performance, and progress against security objectives.

The auditor should inspect management review records and determine whether material AI-related issues were included where relevant.

The assessment should also consider whether decisions and actions were documented and assigned to accountable owners.

### 295. Auditing AI Security Corrective Actions

Corrective actions address the causes of identified nonconformities and aim to prevent recurrence.

The auditor should examine whether findings are analyzed, actions are proportionate to the impact, and effectiveness is evaluated after implementation.

Evidence may include root-cause analyses, action plans, revised controls, implementation records, and follow-up tests.

Closing an issue in a tracking system does not, by itself, establish that the underlying problem has been resolved.

### 296. Auditing AI Control Documentation Quality

Control documentation should be accurate, current, approved where required, and consistent with actual operation.

The auditor should compare policies and procedures with system configurations, operational records, and personnel practices.

Potential issues include outdated architecture diagrams, incorrect ownership records, missing control procedures, and inconsistent risk ratings.

The auditor should evaluate whether documentation weaknesses affect control implementation, accountability, or the reliability of audit evidence.

Documentation should be sufficient for its purpose without requiring unnecessary duplication.

### 297. Auditing AI Evidence Traceability

Evidence traceability enables an auditor to connect a finding or conclusion to the records and procedures that support it.

The auditor should verify that evidence references identify the relevant system, control, period, and test procedure.

For example, an access-control finding should identify the sampled accounts, the expected requirement, the observed permissions, and the basis for concluding that the control failed.

Traceability supports review, reproducibility, and management response.

Where evidence is incomplete, the auditor should identify the limitation and avoid drawing conclusions that exceed the available support.

### 298. Auditing AI Control Test Reproducibility

A reproducible control test can be repeated using documented procedures and comparable conditions to verify the result.

For AI systems, reproducibility may require recording the tested model version, configuration, test account, input conditions, expected result, and observed outcome.

The auditor should determine whether sufficient detail exists to support independent review or retesting.

Some model outputs may be nondeterministic, so exact output reproduction may not always be appropriate. In such cases, the test should define the relevant security property and acceptable result criteria.

The audit record should explain limitations that affect interpretation.

### 299. Auditing AI Control Testing Independence

Independence helps reduce bias when controls are assessed by personnel who did not design or operate them.

The organization should determine appropriate independence arrangements according to the audit type, organizational structure, and applicable requirements.

The auditor should evaluate whether conflicts of interest could affect the assessment and whether any limitations are disclosed.

Where complete independence is impractical, the organization should consider alternative review arrangements appropriate to the risk.

The audit conclusion should reflect the actual level of independence and assurance achieved.

### 300. Documenting AI Security Audit Findings

An AI security audit finding should clearly identify the condition observed, the applicable criterion, the supporting evidence, and the associated risk or impact.

Where appropriate, the finding should distinguish the immediate control failure from its underlying cause.

For example, a finding may identify that an AI service account has permissions beyond its approved purpose, cite the relevant access-control requirement, and reference the configuration evidence and test results.

The finding should avoid unsupported assumptions about intent or impact.

Clear findings help management determine appropriate corrective actions and allow the auditor to verify whether remediation has been effective.

### 301. Core Principles of AI Security Audit Execution

An effective AI security audit combines risk-based planning, clearly defined criteria, reliable evidence, appropriate testing methods, and traceable conclusions.

The auditor should distinguish management-system conformity from specialized technical assurance and avoid overstating what limited testing establishes.

AI-specific testing should focus on risks relevant to the system's architecture, data flows, permissions, dependencies, and business purpose.

Findings should be supported by evidence, assessed against defined criteria, and followed through to effective remediation.

The central principle is that **an AI security audit must establish what was required, what actually occurred, what evidence supports the conclusion, and what action is necessary to address any identified weakness.**

## Part 4 — AI Security Audit Reporting, ISO/IEC 27001 Certification Readiness, Continuous Assurance, and Integrated Governance

### 302. Evaluating AI Security Audit Results

AI security audit results should be evaluated against the predefined audit criteria, objectives, and scope.

The auditor should distinguish between conforming controls, nonconformities, observations, improvement opportunities, and matters requiring further investigation.

For AI systems, the evaluation should consider the significance of the affected information assets, the nature of the risk, the effectiveness of existing safeguards, and the reliability of the evidence collected.

A technically interesting observation is not automatically a nonconformity. A nonconformity requires evidence that an applicable requirement has not been fulfilled.

The auditor should document the reasoning supporting each conclusion and ensure that the final assessment does not extend beyond the evidence available.

### 303. Classifying AI Security Audit Findings

Finding classification helps management prioritize remediation and allocate resources.

An organization may define categories such as critical, high, medium, and low, or use major and minor nonconformities where appropriate to its audit methodology.

Classification criteria should consider factors such as:

- Potential impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
- Sensitivity and criticality of affected AI assets.
- Extent of control failure.
- Exposure to unauthorized access or information disclosure.
- Existence and effectiveness of compensating controls.
- Legal, contractual, and business implications.
- Likelihood of recurrence or exploitation.

The classification method should be documented and applied consistently. An auditor should not assign severity solely because an issue involves AI or because the technology is novel.

### 304. Distinguishing Major and Minor Nonconformities

In management-system audits, major and minor nonconformities are commonly used to distinguish the significance and extent of failures against applicable requirements.

A major nonconformity may involve a significant failure of the management system, the absence of a required process, or evidence that the system cannot achieve its intended outcomes.

A minor nonconformity may involve a more limited failure that does not indicate a broader breakdown of the management system.

For example, a missing risk assessment process for a substantial category of AI systems may indicate a systemic weakness. An isolated failure to retain one required review record may be narrower, depending on the evidence and applicable criteria.

Classification must follow the audit scheme and established criteria rather than assumptions about severity.

### 305. Assessing the Business Impact of AI Security Findings

An AI security finding should be assessed in relation to the business process and information assets affected.

Potential consequences include unauthorized disclosure of confidential information, interruption of a critical service, loss of model integrity, unauthorized business transactions, and failure to meet contractual commitments.

The auditor should consider actual evidence, plausible consequences, affected dependencies, and existing safeguards.

The assessment should distinguish demonstrated impact from potential impact.

For example, an exposed AI endpoint may create a significant risk even if no evidence of unauthorized access has been found. However, the audit report should not claim that a breach occurred unless the evidence supports that conclusion.

### 306. Identifying Root Causes of AI Security Nonconformities

Root-cause analysis identifies why a control failed rather than merely describing the immediate symptom.

Possible causes include unclear ownership, inadequate procedures, insufficient resources, weak technical design, incomplete training, poor supplier oversight, or ineffective monitoring.

For example, an AI application may have excessive permissions because the development team reused a broad service account and the release process did not include an authorization review.

The corrective action should address the underlying cause, not simply remove the excessive permission from one account while leaving the broader process unchanged.

The auditor should evaluate whether the proposed root cause is supported by evidence and whether it explains the observed failure.

### 307. Preparing an AI Security Audit Report

An AI security audit report should communicate the audit's purpose, scope, criteria, methodology, results, and conclusions.

Depending on the audit type, the report may include:

- Audit objectives and boundaries.
- Applicable standards and internal requirements.
- Systems and processes examined.
- Evidence collection and testing methods.
- Findings and their classifications.
- Relevant risk implications.
- Management responses.
- Corrective actions and target dates.
- Limitations affecting assurance.
- Overall conclusions.

The report should distinguish verified evidence from management representations and auditor interpretation.

Confidential technical details should be handled according to the organization's information security and records-management requirements.

### 308. Writing Evidence-Based AI Security Findings

A well-constructed finding should identify the criterion, condition, evidence, and risk or impact.

For example:

**Criterion:** Access to production AI model administration must be restricted to authorized personnel under the organization's access-control requirements.

**Condition:** The audit identified two active accounts with administrative privileges for which current authorization could not be demonstrated.

**Evidence:** The auditor examined the production access listing, sampled the two accounts, and reviewed the available access approval records.

**Risk:** Unauthorized or unnecessary administrative access could permit unapproved model changes or configuration modifications.

**Required action:** Management should validate the accounts' business need, remove or restrict unjustified privileges, and improve periodic access review procedures.

The wording should remain factual and proportionate to the evidence. It should not allege misuse unless misuse has been established.

### 309. Avoiding Unsupported Conclusions in AI Audit Reports

AI security audits may encounter incomplete logs, limited provider transparency, nondeterministic model behavior, or technical restrictions that prevent exhaustive testing.

The auditor should explain material limitations and describe how they affect the conclusion.

For example, testing a limited set of prompt-injection scenarios cannot establish that a model is resistant to all prompt injection attacks.

Similarly, reviewing a provider's general certification does not prove that every customer configuration is secure.

The report should state what was tested, what evidence was obtained, what remains uncertain, and whether additional assurance is needed.

### 310. Communicating Findings to Management

Audit findings should be communicated to the personnel responsible for the affected systems and controls, as well as to relevant risk owners and management.

The communication should explain the evidence, applicable requirement, potential impact, and expected response.

For high-risk AI findings, the organization may require prompt escalation under its established procedures.

The auditor should allow appropriate factual clarification while maintaining independence and preserving the integrity of the assessment.

Management disagreement should be documented and resolved through the organization's audit process rather than by removing a substantiated finding without justification.

### 311. Developing AI Security Corrective Action Plans

A corrective action plan describes how management intends to address an identified nonconformity or control weakness.

A useful plan includes:

- The finding reference.
- Root cause.
- Corrective action.
- Accountable owner.
- Required resources.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards, where necessary.
- Evidence required to demonstrate completion.
- Method for verifying effectiveness.

For AI-related issues, corrective actions may involve changes to architecture, identity permissions, data handling, supplier arrangements, development procedures, or monitoring.

The plan should be proportionate to the risk and should identify dependencies that could delay implementation.

### 312. Evaluating Corrective Action Adequacy

Before accepting a corrective action plan, the auditor or designated reviewer should determine whether the proposed actions address the identified cause and are likely to reduce the risk.

For example, retraining one employee may not adequately address a systemic problem in which an AI application allows confidential information to be sent to an unapproved provider.

The organization may need to improve technical enforcement, revise procedures, and verify that the intended restrictions operate in practice.

Corrective actions should have measurable completion criteria.

The assessment should distinguish between actions that have been planned, actions that have been implemented, and actions whose effectiveness has been verified.

### 313. Verifying AI Security Remediation

Verification determines whether a corrective action has been implemented as intended.

The auditor should identify evidence appropriate to the original finding.

Examples include:

- Repeating an access-control test.
- Reviewing an updated system configuration.
- Inspecting completed supplier assessments.
- Testing a revised deployment approval workflow.
- Examining new monitoring records.
- Reviewing evidence of completed access reviews.

The verification procedure should test the relevant security requirement, not merely confirm that a task was marked complete.

Where the corrective action does not resolve the issue, the finding should remain open or be handled under the organization's established escalation process.

### 314. Assessing Corrective Action Effectiveness

Effectiveness testing evaluates whether remediation has reduced the likelihood of recurrence or otherwise achieved its intended outcome.

For example, if unauthorized AI services were being used because staff lacked approved alternatives, publishing a policy may not resolve the underlying problem. The organization may also need to provide suitable services, improve technical restrictions, and assess actual usage patterns.

The auditor should consider whether the action addresses the root cause and whether similar weaknesses remain elsewhere.

Effectiveness may require follow-up testing after a period of operation rather than immediate confirmation following implementation.

### 315. Managing Overdue AI Security Findings

Overdue findings require review to determine whether the delay creates unacceptable exposure.

The organization should establish escalation criteria based on finding severity, residual risk, contractual obligations, and remediation deadlines.

For high-risk findings, management may need to implement interim safeguards or formally reconsider risk acceptance.

The auditor should examine the reasons for delay, revised completion dates, interim measures, and approval records.

Repeated extensions without meaningful progress may indicate weaknesses in accountability, resource allocation, or risk governance.

### 316. Maintaining an AI Audit Findings Register

A centralized findings register supports tracking, accountability, and management reporting.

The register may include finding ID, audit reference, affected system, applicable criterion, severity, root cause, owner, target date, status, risk acceptance, and verification results.

AI-related findings may also be linked to the AI inventory, risk register, Statement of Applicability, and change management records.

The organization should define how findings are updated, escalated, closed, and retained.

Audit testing should determine whether the register accurately reflects open issues and whether closures are supported by evidence.

### 317. Identifying Recurring AI Security Findings

Recurring findings may indicate that previous corrective actions were insufficient or that the organization has a broader control-design problem.

Examples include repeated excessive permissions, incomplete supplier reviews, missing security approvals, and inadequate logging.

The auditor should analyze trends across systems, teams, and audit periods to identify common causes.

Management may need to introduce organization-wide remediation rather than treating each occurrence independently.

Recurring findings should be reflected in risk assessments and management review where relevant.

### 318. Reporting AI Security Key Performance Indicators

Key performance indicators can help management monitor the implementation and operation of AI security controls.

Examples include the percentage of AI assets with assigned owners, completion rates for access reviews, the number of overdue high-risk findings, and the proportion of production AI changes with documented security approval.

Each KPI should have a defined calculation method, reporting period, data source, and accountable owner.

The organization should avoid using metrics that reward superficial completion rather than meaningful risk reduction.

For example, a high percentage of completed security reviews is less informative if the reviews do not identify or resolve material weaknesses.

### 319. Reporting AI Security Key Risk Indicators

Key risk indicators provide signals that exposure may be increasing or exceeding established thresholds.

Examples may include a rise in unauthorized AI usage, increasing numbers of exposed endpoints, overdue critical vulnerabilities, unreviewed privileged identities, or repeated security incidents involving AI integrations.

The organization should establish thresholds and define actions when indicators exceed acceptable levels.

The indicators should be linked to meaningful risk scenarios and reliable data sources.

A risk indicator should support a decision, such as restricting access, initiating reassessment, escalating an issue, or allocating additional resources.

### 320. Integrating AI Audit Results Into the Risk Register

Audit findings may identify new risks, demonstrate that existing controls are ineffective, or change the assessed likelihood or impact of known risks.

The organization should determine when findings require updates to the risk register.

For example, a failed authorization test in a retrieval system may reveal that sensitive documents can be exposed across user roles.

The updated risk record should identify the scenario, affected assets, assessment, treatment decision, and accountable owner.

The auditor should verify that material findings are not closed administratively while their underlying risks remain unaddressed.

### 321. Integrating AI Audit Results Into the Statement of Applicability

Audit results may reveal that a selected control has not been implemented, is operating ineffectively, or is insufficient for the identified risk.

The organization should evaluate whether changes are needed to control implementation, the risk treatment plan, or the Statement of Applicability.

The SoA should accurately reflect the organization's control decisions and implementation status as required by the applicable standard.

The organization should not change a control's applicability simply to remove an unfavorable audit result.

Changes to the SoA should follow the established risk management and document-control process.

### 322. Integrating AI Audit Results Into Management Review

Management review provides an opportunity to evaluate whether the ISMS remains suitable, adequate, and effective.

AI audit results may contribute information about control weaknesses, risk trends, supplier issues, security incidents, and resource requirements.

The organization should ensure that significant issues are escalated to the appropriate management level.

Audit evidence may include review agendas, meeting records, decisions, assigned actions, and follow-up tracking.

The auditor should assess whether material issues received appropriate consideration rather than assuming that a meeting record alone proves effective management review.

### 323. Using AI Audit Results to Improve Security Objectives

Security objectives should reflect the organization's priorities and relevant information security risks.

Audit findings may reveal that existing objectives do not adequately address AI-related exposure or that progress measures are insufficient.

For example, repeated delays in AI supplier assessments may justify an objective to improve risk-based assessment coverage and reduce overdue high-risk reviews.

Objectives should have defined responsibilities, resources, timeframes, and methods of evaluation where required.

The organization should use audit evidence to determine whether objectives remain appropriate and whether additional action is necessary.

### 324. Auditing AI Security Competence and Resources

The organization should provide the competence and resources necessary to operate its ISMS and implement relevant AI security controls.

The auditor may examine staffing, training, specialist support, budget, tooling, and workload allocation.

Evidence may include competence assessments, resource plans, training records, role assignments, and management decisions.

Where recurring findings result from insufficient technical capability or unclear responsibilities, management should assess whether existing resources are adequate.

The assessment should focus on demonstrated requirements and risks rather than assuming that every organization needs a large dedicated AI security team.

### 325. Evaluating AI Security Governance Effectiveness

Governance effectiveness concerns whether responsibilities, decisions, oversight, and escalation arrangements support the organization's security objectives.

The auditor should evaluate whether material AI risks are assigned to accountable owners, whether decisions are made at appropriate authority levels, and whether significant issues receive timely attention.

Evidence may include governance meeting records, risk acceptance decisions, issue escalations, and completed actions.

A governance structure may be formally documented but ineffective if decisions are not implemented or material risks remain unaddressed.

### 326. Evaluating AI Security Policy Exceptions

Policy exceptions should be reviewed to determine whether they remain justified and adequately controlled.

The auditor should examine exception rationale, risk assessment, approval, expiry, compensating controls, and monitoring.

Repeated exceptions to the same requirement may indicate that the policy is impractical, the architecture is unsuitable, or management is accepting more risk than intended.

The organization should determine whether to strengthen controls, revise the underlying process, or formally reconsider the requirement.

Exceptions should not become an informal substitute for implementing necessary security controls.

### 327. Auditing AI Security Documentation Governance

AI security documentation should be controlled to maintain accuracy, ownership, availability, and traceability.

Relevant records may include architecture diagrams, data-flow diagrams, risk assessments, security procedures, configuration baselines, and audit evidence.

The auditor should examine document ownership, approval, version history, review schedules, and access restrictions.

Testing may compare selected documents with deployed systems and actual operating practices.

Outdated documentation can impair incident response, control implementation, and the reliability of audit conclusions.

### 328. Auditing AI Record Retention for Assurance

The organization should determine which records must be retained to demonstrate that relevant information security processes operate as intended.

Records may include access reviews, change approvals, supplier assessments, security tests, incidents, and management review decisions.

The auditor should examine retention requirements, storage protections, retrieval capability, and disposal arrangements.

The assessment should consider whether records remain available for the period needed to support operational, contractual, legal, and assurance requirements.

Retaining every AI interaction indefinitely is not automatically appropriate; retention should be justified and controlled.

### 329. Auditing AI Security Reporting to Senior Management

Senior management should receive information sufficient to understand material AI-related information security risks and the performance of relevant controls.

Reporting may cover major findings, overdue remediation, significant incidents, risk acceptance, supplier concerns, and progress against security objectives.

The auditor should determine whether reporting is accurate, timely, and appropriate to the recipients' responsibilities.

Evidence may include management reports, dashboards, meeting records, and documented decisions.

The organization should avoid presenting activity metrics without explaining their significance for risk and control effectiveness.

### 330. Conducting an AI Security Audit Closing Meeting

The closing meeting communicates the audit results and provides an opportunity to clarify factual matters.

The auditor should summarize the scope, key findings, overall conclusions, limitations, and required follow-up actions.

Management should have an opportunity to identify factual inaccuracies or provide additional evidence through the established audit process.

Disagreements should be recorded and resolved according to the applicable methodology.

The meeting should not be used to negotiate away substantiated findings without evidence or an appropriate change to the audit conclusion.

### 331. Preparing an AI Security Audit Evidence Pack

An evidence pack organizes the records supporting the audit's findings and conclusions.

It may include control matrices, risk assessments, approved policies, configuration extracts, access records, test results, supplier documents, incident records, and corrective action evidence.

Each item should be linked to the relevant audit procedure or finding.

The evidence pack should preserve context, including the system assessed, the period covered, the evidence source, and any relevant limitations.

Sensitive evidence should be stored and shared according to the organization's access, confidentiality, and retention requirements.

### 332. Protecting Confidential AI Audit Evidence

AI audit evidence may contain confidential business information, personal information, source code, security configurations, prompts, or proprietary model details.

The auditor should collect only the evidence necessary for the assessment and protect it against unauthorized disclosure or alteration.

Where possible, evidence may be minimized, masked, redacted, or reviewed in a controlled environment.

The organization should establish rules for evidence access, transfer, storage, retention, and disposal.

The auditor should also consider whether external auditors or assurance providers require contractual confidentiality arrangements.

### 333. Auditing AI Evidence From External Providers

External AI providers may supply assurance reports, certifications, technical documentation, and security statements.

The auditor should assess whether the evidence applies to the actual service, deployment model, and period being examined.

Relevant considerations include scope, exclusions, exceptions, complementary customer controls, and the competence and independence of the assurance provider.

Where external evidence does not address the organization's material risks, additional procedures may be necessary.

The organization should document reliance on provider evidence and any limitations affecting its conclusions.

### 334. Evaluating Continuous AI Security Assurance

Continuous assurance uses recurring or ongoing activities to provide evidence about control performance and changing risk.

Examples include automated configuration monitoring, vulnerability scanning, privileged access reviews, supplier status monitoring, and periodic control testing.

The organization should define which controls can be monitored continuously and which require manual assessment.

Automated results should be validated for coverage, accuracy, and appropriate handling of exceptions.

Continuous monitoring supplements planned audits and management review; it does not eliminate the need for independent evaluation where required.

### 335. Integrating AI Security Monitoring With the ISMS

AI security monitoring should support the organization's broader information security management processes.

Relevant telemetry may originate from identity platforms, cloud environments, AI gateways, model endpoints, retrieval systems, and application logs.

The organization should determine how events are correlated, investigated, escalated, and used to update risk assessments.

Audit testing may trace a selected alert through detection, investigation, incident classification, remediation, and lessons learned.

The objective is to demonstrate that monitoring contributes to risk treatment and continual improvement rather than operating as an isolated technical capability.

### 336. Evaluating AI Security Control Automation

Control automation can improve consistency and coverage for activities such as configuration checks, access reviews, vulnerability detection, and evidence collection.

The auditor should assess whether automated controls are appropriately designed, configured, maintained, and monitored.

Evidence may include rule definitions, test results, change records, exception handling, and independent validation.

Automation can introduce its own risks, including incorrect rules, incomplete coverage, and silent failure.

The organization should establish how automated control failures are detected and how critical decisions can be reviewed by authorized personnel.

### 337. Evaluating AI-Assisted Audit Tools

Organizations may use AI tools to assist with document analysis, control mapping, evidence summarization, or identification of potential anomalies.

Where such tools are used in audit work, the organization should evaluate their suitability, confidentiality implications, reliability, and human oversight.

The auditor should verify material conclusions against source evidence and retain sufficient records to explain how the conclusion was reached.

AI-generated summaries should not replace inspection of critical source documents when the audit conclusion depends on details that may have been omitted or misinterpreted.

The use of an AI audit tool does not transfer accountability for the audit conclusion from the responsible auditor.

### 338. Auditing AI-Generated Compliance Evidence

AI may be used to prepare control narratives, summarize policies, or draft evidence descriptions.

The auditor should determine whether the resulting material is accurate, complete, traceable, and supported by authentic records.

A generated narrative is not a substitute for evidence that a control actually operated.

Testing may involve comparing the narrative with original configurations, approval records, system logs, or operational documentation.

The organization should establish review and approval requirements for AI-generated compliance material before it is used in formal assurance activities.

### 339. Avoiding Compliance Theater in AI Security

Compliance theater occurs when an organization creates the appearance of conformity without implementing or operating the necessary controls effectively.

Examples may include maintaining a polished AI policy while allowing unrestricted access to sensitive systems, documenting risk assessments without treating material risks, or closing findings without verification.

The auditor should compare documented claims with actual configurations, observed practices, and operational records.

The assessment should focus on whether requirements are fulfilled and risks are appropriately managed.

The existence of a policy library, certification, or dashboard is not sufficient evidence that every relevant control is effective.

### 340. ISO/IEC 27001 Certification Readiness for AI-Enabled Organizations

An organization preparing for ISO/IEC 27001 certification should evaluate its ISMS as a whole, including relevant AI-related risks and controls within scope.

Readiness activities may include confirming scope, reviewing risk assessments, validating the SoA, checking required documentation, assessing control implementation, conducting internal audits, and completing management review.

The organization should ensure that material nonconformities are addressed through its established corrective action process.

AI integration does not create a separate ISO/IEC 27001 certification automatically. The certification concerns the defined ISMS scope and the applicable certification requirements.

### 341. Conducting an AI-Focused Internal Audit Before Certification

An AI-focused internal audit may provide additional assurance where AI systems represent material information security risks.

The audit should be planned using risk, previous findings, system changes, and business importance.

It may examine AI asset identification, risk assessment, supplier oversight, access control, secure development, monitoring, incident response, and evidence retention.

The audit should be conducted with appropriate objectivity and independence in accordance with the organization's internal audit arrangements.

Results should be documented, communicated to relevant management, and addressed through corrective actions where required.

### 342. Evaluating Internal Audit Program Coverage

The internal audit program should cover the relevant ISMS processes and requirements at planned intervals.

AI-related processes may need dedicated audit attention when their risk, complexity, or rate of change justifies it.

The auditor should determine whether the audit program considers the significance of processes, changes affecting the organization, and previous audit results.

Evidence may include the audit schedule, risk-based planning records, scope decisions, and completed reports.

The objective is to ensure that relevant AI risks receive appropriate audit coverage without unnecessarily duplicating other assurance activities.

### 343. Preparing for an External ISO/IEC 27001 Audit

Preparation for an external audit should focus on demonstrating the operation of the ISMS through reliable evidence.

The organization should ensure that personnel understand their responsibilities and can explain relevant processes accurately.

AI-related evidence may include approved system inventories, risk assessments, treatment plans, the SoA, supplier reviews, security test results, incident records, and corrective action tracking.

The organization should identify unresolved issues and disclose relevant limitations rather than relying on unsupported claims of full compliance.

Preparation should improve evidence accessibility and operational readiness, not encourage personnel to rehearse misleading answers.

### 344. Managing AI-Related Audit Sampling

Audit sampling allows the auditor to examine a subset of records or systems and draw conclusions within the limitations of the selected method.

For AI security, sampling may cover applications, access accounts, model releases, supplier assessments, security incidents, or control reviews.

The auditor should define the population, selection method, sample size rationale, and assessment criteria.

High-risk systems or known problem areas may justify targeted testing in addition to representative sampling.

The report should explain material sampling limitations and avoid implying that every item was tested when only a subset was examined.

### 345. Evaluating AI Security Assurance Limitations

Assurance limitations may arise from incomplete records, restricted access, provider confidentiality constraints, unavailable historical logs, or limited testing coverage.

The auditor should determine whether the limitation prevents a reliable conclusion about a material requirement or risk.

Where appropriate, alternative procedures may provide additional evidence.

If sufficient evidence remains unavailable, the report should disclose the limitation and explain its implications.

An inability to verify a control should not automatically be described as proof that the control failed, although the organization may still need to address the resulting assurance gap.

### 346. Integrating ISO/IEC 27001 With ISO/IEC 42001 Audits

ISO/IEC 27001 focuses on information security management, while ISO/IEC 42001 establishes an AI management system framework addressing AI-related organizational management and risks.

Organizations operating both management systems may coordinate audit planning, evidence collection, and reporting where objectives and criteria overlap.

However, integration should preserve the distinct requirements and intended outcomes of each standard.

An information security audit does not automatically establish conformity with ISO/IEC 42001, and an AI management system audit does not automatically demonstrate that all information security controls are effective.

The organization should define the scope and criteria for each assurance conclusion.

### 347. Integrating ISO/IEC 27001 With the NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a voluntary framework for organizing AI risk management activities through its Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage functions.

An organization may use the framework to structure AI risk identification, measurement, treatment, and oversight alongside its ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS.

For example, AI risk mapping may identify threats to information confidentiality, while ISO/IEC 27001 risk treatment determines the necessary information security controls.

The organization should document how the frameworks relate and identify any requirements or risks not fully addressed by the selected mapping.

A mapping between frameworks supports consistency but does not prove conformity with either framework.

### 348. Integrating ISO/IEC 27001 With the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act establishes legal requirements for AI systems and relevant operators according to its scope and applicability.

ISO/IEC 27001 may support aspects of information security management relevant to AI deployment, such as access control, incident management, supplier security, and resilience.

However, ISO/IEC 27001 certification does not establish full compliance with the EU AI Act.

The organization should separately assess the AI Act's applicability, the system's classification and role, relevant obligations, and any required conformity assessment or documentation.

Audit reporting should clearly distinguish information security assurance from a legal compliance determination.

### 349. Evaluating AI Audit Coverage Against Organizational Risk

Audit coverage should be assessed against the organization's AI inventory and risk profile.

The auditor should determine whether material systems, critical suppliers, significant information flows, and high-risk control areas are represented in the audit program.

Coverage analysis may identify systems that have never been reviewed, controls with recurring findings, or major changes that have not received sufficient assurance attention.

The organization should prioritize gaps according to risk and establish appropriate follow-up activities.

A high number of completed audits does not necessarily indicate adequate coverage if the most significant risks remain unassessed.

### 350. Establishing an Integrated AI Assurance Calendar

An integrated assurance calendar coordinates internal audits, control reviews, technical assessments, supplier assessments, management reviews, and other relevant assurance activities.

The calendar should reflect risk, planned changes, previous findings, contractual requirements, and required assessment intervals.

For example, a material change to an AI agent's tool permissions may trigger a targeted security review outside the regular annual audit cycle.

The organization should define ownership, scheduling, evidence expectations, and reporting arrangements.

Coordination can reduce duplication while preserving the independence and scope required for each assurance activity.

### 351. Designing an AI Security Control Assurance Matrix

An assurance matrix links risks, requirements, controls, evidence, testing methods, and conclusions.

For each material AI risk, the matrix may identify:

- The affected system and risk scenario.
- Applicable ISO/IEC 27001 requirements or selected Annex A controls.
- Control owner and implementation status.
- Evidence sources.
- Audit or testing procedure.
- Test result.
- Finding reference, if applicable.
- Residual risk and remediation status.

The matrix should be updated when systems, risks, controls, or audit results change.

It helps management and auditors trace the relationship between risk treatment and the evidence supporting assurance conclusions.

### 352. Assessing AI Security Control Coverage Gaps

A coverage gap exists when a material risk, requirement, asset, or control is not adequately addressed by the assurance program.

The organization should compare its AI inventory and risk register with its control matrix, audit schedule, and available evidence.

Gaps may include unassessed third-party integrations, missing access-control tests, incomplete supplier coverage, or absent verification of corrective actions.

The organization should document the gap, evaluate its significance, and assign a suitable action.

A coverage gap does not necessarily prove that a control is ineffective; it may indicate that the organization lacks sufficient evidence to establish effectiveness.

### 353. Developing an AI Security Assurance Maturity Model

An assurance maturity model can help an organization evaluate the development of its AI security assurance capability.

Possible dimensions include audit governance, risk-based planning, control testing, evidence quality, reporting, corrective action, and continuous monitoring.

The organization should define maturity levels using observable characteristics and evidence.

For example, an informal process may rely on ad hoc reviews, while a more developed process may use a risk-based schedule, standardized test procedures, traceable evidence, and verified corrective actions.

Maturity scoring should supplement, not replace, evaluation of actual conformity and control effectiveness.

### 354. Measuring AI Security Assurance Effectiveness

Assurance effectiveness concerns whether audit and control testing activities identify meaningful weaknesses and support timely risk reduction.

Possible indicators include repeat-finding rates, remediation effectiveness, audit coverage of material risks, evidence quality, and the age of unresolved high-risk issues.

The organization should interpret indicators in context.

A decline in findings may reflect improved control effectiveness, but it could also result from reduced audit scope or weak testing.

Management should review whether assurance activities lead to demonstrable improvements in the ISMS and whether the assurance program remains aligned with changing AI risks.

### 355. Evaluating AI Security Assurance Independence and Objectivity

Assurance activities should be conducted with appropriate objectivity and independence for their purpose.

The organization should identify potential conflicts of interest when personnel assess controls they designed or operate.

Possible safeguards include independent review, separation of responsibilities, external assessments, and documented conflict management.

The required level of independence depends on the assurance activity, applicable requirements, and organizational circumstances.

The auditor should disclose material limitations that may affect confidence in the conclusions.

### 356. Evaluating External AI Security Assurance Providers

External assurance providers may perform security assessments, penetration tests, control reviews, or independent audits.

The organization should evaluate whether the provider has appropriate competence, experience, independence, and a clearly defined scope.

The auditor should review the assessment criteria, testing methods, limitations, findings, and evidence of remediation.

The organization should also determine whether the provider's report covers the actual AI service and deployment configuration in scope.

External assurance can supplement internal oversight but does not remove the organization's responsibility for managing its own information security risks.

### 357. Evaluating AI Security Certifications and Attestations

Certifications and attestations may provide evidence about specific management systems, controls, or service environments.

The auditor should examine the issuing body, scope, validity, covered services, exclusions, and any relevant qualifications.

A certificate or attestation should not be interpreted more broadly than its documented scope.

For example, a provider's ISO/IEC 27001 certification does not automatically establish that a customer's AI implementation has been securely configured or that every AI-specific risk has been addressed.

The organization should combine external assurance with its own risk assessment and control verification.

### 358. Managing Changes to AI Audit Criteria

Audit criteria may change when standards are revised, internal policies are updated, legal obligations change, or the organization's AI architecture evolves.

The auditor should identify the applicable criteria before testing and determine whether changes affect previously collected evidence.

Where criteria change during an audit, the impact should be evaluated and documented.

The organization should avoid comparing evidence against incompatible versions of a requirement without explaining the difference.

Clear criteria management supports consistent findings and defensible conclusions.

### 359. Maintaining AI Audit Methodology and Workpapers

An audit methodology establishes consistent procedures for planning, testing, documenting, and reporting audit results.

Workpapers preserve the rationale and evidence supporting the auditor's conclusions.

For AI security audits, workpapers may include system profiles, sampling rationale, test scripts, configuration extracts, interview notes, evidence references, and finding evaluations.

The organization should control access to workpapers and retain them according to applicable requirements.

A reviewer should be able to understand what was tested, what evidence was obtained, and how the auditor reached the conclusion.

### 360. Reviewing AI Audit Quality

Audit quality review evaluates whether the audit was planned, executed, documented, and reported according to the applicable methodology.

The review may assess scope adequacy, criteria selection, evidence sufficiency, test execution, finding classification, and reporting accuracy.

For technically complex AI assessments, the organization may require specialist review of relevant testing methods and conclusions.

Quality review should identify unsupported findings, overlooked evidence, inconsistent classifications, and conclusions that exceed the audit scope.

The organization should use review results to improve its audit methodology and auditor competence.

### 361. Using AI Security Audit Trends for Strategic Decisions

Aggregated audit results can reveal recurring weaknesses across the organization.

For example, repeated access-control findings may indicate that identity governance requires improvement, while repeated supplier findings may indicate weaknesses in procurement or third-party risk management.

Management should analyze trends by system type, business unit, control domain, severity, and root cause where useful.

The analysis should be based on consistent definitions and reliable records.

Strategic actions may include changing architecture standards, investing in centralized controls, improving training, or revising supplier requirements.

### 362. Evaluating AI Security Audit Resource Allocation

Audit resources should be allocated according to risk and the need for reliable assurance.

High-risk AI systems may require specialist technical testing, while lower-risk applications may be assessed through targeted control reviews.

The organization should consider auditor competence, independence, workload, tooling, and access to evidence.

The audit plan should document the rationale for significant resource decisions and identify any material limitations caused by resource constraints.

Where adequate assurance cannot be achieved, management should be informed of the resulting coverage gap and its implications.

### 363. Integrating AI Security Findings With Enterprise Risk Management

AI security findings may affect enterprise risk exposure, business continuity, customer trust, contractual commitments, and strategic objectives.

The organization should determine which findings require escalation beyond the immediate system owner or information security function.

Evidence may include risk committee reports, enterprise risk register updates, management decisions, and approved treatment plans.

The escalation process should reflect the significance of the risk and the authority required to accept or treat it.

Integration with enterprise risk management helps ensure that material AI security issues are addressed at the appropriate organizational level.

### 364. Evaluating AI Security Risk Appetite and Tolerance

Risk appetite expresses the type and amount of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain, while risk tolerance may define acceptable variation or thresholds.

The organization should determine how its risk criteria apply to AI-related information security risks.

Audit evidence may include approved risk policies, assessment criteria, risk acceptance records, and escalation thresholds.

The auditor should examine whether accepted residual risks fall within authorized limits and whether material exceptions receive appropriate approval.

The assessment should not assume that all AI risks can be eliminated or that all residual risks are acceptable simply because a business owner approves them.

### 365. Evaluating AI Security Decision Records

Decision records document important choices concerning risk treatment, control exceptions, system deployment, supplier selection, and residual-risk acceptance.

The auditor should determine whether material decisions identify the issue, available options, rationale, accountable decision-maker, and relevant conditions.

Evidence may include governance minutes, approval workflows, risk assessments, and documented exceptions.

Decision records support accountability and help explain why a particular control approach was selected.

Where a decision is undocumented, the auditor should assess whether the absence affects compliance, traceability, or confidence in the risk management process.

### 366. Auditing AI Security in Mergers, Acquisitions, and Integrations

Organizational changes may introduce AI systems, datasets, suppliers, and security practices that have not yet been incorporated into the existing ISMS.

The auditor should determine whether relevant AI assets and dependencies are identified during integration and whether risks are assessed before access or operational reliance is expanded.

Evidence may include due diligence records, asset inventories, access changes, supplier assessments, and integration plans.

The organization should identify incompatible policies, unmanaged systems, and gaps in security ownership.

### 367. Auditing AI Security in Outsourced Business Processes

Outsourced processes may use AI to support customer service, document processing, analytics, or operational decisions.

The organization should determine whether relevant information security risks and responsibilities are addressed in supplier governance.

Audit procedures may examine contracts, data-flow documentation, access restrictions, incident notification, and assurance reports.

The auditor should assess whether the organization retains sufficient oversight of information security risks affecting outsourced processes.

### 368. Evaluating AI Security in Business Process Automation

AI-enabled automation may interact with financial systems, customer records, workflow engines, and operational applications.

The auditor should identify the permissions, decisions, and actions available to the automated process.

Testing may examine authorization, transaction limits, approval gates, logging, error handling, and recovery procedures.

The organization should determine whether automation can produce unauthorized or unintended changes and whether appropriate safeguards limit the impact.

### 369. Evaluating AI Security in Customer-Facing Systems

Customer-facing AI systems may expose information through conversational interfaces, APIs, support channels, and integrated account services.

The auditor should assess authentication, authorization, data handling, monitoring, and secure integration controls.

Testing may include verifying that users cannot access other customers' information or perform actions beyond their permissions.

The assessment should consider the security of the entire application workflow, not just the model response.

### 370. Evaluating AI Security in Internal Knowledge Assistants

Internal knowledge assistants may retrieve information from enterprise repositories and summarize it for employees.

The auditor should assess identity integration, document permissions, indexing behavior, data classification, logging, and information retention.

Testing may verify that users cannot retrieve restricted documents through the AI interface and that changes to source permissions are reflected appropriately.

The organization should also assess whether sensitive information is inadvertently exposed through generated summaries or logs.

### 371. Evaluating AI Security in Software Engineering Assistants

Software engineering assistants may process source code, design documents, credentials, vulnerability reports, and proprietary technical information.

The auditor should determine whether approved tools, data-handling requirements, and access restrictions are in place.

Testing may examine repository permissions, extension configuration, code review controls, secrets scanning, and supplier terms.

The organization should assess whether generated code is subject to existing secure development requirements and whether confidential code is transmitted only through approved channels.

### 372. Evaluating AI Security in SOC and Security Operations Tools

AI-enabled security operations tools may summarize alerts, correlate events, recommend actions, or invoke response workflows.

The auditor should examine the tool's access to security data, permissions to execute actions, logging, and approval requirements.

Testing may verify that automated response actions remain within authorized boundaries and that significant decisions are traceable.

The organization should assess the consequences of incorrect recommendations, compromised integrations, and unavailable AI services.

### 373. Evaluating AI Security in Network and Infrastructure Management

AI tools used for infrastructure management may access configuration data, administrative interfaces, network devices, and cloud control planes.

The auditor should examine identity controls, privilege restrictions, change approval, logging, and rollback arrangements.

Testing should determine whether the tool can execute unauthorized changes or bypass established change management procedures.

Where AI provides recommendations without direct execution rights, the auditor should assess how recommendations are validated before implementation.

### 374. Evaluating AI Security in Cloud Governance

AI workloads may be deployed across cloud environments with different identity, networking, logging, and configuration mechanisms.

The auditor should determine whether the organization applies consistent security requirements across relevant cloud services.

Evidence may include cloud security standards, configuration policies, identity reviews, monitoring, and exception records.

The assessment should identify differences in shared responsibility and determine whether cloud-specific risks are reflected in the ISMS.

### 375. Evaluating AI Security in Hybrid Environments

Hybrid AI architectures may connect on-premises systems, private cloud environments, public cloud services, and external model providers.

The auditor should assess trust boundaries, identity federation, network connectivity, data transfer, logging, and responsibility allocation.

Testing may trace a representative information flow across the hybrid environment and verify that security controls remain effective at each boundary.

The assessment should consider whether inconsistencies between environments create unprotected paths or gaps in monitoring.

### 376. Evaluating AI Security in Multi-Provider Architectures

An organization may use several AI providers for different workloads or route requests between multiple models.

The auditor should determine whether each provider and integration is identified, assessed, and governed according to risk.

Evidence may include provider inventories, service approvals, data-flow records, contracts, and monitoring arrangements.

The organization should evaluate whether information security requirements remain consistent when requests are redirected or processed by alternative providers.

### 377. Evaluating AI Security in Model Routing and Gateways

AI gateways may manage authentication, provider selection, policy enforcement, logging, and traffic routing.

The auditor should examine gateway configuration, administrative access, routing rules, data-handling policies, and failure behavior.

Testing may determine whether unauthorized routes can bypass approved providers or whether security controls are consistently applied across destinations.

The organization should assess whether gateway changes are authorized and monitored.

### 378. Evaluating AI Security in Retrieval and Vector Databases

Vector databases and retrieval indexes may contain representations of confidential documents or other sensitive information.

The auditor should assess access controls, tenant separation, index update procedures, retention, backup, and deletion behavior.

Testing may verify that users cannot retrieve restricted content and that deleted or reclassified source information is handled appropriately.

The organization should not assume that transformed or embedded information is automatically free from confidentiality risks.

### 379. Evaluating AI Security in Data Pipelines

AI data pipelines may collect, transform, validate, transfer, and store information used for training, retrieval, or inference.

The auditor should examine pipeline identities, permissions, data validation, logging, change management, and failure handling.

Testing may trace a representative dataset through the pipeline and verify that expected security controls are applied.

The assessment should identify unauthorized data sources, excessive privileges, and insufficient traceability.

### 380. Evaluating AI Security in Model Hosting

Model hosting environments may expose inference endpoints, model files, configuration settings, and supporting services.

The auditor should assess endpoint access, infrastructure configuration, artifact integrity, monitoring, and change management.

Evidence may include deployment records, access configurations, vulnerability assessments, and security test results.

The organization should determine whether hosting responsibilities are clearly allocated between the customer and provider.

### 381. Evaluating AI Security in Fine-Tuning Workflows

Fine-tuning workflows may involve sensitive datasets, model checkpoints, training environments, and specialized access permissions.

The auditor should determine whether the workflow is governed by approved data handling, access, change, and integrity controls.

Testing may examine dataset authorization, training job permissions, artifact versioning, output storage, and release approval.

The organization should assess whether training activities can introduce unauthorized information or compromise the integrity of the resulting artifact.

### 382. Evaluating AI Security in Model Evaluation Environments

Model evaluation environments may contain test datasets, prompts, expected outputs, benchmark results, and security test cases.

The auditor should assess access restrictions, data protection, test integrity, and control over changes to evaluation criteria.

Testing may examine whether results can be altered without authorization and whether evidence is linked to the tested model version.

The organization should distinguish evaluation security from the broader question of whether the model meets every quality or safety objective.

### 383. Evaluating AI Security in Production Monitoring

Production monitoring should provide visibility into the security-relevant behavior of deployed AI systems.

The auditor should determine whether the organization monitors important events, maintains appropriate records, and investigates significant anomalies.

Testing may trace selected events through detection and response processes.

The assessment should consider whether monitoring coverage changes when new tools, data sources, or providers are introduced.

### 384. Evaluating AI Security in Model Retirement and Replacement

Model retirement or replacement may affect application compatibility, access controls, data handling, and audit traceability.

The auditor should determine whether the organization assesses security implications before replacing a model or changing providers.

Evidence may include change assessments, testing results, release approvals, rollback plans, and retirement records.

The organization should verify that obsolete endpoints, artifacts, and credentials are handled appropriately and that required historical evidence remains available.

### 385. Evaluating AI Security During Major System Changes

Major changes may materially alter the risk profile of an AI system.

Examples include adding external tools, changing the model provider, connecting a new data source, expanding the user population, or enabling autonomous execution.

The auditor should determine whether changes trigger appropriate security reassessment, testing, and approval.

Evidence may include change requests, updated risk assessments, architecture reviews, and release records.

The organization should avoid treating changes to AI behavior or capability as routine updates when they materially affect access, information flows, or business impact.

### 386. Evaluating AI Security During Provider Model Updates

A provider may update a hosted model without requiring the customer to deploy a new artifact manually.

The organization should determine what version information and change notifications are available and how material changes are assessed.

Audit evidence may include provider documentation, release notifications, regression tests, service configuration, and risk reviews.

Where model updates are opaque, the organization should assess the limitation and consider safeguards such as restricted use, additional validation, or alternative arrangements.

### 387. Evaluating AI Security for New AI Use Cases

An approved AI platform may be repurposed for a new business process with different data, users, or permissions.

The auditor should determine whether the new use case receives an appropriate security assessment before use.

Testing may examine use-case approval, data classification, access requirements, supplier conditions, and security testing.

The organization should not assume that approval of a platform automatically authorizes every future use case.

### 388. Evaluating AI Security for High-Impact Business Processes

AI systems supporting critical business processes may require enhanced assurance based on the potential consequences of compromise or disruption.

The auditor should determine whether control requirements, monitoring, recovery arrangements, and escalation procedures reflect the business impact.

Evidence may include business impact assessments, risk treatment plans, continuity tests, and management approvals.

The organization should define high-impact status using established criteria rather than treating all AI systems as equally critical.

### 389. Evaluating AI Security for Low-Risk Business Processes

Lower-risk AI use cases may be managed through simplified controls where the organization's risk methodology supports a proportionate approach.

The auditor should determine whether the classification is justified and whether minimum information security requirements remain satisfied.

Evidence may include risk assessments, approved-use criteria, and defined restrictions.

A low-risk designation should not be used to bypass essential controls or avoid reassessment when the use case changes.

### 390. Evaluating AI Security Across Business Units

Different business units may adopt AI services independently, creating inconsistent policies, inventories, and control practices.

The auditor should determine whether the organization has sufficient visibility and oversight across business units within the ISMS scope.

Testing may compare approved-service records, risk assessments, access practices, supplier reviews, and training across selected units.

The organization should identify inconsistent controls and determine whether common standards or centralized services would improve risk management.

### 391. Evaluating AI Security Across Geographical Locations

Organizations operating across multiple locations may face differences in infrastructure, supplier arrangements, legal requirements, and operational practices.

The auditor should determine whether relevant AI systems and processes are included in the ISMS scope and whether local differences are addressed.

Evidence may include location inventories, applicable requirements assessments, access controls, and audit records.

The organization should ensure that geographical boundaries do not create unmanaged information flows or gaps in accountability.

### 392. Evaluating AI Security in Regulated Environments

AI systems used in regulated environments may be subject to additional information security, recordkeeping, operational, or contractual requirements.

The auditor should identify the requirements applicable to the defined audit scope and determine how they are reflected in the ISMS.

Evidence may include requirements registers, risk assessments, procedures, and control testing records.

The audit should distinguish ISO/IEC 27001 conformity from compliance with sector-specific or jurisdiction-specific obligations.

### 393. Evaluating AI Security in Customer Contracts

Customer contracts may impose requirements concerning confidentiality, security controls, incident notification, service availability, and audit rights.

The auditor should determine whether applicable contractual requirements are identified and translated into operational controls.

Testing may compare contract terms with supplier arrangements, security procedures, and incident processes.

The organization should identify obligations that cannot currently be met and ensure they are escalated through appropriate commercial and risk governance processes.

### 394. Evaluating AI Security Assurance for Business Partners

Business partners may exchange information with AI-enabled systems or participate in joint AI services.

The auditor should determine whether information security responsibilities, access boundaries, and incident coordination arrangements are documented.

Evidence may include agreements, architecture diagrams, access records, and joint incident procedures.

The assessment should identify whether a partner can access information or capabilities beyond the agreed purpose.

### 395. Evaluating AI Security Assurance for Open-Source Components

Open-source AI components may be used in development frameworks, model-serving platforms, orchestration libraries, and supporting tools.

The auditor should assess how the organization identifies components, monitors relevant vulnerabilities, evaluates licenses where applicable, and controls updates.

Evidence may include dependency inventories, scanning results, approved package records, and remediation tracking.

The use of open-source software does not automatically indicate unacceptable risk, but the organization should manage relevant security dependencies.

### 396. Evaluating AI Security Assurance for Proprietary Components

Proprietary AI components may provide limited transparency into implementation details or internal model behavior.

The auditor should assess the assurance evidence available, including provider documentation, independent reports, contractual commitments, technical testing, and observed service behavior.

The organization should identify limitations and determine whether additional safeguards are required.

Lack of complete source-code access does not automatically make a service unacceptable, but it may affect the assurance methods available.

### 397. Evaluating AI Security Assurance for Critical Suppliers

Critical suppliers may have a material effect on the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of the organization's information assets and services.

The auditor should determine whether supplier criticality is defined and whether assurance activities are proportionate to the associated risk.

Evidence may include supplier classification, due diligence, contractual controls, periodic reviews, incident history, and continuity arrangements.

The organization should establish how assurance gaps and material supplier changes are escalated.

### 398. Evaluating AI Security Assurance During Organizational Growth

As AI adoption grows, existing governance and control processes may become insufficient for the increasing number and complexity of systems.

The auditor should examine whether inventory management, access governance, supplier oversight, monitoring, and audit coverage scale appropriately.

Evidence may include trend analysis, resource plans, system inventories, and control performance metrics.

The organization should reassess its assurance program when adoption patterns or dependencies change materially.

### 399. Evaluating AI Security Assurance for Continual Improvement

Continual improvement requires the organization to use audit results, monitoring, incidents, risk assessments, and management review to improve the ISMS.

The auditor should determine whether improvement actions are prioritized, assigned, implemented, and evaluated for effectiveness.

Evidence may include improvement registers, corrective action records, revised controls, and follow-up testing.

The organization should be able to demonstrate how identified weaknesses lead to measurable changes in its information security management practices.

### 400. Core Principles of ISO/IEC 27001 and AI Integration Assurance

Integrating AI into ISO/IEC 27001 requires the organization to apply risk-based information security management to AI-related assets, data flows, systems, suppliers, and business processes.

Effective assurance depends on clear scope, relevant criteria, documented risk treatment, appropriate control selection, reliable evidence, and verified corrective action.

The organization should distinguish information security management conformity from broader AI governance, model safety, and legal conformity assessments. Related frameworks can complement the ISMS, but a mapping between frameworks does not automatically demonstrate compliance with every requirement.

The auditor should evaluate the actual operation of controls and clearly communicate any limitations in the evidence or scope.

**The central principle of ISO/IEC 27001 and AI integration is that AI-related information security risks must be identified, treated, monitored, and independently evaluated through the ISMS, with conclusions supported by reliable evidence rather than documentation alone.**
