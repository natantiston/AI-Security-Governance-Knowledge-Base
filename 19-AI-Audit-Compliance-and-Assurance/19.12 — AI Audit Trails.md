# 19.12 — AI Audit Planning, Scope, Risk Assessment, Criteria and Methodology

## Part 1 — AI Audit Foundations, Audit Mandates, Scope Definition and Planning Methodology

### 1. Understanding the Purpose of AI Auditing

AI auditing is a structured examination of an organization’s AI-related governance arrangements, risk management practices, technical controls, operational processes, and compliance obligations. Its purpose is to determine whether the relevant activities are appropriately designed, implemented, documented, and operating effectively.

Unlike a conventional IT audit that may concentrate on infrastructure, applications, access controls, and change management, an AI audit may also need to examine data quality, model behavior, evaluation methods, human oversight, AI-specific threats, and the limitations of automated decision-making.

The audit objective depends on the engagement. An audit may evaluate compliance with applicable requirements, assess the effectiveness of an AI management system, examine a specific AI application, review third-party AI dependencies, or investigate a particular incident.

A properly designed AI audit should establish:

- Whether the audit criteria have been identified correctly.
- Whether relevant AI risks have been assessed.
- Whether appropriate governance and controls exist.
- Whether controls operate as intended.
- Whether conclusions are supported by sufficient and appropriate evidence.
- Whether identified deficiencies receive effective remediation.

AI auditing does not automatically establish that a system is safe, unbiased, secure, or legally compliant in every circumstance. Conclusions must remain within the defined scope, criteria, evidence, and limitations of the engagement.

### 2. Distinguishing AI Audits from AI Assessments and Certifications

AI governance uses several assurance activities that may appear similar but have different objectives, levels of independence, and evidentiary requirements.

An AI audit systematically evaluates an organization, process, system, or control against defined criteria. An assessment may be narrower, focusing on a particular risk, technical characteristic, or implementation question. Certification involves a formal determination against a specified certification scheme or standard by an appropriately authorized certification body.

Examples include:

| Activity                              | Primary objective                                       | Typical output                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance audit                   | Evaluate governance and control effectiveness           | Audit report and findings                       |
| AI risk assessment                    | Identify, analyze, and evaluate AI-related risks        | Risk register and treatment plan                |
| AI security assessment                | Evaluate security weaknesses and safeguards             | Security assessment report                      |
| AI model evaluation                   | Measure model performance against defined criteria      | Evaluation results                              |
| Regulatory compliance assessment      | Determine conformity with applicable requirements       | Compliance assessment and gap register          |
| Management-system certification audit | Assess conformity with a defined certification standard | Certification audit findings and recommendation |

These activities can complement one another, but their outputs are not interchangeable. A successful model evaluation does not establish the effectiveness of enterprise AI governance, and an audit report does not automatically constitute certification.

Audit planning should identify the assurance activity being performed, the authority under which it operates, and the conclusions it is permitted to support.

### 3. Establishing the AI Audit Mandate

The audit mandate establishes the authority, purpose, and organizational expectations for the audit. It defines why the engagement is being conducted, who has authorized it, and what responsibilities the audit function has been assigned.

An audit mandate may arise from an internal audit charter, an approved risk-based audit plan, a board or audit committee request, a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, or a formally authorized investigation.

The mandate should clarify:

- The authority under which the audit is performed.
- The intended objectives and expected outcomes.
- The organizational units and systems potentially covered.
- The access rights and information-gathering authority.
- The reporting line and escalation arrangements.
- The expected independence of the audit team.
- The relationship with other assurance functions.
- Any restrictions on access, disclosure, or reporting.

For internal audit, the mandate should be consistent with the organization’s internal audit charter and applicable professional requirements. For external assurance, the engagement authority and responsibilities should be established through the relevant contract, appointment, or legal instrument.

An audit team should not assume that a general management request provides unrestricted authority to access every dataset, supplier environment, or confidential record. Access must be supported by appropriate authorization and applicable legal and contractual rights.

### 4. Defining the AI Audit Objectives

Audit objectives describe the specific questions the engagement is intended to answer. Clear objectives guide the selection of audit criteria, evidence, sampling methods, interviews, technical tests, and reporting conclusions.

An objective such as “review AI governance” may be too broad to guide a rigorous engagement. More precise objectives could examine whether AI systems are inventoried, whether risk assessments are completed before deployment, whether model changes receive appropriate approval, or whether supplier controls provide sufficient assurance.

A well-defined objective should identify:

- The governance or operational question.
- The relevant system, process, or organizational boundary.
- The criteria against which performance will be evaluated.
- The intended assurance conclusion.
- The principal risks that motivate the work.
- Any important limitations.

Examples of AI audit objectives include determining whether an organization has implemented an effective AI governance operating model, assessing whether a high-impact AI application follows approved human oversight procedures, or evaluating whether model evaluation evidence supports a production deployment decision.

Objectives should be sufficiently specific to support evidence-based conclusions while allowing the audit team to investigate unexpected risks discovered during fieldwork.

### 5. Identifying the Auditable Entity

The auditable entity is the organization, business unit, process, AI system, service, or control environment subject to examination.

AI audit planning must identify the entity precisely because AI capabilities are often distributed across multiple components and providers. A single business application may depend on an externally hosted foundation model, internal orchestration software, retrieval databases, third-party APIs, and human review processes.

The audit team should determine whether the auditable entity is:

- An enterprise-wide AI governance program.
- An AI management system.
- A particular AI application or use case.
- A model development or deployment process.
- An AI data governance process.
- A third-party AI service or supplier relationship.
- An AI security or monitoring control.
- A specific decision-making workflow involving AI.

The entity definition should include relevant organizational ownership, technical boundaries, interfaces, and dependencies.

Where multiple systems share a model or platform, the audit team should distinguish common controls from application-specific controls. This prevents an assessment of one component from being incorrectly generalized to every application that uses it.

### 6. Defining the AI Audit Scope

The audit scope specifies what is included in and excluded from the engagement. It establishes the boundaries within which the audit team will gather evidence and formulate conclusions.

AI audit scope may be defined by systems, organizational units, business processes, jurisdictions, model versions, datasets, time periods, control categories, or regulatory obligations.

For example, an audit of an AI-powered customer service platform might include model configuration, retrieval sources, access controls, data protection, human escalation, logging, incident management, and supplier governance. It might exclude unrelated enterprise applications that do not affect the platform’s risk profile.

Scope definition should address:

- Systems and components covered.
- Business processes and intended uses.
- Organizational units and responsible functions.
- Relevant model and configuration versions.
- Data sources and external dependencies.
- Applicable locations and jurisdictions.
- The period under review.
- Included control domains.
- Explicit exclusions and their rationale.

The scope should be documented and communicated to relevant stakeholders before detailed fieldwork begins.

Scope limitations should not be hidden. If the audit cannot examine an important supplier component or obtain necessary technical evidence, the report should explain the resulting limitation and its effect on the assurance conclusion.

### 7. Understanding the AI System Context Before Audit Fieldwork

Effective AI audit planning begins with understanding how the system is intended to operate and how it contributes to the organization’s business activities.

Without sufficient context, auditors may select irrelevant controls, overlook critical dependencies, or evaluate the system against assumptions that do not reflect its actual deployment.

The preliminary understanding should cover:

- Business objectives and intended purpose.
- User groups and affected stakeholders.
- System architecture and principal components.
- Model providers and deployment arrangements.
- Data sources and processing activities.
- Inputs, outputs, and downstream actions.
- Human oversight and intervention points.
- Security boundaries and trust relationships.
- Operational dependencies and failure scenarios.
- Known limitations and prior incidents.

The audit team should review existing documentation, interview relevant stakeholders, and inspect the system inventory before finalizing the audit approach.

For complex AI applications, the team should distinguish the underlying model from the complete deployed system. Application logic, prompts, retrieval mechanisms, tool permissions, and human workflows may materially influence behavior and risk.

### 8. Establishing the Applicable Audit Criteria

Audit criteria are the requirements, standards, policies, control objectives, contractual commitments, or other benchmarks used to evaluate the auditable entity.

The criteria must be relevant, sufficiently precise, and appropriate to the engagement. An audit cannot produce a defensible conformity conclusion if its evaluation benchmark is unclear or incorrectly applied.

Potential criteria include:

- Applicable legislation and regulatory requirements.
- ISO/IEC 42001 requirements for AI management systems.
- Relevant guidance from ISO/IEC 23894 on AI risk management.
- The NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework or other applicable security frameworks.
- Internal AI governance policies and procedures.
- Contractual security and assurance obligations.
- Approved risk acceptance criteria.
- Technical requirements and control specifications.

These sources do not all have the same legal status. Legislation may impose binding obligations, while standards and frameworks may be voluntary unless incorporated into law, regulation, contract, or organizational policy.

The audit criteria register should identify the source, relevant version, applicability rationale, and specific requirement or control objective being tested.

Where several criteria overlap, the audit team should map them carefully to avoid double-counting findings or treating different requirements as identical.

### 9. Distinguishing Legal Compliance from Control Effectiveness

Legal compliance and control effectiveness are related but separate audit questions.

A compliance audit determines whether applicable legal or regulatory requirements have been met. A control-effectiveness audit evaluates whether the organization’s safeguards are appropriately designed and operating as intended.

An organization may have a documented control that is consistently operated but still fail to satisfy a legal requirement. Conversely, a legal obligation may be met through a combination of technical and organizational measures without a single control corresponding directly to the wording of the law.

Audit planning should distinguish:

- **Legal applicability:** Whether a legal obligation applies to the entity and use case.
- **Control design:** Whether the selected control is capable of addressing the relevant objective.
- **Implementation:** Whether the control has been put into operation.
- **Operating effectiveness:** Whether the control functions as intended over the relevant period.
- **Compliance conclusion:** Whether the available evidence supports a conclusion about the applicable obligation.

The audit team should avoid declaring legal noncompliance solely because an internal policy was not followed, unless the policy failure also establishes a breach of an applicable requirement or another defined audit criterion.

Similarly, a documented policy should not be treated as proof that the underlying legal obligation has been fulfilled.

### 10. Assessing AI Audit Risk

AI audit risk is the risk that the audit will reach an inappropriate conclusion because significant deficiencies, noncompliance, or other relevant conditions are not identified or adequately evaluated.

AI audits may face particular challenges because model behavior can be probabilistic, technical dependencies may be opaque, evaluation data may be incomplete, and the system may change during the audit period.

Audit planning should consider risks arising from:

- Incomplete or inaccurate AI inventories.
- Incorrect regulatory classification.
- Inadequate system documentation.
- Unreliable or insufficient evaluation evidence.
- Unauthorized model or configuration changes.
- Data quality, provenance, or licensing weaknesses.
- Security vulnerabilities and adversarial manipulation.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Supplier opacity and limited assurance rights.
- Insufficient logging or evidence retention.
- Bias, privacy, safety, and reliability concerns.
- Management bias or unsupported compliance claims.

The audit team should evaluate which risks are most likely to affect the engagement’s objectives and which could have the greatest consequences if overlooked.

The resulting assessment should inform the audit program, sampling strategy, technical testing, specialist involvement, and level of review required for significant findings.

### 11. Applying a Risk-Based AI Audit Approach

A risk-based audit approach allocates audit effort according to the significance of the risks associated with the system, process, or control under examination.

The approach recognizes that not every AI system warrants the same audit depth. A low-impact internal summarization tool may require a different level of scrutiny from an AI system used in consequential employment, financial, healthcare, or public-sector decisions.

Risk prioritization may consider:

- Potential impact on individuals and organizations.
- Severity of possible harm.
- System scale and exposure.
- Degree of automation and human intervention.
- Sensitivity of processed information.
- Regulatory significance.
- Technical complexity and model uncertainty.
- Dependence on external suppliers.
- History of incidents or control failures.
- Difficulty detecting or reversing erroneous outcomes.

The audit team should document how risk considerations influenced the selection of audit procedures and the allocation of effort.

Risk-based planning does not justify ignoring mandatory requirements or excluding material areas merely because they are difficult to test. Any exclusions should be justified against the engagement objectives, and significant residual uncertainty should be communicated.

### 12. Evaluating Inherent Risk and Residual Risk for Audit Planning

Inherent risk is the risk associated with an activity before considering the effect of relevant controls. Residual risk is the risk remaining after controls and other risk treatments are considered.

These concepts can help auditors identify where deeper examination is necessary, but the organization’s risk methodology should be applied consistently.

Inherent AI risk may arise from the system’s intended use, technical characteristics, affected population, autonomy, or potential consequences of failure. Residual risk depends on the effectiveness of safeguards, the operating environment, and the remaining uncertainties.

The audit team should examine:

- The basis of the original risk assessment.
- Whether relevant hazards and threat scenarios were identified.
- Whether the selected controls address those risks.
- Whether implementation evidence supports the assumed control effectiveness.
- Whether residual risk was evaluated using approved criteria.
- Whether acceptance was authorized at the correct level.
- Whether material changes require reassessment.

Auditors should not accept a low residual-risk rating merely because management recorded one. The rating should be evaluated against the underlying evidence and the organization’s established methodology.

Where the risk assessment lacks adequate evidence, the audit team should investigate the deficiency and determine whether it affects the system’s authorization or continued operation.

### 13. Defining the AI Audit Period and Temporal Boundaries

The audit period identifies the timeframe during which activities, controls, and records are being examined.

This is particularly important for AI systems because the model, application configuration, data sources, and provider services may change over time. Evidence from one version may not support conclusions about a different version or period.

Audit planning should establish:

- The start and end dates of the period under review.
- The system versions relevant to that period.
- The timing of significant changes.
- The availability and retention of operational logs.
- The treatment of incidents occurring outside the period.
- The handling of changes implemented during fieldwork.
- The basis for any retrospective testing.

Where the audit examines a control over time, the team should collect evidence that covers the relevant period rather than relying solely on a current snapshot.

For example, inspecting current access permissions does not necessarily demonstrate that access was appropriately restricted throughout the previous twelve months.

If material changes occur during the audit, the team should determine whether separate testing is needed for different versions or periods and clearly state the temporal limits of the resulting conclusions.

### 14. Identifying Relevant Stakeholders and Control Owners

AI audits frequently require cooperation among functions with different responsibilities, technical knowledge, and information access.

Stakeholder identification helps the audit team determine who can explain the system, provide evidence, demonstrate controls, and authorize access.

Relevant stakeholders may include:

- AI system and business owners.
- AI governance leaders.
- Model developers and engineers.
- Data owners and data stewards.
- Information security personnel.
- Privacy and data protection officers.
- Legal and compliance teams.
- Procurement and supplier managers.
- Operations and monitoring teams.
- Human reviewers and frontline users.
- Enterprise risk management.
- Internal audit and external assurance providers.

The audit plan should identify the responsible control owner for each major audit area. This supports efficient evidence requests and ensures that findings are directed to personnel capable of implementing remediation.

The audit team should remain alert to conflicts of interest and avoid relying exclusively on the individuals responsible for designing or operating the controls being tested.

Where a supplier controls relevant evidence, the audit team should establish the contractual or authorized means of access before relying on that evidence as a required audit input.

### 15. Assessing Auditor Competence and Specialist Requirements

AI audits may require a combination of auditing expertise, governance knowledge, legal interpretation, cybersecurity skills, data analysis, and technical understanding of AI systems.

The engagement leader should assess whether the audit team collectively has the competence necessary to evaluate the relevant risks and criteria.

Potential specialist areas include:

- AI management systems and governance.
- Machine learning evaluation and model limitations.
- Data governance and provenance.
- Cloud architecture and platform security.
- Application security and adversarial testing.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Sector-specific regulation.
- Statistical analysis and fairness evaluation.
- Third-party risk management.
- Safety engineering and resilience.

Specialist involvement should be driven by the engagement’s objectives and risk profile rather than by a generic assumption that every AI audit requires the same technical team.

The audit plan should define the specialist’s responsibilities, access to evidence, expected deliverables, and review arrangements.

The audit leader remains responsible for ensuring that the overall engagement conclusions are appropriately supported. Technical specialists provide expertise, but their contributions must be integrated into the audit methodology and documented in a manner that supports review.

### 16. Establishing Auditor Independence and Objectivity

Auditor independence and objectivity are essential to credible assurance. The audit team must be able to evaluate evidence and report findings without inappropriate influence from the individuals or functions responsible for the activities being examined.

Potential threats include reviewing controls the auditor recently designed or operated, financial or personal interests in the outcome, management pressure to avoid adverse findings, and restrictions on access to relevant evidence.

Safeguards may include:

- Conflict-of-interest declarations.
- Separation between control ownership and independent assurance.
- Independent review of significant conclusions.
- Appropriate reporting lines.
- Escalation procedures for management interference.
- Transparent documentation of scope limitations.
- Restrictions on self-review where necessary.

For internal audit, independence should be considered in the context of the function’s organizational position and applicable professional standards. For external assurance, the relevant engagement and professional independence requirements should be followed.

Where a potential conflict cannot be adequately mitigated, the organization should consider reassignment, additional independent review, or another suitable assurance arrangement.

The audit report should not imply independent assurance where material restrictions have prevented the audit team from exercising appropriate professional judgment.

### 17. Determining the Appropriate Audit Methodology

The audit methodology defines the procedures used to obtain and evaluate evidence against the audit criteria.

AI audit methods may combine document review, interviews, process walkthroughs, configuration inspection, data analysis, control testing, sampling, technical evaluation, and observation of operational practices.

The methodology should be tailored to the system’s architecture, risk profile, objectives, and available evidence.

Potential procedures include:

- Reviewing policies, procedures, and governance records.
- Tracing requirements to controls and evidence.
- Inspecting system configurations and permissions.
- Reperforming selected calculations or control activities.
- Sampling transactions, outputs, or approval records.
- Evaluating model test results and evaluation methods.
- Examining monitoring and incident records.
- Conducting authorized security tests.
- Comparing documented processes with observed practice.
- Corroborating management assertions with independent evidence.

The audit program should specify the objective of each procedure, the evidence expected, the population or sample involved, and the criteria for evaluating results.

The methodology should also define how unexpected findings, missing evidence, contradictory information, and changes during fieldwork will be handled.

### 18. Establishing Evidence Sufficiency and Appropriateness

Audit evidence must be sufficient and appropriate to support the conclusions drawn. Sufficiency concerns the quantity of evidence, while appropriateness concerns its relevance and reliability.

AI audits can be particularly vulnerable to overreliance on policy documents, management statements, vendor marketing materials, or aggregate performance metrics that do not directly establish control effectiveness.

Evidence evaluation should consider:

- Relevance to the audit objective.
- Reliability of the source.
- Completeness and accuracy.
- Coverage of the applicable period.
- Relationship to the correct system version.
- Independence from the party making the assertion.
- Reproducibility where relevant.
- Limitations and potential bias.

For example, a policy describing mandatory model evaluation is evidence of the organization’s stated process, but not necessarily evidence that evaluation was performed for a particular release.

A completed evaluation report may establish that testing occurred, but further examination may be required to determine whether the test design was adequate, failures were resolved, and the release was properly authorized.

When evidence is insufficient, the auditor should obtain additional evidence, narrow the conclusion, identify a scope limitation, or report the unresolved uncertainty as appropriate.

### 19. Establishing the Preliminary AI Audit Work Program

The audit work program translates the engagement objectives, scope, risk assessment, and criteria into specific audit procedures.

It should provide enough structure to ensure consistent execution while allowing justified adjustments when new evidence changes the auditor’s understanding of the system.

An AI audit work program may contain:

| Work program element  | Purpose                                             |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Audit objective       | Defines the question to be answered                 |
| Risk statement        | Identifies the risk motivating the procedure        |
| Audit criterion       | Establishes the benchmark for evaluation            |
| Control or process    | Identifies the activity being examined              |
| Audit procedure       | Describes the test or examination                   |
| Evidence requirement  | Specifies expected supporting records               |
| Population and sample | Defines the items selected for testing              |
| Responsible auditor   | Assigns execution responsibility                    |
| Expected outcome      | Defines what the procedure is intended to establish |
| Exception handling    | Defines how deviations are evaluated                |
| Review and approval   | Establishes supervision and quality control         |

The work program should distinguish planned procedures from procedures actually completed. Deviations from the plan should be documented with their rationale and any resulting effect on the audit coverage.

The engagement leader should review the work program before significant fieldwork begins and approve material changes to the methodology or scope.

### 20. Defining AI Audit Planning Deliverables and Approval Gates

AI audit planning should produce a controlled set of deliverables that establish the engagement’s objectives, boundaries, criteria, risks, resources, and procedures.

Core planning deliverables typically include:

- Approved audit mandate or engagement authorization.
- Audit objectives and scope statement.
- Preliminary understanding of the AI system.
- Audit criteria and requirements register.
- Risk assessment and prioritization.
- Stakeholder and control-owner map.
- Competence and specialist requirements.
- Independence and conflict-of-interest assessment.
- Evidence request list.
- Audit work program.
- Schedule, milestones, and resource plan.
- Reporting and escalation arrangements.

Before fieldwork begins, the engagement leader should confirm that the scope is sufficiently defined, the criteria are appropriate, material risks have been considered, and the audit team can obtain the evidence necessary to support its intended conclusions.

Planning approval does not mean that every uncertainty has been resolved. It establishes a controlled starting point and a process for responding to new information.

The audit plan should be revisited when material facts emerge, such as the discovery of an unrecorded AI system, an unexpected supplier dependency, a significant incident, or evidence that the original risk assessment understated the system’s impact.

The objective of disciplined AI audit planning is to ensure that the engagement is focused on material risks, evaluates the right criteria, uses appropriate methods, and produces conclusions that are transparent, defensible, and proportionate to the evidence obtained.
