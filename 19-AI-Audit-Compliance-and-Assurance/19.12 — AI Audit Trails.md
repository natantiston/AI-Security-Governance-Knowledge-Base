# 19.12 — AI Audit Planning, Scope, Risk Assessment, Criteria and Methodology

## Part 1 — AI Audit Foundations, Audit Mandates, Scope Definition and Planning Methodology

### 1. Understanding the Purpose of AI Auditing

AI auditing is a structured examination of an organization’s AI-related governance arrangements, risk management practices, technical controls, operational processes, and compliance obligations. Its purpose is to determine whether the relevant activities are appropriately designed, implemented, documented, and operating effectively.

Unlike a conventional IT audit that may concentrate on infrastructure, applications, access controls, and change management, an AI audit may also need to examine data quality, model behavior, evaluation methods, human oversight, AI-specific threats, and the limitations of automated decision-making.

The audit objective depends on the engagement. An audit may evaluate compliance with applicable requirements, assess the effectiveness of an AI management system, examine a specific AI application, review third-party AI dependencies, or investigate a particular incident.

A properly designed AI audit should establish:

- Whether the audit criteria have been identified correctly.
- Whether relevant AI risks have been assessed.
- Whether appropriate governance and controls exist.
- Whether controls operate as intended.
- Whether conclusions are supported by sufficient and appropriate evidence.
- Whether identified deficiencies receive effective remediation.

AI auditing does not automatically establish that a system is safe, unbiased, secure, or legally compliant in every circumstance. Conclusions must remain within the defined scope, criteria, evidence, and limitations of the engagement.

### 2. Distinguishing AI Audits from AI Assessments and Certifications

AI governance uses several assurance activities that may appear similar but have different objectives, levels of independence, and evidentiary requirements.

An AI audit systematically evaluates an organization, process, system, or control against defined criteria. An assessment may be narrower, focusing on a particular risk, technical characteristic, or implementation question. Certification involves a formal determination against a specified certification scheme or standard by an appropriately authorized certification body.

Examples include:

| Activity                              | Primary objective                                       | Typical output                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance audit                   | Evaluate governance and control effectiveness           | Audit report and findings                       |
| AI risk assessment                    | Identify, analyze, and evaluate AI-related risks        | Risk register and treatment plan                |
| AI security assessment                | Evaluate security weaknesses and safeguards             | Security assessment report                      |
| AI model evaluation                   | Measure model performance against defined criteria      | Evaluation results                              |
| Regulatory compliance assessment      | Determine conformity with applicable requirements       | Compliance assessment and gap register          |
| Management-system certification audit | Assess conformity with a defined certification standard | Certification audit findings and recommendation |

These activities can complement one another, but their outputs are not interchangeable. A successful model evaluation does not establish the effectiveness of enterprise AI governance, and an audit report does not automatically constitute certification.

Audit planning should identify the assurance activity being performed, the authority under which it operates, and the conclusions it is permitted to support.

### 3. Establishing the AI Audit Mandate

The audit mandate establishes the authority, purpose, and organizational expectations for the audit. It defines why the engagement is being conducted, who has authorized it, and what responsibilities the audit function has been assigned.

An audit mandate may arise from an internal audit charter, an approved risk-based audit plan, a board or audit committee request, a regulatory requirement, a contractual obligation, or a formally authorized investigation.

The mandate should clarify:

- The authority under which the audit is performed.
- The intended objectives and expected outcomes.
- The organizational units and systems potentially covered.
- The access rights and information-gathering authority.
- The reporting line and escalation arrangements.
- The expected independence of the audit team.
- The relationship with other assurance functions.
- Any restrictions on access, disclosure, or reporting.

For internal audit, the mandate should be consistent with the organization’s internal audit charter and applicable professional requirements. For external assurance, the engagement authority and responsibilities should be established through the relevant contract, appointment, or legal instrument.

An audit team should not assume that a general management request provides unrestricted authority to access every dataset, supplier environment, or confidential record. Access must be supported by appropriate authorization and applicable legal and contractual rights.

### 4. Defining the AI Audit Objectives

Audit objectives describe the specific questions the engagement is intended to answer. Clear objectives guide the selection of audit criteria, evidence, sampling methods, interviews, technical tests, and reporting conclusions.

An objective such as “review AI governance” may be too broad to guide a rigorous engagement. More precise objectives could examine whether AI systems are inventoried, whether risk assessments are completed before deployment, whether model changes receive appropriate approval, or whether supplier controls provide sufficient assurance.

A well-defined objective should identify:

- The governance or operational question.
- The relevant system, process, or organizational boundary.
- The criteria against which performance will be evaluated.
- The intended assurance conclusion.
- The principal risks that motivate the work.
- Any important limitations.

Examples of AI audit objectives include determining whether an organization has implemented an effective AI governance operating model, assessing whether a high-impact AI application follows approved human oversight procedures, or evaluating whether model evaluation evidence supports a production deployment decision.

Objectives should be sufficiently specific to support evidence-based conclusions while allowing the audit team to investigate unexpected risks discovered during fieldwork.

### 5. Identifying the Auditable Entity

The auditable entity is the organization, business unit, process, AI system, service, or control environment subject to examination.

AI audit planning must identify the entity precisely because AI capabilities are often distributed across multiple components and providers. A single business application may depend on an externally hosted foundation model, internal orchestration software, retrieval databases, third-party APIs, and human review processes.

The audit team should determine whether the auditable entity is:

- An enterprise-wide AI governance program.
- An AI management system.
- A particular AI application or use case.
- A model development or deployment process.
- An AI data governance process.
- A third-party AI service or supplier relationship.
- An AI security or monitoring control.
- A specific decision-making workflow involving AI.

The entity definition should include relevant organizational ownership, technical boundaries, interfaces, and dependencies.

Where multiple systems share a model or platform, the audit team should distinguish common controls from application-specific controls. This prevents an assessment of one component from being incorrectly generalized to every application that uses it.

### 6. Defining the AI Audit Scope

The audit scope specifies what is included in and excluded from the engagement. It establishes the boundaries within which the audit team will gather evidence and formulate conclusions.

AI audit scope may be defined by systems, organizational units, business processes, jurisdictions, model versions, datasets, time periods, control categories, or regulatory obligations.

For example, an audit of an AI-powered customer service platform might include model configuration, retrieval sources, access controls, data protection, human escalation, logging, incident management, and supplier governance. It might exclude unrelated enterprise applications that do not affect the platform’s risk profile.

Scope definition should address:

- Systems and components covered.
- Business processes and intended uses.
- Organizational units and responsible functions.
- Relevant model and configuration versions.
- Data sources and external dependencies.
- Applicable locations and jurisdictions.
- The period under review.
- Included control domains.
- Explicit exclusions and their rationale.

The scope should be documented and communicated to relevant stakeholders before detailed fieldwork begins.

Scope limitations should not be hidden. If the audit cannot examine an important supplier component or obtain necessary technical evidence, the report should explain the resulting limitation and its effect on the assurance conclusion.

### 7. Understanding the AI System Context Before Audit Fieldwork

Effective AI audit planning begins with understanding how the system is intended to operate and how it contributes to the organization’s business activities.

Without sufficient context, auditors may select irrelevant controls, overlook critical dependencies, or evaluate the system against assumptions that do not reflect its actual deployment.

The preliminary understanding should cover:

- Business objectives and intended purpose.
- User groups and affected stakeholders.
- System architecture and principal components.
- Model providers and deployment arrangements.
- Data sources and processing activities.
- Inputs, outputs, and downstream actions.
- Human oversight and intervention points.
- Security boundaries and trust relationships.
- Operational dependencies and failure scenarios.
- Known limitations and prior incidents.

The audit team should review existing documentation, interview relevant stakeholders, and inspect the system inventory before finalizing the audit approach.

For complex AI applications, the team should distinguish the underlying model from the complete deployed system. Application logic, prompts, retrieval mechanisms, tool permissions, and human workflows may materially influence behavior and risk.

### 8. Establishing the Applicable Audit Criteria

Audit criteria are the requirements, standards, policies, control objectives, contractual commitments, or other benchmarks used to evaluate the auditable entity.

The criteria must be relevant, sufficiently precise, and appropriate to the engagement. An audit cannot produce a defensible conformity conclusion if its evaluation benchmark is unclear or incorrectly applied.

Potential criteria include:

- Applicable legislation and regulatory requirements.
- ISO/IEC 42001 requirements for AI management systems.
- Relevant guidance from ISO/IEC 23894 on AI risk management.
- The NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework or other applicable security frameworks.
- Internal AI governance policies and procedures.
- Contractual security and assurance obligations.
- Approved risk acceptance criteria.
- Technical requirements and control specifications.

These sources do not all have the same legal status. Legislation may impose binding obligations, while standards and frameworks may be voluntary unless incorporated into law, regulation, contract, or organizational policy.

The audit criteria register should identify the source, relevant version, applicability rationale, and specific requirement or control objective being tested.

Where several criteria overlap, the audit team should map them carefully to avoid double-counting findings or treating different requirements as identical.

### 9. Distinguishing Legal Compliance from Control Effectiveness

Legal compliance and control effectiveness are related but separate audit questions.

A compliance audit determines whether applicable legal or regulatory requirements have been met. A control-effectiveness audit evaluates whether the organization’s safeguards are appropriately designed and operating as intended.

An organization may have a documented control that is consistently operated but still fail to satisfy a legal requirement. Conversely, a legal obligation may be met through a combination of technical and organizational measures without a single control corresponding directly to the wording of the law.

Audit planning should distinguish:

- **Legal applicability:** Whether a legal obligation applies to the entity and use case.
- **Control design:** Whether the selected control is capable of addressing the relevant objective.
- **Implementation:** Whether the control has been put into operation.
- **Operating effectiveness:** Whether the control functions as intended over the relevant period.
- **Compliance conclusion:** Whether the available evidence supports a conclusion about the applicable obligation.

The audit team should avoid declaring legal noncompliance solely because an internal policy was not followed, unless the policy failure also establishes a breach of an applicable requirement or another defined audit criterion.

Similarly, a documented policy should not be treated as proof that the underlying legal obligation has been fulfilled.

### 10. Assessing AI Audit Risk

AI audit risk is the risk that the audit will reach an inappropriate conclusion because significant deficiencies, noncompliance, or other relevant conditions are not identified or adequately evaluated.

AI audits may face particular challenges because model behavior can be probabilistic, technical dependencies may be opaque, evaluation data may be incomplete, and the system may change during the audit period.

Audit planning should consider risks arising from:

- Incomplete or inaccurate AI inventories.
- Incorrect regulatory classification.
- Inadequate system documentation.
- Unreliable or insufficient evaluation evidence.
- Unauthorized model or configuration changes.
- Data quality, provenance, or licensing weaknesses.
- Security vulnerabilities and adversarial manipulation.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Supplier opacity and limited assurance rights.
- Insufficient logging or evidence retention.
- Bias, privacy, safety, and reliability concerns.
- Management bias or unsupported compliance claims.

The audit team should evaluate which risks are most likely to affect the engagement’s objectives and which could have the greatest consequences if overlooked.

The resulting assessment should inform the audit program, sampling strategy, technical testing, specialist involvement, and level of review required for significant findings.

### 11. Applying a Risk-Based AI Audit Approach

A risk-based audit approach allocates audit effort according to the significance of the risks associated with the system, process, or control under examination.

The approach recognizes that not every AI system warrants the same audit depth. A low-impact internal summarization tool may require a different level of scrutiny from an AI system used in consequential employment, financial, healthcare, or public-sector decisions.

Risk prioritization may consider:

- Potential impact on individuals and organizations.
- Severity of possible harm.
- System scale and exposure.
- Degree of automation and human intervention.
- Sensitivity of processed information.
- Regulatory significance.
- Technical complexity and model uncertainty.
- Dependence on external suppliers.
- History of incidents or control failures.
- Difficulty detecting or reversing erroneous outcomes.

The audit team should document how risk considerations influenced the selection of audit procedures and the allocation of effort.

Risk-based planning does not justify ignoring mandatory requirements or excluding material areas merely because they are difficult to test. Any exclusions should be justified against the engagement objectives, and significant residual uncertainty should be communicated.

### 12. Evaluating Inherent Risk and Residual Risk for Audit Planning

Inherent risk is the risk associated with an activity before considering the effect of relevant controls. Residual risk is the risk remaining after controls and other risk treatments are considered.

These concepts can help auditors identify where deeper examination is necessary, but the organization’s risk methodology should be applied consistently.

Inherent AI risk may arise from the system’s intended use, technical characteristics, affected population, autonomy, or potential consequences of failure. Residual risk depends on the effectiveness of safeguards, the operating environment, and the remaining uncertainties.

The audit team should examine:

- The basis of the original risk assessment.
- Whether relevant hazards and threat scenarios were identified.
- Whether the selected controls address those risks.
- Whether implementation evidence supports the assumed control effectiveness.
- Whether residual risk was evaluated using approved criteria.
- Whether acceptance was authorized at the correct level.
- Whether material changes require reassessment.

Auditors should not accept a low residual-risk rating merely because management recorded one. The rating should be evaluated against the underlying evidence and the organization’s established methodology.

Where the risk assessment lacks adequate evidence, the audit team should investigate the deficiency and determine whether it affects the system’s authorization or continued operation.

### 13. Defining the AI Audit Period and Temporal Boundaries

The audit period identifies the timeframe during which activities, controls, and records are being examined.

This is particularly important for AI systems because the model, application configuration, data sources, and provider services may change over time. Evidence from one version may not support conclusions about a different version or period.

Audit planning should establish:

- The start and end dates of the period under review.
- The system versions relevant to that period.
- The timing of significant changes.
- The availability and retention of operational logs.
- The treatment of incidents occurring outside the period.
- The handling of changes implemented during fieldwork.
- The basis for any retrospective testing.

Where the audit examines a control over time, the team should collect evidence that covers the relevant period rather than relying solely on a current snapshot.

For example, inspecting current access permissions does not necessarily demonstrate that access was appropriately restricted throughout the previous twelve months.

If material changes occur during the audit, the team should determine whether separate testing is needed for different versions or periods and clearly state the temporal limits of the resulting conclusions.

### 14. Identifying Relevant Stakeholders and Control Owners

AI audits frequently require cooperation among functions with different responsibilities, technical knowledge, and information access.

Stakeholder identification helps the audit team determine who can explain the system, provide evidence, demonstrate controls, and authorize access.

Relevant stakeholders may include:

- AI system and business owners.
- AI governance leaders.
- Model developers and engineers.
- Data owners and data stewards.
- Information security personnel.
- Privacy and data protection officers.
- Legal and compliance teams.
- Procurement and supplier managers.
- Operations and monitoring teams.
- Human reviewers and frontline users.
- Enterprise risk management.
- Internal audit and external assurance providers.

The audit plan should identify the responsible control owner for each major audit area. This supports efficient evidence requests and ensures that findings are directed to personnel capable of implementing remediation.

The audit team should remain alert to conflicts of interest and avoid relying exclusively on the individuals responsible for designing or operating the controls being tested.

Where a supplier controls relevant evidence, the audit team should establish the contractual or authorized means of access before relying on that evidence as a required audit input.

### 15. Assessing Auditor Competence and Specialist Requirements

AI audits may require a combination of auditing expertise, governance knowledge, legal interpretation, cybersecurity skills, data analysis, and technical understanding of AI systems.

The engagement leader should assess whether the audit team collectively has the competence necessary to evaluate the relevant risks and criteria.

Potential specialist areas include:

- AI management systems and governance.
- Machine learning evaluation and model limitations.
- Data governance and provenance.
- Cloud architecture and platform security.
- Application security and adversarial testing.
- Privacy and data protection.
- Sector-specific regulation.
- Statistical analysis and fairness evaluation.
- Third-party risk management.
- Safety engineering and resilience.

Specialist involvement should be driven by the engagement’s objectives and risk profile rather than by a generic assumption that every AI audit requires the same technical team.

The audit plan should define the specialist’s responsibilities, access to evidence, expected deliverables, and review arrangements.

The audit leader remains responsible for ensuring that the overall engagement conclusions are appropriately supported. Technical specialists provide expertise, but their contributions must be integrated into the audit methodology and documented in a manner that supports review.

### 16. Establishing Auditor Independence and Objectivity

Auditor independence and objectivity are essential to credible assurance. The audit team must be able to evaluate evidence and report findings without inappropriate influence from the individuals or functions responsible for the activities being examined.

Potential threats include reviewing controls the auditor recently designed or operated, financial or personal interests in the outcome, management pressure to avoid adverse findings, and restrictions on access to relevant evidence.

Safeguards may include:

- Conflict-of-interest declarations.
- Separation between control ownership and independent assurance.
- Independent review of significant conclusions.
- Appropriate reporting lines.
- Escalation procedures for management interference.
- Transparent documentation of scope limitations.
- Restrictions on self-review where necessary.

For internal audit, independence should be considered in the context of the function’s organizational position and applicable professional standards. For external assurance, the relevant engagement and professional independence requirements should be followed.

Where a potential conflict cannot be adequately mitigated, the organization should consider reassignment, additional independent review, or another suitable assurance arrangement.

The audit report should not imply independent assurance where material restrictions have prevented the audit team from exercising appropriate professional judgment.

### 17. Determining the Appropriate Audit Methodology

The audit methodology defines the procedures used to obtain and evaluate evidence against the audit criteria.

AI audit methods may combine document review, interviews, process walkthroughs, configuration inspection, data analysis, control testing, sampling, technical evaluation, and observation of operational practices.

The methodology should be tailored to the system’s architecture, risk profile, objectives, and available evidence.

Potential procedures include:

- Reviewing policies, procedures, and governance records.
- Tracing requirements to controls and evidence.
- Inspecting system configurations and permissions.
- Reperforming selected calculations or control activities.
- Sampling transactions, outputs, or approval records.
- Evaluating model test results and evaluation methods.
- Examining monitoring and incident records.
- Conducting authorized security tests.
- Comparing documented processes with observed practice.
- Corroborating management assertions with independent evidence.

The audit program should specify the objective of each procedure, the evidence expected, the population or sample involved, and the criteria for evaluating results.

The methodology should also define how unexpected findings, missing evidence, contradictory information, and changes during fieldwork will be handled.

### 18. Establishing Evidence Sufficiency and Appropriateness

Audit evidence must be sufficient and appropriate to support the conclusions drawn. Sufficiency concerns the quantity of evidence, while appropriateness concerns its relevance and reliability.

AI audits can be particularly vulnerable to overreliance on policy documents, management statements, vendor marketing materials, or aggregate performance metrics that do not directly establish control effectiveness.

Evidence evaluation should consider:

- Relevance to the audit objective.
- Reliability of the source.
- Completeness and accuracy.
- Coverage of the applicable period.
- Relationship to the correct system version.
- Independence from the party making the assertion.
- Reproducibility where relevant.
- Limitations and potential bias.

For example, a policy describing mandatory model evaluation is evidence of the organization’s stated process, but not necessarily evidence that evaluation was performed for a particular release.

A completed evaluation report may establish that testing occurred, but further examination may be required to determine whether the test design was adequate, failures were resolved, and the release was properly authorized.

When evidence is insufficient, the auditor should obtain additional evidence, narrow the conclusion, identify a scope limitation, or report the unresolved uncertainty as appropriate.

### 19. Establishing the Preliminary AI Audit Work Program

The audit work program translates the engagement objectives, scope, risk assessment, and criteria into specific audit procedures.

It should provide enough structure to ensure consistent execution while allowing justified adjustments when new evidence changes the auditor’s understanding of the system.

An AI audit work program may contain:

| Work program element  | Purpose                                             |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Audit objective       | Defines the question to be answered                 |
| Risk statement        | Identifies the risk motivating the procedure        |
| Audit criterion       | Establishes the benchmark for evaluation            |
| Control or process    | Identifies the activity being examined              |
| Audit procedure       | Describes the test or examination                   |
| Evidence requirement  | Specifies expected supporting records               |
| Population and sample | Defines the items selected for testing              |
| Responsible auditor   | Assigns execution responsibility                    |
| Expected outcome      | Defines what the procedure is intended to establish |
| Exception handling    | Defines how deviations are evaluated                |
| Review and approval   | Establishes supervision and quality control         |

The work program should distinguish planned procedures from procedures actually completed. Deviations from the plan should be documented with their rationale and any resulting effect on the audit coverage.

The engagement leader should review the work program before significant fieldwork begins and approve material changes to the methodology or scope.

### 20. Defining AI Audit Planning Deliverables and Approval Gates

AI audit planning should produce a controlled set of deliverables that establish the engagement’s objectives, boundaries, criteria, risks, resources, and procedures.

Core planning deliverables typically include:

- Approved audit mandate or engagement authorization.
- Audit objectives and scope statement.
- Preliminary understanding of the AI system.
- Audit criteria and requirements register.
- Risk assessment and prioritization.
- Stakeholder and control-owner map.
- Competence and specialist requirements.
- Independence and conflict-of-interest assessment.
- Evidence request list.
- Audit work program.
- Schedule, milestones, and resource plan.
- Reporting and escalation arrangements.

Before fieldwork begins, the engagement leader should confirm that the scope is sufficiently defined, the criteria are appropriate, material risks have been considered, and the audit team can obtain the evidence necessary to support its intended conclusions.

Planning approval does not mean that every uncertainty has been resolved. It establishes a controlled starting point and a process for responding to new information.

The audit plan should be revisited when material facts emerge, such as the discovery of an unrecorded AI system, an unexpected supplier dependency, a significant incident, or evidence that the original risk assessment understated the system’s impact.

The objective of disciplined AI audit planning is to ensure that the engagement is focused on material risks, evaluates the right criteria, uses appropriate methods, and produces conclusions that are transparent, defensible, and proportionate to the evidence obtained.

# Part 2 — AI Audit Risk Assessment, Control Mapping, Evidence Collection and Testing Design

## 21. Establishing a Structured AI Audit Risk Assessment Methodology

An AI audit risk assessment is the systematic process of identifying, analyzing, and prioritizing risks that may affect an AI system's compliance, security, reliability, transparency, accountability, and intended performance. It helps auditors determine which areas require the greatest scrutiny, which controls should be tested, and how audit resources should be allocated.

Traditional IT audit methodologies provide a useful foundation, but AI audits require additional consideration of model behavior, training data, data drift, probabilistic outputs, third-party model dependencies, and risks that emerge after deployment.

The auditor should establish a documented methodology before performing detailed testing. This methodology should define how risks are identified, how inherent and residual risk are evaluated, how risk ratings are assigned, and how risk levels influence audit scope and test depth.

A structured methodology should address:

- **Inherent risk:** The level of risk before considering the effectiveness of controls.
- **Control risk:** The possibility that controls will fail to prevent, detect, or correct a risk.
- **Residual risk:** The risk remaining after controls are considered.
- **Audit risk:** The possibility that the auditor reaches an inappropriate conclusion based on insufficient or misleading evidence.
- **Emerging risk:** A new or changing risk caused by model updates, changing data, new use cases, evolving threats, or regulatory developments.

The auditor must distinguish organizational risk assessments from audit risk assessments. Management owns the AI system's risks and treatment decisions; the auditor independently evaluates whether the risk identification, assessment, and treatment arrangements are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

## 22. Identifying Inherent AI Risks Before Evaluating Controls

Inherent AI risks arise from the system's intended purpose, technical characteristics, operating environment, data dependencies, and potential consequences of failure. These risks exist even when the organization has not yet evaluated its safeguards.

The auditor should begin by understanding what the AI system does, who uses it, which decisions it influences, and what could happen if its outputs are inaccurate, biased, manipulated, unavailable, or misunderstood.

Important inherent risk categories include:

- **Decision risk:** Incorrect or inappropriate AI outputs influence operational or business decisions.
- **Data risk:** Training, validation, or production data is inaccurate, incomplete, unrepresentative, unlawfully obtained, or improperly protected.
- **Model risk:** The model produces unreliable results, fails under changed conditions, or performs inconsistently across relevant populations.
- **Security risk:** Attackers manipulate inputs, extract sensitive information, compromise model assets, or exploit connected components.
- **Privacy risk:** Personal data is processed inappropriately, retained unnecessarily, exposed, or used beyond authorized purposes.
- **Fairness risk:** The system creates or amplifies unjustified disparities or discriminatory outcomes.
- **Transparency risk:** Relevant stakeholders cannot understand the system's intended use, limitations, or basis for decisions at the level required for their role.
- **Operational risk:** The system becomes unavailable, degrades, or disrupts dependent business processes.
- **Third-party risk:** External model providers, cloud platforms, datasets, APIs, or other suppliers introduce dependencies that the organization cannot fully control.
- **Regulatory and contractual risk:** The system's use or governance fails to meet applicable legal, regulatory, or contractual obligations.

The auditor should record each material inherent risk with its cause, potential event, business or stakeholder impact, affected system component, and relevant audit objective. Risks should be specific enough to support meaningful control selection and testing.

For example, “AI security risk” is too broad to serve as a useful audit risk statement. A more precise statement would be: “An external user may exploit insufficient input validation to manipulate a production AI application into disclosing restricted information.”

## 23. Assessing AI Risk Across the System Lifecycle

AI risks change as a system moves from conception and development to deployment, operation, modification, and retirement. An audit that examines only the production model may overlook weaknesses introduced during data preparation, development, procurement, testing, or change management.

The auditor should identify the lifecycle stages relevant to the system and assess the risks associated with each stage.

| Lifecycle stage                  | Illustrative audit risk                                                                          |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Use-case definition              | The system is deployed for an unsuitable or insufficiently authorized purpose.                   |
| Data acquisition                 | Data is obtained without appropriate rights, provenance, or quality controls.                    |
| Data preparation                 | Data transformations introduce errors, leakage, or unrepresentative samples.                     |
| Model development or procurement | Model selection, development, or supplier evaluation is insufficiently controlled.               |
| Validation and testing           | Testing fails to identify material performance, fairness, safety, or security weaknesses.        |
| Deployment                       | Production configuration differs materially from the approved configuration.                     |
| Operation                        | Drift, incidents, unauthorized access, or deteriorating performance go undetected.               |
| Change and retraining            | Updates introduce unassessed behavior or invalidate previous validation results.                 |
| Retirement                       | Data, credentials, model artifacts, and connected services are not appropriately decommissioned. |

The audit plan should reflect the system's actual lifecycle and delivery model. A third-party foundation model integrated through an API may require a different testing approach from a model developed and trained internally.

The auditor should also consider lifecycle handoffs. For example, a development team may document a model limitation, but the deployment team may fail to translate that limitation into production restrictions or monitoring requirements.

## 24. Evaluating Risk Impact, Likelihood and Uncertainty

Risk prioritization requires consistent evaluation criteria. The auditor should determine how impact, likelihood, and uncertainty will influence the assessment and document the rationale for significant ratings.

**Impact** concerns the severity of potential consequences. Depending on the AI use case, impact may involve individuals, customers, employees, public safety, financial performance, legal rights, regulatory compliance, information security, or organizational reputation.

**Likelihood** concerns the probability or plausibility of the risk event occurring within the defined assessment period. Evidence may include historical incidents, known attack techniques, operational experience, testing results, system exposure, and the effectiveness of existing safeguards.

**Uncertainty** concerns the limits of available knowledge about the system or the probability and consequences of a risk. AI systems may involve uncertain behavior, limited explainability, changing inputs, incomplete supplier information, or insufficient operational history.

A risk assessment should not automatically assign a low rating simply because there is insufficient evidence to estimate likelihood. Where uncertainty is material, the auditor should record the evidence limitation and consider whether additional testing, specialist review, or a more conservative assessment is warranted.

A simple qualitative rating model may be used:

| Rating   | Impact                                                                        | Likelihood                                                     |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low      | Limited operational or stakeholder consequences                               | Unlikely under the assessed conditions                         |
| Moderate | Meaningful disruption, loss, or stakeholder impact                            | Plausible under the assessed conditions                        |
| High     | Serious harm, significant financial loss, or material compliance consequences | Likely or supported by credible exposure evidence              |
| Critical | Potentially severe or widespread consequences requiring urgent attention      | Credible and sufficiently serious to demand immediate scrutiny |

These definitions are illustrative. The auditor should use the organization's approved risk taxonomy when appropriate, assess whether that taxonomy is suitable for AI risks, and document any necessary adjustments.

## 25. Applying a Risk Scoring and Prioritization Model

A scoring model can help auditors compare risks consistently, prioritize testing, and explain the rationale for audit decisions. The model should support professional judgment rather than replace it.

One illustrative approach assigns impact and likelihood scores from 1 to 5 and multiplies them:

[\
\text{Inherent Risk Score}=\text{Impact}\times\text{Likelihood}\
]

For example, an impact score of 5 and a likelihood score of 4 produce a score of 20 out of 25. Under a hypothetical scoring policy, that result might be classified as high or critical risk.

The organization or audit function should define the score thresholds before applying the model. The auditor should avoid changing thresholds simply to obtain a preferred result.

Scoring models have important limitations:

- Multiplication can imply a level of mathematical precision that qualitative judgments do not support.
- Two risks with the same score may have very different consequences.
- Rare events with catastrophic consequences may require special treatment.
- Legal obligations and mandatory controls cannot be disregarded because a numerical score is low.
- A high degree of uncertainty may require further investigation even when the calculated score is moderate.
- Risk scores do not independently establish whether a control is effective.

A risk-prioritization decision should therefore consider the numerical or qualitative score, the nature of the potential harm, applicable obligations, the quality of available evidence, and the consequences of audit failure.

The auditor should retain the reasoning behind material judgments, including any management disagreement with the assigned risk level.

## 26. Mapping AI Risks to Control Objectives

After identifying and prioritizing risks, the auditor should map them to control objectives. A control objective describes the outcome that must be achieved to manage a risk adequately. It is not necessarily a specific procedure or technology.

For example, the risk of unauthorized model changes may be addressed by the control objective: “Changes to production AI models and configurations are authorized, traceable, tested, and approved before release.”

The auditor should establish a traceable relationship between risks, control objectives, controls, evidence, and audit procedures.

A basic mapping structure is:

| AI risk                    | Control objective                                | Illustrative control                          | Audit evidence                                  |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized model changes | Production changes are controlled                | Approval and release workflow                 | Change tickets, approvals, deployment logs      |
| Poor data quality          | Critical data meets defined quality criteria     | Data validation and exception handling        | Data quality reports, validation results        |
| Undetected model drift     | Performance deterioration is identified          | Drift monitoring and escalation               | Monitoring dashboards, alerts, incident records |
| Unauthorized access        | AI assets and interfaces are restricted          | Role-based access and periodic reviews        | Access lists, identity logs, review records     |
| Inadequate human oversight | High-impact decisions receive appropriate review | Defined review and override process           | Review records, override logs, procedures       |
| Supplier dependency        | Third-party risks are assessed and managed       | Supplier due diligence and ongoing monitoring | Assessments, contracts, assurance reports       |

The auditor should evaluate whether the selected controls address the actual risk mechanism. A policy stating that model changes require approval is not sufficient evidence that approval controls operate effectively.

Control mapping should also identify risks that lack an appropriate control, controls that address multiple risks, and controls that exist but are not supported by reliable evidence.

## 27. Evaluating Control Design, Implementation and Operating Effectiveness

AI audit testing should distinguish three related but different questions.

**Control design effectiveness** asks whether the control, if performed as intended, can reasonably prevent, detect, or correct the relevant risk.

**Control implementation** asks whether the control has actually been established and put into use. A documented process may exist without being implemented in the operational environment.

**Operating effectiveness** asks whether the control performed consistently and appropriately over the period being audited.

For example, an AI model monitoring control may be well designed because it defines performance thresholds, alert recipients, escalation requirements, and response deadlines. Implementation requires evidence that the monitoring process is configured and active. Operating effectiveness requires evidence that monitoring occurred, exceptions were identified, and required actions were completed.

The auditor should avoid treating these conclusions as interchangeable. A control may be appropriately designed but not implemented, or implemented but inconsistently performed.

Where a control is not operating effectively, the auditor should assess whether compensating controls exist and whether they adequately reduce the relevant risk. The presence of another control should not automatically be treated as compensation without evidence that it addresses the same risk.

## 28. Developing an AI Audit Control Matrix

A control matrix provides a structured record of risks, control objectives, controls, ownership, testing procedures, and results. It supports audit traceability and helps prevent important risks from being omitted during fieldwork.

A practical AI audit control matrix may contain the following fields:

- Risk ID and risk description
- Risk category and inherent risk rating
- Applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal criteria
- Control objective
- Control ID and control description
- Control owner and performing role
- Control type: preventive, detective, or corrective
- Control nature: manual, automated, or hybrid
- Frequency and population
- Design and implementation assessment
- Audit procedure and sample selection
- Evidence reference
- Test result and exception description
- Residual risk assessment
- Finding reference and remediation status

An illustrative traceability chain is:

```
Applicable requirement or audit criterion
                 |
                 v
             AI risk
                 |
                 v
          Control objective
                 |
                 v
           Existing control
                 |
                 v
        Audit procedure and test
                 |
                 v
        Evidence and test result
                 |
                 v
       Finding and risk conclusion
```

The matrix should remain a working audit document. When the auditor identifies a new risk, a control deficiency, or an unexpected system dependency, the matrix should be updated and related test procedures reconsidered.

The auditor should also verify that the matrix reflects the actual deployed system and not merely the design described in policies or architecture documents.

## 29. Selecting the Appropriate Type of Audit Procedure

The auditor should select procedures that produce evidence relevant to the audit objective and proportionate to the risk. AI audits commonly combine interviews, document inspection, observation, reperformance, data analysis, configuration review, and technical testing.

| Audit procedure      | Primary purpose                                       | Example                                                          |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Inquiry              | Understand processes, responsibilities, and decisions | Interview the model owner about drift escalation                 |
| Inspection           | Examine documentary or electronic evidence            | Review model cards, risk assessments, or approvals               |
| Observation          | Confirm that a process is performed as described      | Observe a model release approval workflow                        |
| Reperformance        | Independently repeat a control or calculation         | Recalculate a sample of model performance metrics                |
| Data analysis        | Identify patterns, exceptions, or anomalies           | Analyze access logs for unusual model API activity               |
| Configuration review | Examine system settings and technical safeguards      | Inspect access controls and logging configuration                |
| Technical testing    | Evaluate behavior or resistance to defined threats    | Test for prompt injection or unauthorized data exposure          |
| Walkthrough          | Trace an activity from initiation to completion       | Follow a model change from request through production deployment |

Inquiry alone is generally insufficient to establish that a control operated effectively. For important controls, the auditor should seek corroborating evidence, such as system-generated logs, approval records, configuration exports, or independently reproduced results.

Technical testing must be authorized, scoped, and conducted in a controlled manner. The auditor should define permitted targets, test accounts, data handling rules, testing windows, and escalation procedures before testing systems that could affect production services or sensitive information.

## 30. Designing Tests for AI-Specific Risks

AI systems require audit procedures that address both conventional technology controls and risks arising from data-driven or probabilistic behavior.

The precise tests depend on the system architecture, intended purpose, risk profile, and audit criteria. Not every AI system requires every test category.

Relevant AI-specific test areas include:

- **Data lineage and provenance:** Trace selected data elements to their sources and examine evidence of authorization, transformation, and validation.
- **Model version integrity:** Compare approved model versions with deployed versions and inspect release records.
- **Performance validation:** Recalculate or independently verify selected performance measures against defined acceptance criteria.
- **Fairness and subgroup performance:** Examine whether relevant evaluation populations and subgroup metrics are appropriate for the intended use and applicable obligations.
- **Robustness:** Assess how outputs change under relevant input variations or foreseeable operating conditions.
- **Security:** Test relevant threats, such as prompt injection, insecure tool access, unauthorized model access, or data leakage, where applicable.
- **Explainability and transparency:** Verify whether required users receive accurate information about the system's capabilities, limitations, and decision role.
- **Human oversight:** Inspect evidence that required reviews, escalations, and overrides are performed.
- **Monitoring and drift:** Examine whether defined indicators, thresholds, alerts, and corrective actions operate as intended.
- **Third-party dependencies:** Review supplier information, contractual controls, service limitations, and available assurance evidence.

The auditor should distinguish a model's technical performance from the effectiveness of the organization's governance controls. A model may achieve strong accuracy metrics while the organization lacks adequate approval, monitoring, documentation, or incident response processes.

Likewise, a successful security test does not prove that an AI system is secure against all attacks. Test conclusions should be limited to the conditions, techniques, configurations, and evidence actually examined.

## 31. Establishing Evidence Requirements Before Fieldwork

Evidence requirements should be defined during planning rather than improvised after testing begins. This helps ensure that auditors obtain the information necessary to evaluate controls and reach defensible conclusions.

For each significant risk and control, the audit work program should specify:

1. The audit objective and assertion being evaluated.
2. The control or system behavior under examination.
3. The evidence required to support the conclusion.
4. The source and expected custodian of the evidence.
5. The relevant period and system version.
6. The population and sample-selection method, where applicable.
7. The procedure used to obtain and evaluate evidence.
8. The criteria for identifying an exception.
9. The documentation required to reproduce or review the test.

Evidence may include approved policies, risk assessments, technical documentation, source and configuration records, model evaluation results, monitoring data, incident records, supplier documentation, access logs, and interviews corroborated by other sources.

The auditor should consider whether evidence is authentic, complete, accurate, relevant, sufficiently current, and appropriate for the conclusion being drawn.

For example, a dashboard screenshot may demonstrate that a monitoring interface exists, but it may not establish that monitoring operated continuously throughout the audit period. Historical alert records, configuration evidence, and incident tickets may be needed to substantiate operating effectiveness.

Where evidence is unavailable, incomplete, or unreliable, the auditor should document the limitation and assess its impact on the audit conclusion.

## 32. Evaluating AI Data Quality and Representativeness

Data quality is a central audit consideration because weaknesses in data may affect model performance, fairness, reliability, and compliance. The auditor should determine which data characteristics are material to the system's intended purpose and whether the organization has defined appropriate acceptance criteria.

Relevant data quality dimensions may include:

- Accuracy and correctness
- Completeness
- Consistency
- Timeliness
- Validity and conformance to defined rules
- Representativeness of relevant operating populations
- Provenance and traceability
- Authorization and permitted use
- Integrity throughout storage and processing

The audit should consider training, validation, testing, and production data separately because each serves a different purpose. Evidence that production data passes schema validation does not establish that training data was representative or that evaluation data was independent of training data.

Where relevant, the auditor may examine dataset documentation, sampling procedures, labeling standards, transformation pipelines, data-quality exceptions, and controls against leakage between training and evaluation datasets.

Representativeness must be assessed in relation to the intended use. A dataset may be statistically large yet still fail to represent important operating conditions or stakeholder groups.

The auditor should not independently conclude that a dataset is fair or legally compliant solely because selected statistical tests pass. Conclusions must reflect the evaluation method, relevant context, applicable criteria, and limitations of the evidence.

## 33. Testing Model Performance, Validation and Acceptance Criteria

Model validation should be evaluated against documented requirements appropriate to the intended use. The auditor should establish whether acceptance criteria were defined before approval and whether validation evidence supports the decision to deploy the system.

Depending on the use case, relevant measures may include accuracy, precision, recall, false-positive rates, false-negative rates, calibration, latency, robustness, or task-specific quality measures.

The auditor should determine:

- Whether validation criteria are aligned with the system's intended purpose and risk level.
- Whether the evaluation data and methodology are appropriate.
- Whether material limitations and known failure modes are documented.
- Whether testing covers relevant operating conditions and user populations.
- Whether significant deviations from acceptance criteria were resolved or formally accepted by authorized decision-makers.
- Whether the approved model and configuration correspond to the version actually deployed.
- Whether post-deployment monitoring checks the assumptions and performance characteristics established during validation.

The auditor should avoid relying exclusively on a single aggregate metric. Strong average performance can conceal unacceptable results for particular groups, input types, or operating environments.

Where independent recalculation is feasible, the auditor should reproduce selected results or inspect the evaluation pipeline to establish whether reported figures are accurate and reproducible.

If the organization cannot provide sufficient validation evidence, the auditor should distinguish the lack of evidence from proof that the model performs poorly. The audit finding should describe the evidential deficiency and its implications without making unsupported technical claims.

## 34. Auditing AI Security Threats and Technical Safeguards

AI security testing should be based on the system's architecture, interfaces, privileges, data flows, and threat model. The auditor should identify which threats are relevant rather than applying a generic checklist without regard to the system.

Potential areas of examination include:

- Unauthorized access to models, datasets, prompts, tools, and administrative interfaces
- Prompt injection and instruction manipulation in generative AI systems
- Insecure output handling and downstream execution
- Exposure of confidential information through model responses or connected services
- Excessive permissions granted to AI agents or integrated tools
- Model artifact or dependency integrity
- Vulnerabilities in APIs, orchestration layers, plugins, and retrieval systems
- Insufficient logging, monitoring, or incident escalation
- Weak segregation between development, testing, and production environments
- Inadequate controls for secrets, credentials, and sensitive configuration data

The auditor should examine both technical controls and governance arrangements. For example, an AI agent may have strong authentication but still present excessive business risk if it can execute high-impact actions without authorization boundaries or meaningful human approval.

Security testing should be designed to avoid unauthorized disclosure, service disruption, destructive actions, or unintended changes to production systems. Where testing could create material operational risk, the auditor should use an approved test environment or an explicitly authorized testing method.

Findings should describe the tested scenario, observed behavior, expected control, evidence collected, and risk implications. They should not imply that untested attack paths have been ruled out.

## 35. Auditing Human Oversight and Decision Accountability

Human oversight is relevant where people are expected to supervise AI behavior, review outputs, intervene in decisions, or remain accountable for outcomes. The auditor should establish whether the oversight model is appropriate to the system's risk and intended use.

A policy requiring human review does not prove that meaningful oversight occurs. The auditor should evaluate whether reviewers have the necessary authority, competence, information, time, and access to challenge AI outputs.

Audit procedures may include:

- Inspecting documented oversight responsibilities and escalation criteria.
- Reviewing samples of human decisions, overrides, and escalations.
- Comparing actual review records with required review procedures.
- Examining whether reviewers can access relevant output explanations or supporting information.
- Evaluating whether users are trained to recognize limitations and failure modes.
- Reviewing evidence of excessive reliance on AI recommendations.
- Assessing whether accountability is clearly assigned when AI outputs influence consequential decisions.

The auditor should consider automation bias, where users may place undue confidence in AI recommendations, and the risk of superficial review, where a human approves outputs without meaningful evaluation.

Where human oversight is a critical safeguard, the audit should establish whether it operates in practice and whether exceptions are detected and addressed. If the system's design or operating model makes meaningful intervention impossible, the auditor should assess whether the stated oversight arrangement is credible.

## 36. Assessing Third-Party AI Controls and Supplier Evidence

AI systems frequently depend on external model providers, cloud services, data vendors, software libraries, APIs, and specialist service providers. These dependencies may introduce risks that the organization cannot directly verify or control.

The auditor should identify material suppliers and determine how responsibilities are divided between the organization and each provider. The assessment should consider contractual commitments, technical boundaries, available assurance reports, incident notification obligations, change notifications, data handling, service continuity, and exit arrangements.

Potential evidence includes:

- Supplier due diligence and risk assessments
- Contracts, service-level agreements, and data-processing terms
- Security and assurance reports
- Model documentation and stated limitations
- Records of supplier changes and version updates
- Incident notification and escalation records
- Service continuity and recovery arrangements
- Evidence of periodic supplier reviews

Supplier-provided documentation should be assessed for scope, period, applicability, and limitations. A general security certification or assurance report may not cover the specific AI model, API, data flow, or service configuration used by the audited organization.

Where direct testing is not possible, the auditor should document the alternative evidence used and the residual assurance limitation. A lack of access to a supplier's internal model does not automatically mean the system is unacceptable, but it may constrain the conclusions that can be supported.

## 37. Designing Sampling Strategies for AI Audit Testing

Sampling is used when examining an entire population is impractical. The auditor should define the population, sampling objective, selection method, sample size rationale, and treatment of exceptions before drawing conclusions.

Relevant populations may include model changes, access reviews, AI-generated decisions, incidents, supplier assessments, monitoring alerts, dataset approvals, or human oversight records.

The sampling method should reflect the audit objective:

- **Random sampling:** Gives eligible population items an appropriate chance of selection under the chosen method.
- **Risk-based sampling:** Prioritizes items with higher exposure or potential consequences.
- **Targeted sampling:** Selects specific items because of known concerns, unusual characteristics, or material changes.
- **Stratified sampling:** Divides a population into meaningful groups and selects items from each group.
- **Full-population analysis:** Uses automated analysis to examine all available records where data quality, tooling, and the audit objective permit.

Targeted samples can identify important exceptions but generally do not support statistical conclusions about the entire population. Random or statistical sampling may support broader inferences only when the method, sample design, and assumptions justify them.

For AI systems, sampling may need to consider model versions, user groups, input categories, geographic locations, deployment environments, or periods before and after significant changes.

The auditor should document why the sample is appropriate and how exceptions affect the conclusion. A small sample with no exceptions should not automatically be treated as proof that a high-risk control is consistently effective.

## 38. Evaluating Model Drift, Change Management and Ongoing Monitoring

AI systems may change in behavior because of changing input distributions, changing relationships between inputs and outcomes, model updates, altered prompts, updated retrieval sources, or changes to connected tools and services.

The auditor should establish whether the organization has defined relevant monitoring indicators, thresholds, escalation responsibilities, and response procedures. The specific indicators should reflect the model's purpose and risk profile.

Potential monitoring areas include:

- Input and output distribution changes
- Model performance deterioration
- Data quality exceptions
- Changes in relevant subgroup performance
- Security events and unusual usage patterns
- Increases in human overrides or complaints
- Unexpected tool execution or access behavior
- Supplier updates or changes to external dependencies

The audit should examine whether alerts lead to documented investigation and appropriate action. A monitoring dashboard with thresholds is not sufficient if alerts are ignored, thresholds are inappropriate, or no one is accountable for response.

Change management should also ensure that material changes receive appropriate assessment, testing, approval, documentation, and deployment controls. The auditor should verify whether changes invalidate prior validation results or require renewed risk assessment.

Where monitoring data is incomplete or not retained long enough to support the audit period, the limitation should be documented. The auditor should not infer continuous monitoring from isolated snapshots.

## 39. Documenting Exceptions, Control Deficiencies and Audit Findings

An audit exception is a condition in which observed evidence does not meet the applicable audit criterion or expected control requirement. Not every exception represents a material control deficiency, and not every deficiency necessarily produces an actual adverse event.

The auditor should evaluate each exception in context, considering its cause, scope, recurrence, potential impact, relationship to other deficiencies, and the effectiveness of compensating controls.

A well-structured AI audit finding commonly includes:

- **Criteria:** The requirement, policy, control objective, or approved standard that should have been met.
- **Condition:** The observed situation supported by evidence.
- **Cause:** The underlying reason for the deficiency, where it can be established.
- **Effect or risk:** The actual or potential consequence of the condition.
- **Recommendation:** The action proposed to address the deficiency.
- **Management response:** The responsible party's response, planned action, and target date, where applicable.

For example:

**Criteria:** Production model changes must be approved and traceable.

**Condition:** A sample of production deployments contained changes for which the required approval record could not be located.

**Cause:** The release workflow did not consistently enforce approval-record completion before deployment.

**Effect or risk:** Unauthorized or inadequately reviewed changes may enter production, potentially affecting system behavior, security, or compliance.

**Recommendation:** Enforce approval checks within the deployment workflow and retain verifiable records of authorization.

The auditor should avoid overstating findings. If the evidence establishes that approval records are missing, the auditor should not automatically conclude that every change was unauthorized. The finding should distinguish the documented condition from the possible underlying control failure.

## 40. Establishing the Audit Work Program and Fieldwork Readiness Gate

The audit work program converts the approved audit scope and risk assessment into executable procedures. It should identify what will be tested, why it will be tested, who will perform the work, what evidence is required, and how results will be evaluated.

A practical AI audit work program should include:

1. Audit objective and scope boundaries.
2. Applicable criteria and relevant requirements.
3. Prioritized AI risks and control objectives.
4. Planned interviews, walkthroughs, inspections, and technical tests.
5. Evidence requests and responsible evidence owners.
6. Populations, sampling methods, and sample-size rationale.
7. Testing procedures and expected results.
8. Exception criteria and escalation thresholds.
9. Required specialist involvement.
10. Workpaper and evidence-retention requirements.
11. Dependencies, access requirements, and testing constraints.
12. Planned review and approval points.

Before fieldwork begins, the audit lead should confirm that the scope is authorized, the relevant system and environment are identified, evidence access is arranged, testing permissions are documented, and material safety or operational constraints are understood.

A fieldwork readiness review may follow this sequence:

```
Approved audit mandate and scope
              |
              v
Risk assessment and control mapping
              |
              v
Audit criteria and test procedures
              |
              v
Evidence requests and access confirmed
              |
              v
Technical testing authorization confirmed
              |
              v
Auditor competence and independence checked
              |
              v
Fieldwork readiness approval
```

If a critical dependency remains unresolved, the audit lead should determine whether fieldwork can proceed safely and effectively, whether the audit plan must be revised, or whether the limitation must be escalated.

The approved work program should remain adaptable. New evidence, material incidents, unexpected system behavior, or previously unidentified risks may require additional procedures. Any significant change should be documented, justified, and approved in accordance with the audit function's methodology.

The objective is to establish a defensible connection between the audit mandate, risk-based scope, applicable criteria, control evaluation, evidence collected, and conclusions ultimately reported.

## 41. Executing AI Audit Fieldwork in Accordance with the Approved Plan

AI audit execution is the phase in which the auditor performs the approved procedures, obtains evidence, evaluates control effectiveness, and determines whether the audited AI system meets the established criteria. Fieldwork must remain aligned with the audit mandate, approved scope, risk assessment, and documented work program.

Although the audit plan establishes the initial direction, execution may reveal conditions that were not apparent during planning. Examples include undocumented model dependencies, inconsistent control ownership, missing monitoring records, unexpected system behavior, or discrepancies between approved documentation and the deployed environment.

The auditor should execute procedures consistently and maintain a clear record of work performed. Deviations from the approved plan should be justified and documented, particularly when they affect coverage of high-risk areas or the sufficiency of evidence.

Core fieldwork activities include:

- Conducting opening meetings and confirming responsibilities.
- Validating system boundaries, versions, and operating environments.
- Performing interviews and process walkthroughs.
- Inspecting governance documents and technical records.
- Testing selected controls and system behaviors.
- Recording exceptions and contradictory evidence.
- Evaluating the need for additional procedures.
- Communicating significant issues through established escalation channels.

The auditor should not treat completion of every planned procedure as proof that the audit objective has been achieved. Procedures must produce sufficient, appropriate evidence to support the conclusions.

Where a planned procedure cannot be completed, the auditor should document the reason, the resulting evidence limitation, and the impact on the audit conclusion. Alternative procedures may be appropriate if they provide reliable evidence addressing the same audit objective.

## 42. Conducting AI Audit Interviews and Process Walkthroughs

Interviews and walkthroughs help auditors understand how AI governance and operational controls function in practice. They can identify undocumented activities, unclear accountability, inconsistencies between policy and practice, and weaknesses in the flow of information between teams.

Relevant interview participants may include AI system owners, model developers, data engineers, security personnel, privacy specialists, risk managers, compliance officers, procurement teams, business users, and third-party service representatives.

Interview questions should be linked to specific audit objectives. Rather than asking only whether a control exists, the auditor should ask how the control operates, who performs it, what evidence is retained, how exceptions are handled, and what happens when the expected process fails.

A walkthrough should trace a representative activity from initiation to completion. For example, a model-change walkthrough may follow a change request through risk assessment, testing, approval, deployment, monitoring, and record retention.

The auditor should compare interview statements with independent evidence. A process owner may accurately describe the intended procedure without being aware that the production workflow does not enforce it.

Effective interview and walkthrough practices include:

- Identifying the purpose and scope before each session.
- Recording participants, dates, systems, and processes examined.
- Asking open-ended questions followed by evidence-specific questions.
- Requesting demonstrations or records that corroborate important statements.
- Distinguishing observed facts from participant opinions.
- Recording disagreements and unresolved questions.
- Confirming factual accuracy where appropriate without surrendering independent audit judgment.

Interviews are particularly useful for understanding context, but unsupported verbal assurances should not normally be the sole basis for conclusions about the operating effectiveness of high-risk controls.

## 43. Verifying the Actual AI System Against Approved Documentation

AI audit documentation may describe an intended architecture or approved configuration that differs from the system operating in production. The auditor should therefore establish whether the artifacts, components, configurations, and processes examined correspond to the actual deployed system.

The comparison should consider relevant elements such as:

- Model identity, version, and deployment configuration.
- Data sources, transformation pipelines, and retrieval components.
- APIs, plugins, tools, and external service integrations.
- Identity and access management settings.
- Logging, monitoring, and alerting configuration.
- Human review and escalation workflows.
- Security boundaries and network connectivity.
- Approved use cases and restrictions.
- Change records and deployment timestamps.

Evidence may include deployment records, configuration exports, infrastructure inventories, model registries, system diagrams, access logs, and direct observation of relevant interfaces.

The auditor should identify discrepancies between documentation and actual operation. For example, an architecture diagram may indicate that a human approval step is mandatory, while the deployed workflow allows the system to execute an action without that approval.

A discrepancy does not automatically establish a material deficiency. The auditor should determine whether it is authorized, whether the documentation is outdated, whether the difference affects risk, and whether the relevant audit criteria have been met.

Where system access is restricted, the auditor should document the limitation and assess whether alternative evidence can establish the relevant facts with sufficient reliability.

## 44. Evaluating Evidence Reliability, Relevance and Sufficiency

Audit conclusions depend on the quality of the evidence obtained. Evidence should be evaluated for its reliability, relevance to the audit objective, completeness, accuracy, timeliness, and sufficiency.

**Reliability** concerns the degree to which evidence can be trusted. Evidence generated directly by a controlled system may be more reliable than an unverified summary, although system-generated evidence is not automatically accurate or complete.

**Relevance** concerns whether the evidence addresses the specific assertion or control being evaluated.

**Sufficiency** concerns whether the quantity and coverage of evidence are adequate to support the conclusion.

**Appropriateness** concerns the quality of evidence, including its reliability and relevance.

The auditor should consider the source of the evidence, the method used to generate it, the possibility of manipulation or omission, and whether it can be independently corroborated.

For example, a screenshot showing that a security alert exists may establish that an interface displays an alert. It may not establish that the alert was generated at the correct time, delivered to the responsible team, investigated, and resolved according to policy.

Evidence evaluation should also account for AI-specific limitations. A model-generated explanation of its own behavior should not automatically be treated as authoritative evidence of the underlying technical process. The auditor should seek supporting records, testing results, or independent technical analysis where necessary.

When evidence is inconsistent, incomplete, or unreliable, the auditor should perform additional procedures or explicitly document the limitation. The absence of sufficient evidence must not be silently converted into a conclusion that the control is effective.

## 45. Maintaining Audit Evidence Integrity and Chain of Custody

AI audits may involve sensitive datasets, model artifacts, configuration files, security logs, prompts, outputs, and incident records. These materials must be collected and retained in a manner that preserves their integrity, confidentiality, provenance, and auditability.

The auditor should establish appropriate evidence-handling procedures before collecting sensitive or technically significant artifacts.

These procedures may include:

- Assigning unique identifiers to evidence items.
- Recording the source, custodian, collection date, and collection method.
- Preserving relevant timestamps, version identifiers, and metadata.
- Recording transformations, filtering, or redactions applied to evidence.
- Restricting access to authorized audit personnel.
- Using secure repositories with access logging.
- Applying integrity verification methods, such as cryptographic hashes, where appropriate.
- Documenting transfers between custodians.
- Retaining evidence according to applicable retention requirements.
- Securely disposing of evidence when retention is no longer authorized.

Forensic or incident-related evidence may require more rigorous chain-of-custody procedures than routine policy documents. The auditor should apply controls proportionate to the purpose of the audit and the evidentiary importance of the artifact.

Sensitive data should not be copied into ordinary workpapers unnecessarily. Where possible, the auditor should use controlled extracts, redacted records, synthetic test data, or references to approved evidence repositories.

The audit file should make it possible for an authorized reviewer to identify the evidence supporting a conclusion and understand how it was obtained and evaluated.

## 46. Performing Control Testing and Recording Results

Control testing determines whether a control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating in accordance with the defined criteria. Each test should have a clear objective, a defined population where relevant, a documented procedure, expected results, and a method for evaluating exceptions.

The auditor should record enough information for another qualified reviewer to understand and reproduce the work where practicable.

A control test record should normally include:

- Risk and control identifiers.
- Control owner and control frequency.
- Audit period covered.
- Population description and source.
- Sample-selection method and selected items.
- Testing steps performed.
- Evidence references.
- Expected control behavior.
- Actual results.
- Exceptions identified.
- Auditor conclusion and rationale.
- Reviewer comments and resolution.

For example, a test of AI model-change approvals might select a sample of deployments, inspect the corresponding change requests, verify approval timestamps, compare approved versions with deployed versions, and determine whether required testing was completed before release.

The auditor should distinguish between an isolated documentation error, a control that failed in one instance, and a systemic process weakness. The significance of an exception depends on the control's purpose, the nature of the failure, the risk involved, and the broader evidence.

Where the results indicate that the original testing approach was inadequate, the auditor should revise the approach and document the rationale rather than continue using a procedure that cannot support a defensible conclusion.

## 47. Evaluating AI Model Testing Results and Technical Evidence

Technical AI evidence may include model evaluation reports, benchmark results, validation records, robustness tests, fairness assessments, red-team results, security test outputs, monitoring data, and performance dashboards.

The auditor should determine whether the evidence is relevant to the system version, operating conditions, and audit period under examination. Results from an earlier model version may not substantiate the behavior of the current deployment.

Important evaluation considerations include:

- Whether the test objective was clearly defined.
- Whether the test environment reflects relevant operating conditions.
- Whether datasets and test cases are appropriate.
- Whether evaluation methods are documented and reproducible.
- Whether metrics are interpreted in context.
- Whether material limitations and excluded cases are disclosed.
- Whether the reported results can be traced to underlying records.
- Whether failures and adverse results were retained rather than selectively omitted.
- Whether the organization acted on results that did not meet acceptance criteria.

For generative AI, technical testing may examine prompt injection, unsafe output handling, sensitive-data exposure, hallucination-related risks, retrieval errors, and misuse of connected tools. For predictive models, the emphasis may include data quality, calibration, performance stability, subgroup results, and decision thresholds.

The auditor should not treat benchmark performance as proof of overall safety or compliance. A benchmark measures defined capabilities under particular conditions; it does not establish that every relevant risk has been assessed or controlled.

If an organization provides only a summary report without underlying methodology or supporting evidence, the auditor should evaluate whether that summary is adequate for the intended conclusion.

## 48. Evaluating AI Governance and Regulatory Compliance Evidence

AI governance and compliance audits require evidence that the organization has identified applicable obligations, assigned accountability, implemented relevant controls, and retained records demonstrating how requirements are addressed.

The auditor should distinguish between evidence that a governance document exists and evidence that the related process operates effectively.

Relevant evidence may include:

- AI governance policies and approved standards.
- AI system inventories and classification records.
- Risk assessments and risk-treatment decisions.
- Assigned roles, responsibilities, and decision authorities.
- Records of approvals and prohibited-use decisions.
- Impact assessments where required or appropriate.
- Human oversight procedures and operating records.
- Training and competency records.
- Supplier due diligence and contractual provisions.
- Monitoring, incident, and corrective-action records.
- Regulatory reporting and notification records where applicable.
- Management reviews and internal assurance reports.

The auditor should map applicable criteria to evidence and test procedures. A legal requirement may impose a specific obligation, while a voluntary framework may provide guidance for implementing a control. These sources should not be treated as interchangeable.

For each applicable requirement, the auditor should determine whether the organization has established an appropriate process, whether the process is implemented, whether evidence supports its operation, and whether exceptions have been addressed.

Where the legal applicability of a requirement is uncertain, the auditor should document the uncertainty and obtain appropriate legal or compliance input. The auditor should not make an unsupported legal determination merely because a control appears similar to a regulatory provision.

## 49. Assessing Exceptions, Deviations and Contradictory Evidence

During fieldwork, auditors may identify exceptions, conflicting records, unexplained differences, or evidence that contradicts management's description of a control. These conditions require investigation before conclusions are reached.

An exception may indicate a control failure, incomplete documentation, an approved deviation, a system configuration issue, or a misunderstanding of the applicable criteria. The auditor should determine which explanation is supported by evidence.

A disciplined exception assessment should address:

1. What was expected under the applicable criterion?
2. What was observed?
3. Which evidence supports the observation?
4. Is the evidence complete and reliable?
5. Is the deviation authorized and within approved limits?
6. Does the issue affect one instance or a wider population?
7. What is the potential impact on the AI system and relevant stakeholders?
8. Are compensating controls present and effective?
9. Is additional testing necessary?
10. Does the issue require immediate escalation?

Contradictory evidence should not be ignored simply because other evidence supports management's position. For example, a policy may require access reviews every quarter, while system records indicate that several reviews were missed. The auditor should reconcile the discrepancy or report the unresolved limitation.

Management explanations can help establish context but do not replace objective evidence. If management disputes a finding, the auditor should consider any new evidence fairly, reassess the conclusion where warranted, and retain the rationale for the final determination.

Potentially serious issues should be escalated according to the audit mandate and reporting protocol rather than being held until the final report.

---

## 50. Determining the Root Cause and Systemic Nature of AI Control Deficiencies

Identifying the immediate condition is not always sufficient to understand why an AI control failed. Root-cause analysis examines the underlying factors that allowed the deficiency to occur or persist.

Potential causes include:

- Unclear accountability or overlapping responsibilities.
- Inadequate governance policies or control design.
- Insufficient staff competence or training.
- Weak workflow enforcement or excessive reliance on manual procedures.
- Incomplete system inventories or architecture documentation.
- Poor integration between AI development, security, risk, and compliance teams.
- Inadequate monitoring or escalation mechanisms.
- Supplier limitations or unclear contractual responsibilities.
- Insufficient resources or competing operational priorities.
- Failure to incorporate lessons from prior incidents or audit findings.

The auditor should avoid assuming a root cause without evidence. A missing approval record may result from a failed approval process, incomplete record retention, or an evidence retrieval problem. Each explanation has different implications and may require different corrective action.

The auditor should also assess whether the deficiency is isolated or systemic. Similar exceptions across multiple models, teams, suppliers, or deployment environments may indicate a broader governance weakness.

Systemic analysis is particularly important when one control supports several AI systems. A weakness in centralized identity management, model registry controls, or incident escalation may affect a large portion of the AI portfolio.

Root-cause analysis should support practical recommendations that address the underlying weakness rather than merely correcting individual exceptions. The final finding should clearly distinguish confirmed causes from hypotheses requiring further investigation.

---

## 51. Assessing the Significance and Materiality of AI Audit Findings

The significance of an AI audit finding depends on more than the number of exceptions identified. The auditor should evaluate the potential consequences, the importance of the affected control, the likelihood of adverse outcomes, the scope of exposure, and the relevance of applicable legal or contractual obligations.

Traditional financial materiality concepts may be insufficient for some AI risks. An issue involving a small number of records could still be significant if it affects fundamental rights, exposes sensitive information, creates a serious safety hazard, or compromises a critical decision process.

Factors to consider include:

- Severity of potential harm to individuals or other stakeholders.
- Number and type of systems, users, or decisions affected.
- Duration of the deficiency.
- Frequency and recurrence of exceptions.
- Exposure to external threats or unauthorized activity.
- Effect on regulatory compliance or contractual commitments.
- Reliability of compensating controls.
- Potential for the issue to propagate through dependent systems.
- Degree of uncertainty surrounding the risk.
- Whether the deficiency concerns a mandatory requirement.

The auditor should apply the audit function's approved severity taxonomy where available and adapt the analysis to AI-specific consequences when necessary.

A materiality assessment should be documented with a clear rationale. A low number of exceptions should not automatically lead to a low-severity rating, and a high number of minor documentation errors should not automatically imply a critical risk.

Where a finding may represent an immediate threat to safety, security, privacy, or legal compliance, the auditor should follow the required escalation process without waiting for all routine fieldwork to conclude.

---

## 52. Aggregating Related Findings and Identifying Cross-Cutting Themes

Individual audit findings may reveal a broader weakness that is not apparent when each exception is considered separately. The auditor should evaluate whether related findings share a common cause, affect the same governance process, or indicate a portfolio-wide control problem.

For example, separate findings involving missing model approvals, incomplete validation records, and inconsistent deployment logs may collectively indicate weaknesses in the organization's AI change-management lifecycle.

Aggregation should consider:

- Common root causes.
- Shared control owners or governance processes.
- Dependencies between affected systems.
- Repeated deficiencies across business units.
- Common suppliers or technical platforms.
- Combined exposure from multiple control failures.
- Patterns in prior audit findings and incident records.

Cross-cutting themes may include inadequate AI inventory management, inconsistent risk classification, weak third-party oversight, insufficient monitoring, or unclear accountability for human intervention.

The auditor should avoid double-counting the same underlying issue as several independent risks without explaining their relationship. At the same time, aggregation should not conceal distinct consequences that require separate remediation.

Where findings are grouped, each underlying exception should remain traceable to its supporting evidence. The audit report should explain both the individual deficiencies and the broader pattern when that pattern materially affects the conclusion.

---

## 53. Evaluating Compensating Controls and Alternative Safeguards

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard that reduces a risk when the primary control is absent, ineffective, or not fully implemented. The auditor should determine whether the alternative control addresses the same risk and provides a sufficient level of protection.

For example, if automated model-change approval enforcement is unavailable, an independently operated release review may provide some protection. However, the auditor must establish that the review occurs before deployment, covers the relevant changes, is performed by an authorized person, and retains reliable evidence.

Compensating controls should be evaluated for:

- Alignment with the original control objective.
- Coverage of the affected risk and population.
- Independence from the failed control where relevant.
- Frequency and timeliness.
- Consistency of operation.
- Evidence availability and reliability.
- Susceptibility to the same failure mechanism.
- Limitations and residual exposure.

An alternative safeguard should not be accepted merely because management describes it as a compensating control. Its effectiveness must be supported by evidence.

For AI systems, the auditor should consider whether an alternative safeguard remains effective when model behavior changes, transaction volume increases, external dependencies fail, or human reviewers face time pressure.

Even where a compensating control reduces risk, the original deficiency may remain reportable. The auditor should distinguish the existence of a deficiency from the resulting residual risk and explain how the alternative safeguard affects the final assessment.

---

## 54. Evaluating Management Responses and Proposed Remediation

Management responses are an important part of the audit process, but they should not replace the auditor's independent assessment. The auditor should evaluate whether proposed corrective actions address the identified condition and underlying cause, reduce the relevant risk, and establish a sustainable control.

A remediation plan should identify:

- The finding being addressed.
- The confirmed or accepted root cause.
- Corrective actions and expected outcomes.
- Accountable action owners.
- Target completion dates.
- Dependencies and required resources.
- Interim safeguards where immediate remediation is not possible.
- Evidence required to demonstrate completion.
- The method for validating effectiveness.

For example, retraining employees may be appropriate when a deficiency results from a demonstrated knowledge gap. It may be insufficient when the actual cause is a workflow that allows deployment without required approval.

The auditor should assess whether the proposed action is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant to the risk, and time-bound. These characteristics help make remediation verifiable.

Management may choose to accept a risk rather than implement the auditor's preferred recommendation. The auditor should determine whether the decision is made by an authorized risk owner, whether the rationale is documented, and whether the acceptance complies with applicable obligations and organizational policy.

The auditor should not automatically treat a management commitment as evidence that a deficiency has been corrected. Closure requires appropriate supporting evidence and, for significant findings, validation that the revised control operates effectively.

---

## 55. Establishing Evidence-Based Audit Conclusions

An audit conclusion should follow from the evidence obtained and the criteria applied. It should not be based solely on the auditor's general impression of the organization, the existence of policies, or management's confidence in the system.

The auditor should determine whether the evidence supports a conclusion about each relevant audit objective, control, or compliance requirement. The conclusion should reflect the nature and extent of testing, identified exceptions, unresolved limitations, and any contradictory evidence.

Possible conclusions may include:

- The control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively within the tested scope.
- The control is appropriately designed but not implemented or not operating consistently.
- The control is partially effective, with identified deficiencies.
- The control is ineffective against the assessed risk.
- The evidence is insufficient to reach a conclusion.
- The requirement is not applicable, based on a documented and supportable applicability assessment.

These are illustrative categories. The auditor should use the audit function's approved conclusion framework and clearly define the meaning of each rating.

Conclusions must be bounded by the audit scope. A successful test of selected controls does not establish that the entire AI system is safe, fair, secure, or compliant in every circumstance.

The auditor should also distinguish a control conclusion from a broader system-level assurance conclusion. A control may operate effectively while the residual risk remains unacceptable, or an AI system may perform well technically while governance arrangements remain inadequate.

Where the evidence does not support a definitive conclusion, the auditor should state the limitation and explain its implications rather than force a positive or negative rating.

---

## 56. Managing Audit Scope Changes During Fieldwork

AI audit fieldwork may reveal new components, dependencies, risks, or business uses that were not fully understood during planning. The auditor should have a documented process for evaluating whether such discoveries require changes to the scope, testing approach, timetable, or resource allocation.

Examples of scope-changing discoveries include:

- An unregistered AI system is identified in a critical business process.
- A supposedly isolated model has access to additional data sources or tools.
- A supplier has introduced a material model update.
- A significant security incident affects the system under review.
- A control deficiency appears across multiple deployments.
- The organization uses AI outputs for a higher-impact purpose than originally described.
- Evidence access restrictions prevent testing of a material control.

The auditor should assess the significance of the new information and determine whether it is within the existing mandate. If the proposed change exceeds the authorized scope, the audit lead should seek the required approval.

Scope changes should be documented with their rationale, impact on planned procedures, additional resources required, and implications for reporting.

A scope change should not be used to exclude an inconvenient finding merely because it complicates the original audit plan. If a matter falls outside the mandate but is material, the auditor should follow the appropriate escalation and referral process.

When scope cannot be expanded, the final report should clearly describe the relevant limitation and its effect on the conclusions.

---

## 57. Applying Quality Assurance and Supervisory Review to Audit Workpapers

Audit workpapers provide the documented basis for audit findings, conclusions, and reporting. Supervisory review helps confirm that procedures were performed appropriately, evidence supports the recorded results, and conclusions are consistent with the audit methodology.

Review procedures should be proportionate to the significance and complexity of the work. High-risk AI security testing, legal compliance conclusions, and assessments involving potential harm to individuals may require additional specialist review.

A reviewer should examine whether:

- The audit objective and criteria are clear.
- The procedure addresses the stated objective.
- Evidence is sufficient, relevant, and reliable.
- Sample selection and testing are documented.
- Exceptions are accurately recorded.
- Contrary evidence has been considered.
- Conclusions follow logically from the evidence.
- Findings are correctly classified and supported.
- Scope limitations are disclosed.
- Required approvals and specialist consultations are documented.

Review comments should be resolved or explicitly carried forward as unresolved matters in accordance with audit policy. The auditor should retain an appropriate record of material review decisions.

Quality assurance should not become a purely administrative exercise. The purpose is to challenge assumptions, identify unsupported judgments, and improve the defensibility and consistency of audit results.

For complex AI systems, the audit lead should ensure that technical specialists and governance or legal specialists understand the boundaries of their respective assessments and that the final conclusion integrates their work appropriately.

---

## 58. Integrating Specialist Opinions into the Audit Conclusion

AI audits may require expertise in machine learning, cybersecurity, data science, privacy, legal compliance, safety engineering, or sector-specific regulation. Specialist input can strengthen audit quality, but the audit lead remains responsible for integrating the evidence into the overall audit conclusion.

The auditor should define the specialist's scope, required expertise, expected deliverables, and reporting relationship. The specialist should understand the audit objective and relevant criteria before performing the work.

Specialist evidence may include:

- Independent model evaluation.
- Security testing and threat analysis.
- Privacy impact analysis.
- Data quality or statistical assessments.
- Architecture and configuration reviews.
- Legal or regulatory interpretation.
- Safety and operational resilience analysis.

The auditor should assess whether the specialist's work is relevant, methodologically sound, sufficiently documented, and appropriate for the intended conclusion. Where specialist assumptions or limitations affect the result, these should be recorded.

The audit lead should not automatically adopt a specialist's conclusion without understanding its basis and limitations. Similarly, technical findings should not be translated into legal conclusions without appropriate analysis of the applicable requirements.

Where specialist opinions conflict, the audit team should investigate the basis for the disagreement, consider additional evidence, and document the resolution or remaining uncertainty.

The final audit report should communicate specialist findings in language appropriate for its intended audience while preserving the technical accuracy and limitations of the underlying work.

---

## 59. Assessing the Implications of Unresolved Evidence Limitations

Evidence limitations arise when the auditor cannot obtain or verify information necessary to support a conclusion. Such limitations may result from restricted access, missing records, unreliable data, supplier confidentiality, technical constraints, or insufficient retention of historical evidence.

The auditor should determine whether alternative procedures can address the limitation. For example, if direct access to a supplier's model is unavailable, contractual evidence, independent assurance reports, controlled testing, and operational monitoring records may provide partial or complementary assurance.

However, alternative evidence should not be treated as equivalent without evaluating its coverage and reliability.

The auditor should document:

- The information that could not be obtained or verified.
- The reason for the limitation.
- The audit objectives and risks affected.
- Alternative procedures attempted.
- The evidence obtained through those procedures.
- The conclusions that remain supportable.
- The residual uncertainty and reporting implications.

A limitation affecting a low-risk administrative control may have a different consequence from one preventing examination of a critical security boundary or a mandatory compliance obligation.

If the limitation is sufficiently significant, the auditor may need to qualify the conclusion, restrict the assurance statement, escalate the issue, or state that no conclusion can be reached on the affected objective.

The report should distinguish an absence of evidence from evidence that a control failed. Both may be important, but they support different conclusions.

---

## 60. Preparing the Audit Results for Formal Reporting

Before formal reporting begins, the audit team should consolidate test results, validate finding evidence, resolve review comments, evaluate the overall risk picture, and ensure that each conclusion is supported by the audit file.

The auditor should confirm that findings are factually accurate, appropriately classified, and clearly linked to applicable criteria. Material disagreements, unresolved evidence limitations, and scope restrictions should be identified before the report is finalized.

Preparation should include:

- Reconciling the risk register with completed audit procedures.
- Confirming that significant risks received appropriate audit coverage.
- Reviewing all exceptions and their supporting evidence.
- Validating root-cause analysis and severity ratings.
- Evaluating compensating controls and residual risks.
- Confirming management responses and action owners where available.
- Checking the consistency of findings across related systems.
- Identifying matters requiring immediate escalation.
- Confirming that conclusions reflect the actual scope and limitations.
- Completing supervisory and specialist reviews.

The audit team should also distinguish findings that require immediate management attention from routine observations that can be communicated through the normal reporting process.

The preparation stage should preserve a clear traceability chain:

```
Audit criteria
      |
      v
Risk and control objectives
      |
      v
Procedures performed
      |
      v
Evidence and test results
      |
      v
Exceptions and root-cause analysis
      |
      v
Severity and residual-risk assessment
      |
      v
Evidence-based audit conclusions
      |
      v
Formal audit reporting
```

A disciplined transition from fieldwork to reporting reduces the risk of unsupported conclusions, inconsistent ratings, or material issues being omitted. It also provides the foundation for a clear, defensible report that communicates what was examined, what the evidence demonstrated, and what action is required.

## Part 4 — AI Audit Reporting, Assurance Communication, Remediation Validation and Continuous Improvement

## 61. Structuring the AI Audit Report and Establishing Reporting Requirements

The AI audit report communicates the engagement's objectives, scope, methodology, evidence-based findings, assurance conclusions, and required management actions. It is the formal record of what the audit established and the limitations affecting those conclusions.

A technically sound audit can lose much of its value if the report does not clearly distinguish verified facts, control deficiencies, potential consequences, and recommendations. Reporting should therefore be treated as a controlled assurance activity rather than an administrative exercise performed after fieldwork.

Before drafting the report, the audit lead should establish the intended audience, reporting format, approval requirements, distribution restrictions, and expected level of detail.

The report structure should normally address:

- Executive summary: The overall audit conclusion, most significant risks, and principal actions required.
- Audit objectives: The questions the engagement was designed to answer.
- Scope and boundaries: The AI systems, organizational units, processes, model versions, and periods examined.
- Audit criteria: The applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, technical, and internal requirements.
- Methodology: The procedures, sampling methods, evidence sources, and specialist assessments used.
- Overall conclusion: The assurance conclusion supported by the evidence obtained.
- Detailed findings: The criteria, observed conditions, causes, consequences, and recommended corrective actions.
- Management responses: Agreed actions, responsible owners, and target completion dates.
- Limitations: Material restrictions on evidence, testing, scope, or interpretation.
- Appendices: Supporting matrices, technical details, definitions, or supplementary results where appropriate.

The report should maintain traceability between the audit workpapers and every material conclusion. Readers should be able to understand why a finding was raised, which evidence supports it, and what risk requires management attention.

The level of detail should be proportionate to the audience. Senior management may require concise risk implications and decisions, while technical control owners may need detailed configurations, test scenarios, and remediation specifications.

The report must not imply that the audit examined components, periods, or control populations that were outside the actual scope.

## 62. Formulating the Overall AI Audit Opinion

The overall AI audit opinion summarizes the auditor's conclusion about the subject examined against the defined audit criteria. It should reflect the combined significance of the findings, the reliability of the evidence, the effectiveness of relevant controls, and any material scope limitations.

The audit function should establish a consistent conclusion framework before applying it to individual engagements. Possible conclusion categories include:

- Effective: The examined governance arrangements and controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively, subject to the defined scope.
- Generally effective with improvements required: The principal objectives are achieved, but identified deficiencies require corrective action.
- Partially effective: Material weaknesses prevent the examined arrangements from consistently achieving their intended objectives.
- Ineffective: Significant deficiencies undermine the ability of the examined arrangements to manage relevant risks.
- Unable to conclude: Evidence limitations or scope restrictions prevent a sufficiently supported overall conclusion.

These categories are illustrative. Their definitions and thresholds should be established by the audit function rather than improvised after findings are known.

The auditor should consider the following when formulating the opinion:

1. Whether the audit covered the material risks associated with the defined objectives.
2. Whether sufficient and appropriate evidence was obtained.
3. Whether significant controls operated effectively over the relevant period.
4. Whether mandatory criteria were met.
5. Whether findings indicate isolated failures or systemic weaknesses.
6. Whether compensating controls adequately address identified deficiencies.
7. Whether residual risks remain within approved acceptance criteria.
8. Whether unresolved limitations materially restrict assurance.

An overall rating should not be calculated solely by averaging individual control scores. A single critical weakness may undermine the assurance conclusion even if numerous lower-risk controls operate effectively.

For example, an AI governance program may have comprehensive policies, documented responsibilities, and regular management reviews. However, if a high-impact AI application can make consequential decisions without the required authorization or oversight, the overall conclusion may still need to reflect that material weakness.

The auditor should explain the reasoning behind the overall opinion and identify the conditions that most strongly influenced it.

## 63. Communicating AI Audit Findings According to Audience and Risk

AI audit findings often contain information that must be understood by different audiences, including executive management, technical teams, legal advisers, risk owners, regulators, and external assurance providers.

The auditor should communicate the same underlying facts consistently while adjusting the level of technical detail to the audience's responsibilities and decision-making needs.

Executive communication should emphasize:

- The business or stakeholder risk.
- The significance and urgency of the deficiency.
- The systems, processes, or populations affected.
- The organization's exposure to legal, regulatory, security, or operational consequences.
- The decisions and resources required from management.
- The consequences of delayed or incomplete remediation.

Technical communication should explain the specific control weakness, the conditions under which it was observed, the evidence supporting the finding, and the technical or procedural changes required to address it.

For example, a finding involving excessive permissions granted to an AI agent may require two complementary explanations.

At the executive level, the finding should explain that the agent can perform actions beyond its approved business purpose, creating an unauthorized-action risk.

At the technical level, the finding may identify excessive tool permissions, missing authorization checks, inadequate separation of duties, or insufficient enforcement of approval requirements.

The auditor should avoid two reporting failures.

First, excessive technical detail can obscure the risk and decision required. Second, vague executive language can prevent control owners from understanding how to correct the deficiency.

Where findings involve potentially serious security vulnerabilities, personal information, exploitable system weaknesses, or confidential supplier details, the report should use appropriate access restrictions and controlled distribution.

The reporting process should preserve factual accuracy, confidentiality, and the independence of the audit conclusion.

## 64. Validating Findings Through a Controlled Factual Review

A factual review gives relevant stakeholders an opportunity to identify errors in the description of the audited environment, evidence, processes, or circumstances surrounding a finding.

The purpose is to improve factual accuracy, not to provide management with unrestricted authority to remove adverse findings or change independent audit judgments.

Before finalizing significant findings, the auditor should provide an appropriate description of the observed condition and supporting facts to the relevant stakeholders.

The review should establish whether:

- The affected system, model version, and environment are correctly identified.
- The audit period and relevant dates are accurate.
- The cited evidence supports the described condition.
- The applicable criteria are represented correctly.
- Relevant mitigating circumstances have been considered.
- Management has supplied additional evidence that changes the assessment.
- The proposed finding distinguishes verified facts from assumptions.
- Confidentiality and information-handling requirements are maintained.

Management may provide additional evidence showing that an apparent control failure was authorized under an approved exception. The auditor should assess that evidence and revise the finding where the original interpretation is no longer supported.

However, disagreement with the severity rating, potential consequences, or recommendation does not by itself invalidate the finding.

If management disputes a conclusion, the auditor should document the disagreement, evaluate the supporting arguments, and retain the independent rationale for the final determination.

Unresolved disagreements should be addressed through the audit function's established escalation and reporting arrangements.

The factual review should have defined deadlines so that it does not unnecessarily delay reporting of material risks.

Where immediate threats to security, safety, privacy, or legal compliance are identified, required escalation should proceed without waiting for completion of the ordinary review process.

## 65. Assessing Management Responses and Remediation Commitments

Management responses explain how the organization intends to address identified deficiencies, who is responsible for the corrective action, and when remediation is expected to be completed.

The auditor should assess whether proposed actions address the underlying risk and root cause rather than merely correcting the immediate condition observed during fieldwork.

A useful remediation commitment should specify:

- The finding being addressed.
- The underlying cause or control weakness.
- The corrective action to be implemented.
- The accountable management owner.
- The implementation milestones.
- The target completion date.
- The evidence that will demonstrate completion.
- Any dependencies or resource requirements.
- Interim safeguards where the risk remains material.
- The residual risk expected after remediation.

For example, if an audit identifies that AI model changes can enter production without required approval, management may propose retraining employees to follow the change-management procedure.

Training may be useful, but it may not address the underlying weakness if the deployment pipeline technically permits unapproved changes.

A stronger remediation plan could enforce approval requirements within the release workflow, maintain immutable or otherwise appropriately protected deployment records, and prevent production deployment when mandatory checks fail.

The auditor should evaluate whether the proposed action is proportionate to the severity and likelihood of the risk.

Management may decide to accept a residual risk rather than implement the recommended control. Such a decision should be made by an authorized risk owner, supported by a documented rationale, and consistent with applicable legal obligations and organizational risk-acceptance rules.

The auditor should not treat a management commitment as evidence that remediation has already occurred.

Until implementation and effectiveness are verified, the finding should remain subject to the organization's established tracking and reporting process.

## 66. Establishing Risk-Based Remediation Priorities and Deadlines

AI audit findings may range from minor documentation weaknesses to deficiencies that expose individuals, critical business processes, or regulated activities to significant harm.

Remediation priorities should reflect the actual risk rather than the administrative convenience of the responsible team.

The audit function should use the organization's approved severity and remediation framework, taking into account AI-specific consequences where necessary.

A risk-based prioritization approach may consider:

| Priority | Typical characteristics                                                                                     | Expected management response                                          |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Immediate or potentially severe harm, major security exposure, or an urgent unresolved mandatory obligation | Immediate escalation, appropriate containment, and urgent remediation |
| High     | Significant control failure affecting important AI risks or compliance obligations                          | Prioritized corrective action with close oversight                    |
| Medium   | Meaningful weakness requiring correction but with more limited immediate exposure                           | Planned remediation within an approved deadline                       |
| Low      | Limited deficiency with comparatively low residual risk                                                     | Routine corrective action or process improvement                      |

These categories are illustrative. The organization should define formal thresholds, escalation requirements, and target completion periods.

The auditor should consider whether interim measures are necessary while permanent remediation is being developed.

For example, where an AI agent has excessive privileges, interim safeguards might include disabling high-impact tools, restricting access to approved resources, requiring additional human authorization, or limiting the system to a controlled environment.

Interim safeguards should be assessed for effectiveness and should not automatically replace the permanent control.

Remediation deadlines should account for the severity of the finding, the complexity of implementation, relevant dependencies, and the risk of continued operation.

Where management requests an extension, the auditor should evaluate the rationale, the remaining exposure, the adequacy of interim controls, and the authority approving the revised date.

Repeated extensions for a significant finding may indicate that remediation governance is ineffective or that the organization is implicitly accepting a risk without an adequate formal decision.

## 67. Maintaining an AI Audit Finding and Remediation Register

An AI audit finding register provides a controlled record of deficiencies, management commitments, remediation progress, and outstanding assurance issues.

The register should support accountability across individual audits and enable management to identify recurring weaknesses across the AI portfolio.

A practical register may include the following fields:

- Finding identifier and originating audit.
- Finding title and description.
- Applicable audit criteria.
- Affected AI system, model, process, or supplier.
- Risk category and severity rating.
- Root cause, where established.
- Potential consequences and residual risk.
- Responsible management owner.
- Agreed corrective action.
- Interim safeguards.
- Original target completion date.
- Approved revised completion date, where applicable.
- Current implementation status.
- Evidence submitted by management.
- Validation procedures performed.
- Validation outcome.
- Closure approval and date.
- Escalation history.
- Related findings and systemic themes.

Finding status should use clearly defined categories, such as:

- Open — remediation has not been completed.
- In progress — corrective actions are being implemented.
- Pending validation — management reports completion, but independent verification remains outstanding.
- Partially remediated — some actions are complete, but material deficiencies remain.
- Closed — remediation has been verified and closure approved under the applicable methodology.
- Risk accepted — an authorized decision-maker has formally accepted the residual risk, where permitted.

Risk acceptance and finding closure should not automatically be treated as equivalent. Depending on the audit methodology, a finding may remain recorded as an accepted risk even when management has chosen not to implement the recommended control.

The register should also identify overdue high-risk findings and deficiencies affecting multiple systems.

Periodic reconciliation against the audit workpapers, risk register, and management reporting records helps prevent findings from being lost, duplicated, or incorrectly marked as complete.

## 68. Verifying Remediation Implementation and Operating Effectiveness

Remediation verification determines whether management has implemented the agreed corrective actions and whether those actions adequately address the original deficiency.

The auditor should distinguish implementation verification from operating-effectiveness testing.

Implementation verification establishes whether the corrective action has been put into operation. Evidence may include approved procedures, configuration changes, workflow restrictions, updated contracts, or revised access permissions.

Operating-effectiveness testing establishes whether the remediated control performs consistently and appropriately under relevant operating conditions.

For each significant finding, the auditor should determine:

1. What condition originally caused the finding?
2. What corrective action was approved?
3. What evidence demonstrates implementation?
4. Does the new control address the original risk mechanism?
5. Has the relevant system or process been tested?
6. Has the control operated for a sufficient period to support the intended conclusion?
7. Are exceptions still occurring?
8. Has residual risk been reassessed?
9. Is independent validation required before closure?

For example, if a finding concerns missing approvals for AI model releases, a new approval policy does not establish that the deficiency has been corrected.

The auditor may need to inspect the deployment workflow, confirm that required approval checks are enforced, test whether an unapproved release is blocked in an authorized test environment, and examine production release records after implementation.

Where the control operates periodically, verification should cover an appropriate number of control instances or operating cycles.

If sufficient operating history is not yet available, the auditor may record that implementation has been verified while operating effectiveness remains unconfirmed.

A finding should not be closed solely because management reports completion or because a project milestone has been achieved.

Closure should follow documented validation criteria, independent review where required, and the audit function's approved authority structure.

## 69. Reassessing Residual Risk After AI Control Remediation

Implementing a corrective action does not automatically eliminate the original risk. The auditor should determine whether the revised controls reduce the risk to an acceptable level under the organization's established methodology.

Residual-risk reassessment should consider:

- Whether the original risk scenario remains possible.
- Whether the corrected control addresses the identified root cause.
- Whether other components create alternative paths to the same outcome.
- Whether compensating controls remain necessary.
- Whether the new control introduces additional dependencies.
- Whether the system's intended use or operating environment has changed.
- Whether new testing reveals additional weaknesses.
- Whether the remaining risk is within approved acceptance criteria.
- Whether the risk owner has formally approved the residual exposure.

For example, restricting an AI agent's tool permissions may reduce the likelihood of unauthorized actions. However, excessive privileges may remain in another service account, or the agent may still access sensitive information through a retrieval interface.

The auditor should therefore assess the complete risk scenario rather than validating only the configuration change named in the remediation plan.

Risk reduction should be supported by evidence. A revised risk score without corresponding control evidence does not establish that the risk has materially decreased.

Where the risk remains above the organization's acceptance threshold, management should determine whether further treatment, additional restrictions, suspension of the affected capability, or another risk response is required.

Where legal requirements or mandatory safety obligations apply, residual-risk acceptance cannot be used to override those obligations.

The final validation record should explain the original exposure, the controls implemented, the evidence obtained, and the rationale for the revised risk conclusion.

## 70. Escalating Overdue Findings and Unaccepted Residual Risks

An effective AI audit program requires a defined escalation process for findings that remain unresolved, exceed their approved deadlines, or expose the organization to risks beyond its authorized tolerance.

Escalation is particularly important when remediation depends on multiple departments, external suppliers, major system changes, or executive funding decisions.

The audit function should establish escalation thresholds based on factors such as:

- Finding severity and residual risk.
- Time elapsed since the original target date.
- Number of missed remediation milestones.
- Repeated failures of the same control.
- Changes in the threat or regulatory environment.
- Inadequacy of interim safeguards.
- Disagreement about risk ownership.
- Lack of an authorized risk-acceptance decision.
- Potential harm to affected individuals or other stakeholders.

A typical escalation path may involve the control owner, business or system owner, responsible executive, enterprise risk management function, and audit committee or equivalent oversight body.

The exact reporting line depends on the organization's governance structure and the audit mandate.

The auditor should distinguish an overdue action from an unauthorized risk acceptance. Both may require attention, but they represent different governance failures.

A remediation delay may result from a legitimate technical dependency. The auditor should assess whether the delay is controlled, whether interim safeguards are effective, and whether the revised deadline has been properly authorized.

By contrast, an organization that continues operating a high-risk AI system without effective controls or an authorized risk decision may have a broader governance deficiency.

The audit function should retain evidence of significant escalations, management decisions, revised deadlines, and unresolved disagreements.

Escalation should be proportionate to the risk and should not be withheld merely because a finding is commercially sensitive or inconvenient to senior management.

## 71. Reporting AI Audit Results to Senior Management and the Board

Senior management and the board require an integrated view of AI governance effectiveness, significant exposures, remediation progress, and decisions requiring oversight.

The audit report should translate detailed findings into information that supports governance decisions without weakening the underlying evidence or obscuring important limitations.

A management or board-level summary should address:

- Overall audit conclusion and assurance limitations.
- Most significant findings and their potential consequences.
- Material legal, regulatory, security, privacy, and operational exposures.
- Weaknesses affecting multiple AI systems or business units.
- Findings that remain overdue or subject to unresolved risk acceptance.
- Progress against agreed remediation commitments.
- Resource or investment decisions required.
- Significant changes in the organization's AI risk profile.
- Matters requiring escalation or further independent assurance.

Where several findings arise from the same underlying weakness, the report should identify the cross-cutting issue rather than presenting unrelated lists of individual exceptions.

For example, recurring failures in model approval, version control, and deployment evidence may indicate a broader weakness in AI lifecycle governance.

Board reporting should distinguish current audit findings from management-reported metrics and risk assessments. Management information may be useful, but it should not be presented as independently verified audit evidence unless the audit work supports that characterization.

The report should also explain material scope limitations. A favorable conclusion based on a limited sample of selected systems should not be interpreted as assurance over the entire AI portfolio.

Significant changes in risk exposure should be communicated promptly through established governance channels rather than waiting for the next scheduled audit committee meeting.

The objective is to enable informed oversight, clear accountability, and timely decisions about material AI risks.

## 72. Coordinating AI Audit Activities with Other Assurance Functions

Organizations may obtain AI-related assurance from internal audit, compliance monitoring, cybersecurity testing, privacy reviews, risk management, external auditors, certification bodies, and independent technical assessors.

Coordination can improve coverage and reduce unnecessary duplication, but it must not weaken auditor independence or create unsupported reliance on other parties' conclusions.

The audit lead should identify assurance activities relevant to the engagement and assess whether their results can appropriately inform the audit.

Relevant considerations include:

- The other function's mandate and objectives.
- Its independence and objectivity.
- The competence of its personnel.
- The criteria and methodology used.
- The systems, versions, and periods covered.
- The reliability and completeness of its evidence.
- The testing performed and exceptions identified.
- The review and quality-assurance arrangements.
- Any limitations affecting its conclusions.

For example, a cybersecurity team may have completed penetration testing of an AI application. The audit team may use the results as relevant evidence, but it should establish whether the testing covered the necessary interfaces, threat scenarios, environment, and period.

Similarly, an ISO/IEC 42001 management-system certification does not automatically establish that every AI system operated by the organization complies with all applicable laws or is free from technical vulnerabilities.

The audit team should define the extent of reliance on other assurance work and perform additional procedures where necessary.

A coordinated assurance map can identify which risks are covered by each function, which evidence is available, and where material assurance gaps remain.

The organization should retain clear accountability for the conclusions issued by each assurance provider.

Coordination is successful when it improves coverage and evidence quality while preserving the independence and specific responsibilities of each assurance activity.

## 73. Integrating AI Audit Results into Enterprise Risk Management

AI audit findings should inform enterprise risk management when they reveal material weaknesses, changes in risk exposure, ineffective controls, or emerging threats.

The integration process helps ensure that audit results influence organizational decisions rather than remaining isolated within audit reports.

The audit function should identify findings with implications for:

- Enterprise risk registers.
- AI-specific risk registers.
- Information security risk management.
- Privacy and data protection risk assessments.
- Operational resilience and business continuity.
- Third-party and supply-chain risk.
- Regulatory compliance monitoring.
- Product and service risk assessments.
- Risk appetite and acceptance decisions.
- Investment and control improvement priorities.

For each material finding, the responsible management function should determine whether existing risk records remain accurate.

For example, an audit that identifies inadequate oversight of an AI agent may reveal that the organization's risk register understates the consequences of autonomous tool execution.

The risk owner may need to revise the risk scenario, likelihood assessment, impact analysis, treatment plan, and residual-risk rating.

The auditor should verify that significant risk implications have been communicated to the appropriate governance function. The audit function should not assume ownership of management's risk treatment decisions.

Risk aggregation is particularly important where a single weakness affects many AI systems. A deficient model registry or centralized identity control may create a common exposure across the entire AI portfolio.

The organization should also consider whether audit findings indicate that existing risk-assessment methods need improvement.

Repeated misclassification of AI systems, for example, may indicate that the classification methodology does not adequately account for intended use, affected stakeholders, autonomy, or potential consequences.

Integrating audit results into enterprise risk management supports consistent prioritization and helps management allocate resources to the most material exposures.

## 74. Using AI Audit Results to Improve the AI Governance Management System

AI audit findings can provide evidence about whether the organization's governance arrangements are appropriately designed, implemented, maintained, and improved.

Where an AI management system is established, audit results should be considered alongside risk assessments, performance monitoring, incident records, management reviews, and other assurance activities.

The improvement process should examine:

- Whether governance policies remain appropriate.
- Whether roles and decision authorities are clearly assigned.
- Whether the AI system inventory is complete.
- Whether risk assessments use suitable criteria.
- Whether controls address the identified risks.
- Whether performance and security monitoring are effective.
- Whether supplier oversight is adequate.
- Whether personnel possess the necessary competence.
- Whether incidents and audit findings produce meaningful corrective action.
- Whether management review identifies systemic weaknesses.
- Whether changes in technology or regulation require adjustments.

The organization should distinguish corrective action from broader continual improvement.

Corrective action addresses an identified nonconformity or deficiency and seeks to prevent recurrence.

Continual improvement seeks to enhance the management system's suitability, adequacy, or effectiveness, including where no formal nonconformity has been identified.

For example, correcting missing approval records may resolve a specific finding. Introducing automated approval enforcement, improved release traceability, and portfolio-wide monitoring may provide a broader improvement in AI lifecycle governance.

Where ISO/IEC 42001 is an applicable audit criterion, the organization should map relevant findings to the specific management-system requirements and evaluate corrective action within that context.

However, improvement claims should remain evidence-based. Updating a policy or completing a governance project does not, by itself, establish that the management system has become more effective.

The organization should define measurable indicators and review whether the changes produce the intended outcomes.

## 75. Measuring AI Audit Program Performance and Assurance Coverage

An AI audit program should be evaluated to determine whether it provides appropriate coverage of material AI risks and produces useful, timely, and defensible assurance.

Program-level measurement differs from evaluating an individual audit. It considers the quality, consistency, coverage, and impact of the audit portfolio over time.

Useful indicators may include:

| Indicator                           | What it helps evaluate                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk-based audit coverage           | Whether high-risk AI systems and processes receive appropriate audit attention         |
| Planned versus completed audits     | Whether the audit plan is being delivered                                              |
| High-risk findings overdue          | Whether significant deficiencies remain unresolved                                     |
| Remediation validation rate         | Whether completed corrective actions receive independent verification                  |
| Repeat finding rate                 | Whether similar weaknesses recur after remediation                                     |
| Scope limitation frequency          | Whether evidence access or auditability repeatedly constrains assurance                |
| Finding escalation timeliness       | Whether material issues are communicated promptly                                      |
| Audit report turnaround time        | Whether results reach decision-makers within a useful period                           |
| Cross-functional assurance coverage | Whether significant AI risks have adequate assurance and identified gaps are addressed |
| Corrective action effectiveness     | Whether remediation reduces the underlying risk                                        |

Metrics should be interpreted in context.

A low number of findings does not necessarily demonstrate strong AI governance. It may reflect effective controls, but it may also result from limited audit coverage, inadequate testing, or weak evidence access.

Similarly, a high number of findings may reflect poor controls, but it may also indicate improved audit coverage and more effective detection.

The audit function should combine quantitative indicators with qualitative analysis of finding significance, risk coverage, and remediation outcomes.

Program reporting should identify trends across systems, business units, suppliers, and audit periods.

The audit function should also assess whether its own methods remain appropriate as AI technologies, organizational dependencies, and regulatory obligations change.

Performance metrics should support improvement of assurance quality rather than encourage artificial reductions in findings or premature closure of remediation actions.

## 76. Incorporating Emerging AI Risks into the Audit Universe

The AI audit universe is the collection of systems, processes, organizational activities, suppliers, and controls that may be subject to audit.

It should be maintained as a dynamic inventory because AI deployments, business uses, external dependencies, and threat conditions can change more rapidly than conventional audit cycles.

The audit function should periodically reassess the audit universe using information from:

- AI system inventories and approved use-case registers.
- New deployments and material model changes.
- Enterprise and AI risk assessments.
- Security incidents and threat intelligence.
- Privacy and regulatory compliance reviews.
- Procurement and supplier assessments.
- Business transformation initiatives.
- Internal audit findings and remediation records.
- Significant complaints or adverse outcomes.
- Changes in applicable laws, standards, or contractual requirements.
- Emerging technical capabilities, including agentic systems and increased automation.

The audit universe should distinguish the AI management system from the individual AI systems operating within it.

A management-system audit may examine governance, risk management, responsibilities, monitoring, and continual improvement. A system-specific audit may examine the architecture, data flows, model behavior, interfaces, and operational controls of a particular application.

Both perspectives are necessary where the organization operates a diverse AI portfolio.

Risk-based audit planning should determine which areas require detailed examination, which can be covered through thematic reviews, and where specialist assurance is necessary.

The audit function should avoid relying exclusively on management-provided inventories if there are credible reasons to believe that AI capabilities are deployed outside formal governance arrangements.

Where unauthorized or unregistered AI use is identified, the audit lead should assess its significance, establish whether it falls within the existing mandate, and follow the appropriate escalation process.

A regularly refreshed audit universe helps maintain assurance coverage as the organization's AI risk profile evolves.

## 77. Managing AI Audit Records, Confidentiality and Retention

AI audit records may contain sensitive business information, personal data, security configurations, model artifacts, source code, prompts, outputs, supplier information, and details of exploitable vulnerabilities.

The audit function should establish retention, access, confidentiality, and disposal arrangements that preserve the integrity and availability of the audit record while respecting applicable legal and contractual obligations.

Audit record management should address:

- Authorized access and role-based permissions.
- Secure storage and transmission.
- Evidence identifiers and traceability.
- Version control for workpapers and reports.
- Integrity protection for significant artifacts.
- Confidentiality classifications.
- Personal-data minimization and appropriate redaction.
- Retention periods and legal holds.
- Restrictions on external disclosure.
- Secure disposal when retention is no longer authorized.
- Preservation of records needed for regulatory or legal review.

The audit file should retain sufficient documentation to support the procedures performed, evidence evaluated, material judgments made, review decisions, and conclusions reached.

However, retaining every collected dataset or model artifact is not necessarily appropriate. The audit function should determine which evidence is necessary and whether controlled extracts, hashes, metadata, or references to approved repositories can meet the evidentiary requirements with less exposure.

For example, an audit may need to demonstrate that a model artifact examined during testing corresponded to a particular approved version. Retaining the artifact's identifier, relevant metadata, integrity hash, and authorized evidence reference may be sufficient for some objectives, while other engagements may require preservation of the artifact itself.

Retention decisions should be based on the engagement's requirements and the applicable evidence-handling policy.

Where records contain information relevant to an investigation or potential legal proceeding, the audit function should coordinate with appropriate legal, security, or forensic personnel.

The audit report and supporting workpapers should remain protected against unauthorized modification, disclosure, or deletion.

## 78. Evaluating the Limitations of AI-Assisted Auditing

AI tools can support audit activities by assisting with document analysis, control mapping, anomaly detection, evidence classification, and identification of potentially relevant records. However, using AI within the audit process introduces its own reliability, security, privacy, and accountability considerations.

The audit function should define the permitted uses of AI tools and establish controls appropriate to the sensitivity and significance of the work performed.

Potential use cases include:

- Extracting requirements from policies and standards.
- Comparing control descriptions across documents.
- Identifying potentially missing evidence.
- Grouping similar findings.
- Analyzing large volumes of audit logs.
- Identifying unusual patterns in transaction records.
- Supporting draft preparation and summarization.
- Assisting with risk-register analysis.

AI-generated results should not automatically be treated as verified audit evidence or final professional judgments.

The auditor should consider:

- Whether the tool is suitable for the task.
- Whether the input data is complete and accurate.
- Whether sensitive information is authorized for processing.
- Whether the output can be independently checked.
- Whether the tool introduces bias, omissions, or hallucinated details.
- Whether the processing method is reproducible.
- Whether relevant records and decisions are retained.
- Whether human review is proportionate to the risk.
- Whether the tool's limitations affect the audit conclusion.

For example, an AI tool may identify apparent inconsistencies between an AI governance policy and a control register. Those results can guide further examination, but the auditor must verify the underlying documents, determine whether the requirements are actually inconsistent, and assess the significance of any confirmed deficiency.

The audit function should also consider risks arising from external AI services, including unauthorized disclosure of confidential audit information and insufficient control over data retention.

For high-impact audit judgments, AI should support rather than replace accountable professional decision-making.

The use of AI within the audit process should itself be governed, documented, and subject to appropriate oversight.

## 79. Establishing an AI Audit Continuous Improvement Cycle

A mature AI audit function should use experience from completed engagements to improve its methodology, technical competence, evidence standards, risk coverage, and reporting quality.

Continuous improvement should be based on documented lessons and evidence of effectiveness rather than periodic policy updates alone.

The improvement cycle may include:

1. Plan: Identify audit methodology weaknesses, emerging AI risks, and capability gaps.
2. Execute: Apply revised procedures and evidence requirements to selected engagements.
3. Evaluate: Review audit quality, findings, scope limitations, and remediation outcomes.
4. Improve: Update methodology, training, tools, and quality-assurance arrangements.
5. Validate: Determine whether the changes improve consistency, coverage, and evidentiary quality.

Lessons learned may arise from:

- Repeated control deficiencies across audits.
- Findings that were difficult to substantiate.
- Inadequate access to supplier evidence.
- Weaknesses in sampling or testing approaches.
- Incorrect assumptions about AI system architecture.
- Significant changes in AI threats or technology.
- Regulatory developments.
- Internal quality reviews.
- External assessments of the audit function.
- Remediation that failed to address root causes.

The audit function should determine whether lessons apply only to a particular engagement or require broader changes to the audit methodology.

For example, repeated difficulties validating AI model versions may justify standardized evidence requirements covering model identifiers, deployment records, configuration versions, and approval history.

Likewise, recurring uncertainty about human oversight may justify more rigorous testing of actual review records, override mechanisms, escalation paths, and reviewer competence.

Methodology changes should be version-controlled, approved through the appropriate process, and communicated to relevant personnel.

The audit function should monitor whether the revised approach improves outcomes. A new checklist is not evidence of improvement unless it contributes to better audit coverage, more reliable conclusions, or more effective remediation.

A disciplined improvement cycle helps ensure that AI audit capability evolves alongside the systems and risks being examined.

## 80. Establishing the Final AI Audit Assurance and Governance Closure Framework

The final stage of an AI audit establishes that the engagement has been completed in accordance with its mandate, findings have been communicated, reporting requirements have been satisfied, and outstanding risks have been assigned appropriate accountability.

Audit completion does not necessarily mean that every identified deficiency has been remediated. The engagement may be formally reported and closed while findings remain open in the organization's remediation register.

The audit lead should confirm that the following activities have been completed:

- The approved audit procedures have been performed or deviations documented.
- Material risks have received appropriate coverage.
- Evidence has been evaluated for relevance, reliability, and sufficiency.
- Findings have been reviewed and supported by documented evidence.
- Root causes and severity ratings have been assessed where appropriate.
- Management responses and remediation commitments have been recorded.
- Significant disagreements and scope limitations have been addressed.
- Required supervisory and specialist reviews have been completed.
- The overall conclusion is consistent with the evidence obtained.
- The report has received the required approval.
- Results have been communicated to authorized recipients.
- Findings have been entered into the appropriate tracking process.
- Immediate risks have been escalated through the established channels.
- Audit records have been retained in accordance with applicable requirements.
- Lessons learned have been identified for future audit planning.

The engagement closure process should distinguish three outcomes.

Audit closure confirms that the engagement's reporting and documentation requirements have been completed.

Finding closure confirms that the relevant corrective action has been verified according to the established validation criteria, or otherwise handled under the organization's formally defined closure framework.

Risk closure or reassessment concerns the management of the underlying risk. A risk may be reduced, transferred, avoided, or formally accepted where permitted, but it should not be considered eliminated merely because an audit finding has been closed.

These outcomes should remain traceable to the applicable governance records.

The complete AI audit lifecycle can be represented as follows:

```
Audit mandate and authorization
              |
              v
Objectives, scope and criteria
              |
              v
AI risk assessment and audit planning
              |
              v
Control mapping and work program
              |
              v
Fieldwork and evidence collection
              |
              v
Testing and exception evaluation
              |
              v
Findings and assurance conclusions
              |
              v
Formal reporting and management response
              |
              v
Remediation tracking and escalation
              |
              v
Independent remediation validation
              |
              v
Finding closure or formal risk handling
              |
              v
Lessons learned and audit improvement
```

The audit function should retain a clear distinction between the auditor's independent assurance responsibilities and management's responsibility for operating AI systems, implementing controls, and accepting residual risks.

A defensible AI audit process connects the original mandate to the final conclusion through documented criteria, risk-based procedures, reliable evidence, transparent judgments, and controlled reporting.

The ultimate objective is not simply to produce an audit report. It is to provide credible assurance about the areas examined, expose material weaknesses, strengthen accountability, and support demonstrable improvement in AI governance and risk management.
