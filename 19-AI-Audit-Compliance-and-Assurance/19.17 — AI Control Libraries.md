# 19.17 — AI Control Libraries

## Part 1 — Control Library Foundations, Governance Architecture, and Control Design

### 1. Definition of an AI Control Library

An **AI control library** is a structured, governed collection of control objectives, control requirements, implementation expectations, ownership assignments, and assurance criteria designed to manage risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

It translates AI governance policies, risk-management requirements, security principles, privacy obligations, regulatory expectations, and organizational risk appetite into controls that can be assigned, implemented, tested, and monitored.

An AI control library provides a common reference point for determining what an organization expects its AI systems and associated business processes to do in order to satisfy defined governance requirements.

Examples of AI control objectives include:

- Ensuring that AI systems are registered, classified, and assigned accountable owners.
- Requiring risk assessments before systems are approved for production.
- Ensuring that model and system evaluations are completed against defined acceptance criteria.
- Restricting AI access to authorized data and enterprise resources.
- Establishing appropriate human oversight for consequential AI-supported decisions.
- Managing third-party AI providers and material technology dependencies.
- Monitoring AI systems for performance degradation, security events, and emerging risks.
- Ensuring that material changes trigger appropriate reassessment and approval.
- Maintaining evidence that required controls are implemented and operating effectively.
- Ensuring that identified control deficiencies result in corrective action.

The library should distinguish between a control objective and the control activities used to achieve it.

A **control objective** describes the outcome that must be achieved. A **control** specifies the requirement or activity intended to achieve that outcome. A **control procedure** explains how the activity is performed. **Control evidence** demonstrates whether the activity occurred and, where applicable, whether it operated effectively.

For example:

| Element             | Example                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Control objective   | AI systems must be authorized before production deployment                                                                            |
| Control requirement | Production deployment requires documented approval from the designated authority                                                      |
| Control procedure   | The system owner submits the system card, risk assessment, evaluation results, and required approvals through the deployment workflow |
| Control evidence    | Approval record, completed assessment references, deployment authorization, and associated workflow history                           |
| Assurance criterion | A reviewer can verify that deployment approval preceded production release and covered the deployed system version                    |

The AI control library provides the standardized requirements against which individual AI systems and organizational processes can be assessed.

### 2. Purpose of an AI Control Library in Governance

The primary purpose of an AI control library is to make AI governance requirements operational, consistent, measurable, and auditable.

An organization may have comprehensive AI policies and risk-management principles but still struggle to demonstrate how those principles are implemented across individual systems. Policies often state what the organization intends to achieve, while control libraries define the requirements that must be implemented to achieve those objectives.

A governed control library supports several functions.

**2.1 Standardizing control expectations**

Different business units may deploy similar AI systems using different technologies and operating models. A common library establishes consistent control objectives while allowing implementation methods to vary where justified.

**2.2 Translating risk into action**

AI risk assessments identify potential adverse outcomes. The control library provides reusable requirements for addressing those risks, such as access restrictions, evaluation requirements, human approval, monitoring, and change management.

**2.3 Supporting accountability**

Each applicable control can be assigned to an accountable owner responsible for implementation, operation, evidence production, and remediation of deficiencies.

**2.4 Enabling control testing**

Standardized control statements and test criteria allow risk teams, compliance functions, and internal auditors to evaluate whether controls are appropriately designed and operating as intended.

**2.5 Supporting regulatory and framework alignment**

The library can map organizational controls to relevant laws, regulations, standards, contractual requirements, and internal policies. This helps demonstrate how common controls support multiple obligations.

**2.6 Improving enterprise reporting**

Consistent control identifiers, status definitions, and evidence requirements enable management to compare control implementation across systems and business units.

**2.7 Reducing duplicated effort**

Where several systems require equivalent safeguards, the organization can reuse a common control definition rather than creating unrelated controls for each project.

The library should not become a static checklist. It must evolve as the organization's AI portfolio, risk exposure, technology architecture, and applicable obligations change.

A control that was appropriate for a limited internal text-generation assistant may not be sufficient when the system gains access to confidential enterprise records or is permitted to execute autonomous actions.

### 3. AI Control Library Versus Control Framework

An AI control library and a control framework are related but distinct.

A **control framework** establishes the overall structure for organizing, governing, and evaluating controls. It may define control domains, governance principles, responsibilities, assessment approaches, and relationships to external standards.

An **AI control library** contains the specific control requirements maintained within that structure.

For example, an organization may adopt a risk-based AI governance framework that includes governance, risk management, data management, security, transparency, human oversight, and monitoring domains. Its control library translates these domains into individual requirements with defined ownership and evidence expectations.

| Dimension                 | Control framework                                               | AI control library                                                              |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary purpose           | Establishes the structure and principles for control governance | Defines individual control requirements                                         |
| Typical content           | Domains, objectives, governance model, assessment methodology   | Control statements, identifiers, owners, applicability, evidence, test criteria |
| Level of detail           | Strategic and structural                                        | Operational and testable                                                        |
| Relationship to risk      | Defines how risk and controls are organized                     | Specifies controls used to address identified risks                             |
| Relationship to standards | Establishes the mapping approach                                | Contains mappings from individual controls to applicable requirements           |
| Change management         | Changes to the overall governance structure                     | Changes to control statements, implementation expectations, or test criteria    |
| Assurance                 | Defines the overall assurance approach                          | Provides the control-level basis for testing and reporting                      |

An organization may use several frameworks to inform one consolidated AI control library. For example, its control architecture may draw on AI risk-management guidance, information-security standards, privacy requirements, and AI-specific management-system requirements.

The objective is not to create a separate, disconnected control for every statement found in every external framework. It is to establish a coherent set of organizational controls that addresses applicable requirements without unnecessary duplication.

### 4. AI Control Library Versus AI Risk Register

The AI risk register and the AI control library serve different purposes and should remain connected.

The **AI risk register** records the risks identified by the organization, including their causes, potential consequences, assessment results, treatment decisions, and accountable risk owners.

The **AI control library** defines the reusable control requirements that may be used to manage those risks.

A risk register is therefore primarily risk-oriented, while a control library is primarily requirement-oriented.

For example, an AI-enabled recruitment system may have a documented risk that automated recommendations could contribute to discriminatory outcomes. The risk register records the specific risk, its assessment, the affected process, the treatment decision, and the residual-risk position.

The control library may contain reusable controls addressing:

- Evaluation for relevant performance disparities.
- Human review of consequential recommendations.
- Documentation of evaluation scope and limitations.
- Monitoring of material changes in performance.
- Escalation of significant evaluation failures.
- Periodic reassessment of the system's suitability.

The risk owner determines which controls are appropriate for the identified risk and whether additional treatment is necessary.

| Governance artifact           | Primary question                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI risk register              | What could go wrong, and how is the risk being managed?                            |
| AI control library            | What control requirements are available to manage relevant risks?                  |
| System card                   | How does the particular system operate, and which controls and restrictions apply? |
| Control implementation record | How has a control been implemented for a particular system or process?             |
| Control evidence repository   | What records demonstrate implementation or operation?                              |
| Control testing workpaper     | What did the reviewer test, and what conclusion was reached?                       |
| Corrective-action register    | What must be corrected when a control deficiency is identified?                    |

A control library should not assume that every control applies to every AI system. Applicability must be determined using the organization's defined criteria, the system's risk profile, and relevant legal or contractual obligations.

Likewise, the existence of a suitable control in the library does not prove that the risk has been adequately treated. The control must be appropriately selected, implemented, and evaluated.

### 5. Establishing the Scope of an AI Control Library

The scope of an AI control library should reflect the organization's AI governance objectives and the range of systems, processes, and dependencies it must control.

The library may cover controls applicable to:

- AI strategy and governance.
- AI system identification and inventory management.
- Risk classification and assessment.
- Data acquisition, preparation, and governance.
- Model development and selection.
- Model and system evaluation.
- AI application architecture.
- Identity and access management.
- Information security and privacy.
- Third-party and supply-chain management.
- Human oversight and accountability.
- Generative AI and large language model security.
- Agentic AI and tool-use restrictions.
- AI deployment and change management.
- Operational monitoring and incident response.
- AI-specific audit and assurance.
- Documentation, recordkeeping, and evidence retention.
- Corrective action and management review.
- System retirement and decommissioning.

The organization should determine whether controls apply to the entire AI lifecycle, particular lifecycle stages, specific system types, or supporting organizational processes.

For example, a control requiring approval before training a model on a new dataset may apply to internally developed or fine-tuned models but may not apply in the same way to an organization using a provider-hosted model exclusively through an inference API.

However, the same deployment may still require controls governing the data submitted during inference, provider retention, access restrictions, and output handling.

The control library should therefore describe applicability conditions rather than rely on broad labels such as *“AI control”* or *“security control.”*

Scope should also include relevant third-party dependencies and AI-enabled features embedded in conventional enterprise applications. An organization may use AI without operating a separately identifiable AI platform, so the library's scope should align with the organization's AI identification and inventory methodology.

### 6. Designing the AI Control Domain Structure

Control domains organize related requirements into a structure that supports ownership, implementation, testing, and reporting.

A well-designed domain structure should be sufficiently comprehensive to cover material AI risks while avoiding unnecessary fragmentation or duplication.

An illustrative AI control domain model is shown below.

| Domain                                | Control objective                                   | Illustrative control focus                                      |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance and accountability      | Establish oversight and decision authority          | Policies, ownership, approval, escalation                       |
| AI inventory and classification       | Maintain visibility of AI systems                   | Registration, identification, classification, lifecycle status  |
| AI risk management                    | Identify and manage AI-related risks                | Risk assessment, treatment, acceptance, reassessment            |
| Data governance                       | Manage data quality, provenance, and authorized use | Data lineage, quality, access, retention                        |
| Model governance                      | Maintain control over model selection and changes   | Model identification, validation, version management            |
| AI evaluation and testing             | Establish evidence of suitability                   | Acceptance criteria, robustness, performance, failure testing   |
| AI security architecture              | Protect systems, data, and integrations             | Access control, isolation, secure interfaces, threat mitigation |
| Privacy and data protection           | Manage privacy risks and obligations                | Data minimization, processing boundaries, retention             |
| Transparency and documentation        | Maintain understandable and traceable records       | System cards, limitations, documentation quality                |
| Human oversight                       | Define meaningful human involvement                 | Review authority, escalation, override, accountability          |
| Third-party and supply-chain security | Govern external dependencies                        | Due diligence, contracts, provider changes, assurance           |
| Generative AI and LLM security        | Manage generation-specific threats                  | Prompt injection, data leakage, output handling                 |
| Agentic AI security                   | Constrain autonomous behavior                       | Tool permissions, approval gates, action limits                 |
| Monitoring and incident management    | Detect and respond to operational issues            | Telemetry, alerts, incident escalation, recovery                |
| Change and lifecycle management       | Maintain control as systems evolve                  | Change assessment, reapproval, retirement                       |
| AI audit and assurance                | Verify control design and operation                 | Testing, evidence, findings, independent review                 |
| Corrective action and improvement     | Resolve deficiencies                                | Remediation, validation, management review                      |

This structure is illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's existing governance architecture.

An organization with a mature information-security control framework may retain common enterprise domains for identity, vulnerability management, incident response, and supplier risk, while introducing AI-specific controls where conventional controls do not fully address AI-related risks.

For example, the organization may already have a general access-control domain. It may not need a separate duplicate domain for every AI access requirement, but it may need additional control statements addressing retrieval authorization, agent tool permissions, model endpoint access, or the handling of model-generated instructions.

The domain structure should make ownership and reporting clear without creating artificial boundaries between controls that operate together.

### 7. Defining a Standard Control Record

Every control in the library should have a standardized record containing enough information to support interpretation, implementation, applicability decisions, testing, and ongoing maintenance.

A control statement that is too vague may be impossible to test consistently. A statement that prescribes unnecessary implementation detail may become difficult to maintain across different architectures.

A practical control record should include the following fields.

| Field                   | Purpose                                                                      |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Control ID              | Provides a unique, stable identifier                                         |
| Control title           | Summarizes the control requirement                                           |
| Control statement       | Defines the required activity or condition                                   |
| Control objective       | Explains the intended governance outcome                                     |
| Domain                  | Identifies the control's primary governance area                             |
| Risk linkage            | Identifies the risks the control is intended to address                      |
| Applicability criteria  | Defines when the control applies                                             |
| Control type            | Identifies whether it is preventive, detective, corrective, or a combination |
| Control nature          | Identifies whether it is primarily manual, automated, or hybrid              |
| Control owner           | Identifies the role accountable for the control                              |
| Implementation guidance | Explains acceptable ways to implement the requirement                        |
| Frequency or trigger    | Defines when the control operates                                            |
| Evidence requirements   | Identifies records needed to demonstrate operation                           |
| Test procedure          | Describes how a reviewer evaluates the control                               |
| Effectiveness criteria  | Defines what acceptable operation looks like                                 |
| Framework mappings      | Links the control to relevant external and internal requirements             |
| Status                  | Records lifecycle or implementation status                                   |
| Version history         | Records material changes to the control definition                           |

Not every field must appear in every implementation interface. However, the underlying governance record should preserve the information needed to manage the control.

The control ID should remain stable when the control is edited without changing its fundamental purpose. Where a material change creates a substantially different control objective, the organization should consider creating a new identifier and preserving the historical relationship.

Stable identifiers are essential for reliable risk-register references, control testing, management reporting, and framework mappings.

### 8. Writing Clear and Testable Control Statements

A control statement should define an observable requirement that can be evaluated against evidence.

Weak control statements often use broad language such as:

- AI systems should be secure.
- Models should be tested appropriately.
- Data should be handled responsibly.
- Human oversight should be maintained.
- Risks should be managed.

These statements express desirable outcomes but do not establish what must be done, who is responsible, when the activity occurs, or what evidence demonstrates compliance.

A stronger control statement defines a specific requirement while allowing reasonable implementation flexibility.

For example:

**Weak statement:** AI systems should be tested before deployment.

**Stronger statement:** Before production deployment, the designated system owner must ensure that the AI system has been evaluated against documented acceptance criteria appropriate to its intended purpose and risk classification. Evaluation results, material limitations, unresolved findings, and the deployment decision must be recorded and retained.

The stronger statement establishes:

- A defined trigger: before production deployment.
- An accountable role: the designated system owner.
- A required activity: evaluation against documented criteria.
- A basis for proportionality: intended purpose and risk classification.
- Required records: results, limitations, findings, and decision.
- A reviewable outcome: evidence can be examined to determine whether the requirement was satisfied.

Control statements should generally avoid embedding every technical implementation detail in the normative requirement. Detailed implementation guidance can be maintained separately so that the control remains usable across different platforms.

A good control statement should answer four questions:

1. What must happen?
2. Under which conditions or at what point in the lifecycle must it happen?
3. Who is accountable for ensuring it happens?
4. What evidence demonstrates that it happened?

The organization should also distinguish mandatory language from guidance. Terms such as *must*, *shall*, *should*, and *may* should be defined consistently within the control library's documentation standard.

### 9. Distinguishing Control Objectives, Requirements, Procedures, and Evidence

Control governance becomes more reliable when the library distinguishes the intended outcome from the requirement, implementation activity, and evidence used to verify it.

These elements are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**Control objective:** Defines the outcome the organization intends to achieve.

**Control requirement:** States the condition or activity that must exist to achieve the objective.

**Control procedure:** Describes how the control is implemented or operated.

**Control evidence:** Provides a record demonstrating implementation or operation.

**Test procedure:** Describes how a reviewer evaluates the control.

**Test result:** Records the outcome of that evaluation.

Consider a control objective requiring that only authorized users can retrieve sensitive information through an AI application.

| Element        | Example                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Objective      | Prevent unauthorized retrieval of sensitive information                                                                                          |
| Requirement    | Retrieval access must enforce the requesting user's authorized data permissions                                                                  |
| Procedure      | The application passes the authenticated user context to the retrieval service, which checks document-level permissions before returning content |
| Evidence       | Access-control configuration, test results, access logs, and documented exception handling                                                       |
| Test procedure | Select representative users with different permissions and verify that each can retrieve only authorized documents                               |
| Test result    | Record the tested accounts, documents, expected behavior, observed results, exceptions, and conclusion                                           |

The control objective remains stable even if the organization changes its retrieval technology.

The procedure may change when the architecture changes, and the evidence may change when the implementation changes. The control library should preserve the requirement while ensuring that the implementation remains appropriate.

This separation supports reuse, reduces unnecessary duplication, and improves the quality of control testing.

### 10. Classifying Controls by Function

Control libraries commonly classify controls according to their function in managing risk.

The principal categories are preventive, detective, and corrective controls. Some controls perform more than one function.

**Preventive controls** are designed to prevent an undesirable event or condition from occurring.

Examples include:

- Blocking unauthorized access to model endpoints.
- Requiring approval before production deployment.
- Enforcing restrictions on sensitive agent actions.
- Preventing prohibited data categories from entering a processing workflow.
- Restricting deployment to approved model versions.

**Detective controls** identify events, conditions, or deviations that may indicate a failure or emerging risk.

Examples include:

- Monitoring for unusual model endpoint activity.
- Detecting unexpected changes in output quality.
- Alerting on unauthorized tool invocation attempts.
- Reviewing production performance and error metrics.
- Identifying expired approvals or overdue system reviews.

**Corrective controls** support remediation, containment, recovery, or prevention of recurrence after a deficiency or event has been identified.

Examples include:

- Suspending an affected AI system.
- Revoking excessive permissions.
- Rolling back an unsafe configuration change.
- Correcting inaccurate documentation.
- Implementing a remediation plan after an evaluation failure.

A single governance process may include all three categories.

For example, an AI agent may have preventive restrictions on permitted actions, detective monitoring for attempted boundary violations, and corrective procedures for suspending the agent and restoring a known-good configuration after a material incident.

Control classification should reflect the control's actual purpose and behavior rather than its name.

A monitoring control does not prevent every failure simply because it may identify the problem quickly. Likewise, an approval requirement does not guarantee that an approved system is safe if the approval process lacks sufficient evidence or authority.

The library should define the primary control function and, where useful, record secondary functions.

### 11. Classifying Controls by Implementation Method

Controls may also be classified according to how they are implemented and operated.

**Manual controls** depend primarily on human activity, judgment, or approval.

Examples include reviewing an AI risk assessment, approving a system card, or evaluating whether a proposed use case requires additional governance review.

**Automated controls** execute defined logic through technical mechanisms without requiring a person to perform each individual control action.

Examples include blocking unauthorized API requests, enforcing mandatory deployment checks, or generating alerts when defined thresholds are exceeded.

**Hybrid controls** combine automated processing with human judgment or authorization.

Examples include an automated evaluation pipeline that produces test results for a human approval authority, or monitoring that detects anomalies and routes them to an incident responder.

The implementation method affects how the control should be tested.

Manual controls may require inspection of completed records, review of decision criteria, interviews, and sampling of approvals.

Automated controls may require configuration inspection, testing of technical behavior, review of logs, and verification that the deployed configuration matches the approved configuration.

Hybrid controls require testing both the technical mechanism and the human decision process.

For example, an automated system may flag potentially unsafe AI outputs, but the overall control may depend on whether the alert reaches an accountable reviewer and whether required action is taken.

The organization should not assume that automated controls are inherently more effective than manual controls. Effectiveness depends on design, configuration, coverage, reliability, monitoring, and the conditions under which the control operates.

### 12. Applying Control Design to AI-Specific Risks

AI control libraries should incorporate conventional enterprise controls where they remain appropriate and add AI-specific requirements where the technology introduces distinct risks or changes existing risk conditions.

Conventional controls remain important for identity management, secure configuration, vulnerability management, data protection, supplier oversight, incident response, and change management.

However, AI systems may require additional control requirements because of probabilistic outputs, prompt-based interaction, retrieval behavior, model updates, generated content, or autonomous tool execution.

Examples include:

| AI-specific risk              | Potential control objective                  | Illustrative control requirement                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unsupported generated claims  | Reduce reliance on inaccurate outputs        | Systems used for defined consequential workflows must be evaluated against documented factuality or task-specific acceptance criteria, with required human review where applicable |
| Prompt injection              | Limit the effect of malicious instructions   | Untrusted inputs and retrieved content must not independently override enforced authorization boundaries or approved tool permissions                                              |
| Unauthorized retrieval        | Prevent disclosure of restricted information | Data access must be enforced by the applicable authorization layer rather than relying solely on model instructions                                                                |
| Excessive agent autonomy      | Restrict actions to approved boundaries      | Agent tools and permissions must be limited to authorized actions, with approval requirements for designated sensitive operations                                                  |
| Model-version drift           | Maintain control over changes in behavior    | Material model or provider changes must be assessed to determine whether evaluation, documentation updates, or renewed approval are required                                       |
| Inadequate AI evaluation      | Establish evidence of suitability            | Systems must be evaluated against acceptance criteria appropriate to their intended purpose and risk before the required deployment authorization                                  |
| Unclear human accountability  | Preserve appropriate decision authority      | The organization must document which AI-supported decisions require human review and who has authority to approve, reject, or override the result                                  |
| Inadequate incident detection | Identify material operational failures       | AI systems must have monitoring and escalation arrangements proportionate to their risk and operating context                                                                      |

These examples illustrate possible control requirements rather than universal requirements for every AI system. Applicability and implementation should be determined through the organization's governance methodology and relevant obligations.

The control library should also identify dependencies between controls. For example, restrictions on agent actions may depend on identity management, authorization enforcement, secure tool integration, monitoring, and incident response.

A control library that treats each requirement as independent may overlook the combined conditions necessary for effective risk management.

### 13. Defining Control Applicability Criteria

Not every control applies to every AI system. The organization should define objective criteria for determining applicability and record the rationale for important applicability decisions.

Applicability may depend on:

- System lifecycle stage.
- Intended purpose.
- Risk classification.
- Data sensitivity.
- Processing of personal information.
- Use of third-party models.
- Degree of automation.
- Access to enterprise systems.
- Availability of autonomous tools.
- Deployment environment.
- Potential consequences of failure.
- Applicable legal, regulatory, or contractual obligations.

For example, a control requiring approval for autonomous financial transactions may not apply to an AI assistant that only generates internal summaries and has no transaction capability.

However, the organization should verify that the system genuinely lacks the relevant capability. If the assistant can invoke a connected tool that submits transactions, the applicability decision must reflect that capability.

A control may also be applicable only during a particular lifecycle stage. A training-data governance control may apply during model development or fine-tuning, while a production monitoring control applies during operational use.

The library should define how conditional applicability is handled.

An applicability record should include:

1. Control identifier.
2. Applicability decision.
3. Rationale.
4. Relevant system attributes or risk factors.
5. Decision owner, where required.
6. Supporting reference, where appropriate.
7. Review trigger or expiry condition, if applicable.

The organization should distinguish between a control being not applicable and a control being applicable but not implemented.

**Not applicable** means the control requirement does not apply to the system under the defined criteria.

**Not implemented** means the requirement applies but has not been implemented.

**Partially implemented** means only part of the requirement is satisfied.

**Implementation unknown** means the organization lacks sufficient evidence to determine the actual state.

These states must not be treated as equivalent because they have different implications for risk assessment and assurance.

### 14. Managing Control Exceptions and Risk Acceptance

An AI control library should define how deviations from applicable control requirements are requested, assessed, approved, recorded, and reviewed.

Exceptions may arise because of technical limitations, legacy architecture, provider restrictions, operational constraints, or temporary remediation work.

An exception should not be used as an informal mechanism for bypassing mandatory requirements or concealing an unimplemented control.

A governed exception record should identify:

- The control requirement affected.
- The system or process in scope.
- The reason the requirement cannot currently be satisfied.
- The associated risk.
- Available compensating controls.
- The accountable control owner.
- The risk owner.
- The approving authority.
- The decision date.
- The expiry or review date.
- Required remediation actions.
- Evidence supporting closure.

The organization should distinguish between an exception to an internal control requirement and acceptance of the risk associated with the deviation.

A control exception documents an authorized departure from a control requirement where policy permits such a departure. Risk acceptance documents an authorized decision to tolerate the remaining exposure within defined authority and risk-appetite boundaries.

One decision does not automatically substitute for the other.

For example, a provider may not support a particular logging capability. The organization may document the limitation, assess the resulting loss of visibility, introduce compensating monitoring measures, and determine whether the residual risk can be accepted.

If the missing capability prevents compliance with a mandatory obligation or makes the system unsuitable for its intended purpose, internal exception approval cannot remove that underlying obligation.

Exceptions should be monitored for expiry, repeated renewal, and unresolved remediation. A high volume of similar exceptions may indicate that the control design is unrealistic, the technology architecture is inadequate, or the organization has not allocated sufficient resources to implement the required safeguards.

### 15. Part 1 Core Principle

An AI control library translates governance objectives and risk-management requirements into standardized, accountable, and testable controls. Its effectiveness depends on clear control statements, stable identifiers, defined ownership, explicit applicability criteria, evidence requirements, and consistent testing expectations.

The library should distinguish control objectives, requirements, procedures, evidence, and test results while maintaining traceability to AI risks and applicable obligations.

A control's presence in the library does not prove that it has been implemented or that it is effective. Assurance requires evidence that the applicable control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating as intended. The control library becomes a reliable governance mechanism when it connects organizational requirements to the actual controls applied to AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

## Part 2 — AI Control Library Mapping, Implementation, and Operationalization

### 16. Mapping AI Controls to Risks and Governance Requirements

An AI control library becomes operationally useful when each control can be traced to the risks it addresses and the governance requirements it supports. Without this traceability, organizations may maintain extensive control inventories but remain unable to demonstrate whether their controls address material AI risks.

Control mapping establishes relationships between the following elements:

- AI governance objectives.
- Internal policies and standards.
- AI risk scenarios and treatment decisions.
- Applicable legal and regulatory obligations.
- External frameworks and standards.
- Control requirements.
- System-level implementations.
- Assurance evidence and testing results.

A control should not be mapped to a risk merely because the terminology appears similar. The organization should establish a defensible relationship between the risk scenario, the control's intended effect, and the evidence required to determine whether the control is effective.

For example, consider an AI assistant that retrieves confidential information from enterprise repositories.

The associated risk may be unauthorized disclosure caused by insufficient enforcement of document-level permissions. A control requiring retrieval authorization checks may directly address this risk. A general requirement to maintain an AI policy may support the broader governance environment, but it does not directly prevent unauthorized retrieval.

The organization should distinguish direct risk treatment from supporting governance controls.

A practical mapping structure includes:

| Mapping element          | Description                                                                 |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID                  | Reference to the relevant risk register entry                               |
| Control ID               | Reference to the control library record                                     |
| Control objective        | The outcome the control is intended to achieve                              |
| Mapping rationale        | Explanation of how the control addresses the risk                           |
| Treatment relationship   | Preventive, detective, corrective, or supporting                            |
| Implementation reference | Record identifying where the control is implemented                         |
| Evidence reference       | Link to relevant implementation or operating evidence                       |
| Residual-risk relevance  | Explanation of how the control contributes to the remaining risk assessment |

The mapping should support bidirectional traceability. Risk owners should be able to identify the controls supporting risk treatment, while control owners should understand the risks and obligations their controls are intended to address.

This is particularly important when a control is changed or retired. The organization must be able to identify which risks, systems, and compliance requirements may be affected by the change.

### 17. Mapping Controls to External Frameworks and Standards

Organizations often need to demonstrate alignment with multiple frameworks, standards, regulations, contractual requirements, and internal policies.

Examples may include ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, privacy requirements, sector-specific regulations, and applicable AI legislation.

The organization should maintain a structured mapping between its internal control library and the relevant external requirements.

The preferred approach is generally to maintain a common internal control set and map external requirements to it, rather than creating a separate operational control for every external requirement.

For example, several external requirements may concern accountability, documented responsibilities, risk assessment, monitoring, or continual improvement. A single well-designed internal control may support multiple requirements if its scope and implementation genuinely satisfy them.

However, a shared mapping must not conceal important differences between obligations.

One framework may require an organization to establish a process, while another may impose a specific documentation, notification, or assessment obligation. The organization must preserve those distinctions when determining whether its control is sufficient.

A framework mapping record should include:

- Internal control identifier.
- External framework or regulatory source.
- Requirement identifier.
- Requirement description or controlled reference.
- Mapping rationale.
- Coverage assessment.
- Identified limitations or gaps.
- Applicable scope.
- Mapping owner or reviewer.
- Review date and source version.

Coverage can be classified using defined categories such as full, partial, not covered, or not assessed.

**Full coverage** indicates that the internal control and its implementation address the mapped requirement within the relevant scope.

**Partial coverage** indicates that the control addresses only part of the requirement or depends on additional controls or activities.

**Not covered** indicates that the current control set does not adequately address the requirement.

**Not assessed** indicates that sufficient analysis has not been completed to determine coverage.

These classifications should be supported by documented analysis rather than subjective confidence.

A control mapping is not, by itself, proof of regulatory compliance or certification. Compliance conclusions depend on the applicable requirement, the organization's circumstances, the actual implementation, and the evidence available.

### 18. Building a Requirements-to-Controls Traceability Matrix

A requirements-to-controls traceability matrix provides a consolidated view of how external and internal requirements connect to the organization's control environment.

The matrix is particularly valuable during compliance assessments, AI management-system audits, internal audits, and regulatory reviews.

An illustrative matrix is shown below.

| Requirement area             | Internal control                                                            | Implementation evidence                                         | Assurance activity                                                  |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance accountability | GOV-001: Assign accountable AI system ownership                             | Approved ownership records and governance decisions             | Verify ownership for sampled systems                                |
| AI risk assessment           | RSK-004: Complete risk assessment before designated approval gates          | Risk assessment, treatment plan, approval record                | Inspect completed assessments and approval timing                   |

A traceability matrix should be maintained as a controlled governance artifact rather than as an informal spreadsheet that becomes disconnected from the risk register, control library, and evidence repository.

Where feasible, the organization should use stable identifiers and controlled references to reduce the risk of inconsistent records.

Changes to an external requirement should trigger an assessment of affected mappings, controls, systems, and assurance procedures.

### 19. Establishing Control Ownership and Accountability

Every control should have an identifiable owner who is accountable for ensuring that the control is appropriately designed, implemented, maintained, and supported by evidence.

Control ownership should be distinguished from system ownership, risk ownership, control operation, and independent assurance.

These responsibilities may be assigned to different roles because a single person or team should not automatically be assumed to possess all the required authority and expertise.

A practical accountability model includes the following roles.

**Control owner:** Accountable for the control's design, implementation expectations, performance, evidence, and remediation coordination.

**Control operator:** Performs the activity or operates the mechanism through which the control functions.

**AI system owner:** Accountable for the system's business use, lifecycle governance, and compliance with applicable organizational requirements.

**Risk owner:** Accountable for decisions concerning a particular risk, including treatment and residual-risk acceptance within delegated authority.

**AI governance function:** Maintains the governance methodology, control standards, applicability rules, and oversight processes.

**Compliance function:** Interprets relevant compliance obligations and supports mapping and compliance assessments.

**Security and privacy functions:** Provide specialist oversight for information security, privacy, and related technical or legal requirements.

**Internal audit or independent assurance:** Provides independent evaluation of control design and operation within its mandate.

For example, the information-security team may own a control requiring privileged access to AI infrastructure to be restricted. The platform engineering team may operate the technical access mechanism. The AI system owner remains accountable for the system's approved use, while the risk owner is responsible for decisions concerning residual risk.

Internal audit may independently test the control without becoming responsible for its implementation.

A responsibility assignment matrix can clarify the division of duties.

| Activity                         | Control owner                         | Control operator                    | AI system owner                  | Risk owner                             | Independent assurance              |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Define control requirements      | Accountable                           | Consulted                           | Consulted                        | Consulted                              | Informed as appropriate            |
| Implement the control            | Accountable for oversight             | Responsible for execution           | Supports system integration      | Consulted                              | Independent                        |
| Maintain control evidence        | Accountable                           | Responsible for operational records | Provides system records          | Informed as appropriate                | Reviews evidence                   |
| Assess control deficiencies      | Accountable for control assessment    | Provides operational facts          | Assesses system impact           | Assesses risk implications             | Independently evaluates            |
| Approve residual-risk acceptance | Provides control information          | Consulted                           | Consulted                        | Accountable within delegated authority | Independent                        |
| Validate corrective action       | Accountable for control effectiveness | Provides implementation evidence    | Confirms system-level completion | Reviews risk implications              | Independently tests where required |

The organization should adapt this model to its governance structure and formally define decision rights, escalation paths, and separation-of-duty requirements.

Assigning a control owner without granting sufficient authority, resources, or access to evidence creates nominal accountability rather than effective governance.

### 20. Defining Control Implementation Requirements

A control statement defines what must be achieved. Implementation requirements explain the minimum conditions necessary to achieve that outcome in the relevant operating context.

Implementation requirements should be detailed enough to support consistent execution without unnecessarily prescribing a single technology or architecture.

For example, a control requiring authorized access to AI resources may be implemented through different identity platforms, API gateways, cloud-native mechanisms, or application-level authorization services.

The control requirement should focus on the required authorization outcome. Implementation guidance should describe acceptable approaches, dependencies, and conditions under which additional safeguards are necessary.

Implementation requirements may address:

- Required technical or procedural capabilities.
- Responsible operational roles.
- Preconditions and dependencies.
- Applicable system components.
- Required configuration or process settings.
- Integration with enterprise controls.
- Evidence generation and retention.
- Failure handling and exception processing.
- Testing and acceptance criteria.
- Maintenance and review triggers.

The organization should distinguish mandatory implementation requirements from recommended practices.

Mandatory requirements define conditions that must be satisfied for the control to be considered implemented. Recommended practices provide additional guidance that may improve effectiveness but are not automatically mandatory.

Where an implementation alternative is permitted, the organization should define how equivalence is assessed.

For instance, a system may use a different technical mechanism to enforce data-access restrictions. The alternative should be evaluated against the same security objective, including authorization enforcement, bypass resistance, logging, and behavior under failure conditions.

The existence of an alternative implementation does not automatically justify reduced control coverage.

### 21. Establishing Evidence Requirements at Control Design Time

Evidence requirements should be defined when a control is designed rather than added only when an audit is scheduled.

A control that lacks clear evidence expectations may be implemented inconsistently, and the organization may be unable to demonstrate whether the control operated effectively during the relevant period.

Evidence should be sufficient to establish the control's operation, timing, scope, responsible party, and outcome.

Depending on the control, evidence may include:

- Approved policies and procedures.
- System configurations.
- Identity and access records.
- Evaluation plans and results.
- Risk assessments and treatment decisions.
- Change-management records.
- Deployment approvals.
- Monitoring dashboards and alert records.
- Incident reports.
- Provider assessments and contractual records.
- Human review and escalation records.
- Control-testing workpapers.
- Corrective-action validation records.

Evidence requirements should be proportionate to the control's risk and complexity.

For example, a low-risk internal AI assistant may require a documented approval and configuration record. A high-impact system may require more extensive evaluation evidence, independent review, change traceability, monitoring records, and retained decision records.

Evidence must also be relevant to the period being assessed. A current screenshot of a configuration may demonstrate the configuration at the time of capture but may not prove that the same configuration was active six months earlier.

For controls that operate continuously or repeatedly, the organization may need timestamped logs, historical configuration records, workflow histories, or representative operating records.

Evidence should be authentic, attributable, protected from unauthorized alteration, and retained in accordance with applicable requirements and organizational policies.

Where evidence contains sensitive data, the organization should apply appropriate access controls and data-minimization measures. Auditability should not become a reason to retain unrestricted copies of confidential prompts, personal information, or other sensitive AI inputs when less intrusive evidence can adequately demonstrate control operation.

### 22. Defining Control Frequency and Trigger Conditions

Every control should specify when it must operate.

A control may operate continuously, periodically, at a defined lifecycle gate, following a triggering event, or through a combination of these mechanisms.

Examples include:

| Control timing              | Example                                                                   |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Continuous                  | Enforcing access restrictions on AI endpoints                             |
| Per transaction or request  | Applying authorization checks before retrieving restricted records        |
| Before deployment           | Completing required evaluations and obtaining approval                    |
| At a defined interval       | Reviewing AI system documentation every six months                        |
| Following a material change | Reassessing risk after a significant model or provider change             |
| Following an incident       | Evaluating whether control failures contributed to the event              |
| At contract renewal         | Reassessing a material AI provider and relevant contractual protections   |
| Before retirement           | Verifying data handling, access removal, and decommissioning requirements |

The control library should avoid arbitrary frequencies that are disconnected from risk.

A quarterly review may be reasonable for a rapidly changing, high-risk system but excessive for a stable, low-risk system if there is no material change and other monitoring provides sufficient assurance. Conversely, an annual review may be inadequate where the system changes frequently or where a control must operate at every transaction.

Frequency decisions should consider:

- Risk severity and likelihood.
- Rate of system and model change.
- Data sensitivity.
- Operational exposure.
- Regulatory and contractual obligations.
- Historical incidents and control failures.
- Availability and reliability of automated monitoring.
- Time required to detect and contain a failure.

The library should define both scheduled activities and event-driven triggers where necessary.

For example, a periodic system review may be supplemented by a requirement to reassess the system whenever a new tool integration expands its ability to access enterprise resources.

The control owner should be able to demonstrate that the activity occurred at the required time and that missed or overdue executions were identified and addressed.

### 23. Designing Preventive Controls for AI Systems

Preventive controls aim to reduce the likelihood that an undesirable condition will occur.

In AI environments, preventive controls are especially important where a failure could result in unauthorized data disclosure, unsafe actions, consequential decisions, or irreversible business outcomes.

Common preventive control areas include:

**Access restrictions:** Ensure that users, applications, models, and agents can access only authorized resources.

**Deployment gates:** Prevent production release until defined risk assessments, evaluations, and approvals are complete.

**Data-use restrictions:** Ensure that data is used only under authorized conditions and for approved purposes.

**Tool restrictions:** Limit agent capabilities to approved tools, operations, and permission scopes.

**Input and output safeguards:** Apply appropriate validation, filtering, or policy enforcement to reduce identified risks.

**Configuration management:** Restrict changes to approved configurations and deployment artifacts.

**Human authorization:** Require explicit approval for designated actions that should not be performed autonomously.

Preventive controls should be designed around the actual system architecture.

For example, telling a language model not to access a restricted database is not equivalent to enforcing authorization at the database or retrieval-service layer. The control should rely on mechanisms capable of enforcing the required boundary even when model output is incorrect or adversarial instructions are introduced.

Similarly, a policy stating that an agent must not execute high-impact transactions is insufficient if the agent's service identity has unrestricted transaction privileges and the tool interface does not enforce approval requirements.

Preventive control design should therefore consider enforcement location, bypass resistance, privilege scope, failure behavior, and dependencies on other controls.

The organization should document known limitations and determine whether additional controls are necessary to achieve the required risk outcome.

### 24. Designing Detective Controls for AI Systems

Detective controls identify conditions that indicate an AI system may be operating outside its approved boundaries or that a preventive control may have failed.

AI-specific detection may involve monitoring system behavior, model performance, data flows, access patterns, policy violations, and changes in the operating environment.

Illustrative detective controls include:

- Monitoring access to AI services and connected resources.
- Detecting unusual request volumes or suspicious usage patterns.
- Monitoring evaluation metrics for material degradation.
- Identifying unexpected tool calls or authorization failures.
- Detecting changes in model versions or provider configurations.
- Reviewing reports of inaccurate, unsafe, or discriminatory outputs.
- Identifying missing or overdue control activities.
- Monitoring the completion of human review requirements.

A detective control should define the event or condition being monitored, the mechanism used to identify it, the applicable thresholds or decision criteria, the responsible recipient, and the required response.

For example, a monitoring dashboard that records model latency may support operational performance management but may not provide sufficient detection of unauthorized data retrieval.

Detection must be aligned with the risk being addressed.

The organization should also assess whether the monitoring mechanism can observe the relevant failure mode. A system that logs API requests but not the effective authorization decision may provide insufficient evidence to establish whether access restrictions operated correctly.

For AI systems, detection may require a combination of technical telemetry, structured evaluations, user feedback, human review, and risk-based testing.

Detection thresholds should be documented and reviewed when the system's intended purpose, operating environment, or risk profile changes.

### 25. Designing Corrective Controls and Recovery Mechanisms

Corrective controls address identified deficiencies and support the restoration of an acceptable operating condition.

For AI systems, corrective measures may include suspending a system, disabling a tool integration, rolling back a model version, restricting affected users, correcting configuration errors, or requiring additional human review.

Corrective controls should be designed before an incident occurs.

A system may produce an unexpected result that cannot be prevented reliably in every circumstance. The organization must therefore define how the event is contained, how its impact is assessed, who has authority to intervene, and what conditions must be satisfied before normal operation resumes.

A corrective control may include:

1. Detection or receipt of a report.
2. Initial triage and severity classification.
3. Containment or restriction of the affected capability.
4. Investigation of the failure and its scope.
5. Assessment of affected data, decisions, users, and processes.
6. Remediation of the technical or procedural deficiency.
7. Validation that the correction is effective.
8. Authorization to restore operation, where required.
9. Documentation and reporting.
10. Review of whether related controls require improvement.

Corrective controls should address both the immediate symptom and the underlying cause.

For example, disabling an AI agent after unauthorized tool use may contain the incident, but it does not necessarily resolve the weakness that permitted excessive permissions. The organization should investigate the authorization design, deployment approval process, testing coverage, monitoring, and accountability arrangements.

Where a control failure creates a material risk, the organization should reassess residual risk and determine whether the system may continue operating under temporary safeguards.

Recovery decisions should be documented and authorized by the appropriate roles.

### 26. Establishing Control Dependencies and Control Chains

AI risks are often managed through multiple controls that operate together. A single control may be insufficient to address a complex risk scenario.

A **control chain** is a sequence or combination of related controls that collectively supports a defined risk outcome.

For example, preventing an AI agent from executing unauthorized actions may require:

1. Identity controls that establish the agent's identity.
2. Authorization controls that restrict available tools and permissions.
3. Architecture controls that enforce the restrictions outside the model's own instructions.
4. Evaluation controls that test expected and adversarial behavior.
5. Monitoring controls that detect prohibited attempts or unexpected activity.
6. Incident controls that support containment and recovery.

These controls may have different owners, technologies, and operating frequencies.

The organization should document material dependencies so that a failure in one control can be evaluated for its effect on the overall risk treatment.

A control chain should not be treated as effective merely because each component exists. The interactions between components must also work as intended.

For example, a monitoring control may detect prohibited tool use, but its effectiveness depends on whether the relevant events are logged, alerts are generated, the correct personnel receive them, and the response process is sufficiently timely.

Similarly, an approval workflow may exist, but if a deployment pipeline allows an unapproved model version to be released, the overall deployment control may still fail.

Control dependency analysis should therefore identify:

- Upstream controls and prerequisites.
- Downstream controls and response mechanisms.
- Shared technical services.
- Common evidence sources.
- Failure conditions that affect multiple controls.
- Ownership and escalation dependencies.
- Potential single points of failure.

This analysis helps the organization identify systemic weaknesses that may not be visible when controls are assessed independently.

### 27. Identifying Common-Mode Control Failures

A common-mode failure occurs when a single underlying weakness affects multiple controls or systems that are assumed to provide separate layers of protection.

In AI environments, common-mode failures may arise from shared identity infrastructure, common model providers, centralized retrieval services, shared monitoring platforms, or a common evaluation methodology.

For example, several AI applications may rely on the same authorization service. If that service incorrectly evaluates document permissions, all dependent applications may be exposed even if each application appears to have its own access-control configuration.

Other examples include:

- Multiple systems using the same flawed evaluation dataset.
- Several applications inheriting an insecure default configuration.
- Multiple agents sharing an overprivileged service identity.
- Several systems depending on incomplete provider incident notifications.
- A common monitoring pipeline failing to collect relevant AI security events.
- A centralized approval workflow permitting deployment without required evidence.

Control libraries should support the identification of shared dependencies and common control owners.

Where several systems depend on one control, the organization should assess the potential concentration of risk and determine whether independent safeguards are necessary.

The organization should also avoid overstating assurance by counting multiple controls as independent layers when they rely on the same mechanism or evidence source.

For example, three dashboards that display data from the same incomplete logging pipeline do not provide three independent sources of assurance.

Common-mode failure analysis is particularly important for enterprise AI platforms, shared model services, and agent frameworks used by multiple business units.

### 28. Defining Control Effectiveness Criteria

A control should have defined criteria for determining whether it is effective.

Control effectiveness cannot be established solely by confirming that a policy exists, a procedure has been written, or a technical feature has been enabled.

The assessment should consider at least three dimensions.

**Design effectiveness:** Whether the control is appropriately designed to address the relevant risk or requirement.

**Implementation effectiveness:** Whether the control has been implemented in the intended environment and covers the required systems, processes, and users.

**Operating effectiveness:** Whether the control operated consistently and as required over the period being assessed.

For example, an access-control design may require every retrieval request to be checked against the requesting user's permissions.

Design effectiveness requires evaluating whether that requirement adequately addresses the identified unauthorized-access risk.

Implementation effectiveness requires confirming that the authorization mechanism is deployed across the relevant retrieval paths and cannot be bypassed through an alternative interface.

Operating effectiveness requires testing whether authorization checks were performed correctly during the relevant period.

A control assessment should distinguish among these dimensions because a failure in any one may undermine the overall result.

An illustrative set of criteria is shown below.

| Dimension        | Assessment question                                                 | Potential evidence                                                   |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Design           | Does the control address the identified risk?                       | Control specification, risk assessment, design review                |
| Implementation   | Is the control deployed across the required scope?                  | Configuration records, architecture evidence, implementation records |
| Operation        | Did the control operate as required?                                | Logs, completed workflows, sampled records, test results             |
| Coverage         | Are all applicable systems and processes included?                  | AI inventory, scope records, system architecture                     |
| Reliability      | Can the control be relied upon under expected operating conditions? | Failure tests, monitoring results, incident history                  |
| Evidence quality | Is the conclusion supported by relevant and reliable evidence?      | Source records, timestamps, access history, evidence validation      |

The organization should define how deficiencies in these dimensions affect the control's overall rating.

A technically well-designed control that is not deployed remains ineffective in practice. A correctly deployed control with repeated operating failures also cannot be considered effective simply because its design is sound.

### 29. Separating Control Design Deficiencies from Operating Failures

Control deficiencies should be classified accurately because different failure types require different remediation approaches.

A **design deficiency** occurs when the control, as specified, is inadequate to address the intended risk.

For example, an AI agent's control design may rely entirely on the model following a natural-language instruction not to access restricted data, without enforcing authorization at the underlying resource.

An **implementation deficiency** occurs when the required control has not been deployed correctly or does not cover the required scope.

For example, an authorization mechanism may be correctly designed but omitted from one retrieval endpoint.

An **operating deficiency** occurs when a properly designed and implemented control does not operate as required.

For example, a mandatory approval process may exist and be technically enforced in most cases, but operators may repeatedly approve deployments without reviewing required evaluation results.

A **monitoring or evidence deficiency** occurs when the organization cannot adequately establish whether the control is operating as intended.

For example, an access-control mechanism may appear correctly configured, but relevant decision logs are unavailable and the organization cannot substantiate its operating effectiveness.

These categories may overlap. An investigation should identify the primary deficiency and any contributing weaknesses.

| Deficiency type | Typical cause                                                   | Remediation focus                                  |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Design          | Inadequate control objective or enforcement mechanism           | Redesign the control                               |
| Implementation  | Incomplete deployment or incorrect configuration                | Correct implementation and verify coverage         |
| Operation       | Inconsistent execution or failure to follow required procedures | Correct execution, accountability, and monitoring  |
| Evidence        | Inadequate records or unreliable evidence generation            | Improve evidence capture, integrity, and retention |
| Governance      | Unclear ownership or ineffective oversight                      | Clarify authority, responsibility, and escalation  |

Accurate classification helps management distinguish between isolated execution errors and systemic control weaknesses.

It also improves trend analysis. Repeated implementation failures may indicate weak deployment governance, while recurring design deficiencies may indicate that the control library itself does not adequately address the organization's AI risk profile.

### 30. Managing Control Inheritance Across Enterprise Platforms

Organizations frequently operate shared AI platforms, common model services, enterprise retrieval systems, and centrally managed security capabilities.

Controls may be implemented centrally and inherited by multiple AI systems.

**Control inheritance** occurs when a system relies on a shared control implemented by another organizational unit, service, or platform rather than independently implementing the entire control.

Examples include:

- Enterprise identity services enforcing authentication.
- Centralized key-management services protecting encryption keys.
- Shared logging platforms collecting security events.
- Common deployment pipelines enforcing approval gates.
- Enterprise supplier-risk functions conducting provider due diligence.
- Centralized AI gateways enforcing request-level policies.

Inheritance can reduce duplicated work, but it introduces governance dependencies.

A consuming system should not automatically claim that a shared control is effective simply because the central service reports that the control exists. The organization must establish that the inherited control covers the relevant system, supports its risk requirements, and provides appropriate evidence.

An inheritance record should identify:

- The inherited control.
- The providing service or organizational unit.
- The consuming AI systems.
- The boundaries of the inherited capability.
- The responsibilities retained by the consuming system.
- Evidence available from the provider.
- Known limitations and exceptions.
- Review and change-notification arrangements.

For example, a centralized identity platform may enforce authentication and role assignment, but the AI application may remain responsible for ensuring that retrieved documents are authorized for the individual user.

The application cannot assume that authentication automatically satisfies document-level authorization.

Likewise, centralized logging may collect API events, but the system owner may still need to ensure that AI-specific events, model-version changes, tool invocations, and relevant policy decisions are captured.

Inherited controls should therefore be evaluated within their defined boundaries, and shared-service failures should be assessed for their impact across dependent systems.

### 31. Designing Compensating Controls

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard introduced to address a risk when the primary control cannot be implemented, is temporarily unavailable, or does not fully satisfy the required control objective.

Compensating controls may be appropriate in legacy environments, constrained provider integrations, transitional architectures, or situations where a technical limitation prevents immediate implementation of the preferred control.

However, a compensating control should not be accepted merely because it is easier to implement.

The organization should establish that the alternative addresses the relevant risk to an acceptable level and does not introduce additional unmanaged risks.

A compensating-control assessment should document:

1. The original control requirement.
2. The reason the primary control is unavailable or insufficient.
3. The risk created by the gap.
4. The proposed alternative safeguard.
5. The expected risk-reduction effect.
6. The evidence available to demonstrate operation.
7. The accountable control and risk owners.
8. The approval decision.
9. The duration and review conditions.
10. The plan for restoring the primary control, where appropriate.

Consider an AI application that cannot produce a particular type of provider-side telemetry.

The organization might introduce additional gateway logging, restrict access to sensitive functions, perform independent request reviews, and establish explicit incident-escalation procedures.

Whether those measures are sufficient depends on the risk, the visibility they provide, the ability to detect relevant events, and the applicable obligations.

A compensating control may reduce exposure without fully replacing the original safeguard. The organization should document any remaining limitations and evaluate residual risk accordingly.

Compensating controls should be subject to review, particularly when the underlying technology or provider capability changes.

### 32. Controlling Changes to the AI Control Library

An AI control library must be governed as a controlled artifact because changes to control definitions can affect system approvals, risk treatment, compliance mappings, evidence requirements, and assurance conclusions.

Control changes may be triggered by:

- New or amended legal requirements.
- Changes to external standards or frameworks.
- New AI threat patterns.
- Material incidents or audit findings.
- Changes in organizational risk appetite.
- New AI capabilities or deployment models.
- Repeated implementation difficulties.
- Changes to enterprise architecture.
- Identified gaps in control coverage.
- Improvements in control testing methodology.

The organization should establish a controlled change process that includes proposal, impact assessment, review, approval, publication, and communication.

The impact assessment should identify affected systems, risk-register entries, control implementations, framework mappings, procedures, training materials, and assurance plans.

For example, if a new control requirement is introduced for agent tool authorization, the organization may need to assess every registered agent-enabled system, identify existing authorization mechanisms, update risk treatment plans, and define new test procedures.

The organization should also distinguish between editorial changes and substantive changes.

Editorial changes improve wording or clarity without changing the control's meaning. Substantive changes modify the required outcome, scope, applicability, ownership, evidence expectations, or test criteria.

Substantive changes may require formal approval and reassessment of existing control implementations.

Version history should preserve the previous definition, the effective date of the new version, the reason for the change, and the approving authority.

This history is essential when auditors need to determine which control requirement applied at a particular point in time.

### 33. Maintaining Control Versioning and Historical Traceability

Control versioning preserves the history of control definitions and allows the organization to interpret past assessments against the requirements that applied at the time.

Without historical traceability, a revised control statement may make it difficult to determine whether an earlier assessment was valid, whether a remediation requirement was satisfied, or whether a system was approved under the correct criteria.

A controlled versioning model should include:

- Stable control identifiers.
- Version numbers or equivalent revision references.
- Effective dates.
- Change descriptions.
- Approval records.
- Superseded definitions.
- Affected control mappings.
- Relevant implementation and testing references.

A minor wording correction may not require a full reassessment if it does not alter the control's meaning. A material change to the control objective or test criteria may require the organization to reassess implementation and operating effectiveness.

For example, changing a requirement from periodic review of agent permissions to event-triggered reassessment following material permission changes may alter the control's operating expectations.

Existing systems may need revised workflows, monitoring triggers, evidence capture, and test procedures.

The organization should define how control changes affect previously completed assessments.

Historical findings should not be silently reclassified as closed simply because the control definition has changed. Closure should depend on the applicable remediation criteria and documented evidence.

Likewise, the organization should preserve the ability to identify which version of a control was used when a control test or compliance assessment was performed.

### 34. Applying Risk-Based Control Prioritization

Organizations may have more potential control requirements than they can implement simultaneously. A risk-based prioritization method helps determine which controls should receive attention first.

Prioritization should consider the severity of potential consequences, likelihood of occurrence, exposure, existing safeguards, applicable obligations, and the feasibility of remediation.

Relevant factors may include:

- Potential harm to individuals or organizations.
- Sensitivity and volume of processed data.
- System autonomy and decision authority.
- Reach and scale of deployment.
- External exposure and integration complexity.
- Ability to reverse or contain an adverse outcome.
- Dependency on third-party providers.
- History of incidents or control failures.
- Regulatory deadlines or mandatory obligations.
- Residual risk and risk appetite.

An illustrative prioritization model may classify controls as critical, high, moderate, or low priority.

| Priority | Illustrative characteristics                                                 | Management response                                                |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Critical | A control gap creates an unacceptable risk or affects a mandatory obligation | Immediate escalation and risk-appropriate containment or treatment |
| High     | A significant risk is insufficiently controlled                              | Prioritized remediation with defined accountability and oversight  |
| Moderate | A meaningful gap exists but current exposure is more limited                 | Planned remediation within an approved timeframe                   |
| Low      | A limited weakness exists with low assessed exposure                         | Scheduled improvement and periodic review                          |

The organization should define the criteria and approval authority for each category rather than relying on labels alone.

Mandatory legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements must be evaluated according to their applicable obligations. A risk-priority score should not be used to waive a binding requirement.

Prioritization should also account for control dependencies. A shared identity or logging weakness may deserve higher priority than an isolated issue because it affects multiple systems.

The objective is to direct resources toward the most significant control gaps while maintaining a defensible record of decisions, constraints, and accepted residual risks.

### 35. Part 2 Core Principle

An AI control library must connect each control to the risks and requirements it supports, define accountable ownership, establish implementation and evidence expectations, and specify when the control operates.

Effective operationalization also requires accurate framework mappings, clear control dependencies, risk-based prioritization, controlled versioning, and a reliable method for evaluating design, implementation, and operating effectiveness.

Controls should be assessed as part of an interconnected governance environment rather than as isolated checklist items. The organization must be able to demonstrate not only that controls have been defined, but also that they cover the intended scope, function as designed, produce reliable evidence, and contribute meaningfully to the treatment of AI-related risks.

## Part 3 — AI Control Library Assurance, Testing Methodology, and Performance Measurement

### 36. Establishing an AI Control Testing Methodology

An AI control library must be supported by a structured testing methodology that determines whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented across their required scope, and operating effectively.

Control testing provides evidence about the reliability of governance mechanisms. It helps management determine whether controls adequately address identified risks, whether implementation weaknesses exist, and whether corrective action is required.

An AI control testing methodology should define:

- The objectives and scope of control testing.
- The distinction between design, implementation, and operating effectiveness.
- The criteria for selecting controls and systems for assessment.
- The evidence requirements for each control.
- The testing procedures and sampling methodology.
- The classification of exceptions and deficiencies.
- The approach for determining control effectiveness.
- The documentation requirements for test results.
- The escalation process for material weaknesses.
- The validation requirements for remediation.

Testing should be proportionate to the control's purpose, the risk being managed, the complexity of the implementation, and the consequences of failure.

For example, an access-control test for a low-risk internal AI assistant may involve verifying authorization settings and testing representative user permissions. For an AI agent that can modify production infrastructure, testing may also need to cover privilege boundaries, prohibited actions, adversarial instructions, failure conditions, logging, and emergency suspension mechanisms.

A standardized methodology promotes consistency across business units while allowing the test procedures to reflect the technical and operational characteristics of individual systems.

The methodology should distinguish between confirming that a control exists and demonstrating that it achieves its intended objective. A completed checklist may establish that required documentation is present, but it does not necessarily demonstrate that a technical safeguard resists bypass attempts or operates reliably under expected conditions.

### 37. Testing Control Design Effectiveness

Design effectiveness testing determines whether a control, as defined, is capable of addressing the risk or requirement for which it was established.

The reviewer examines the control objective, the stated requirement, its applicability, the implementation approach, and its relationship to the relevant risk scenario.

A control may be consistently performed and still be ineffective if its design does not address the underlying risk.

Consider an AI application that retrieves confidential documents. The organization has defined a control requiring the language model to refuse requests for unauthorized information.

A design review should question whether this requirement establishes an enforceable security boundary. Because model outputs are probabilistic and may be influenced by malicious instructions or retrieved content, a natural-language instruction alone may not provide sufficient access control.

A stronger design would require authorization enforcement at the retrieval or resource-access layer, supported by testing and appropriate monitoring.

Design effectiveness testing should consider:

1. Whether the control objective is clearly defined.
2. Whether the control addresses the relevant risk scenario.
3. Whether the control is appropriate for the system's architecture.
4. Whether responsibilities and decision authority are clear.
5. Whether applicability and scope are sufficiently defined.
6. Whether implementation dependencies are understood.
7. Whether failure conditions and exceptions are addressed.
8. Whether the required evidence can demonstrate operation.
9. Whether the control can be tested against objective criteria.
10. Whether additional controls are needed to address residual weaknesses.

The review should consider both intended operating conditions and credible failure scenarios.

For AI systems, this may include model errors, prompt injection, unexpected tool invocation, data-access failures, provider changes, model-version changes, or degradation in evaluation performance.

Design effectiveness testing should produce a documented conclusion that identifies the control's strengths, limitations, dependencies, and required improvements.

### 38. Testing Implementation Effectiveness

Implementation effectiveness testing determines whether the control has been deployed as intended and covers all systems, processes, interfaces, and operating environments within its defined scope.

A control may be well designed but implemented inconsistently across development, testing, staging, and production environments.

For example, an organization may require approval before deploying an AI model to production. The main deployment pipeline may enforce the approval requirement, but an alternative deployment mechanism may permit direct release without the same checks.

The control exists and may work correctly through the primary pipeline, yet implementation coverage remains incomplete.

Implementation testing should verify:

- The actual configuration of the control.
- The systems and environments covered.
- The interfaces through which the control operates.
- The assigned control owner and operator.
- The integration with upstream and downstream controls.
- The consistency between approved and deployed configurations.
- The presence of required monitoring and evidence mechanisms.
- The handling of exceptions and alternative operating paths.
- The implementation of any approved compensating controls.

Evidence may include architecture diagrams, configuration records, deployment pipeline settings, access-control policies, workflow configurations, system inventories, and technical test results.

Where the control depends on a shared platform, the reviewer should establish whether the platform's implementation covers the assessed system and whether any system-specific responsibilities remain unfulfilled.

Implementation effectiveness testing should not rely solely on design documents. The reviewer should obtain evidence from the actual environment or from reliable records demonstrating the deployed state.

### 39. Testing Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness testing determines whether a control operated consistently and as required over the period under review.

The assessment should establish whether the control was performed at the required frequency, by the appropriate role or mechanism, according to the defined procedure, and with the required outcome.

For example, an organization may require every material model change to undergo an impact assessment before release.

Operating effectiveness testing may examine a sample of model changes and verify that:

- The changes were identified and recorded.
- The impact assessments were completed.
- Relevant risks were considered.
- Required reviewers participated.
- Approval occurred before release.
- Required evaluation activities were completed.
- Exceptions were authorized and documented.
- The deployed version corresponds to the approved change.

The existence of a change-management procedure does not prove that these activities occurred in individual cases.

Operating effectiveness testing should consider the entire period being assessed, relevant system changes, exceptions, and any known control failures.

Where controls operate continuously, the reviewer may examine configuration history, event logs, automated execution records, or selected transactions. Where controls operate periodically, the reviewer may inspect completed reviews, approvals, and associated evidence.

For AI systems, testing may also need to evaluate whether the control continued to operate following changes to models, prompts, data sources, integrations, or provider configurations.

A control that operated effectively before a material architecture change may no longer operate as intended afterward.

The final conclusion should distinguish isolated exceptions from recurring failures and assess whether the observed evidence supports reliance on the control.

### 40. Selecting Controls and Systems for Testing

An organization should establish a risk-based method for selecting controls and systems for assurance activities.

Testing every control in every AI system at the same depth may be impractical. Conversely, testing only the easiest controls to inspect may leave significant risks unexamined.

Selection should consider the potential consequences of failure, the system's risk classification, the importance of the control, the maturity of the implementation, and the results of previous assessments.

Relevant selection factors include:

- High residual-risk ratings.
- Controls supporting mandatory requirements.
- Systems used in consequential decision-making.
- Systems with autonomous action capabilities.
- Systems processing sensitive or confidential information.
- Controls with repeated findings.
- Recent incidents or significant near misses.
- Material model or architecture changes.
- New or significantly modified AI deployments.
- Shared controls supporting multiple systems.
- Controls that management relies on for risk acceptance.
- Areas with limited independent assurance.

Testing plans should also consider coverage across business units, system types, deployment environments, providers, and control domains.

For example, an enterprise may operate several AI applications using one shared retrieval service. Testing only a single low-risk application may not adequately assess the authorization risks associated with higher-risk applications using the same service.

A risk-based testing plan should identify why each selected control or system was chosen, the relevant assessment period, the intended coverage, and any material limitations.

The selection methodology should be documented so that management and independent reviewers can understand whether the assurance program provides reasonable coverage of the organization's AI risk exposure.

### 41. Applying Sampling Techniques to AI Control Testing

Sampling allows a reviewer to examine a subset of records or transactions when testing the entire population is impractical.

The sampling method should be appropriate to the control, the population being tested, the risk level, and the assurance objective.

Common approaches include:

**Random sampling:** Selects items without deliberate preference within the defined population. It can reduce selection bias and support conclusions about the sampled population when applied appropriately.

**Risk-based sampling:** Prioritizes items with higher exposure, greater complexity, or a history of failures. It is useful for identifying material weaknesses but does not automatically support statistical conclusions about the entire population.

**Stratified sampling:** Divides the population into meaningful groups, such as risk classifications, business units, model providers, or deployment environments, and selects samples from each group.

**Judgmental sampling:** Uses professional judgment to select items expected to provide useful evidence. The limitations of this method should be documented.

**Full-population testing:** Examines every item in the defined population, often through automated analysis. It may be appropriate when reliable structured data is available and the test logic can be applied consistently.

For example, a reviewer may select deployment approvals across high-, medium-, and low-risk AI systems. Stratification helps ensure that high-risk deployments are not underrepresented simply because they account for a small proportion of total deployments.

Sampling records should identify the population, selection method, sample size, selected items, exclusions, exceptions, and rationale for the approach.

The organization should not interpret a small sample with no exceptions as definitive proof that every instance of the control operated effectively. The strength of the conclusion depends on the sampling method, the population, the quality of the evidence, and the nature of the control.

### 42. Designing AI-Specific Control Test Procedures

AI control test procedures should address the actual behavior and architecture of the system rather than rely exclusively on documentation review.

A well-designed test procedure defines the control being tested, the expected result, the test conditions, the evidence to be collected, and the criteria for determining whether the test passes or fails.

AI-specific test procedures may include:

- Verifying that the deployed model version matches the approved version.
- Testing whether authorization restrictions apply to retrieved documents.
- Confirming that prohibited agent actions are blocked.
- Evaluating whether required human approvals are enforced.
- Inspecting whether material changes trigger reassessment.
- Testing whether monitoring alerts are generated under defined conditions.
- Confirming that incident escalation reaches the appropriate personnel.
- Verifying that system documentation reflects actual capabilities and limitations.
- Testing whether evaluation failures prevent deployment where required.
- Confirming that retired systems no longer retain unauthorized access to data or connected resources.

Test procedures should be designed with appropriate technical and operational safeguards. Testing an AI agent's ability to execute sensitive actions, for example, should take place in a controlled environment or through an appropriately restricted test configuration.

A test procedure should specify:

1. Control identifier and version.
2. Objective and risk addressed.
3. System and environment in scope.
4. Test method and prerequisites.
5. Test inputs or selected records.
6. Expected behavior.
7. Evidence to be collected.
8. Pass and fail criteria.
9. Handling of unexpected results.
10. Required documentation and review.

For controls involving probabilistic AI behavior, a single successful test may be insufficient to establish reliable performance. The reviewer may need repeated trials, representative scenarios, adversarial cases, or statistical analysis appropriate to the intended assurance claim.

The test plan should also identify limitations. A controlled test cannot establish that every possible prompt, input, or environmental condition has been evaluated.

### 43. Testing Generative AI and Large Language Model Controls

Generative AI systems introduce control-testing challenges because outputs can vary across requests, context, model versions, and configurations.

Traditional configuration checks remain necessary, but they may not be sufficient to establish that a generative AI control achieves its intended objective.

For example, a control intended to reduce sensitive-data disclosure should not be tested solely by confirming that a written policy prohibits disclosure. The reviewer should evaluate whether the implemented system enforces relevant data-access restrictions and behaves appropriately under representative failure conditions.

Testing may include:

**Prompt-injection resistance:** Determine whether untrusted instructions can cause the system to bypass established security or authorization boundaries.

**Sensitive-data protection:** Evaluate whether the system exposes restricted information through direct responses, retrieval, generated summaries, or connected tools.

**Output handling:** Verify that generated content is subject to appropriate validation or restrictions before consequential actions occur.

**Grounding and source traceability:** Where required, evaluate whether responses are appropriately supported by approved sources and whether unsupported claims are handled according to the system's requirements.

**Refusal and escalation behavior:** Test whether designated prohibited requests are handled according to documented requirements, including escalation where appropriate.

**Model-change control:** Verify that material changes trigger the required impact assessment and approval.

**Logging and monitoring:** Confirm that relevant security events and policy decisions are recorded without creating unnecessary retention of sensitive content.

Test scenarios should reflect the system's intended purpose and threat model. A general-purpose internal assistant, a customer-facing support assistant, and an AI tool used to support consequential decisions will have different control requirements.

The organization should define acceptance criteria before conducting the test. Otherwise, reviewers may select favorable examples after observing the results.

Where the control depends on probabilistic behavior, evaluation should use representative test sets and clearly documented limitations. The organization should avoid interpreting a limited number of successful outputs as proof of universal safety or reliability.

### 44. Testing Agentic AI Controls and Autonomous Actions

Agentic AI systems may plan tasks, select tools, invoke external services, modify records, or execute multistep workflows. Their control testing must therefore examine both individual actions and the boundaries governing sequences of actions.

The principal concern is not simply whether the model produces an acceptable response. It is whether the overall system can perform only the actions authorized for its identity, operating context, and approved purpose.

Agentic AI control testing should evaluate:

- Tool allowlists and permission scopes.
- Identity and authorization enforcement.
- Restrictions on sensitive or irreversible actions.
- Approval gates and separation of duties.
- Limits on execution duration, resource use, or action volume.
- Handling of malicious instructions and untrusted content.
- Protection against unauthorized privilege escalation.
- Logging of tool calls and consequential actions.
- Monitoring for unexpected behavior.
- Emergency suspension and recovery mechanisms.

Consider an agent that can create support tickets and modify user permissions.

Testing should verify that ordinary ticket creation follows the approved workflow and that permission changes require the appropriate authorization. The test should also examine whether malicious instructions can cause the agent to use the permission-management tool outside its authorized scope.

The reviewer should assess the enforcement mechanism rather than assume that a model's stated intentions guarantee compliant behavior.

For high-impact actions, controls may need to enforce authorization independently of the model, require human approval, or prevent direct execution by the agent.

Tests should include negative cases, such as attempts to invoke unauthorized tools, bypass approval requirements, or perform actions using another user's privileges.

The organization should also test failure handling. If an agent receives an ambiguous tool response or loses connectivity during a multistep operation, it should not create uncontrolled or duplicated actions.

Testing must be performed within authorized boundaries, with appropriate safeguards to prevent unintended changes to production systems or real-world transactions.

### 45. Evaluating Control Performance Metrics

Control performance metrics help management determine whether controls are being implemented consistently, operating within defined expectations, and producing the required governance outcomes.

Metrics should be directly related to the control objective. A high volume of completed activities does not necessarily indicate effective risk management.

For example, measuring the number of AI risk assessments completed may provide useful information about process throughput. It does not establish whether the assessments were sufficiently detailed, completed before the relevant decision, or followed by effective risk treatment.

Useful control metrics may include:

- Percentage of applicable AI systems with assigned control owners.
- Percentage of required control activities completed on time.
- Percentage of sampled control executions meeting acceptance criteria.
- Number of overdue control reviews.
- Percentage of material changes assessed before deployment.
- Number of unauthorized or prohibited actions detected.
- Percentage of high-risk control deficiencies with approved remediation plans.
- Median time to remediate control failures.
- Percentage of corrective actions independently validated.
- Number of recurring findings associated with the same control.
- Percentage of applicable systems covered by required monitoring.
- Percentage of control records with current evidence and valid ownership.

Each metric should have a defined calculation method, data source, reporting frequency, accountable owner, and interpretation guidance.

Where thresholds are used, the organization should define what happens when a threshold is breached.

For example, a metric showing repeated late completion of high-risk system reviews may trigger escalation to the AI governance committee, reassessment of the affected systems, or a review of the resources allocated to control execution.

Metrics should distinguish between activity, coverage, quality, effectiveness, and outcomes.

**Activity metrics** measure whether a process is being performed.

**Coverage metrics** measure whether the required population is included.

**Quality metrics** assess whether the work meets defined standards.

**Effectiveness metrics** evaluate whether the control achieves its intended objective.

**Outcome metrics** assess whether the broader risk exposure or frequency of adverse events is changing.

No single metric should be treated as a complete representation of control effectiveness.

### 46. Establishing Control Performance Thresholds and Escalation

Control performance thresholds define when a metric, test result, or operating condition requires review or action.

Thresholds should be established according to risk, control purpose, applicable requirements, and the organization's tolerance for failure.

Some controls may require complete compliance with a defined condition. For example, where an internal policy requires authorization before every production deployment, an unauthorized deployment should be treated as a control exception rather than averaged into an acceptable compliance percentage.

Other controls may rely on statistical or operational thresholds. A monitoring control may trigger investigation when an anomaly rate exceeds a defined baseline, provided the threshold is justified and reviewed.

A control threshold record should define:

- The metric or condition being monitored.
- The calculation or detection method.
- The threshold and its rationale.
- The applicable system or population.
- The severity of a breach.
- The required response.
- The escalation recipient.
- The expected response time.
- The evidence required to document the outcome.
- The process for reviewing and adjusting the threshold.

Thresholds should not be selected solely to make performance reports appear favorable.

For example, an organization may be tempted to increase the acceptable number of overdue control reviews when operational teams repeatedly miss deadlines. A more defensible approach is to investigate the underlying cause, assess the resulting exposure, and determine whether process redesign or resource allocation is required.

A threshold breach should result in a documented decision. Depending on the risk, the response may include additional testing, temporary restrictions, incident escalation, corrective action, or management review.

Where the breach may indicate a material AI safety, security, privacy, or compliance issue, the organization should follow the applicable incident and escalation processes rather than treating the event only as a performance-metric deviation.

### 47. Integrating Control Testing with the AI Risk Register

Control testing results should feed back into the AI risk-management process.

A failed or partially effective control may change the organization's understanding of the likelihood, impact, or overall exposure associated with an AI risk.

For example, a risk register may initially assess unauthorized retrieval as adequately controlled because the system enforces document-level authorization. Control testing may subsequently reveal that one retrieval endpoint bypasses the authorization service.

The organization should reassess the affected risk rather than record the issue only as a technical finding.

The integration process should connect:

1. The risk register entry.
2. The relevant control identifiers.
3. The system and components in scope.
4. The test procedure and evidence.
5. The identified deficiency.
6. The revised control-effectiveness assessment.
7. The residual-risk reassessment.
8. The treatment or corrective-action decision.
9. The accountable risk owner.
10. The closure or risk-acceptance record.

The risk register should reflect the actual control state rather than the intended control design.

If a control is not implemented or is operating ineffectively, the risk assessment should not continue to assume the same level of risk reduction without a defensible basis.

The organization should also consider whether the finding affects other systems that rely on the same control, shared platform, provider, or implementation pattern.

A single technical deficiency may have enterprise-wide implications if the affected mechanism supports numerous AI systems.

### 48. Evaluating Shared Controls and Inherited Assurance

When AI systems inherit controls from enterprise platforms or shared services, assurance activities should establish whether the available evidence supports reliance on those controls.

A central service may provide evidence that a control exists and operates, but the consuming system must still establish that the control applies to its specific use case and covers the relevant risk.

For example, a centralized logging service may demonstrate successful collection of API access events. An AI application may additionally require records of model-version changes, retrieval authorization decisions, tool invocation attempts, or other AI-specific events.

The central service's assurance report should be evaluated against the consuming system's actual requirements.

Inherited-control assurance should consider:

- The service's defined scope and boundaries.
- The systems and environments covered.
- The period addressed by the evidence.
- The control objectives evaluated.
- The testing methods and results.
- Any exceptions or qualifications.
- The independence and competence of the reviewer, where relevant.
- Changes since the assessment period.
- Responsibilities retained by the consuming system.

The organization should avoid assuming that an assurance report covering a provider or shared platform automatically covers every application that depends on it.

Where evidence is insufficient, the organization may need supplementary testing, additional technical safeguards, or a documented limitation in its assurance conclusion.

The control library should preserve the relationship between shared controls, consuming systems, and the evidence supporting inherited-control claims.

### 49. Documenting Control Testing Results

Every material control test should produce a documented result that supports review, repeatability, and corrective action.

A testing workpaper should allow another qualified reviewer to understand what was tested, why it was tested, what evidence was examined, what results were observed, and how the conclusion was reached.

A control testing record should normally include:

| Field                       | Description                                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Assessment ID               | Unique identifier for the test                                                        |
| Control ID and version      | Control requirement assessed                                                          |
| System or process           | Scope of the assessment                                                               |
| Assessment period           | Period during which control operation was evaluated                                   |
| Testing objective           | Purpose of the test                                                                   |
| Test methodology            | Inspection, inquiry, observation, technical test, sampling, or another defined method |
| Population and sample       | Records or transactions examined                                                      |
| Evidence references         | Source records supporting the test                                                    |
| Expected results            | Criteria against which evidence was evaluated                                         |
| Observed results            | Actual behavior or evidence obtained                                                  |
| Exceptions                  | Deviations identified during testing                                                  |
| Conclusion                  | Assessment of design, implementation, and operating effectiveness                     |
| Severity                    | Classification of any material deficiency                                             |
| Reviewer                    | Person or function performing the assessment                                          |
| Review and approval         | Required quality review or sign-off                                                   |
| Corrective-action reference | Link to any resulting remediation record                                              |

The workpaper should clearly distinguish facts, interpretations, and conclusions.

For example, the fact that three of twenty sampled deployment records lacked documented approval should be recorded separately from the conclusion about the control's effectiveness and the assessment of the resulting risk.

The organization should preserve enough detail to reproduce the test where practical, subject to appropriate security, privacy, and confidentiality restrictions.

Testing records should be retained according to the applicable retention policy and legal or regulatory requirements.

### 50. Applying Quality Assurance to Control Testing

Control testing itself requires quality assurance to ensure that conclusions are consistent, evidence-based, and proportionate to the underlying risk.

A reviewer may reach an incorrect conclusion because of an inadequate sample, an ambiguous test procedure, incomplete evidence, misunderstanding of the system architecture, or inconsistent application of acceptance criteria.

Quality assurance should examine both the testing process and the resulting conclusions.

Quality-review activities may include:

- Verifying that the test addressed the correct control version.
- Confirming that the system and assessment period were properly defined.
- Reviewing whether the sampling method was appropriate.
- Checking that evidence supports the recorded observations.
- Confirming that acceptance criteria were applied consistently.
- Reviewing the treatment of exceptions.
- Challenging unsupported assumptions.
- Confirming that severity classifications are justified.
- Verifying that required approvals and sign-offs are complete.
- Checking that findings are linked to the appropriate risk and corrective-action records.

The depth of quality assurance should be proportionate to the significance of the assessment and the consequences of an incorrect conclusion.

For example, a test supporting the continued operation of a high-risk AI system may require independent technical review or additional validation beyond the standard review applied to routine low-risk controls.

Quality assurance should also consider reviewer competence. Testing a complex agent authorization architecture may require technical expertise beyond that needed to inspect a policy approval record.

Where a reviewer lacks the necessary expertise, the organization should involve a qualified specialist or limit the conclusion to what the available evidence supports.

Quality assurance strengthens the reliability of control testing and helps prevent unsupported declarations of effectiveness.

### 51. Part 3 Core Principle

AI control assurance requires a disciplined methodology for evaluating design, implementation, and operating effectiveness. Testing must be risk-based, supported by reliable evidence, and appropriate to the technical behavior and governance requirements of the AI system.

Sampling, AI-specific test procedures, performance metrics, control dependency analysis, and quality assurance help determine whether controls operate as intended. Testing results must feed back into the AI risk register, shared-control assessments, and corrective-action processes so that deficiencies are reflected in actual risk decisions.

A defensible assurance conclusion is not simply a statement that a control exists. It is a documented judgment supported by relevant evidence, defined criteria, appropriate testing, and a clear explanation of any limitations.

## Part 4 — AI Control Library Governance, Continuous Improvement, and Enterprise Assurance

### 51. Establishing AI Control Library Governance

An AI control library must operate under a defined governance model that establishes authority over its creation, maintenance, approval, implementation, and retirement. Without formal governance, control definitions can become inconsistent, outdated, duplicated, or disconnected from the organization's actual AI risk exposure.

Control library governance determines who can introduce new controls, who approves material changes, how control applicability is interpreted, and how deficiencies in the library itself are identified and resolved.

A mature governance model should establish the following responsibilities:

- **AI governance committee:** Approves material changes to the control architecture, reviews significant control gaps, and resolves cross-functional governance issues within its delegated authority.
- **AI governance function:** Maintains the control library, coordinates control development, manages control mappings, and monitors consistency across business units.
- **Control owners:** Maintain individual control requirements, implementation guidance, evidence expectations, and effectiveness criteria.
- **Risk management function:** Ensures that controls remain connected to the organization's risk assessment and treatment methodology.
- **Compliance function:** Coordinates mapping to applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and standards-based requirements.
- **Security and privacy functions:** Provide specialist input on technical safeguards, information security, privacy, and related control requirements.
- **Internal audit or independent assurance:** Independently evaluates the governance and effectiveness of the control environment without assuming operational ownership of the controls being assessed.

The organization should document decision rights and escalation thresholds. Routine editorial changes may be approved by the designated library administrator, while changes affecting mandatory requirements, risk acceptance criteria, or enterprise-wide control objectives may require formal governance approval.

Governance arrangements should also distinguish between maintaining the library and operating the controls it contains. The team responsible for defining a control does not necessarily perform the control, and the team responsible for operating it should not automatically determine whether independent assurance has been achieved.

This separation supports accountability, reduces conflicts of interest, and improves the reliability of management reporting.

### 52. Defining the AI Control Library Lifecycle

The control library should follow a defined lifecycle that governs how controls are proposed, designed, approved, implemented, assessed, revised, and retired.

A typical lifecycle includes:

1. **Requirement identification:** Identify a new or changed risk, obligation, policy requirement, technology capability, or assurance need.
2. **Control design:** Define the control objective, requirement, scope, applicability, ownership, implementation guidance, and evidence expectations.
3. **Technical and governance review:** Assess feasibility, risk coverage, dependencies, consistency, and potential duplication.
4. **Approval:** Obtain authorization from the designated governance authority.
5. **Publication:** Assign the control identifier and version, record mappings, and make the approved definition available.
6. **Implementation:** Assign applicable controls to systems, processes, and accountable owners.
7. **Assessment:** Evaluate design, implementation, and operating effectiveness.
8. **Maintenance:** Update the control when risks, obligations, architectures, or operating conditions change.
9. **Retirement or replacement:** Remove obsolete controls from active use while preserving historical records and relevant relationships.

Each lifecycle stage should have defined entry conditions, required evidence, decision authority, and completion criteria.

For example, a proposed control requiring additional evaluation of AI agents may not be ready for publication until the organization has defined which agent capabilities trigger the requirement, what constitutes acceptable evaluation evidence, who approves exceptions, and how the control will be tested.

Publishing an incomplete requirement can create inconsistent interpretations across business units.

The lifecycle should also account for urgent changes. A newly identified vulnerability or material incident may require temporary safeguards before the formal control-development process is complete. Such interim measures should be documented, assigned an owner, reviewed by the appropriate authority, and incorporated into the formal control lifecycle when necessary.

### 53. Establishing Control Library Quality Standards

Control library quality determines whether controls can be interpreted consistently, implemented appropriately, and assessed objectively.

A library containing hundreds of poorly written controls may provide less governance value than a smaller library with clear requirements, reliable mappings, and testable criteria.

Quality standards should address several dimensions.

**Clarity:** Control statements use unambiguous language and define observable requirements.

**Completeness:** Required metadata, ownership, applicability, evidence, and test criteria are documented.

**Consistency:** Controls use standardized terminology, naming conventions, identifiers, and status definitions.

**Traceability:** Controls link to relevant risks, governance objectives, requirements, implementations, and assurance records.

**Testability:** A reviewer can determine whether the control has been satisfied using defined criteria and appropriate evidence.

**Feasibility:** Implementation expectations are technically and operationally realistic for the intended scope.

**Proportionality:** Requirements reflect the system's risk profile and relevant obligations without imposing unnecessary or ineffective activities.

**Maintainability:** Controls can be revised without losing historical traceability or creating uncontrolled duplication.

**Coverage:** The library addresses material AI risks across the relevant lifecycle and operating environment.

The AI governance function should establish review criteria and periodically assess the library against them.

Quality reviews may identify vague control statements, missing applicability conditions, duplicate controls, inconsistent mappings, outdated references, or requirements that cannot be supported by available evidence.

The organization should treat these issues as governance deficiencies requiring documented resolution rather than as minor administrative imperfections.

For example, a control requiring systems to be reviewed *regularly* is difficult to evaluate unless the organization defines the required interval or triggering conditions. A control requiring *appropriate testing* is similarly incomplete if it does not establish how the testing scope and acceptance criteria are determined.

Library quality assurance should ensure that control definitions are sufficiently precise to support consistent implementation and independent evaluation.

### 54. Managing Duplicate, Overlapping, and Conflicting Controls

As organizations combine AI governance requirements with existing information-security, privacy, compliance, and operational control frameworks, duplicate or overlapping controls can accumulate.

Some overlap is intentional because a single risk may require multiple safeguards. The problem arises when different controls impose inconsistent requirements, duplicate the same activity without clear purpose, or create conflicting ownership and evidence expectations.

The organization should distinguish between:

- **Duplicate controls:** Controls with substantially the same objective and requirement.
- **Complementary controls:** Controls that address different aspects of the same risk.
- **Overlapping controls:** Controls that share part of their scope but differ in purpose or implementation.
- **Conflicting controls:** Controls whose requirements cannot be satisfied simultaneously under the same conditions.
- **Inherited controls:** Controls implemented through shared enterprise services.
- **Specialized controls:** Additional controls required for specific AI technologies, risks, or regulatory contexts.

For example, an enterprise may already require privileged access reviews for infrastructure. An AI platform may also require review of permissions assigned to agents and their connected tools.

These requirements may share an access-governance objective, but they are not necessarily duplicates. Agent permissions may introduce distinct risks involving autonomous tool invocation, delegated authority, and multistep actions.

The organization should retain the specialized requirements needed to address those risks while reusing the enterprise control structure where appropriate.

A control consolidation assessment should examine the objective, scope, mandatory requirements, ownership, evidence, testing method, and external mappings of the affected controls.

If controls are consolidated, the organization must verify that the resulting definition preserves all material requirements. It should also update references in risk registers, implementation records, test procedures, and reporting systems.

Conflicting requirements should be escalated to the appropriate governance authority. The organization should not resolve conflicts informally by allowing individual system owners to select whichever control is easier to satisfy.

### 55. Managing AI Control Exceptions Across the Enterprise

Control exceptions should be managed consistently across business units and AI systems.

Without centralized oversight, one business unit may receive approval for a temporary deviation while another is required to implement a compensating control for the same condition. Repeated exceptions may also conceal systemic weaknesses in the organization's architecture or control design.

An enterprise exception process should establish:

- Standard criteria for requesting exceptions.
- Required risk and impact assessments.
- Approval authorities and delegation limits.
- Conditions for compensating controls.
- Maximum exception duration where defined by policy.
- Expiry and renewal requirements.
- Monitoring and escalation arrangements.
- Remediation and closure criteria.
- Reporting of recurring exceptions.

Exceptions should be recorded against the relevant control and system. Where one exception affects multiple systems, the record should identify the complete scope and the responsibilities of each affected owner.

The organization should analyze exception patterns to identify recurring problems.

For example, multiple business units may repeatedly request exceptions because a common AI provider does not support a required logging capability. This may indicate that the provider selection criteria, enterprise architecture, contractual requirements, or compensating safeguards need to be revised.

Exception reporting should distinguish between approved, pending, expired, rejected, and closed exceptions. An expired exception should not remain treated as authorized merely because remediation is incomplete.

The organization should also monitor repeated renewals. A temporary exception that is renewed several times may effectively become a permanent control gap without undergoing the appropriate governance decision.

Where the exception affects a mandatory legal or regulatory obligation, the organization must assess the applicable requirement directly. Internal approval cannot make an otherwise unlawful or prohibited activity permissible.

### 56. Integrating the Control Library with AI Governance Platforms

An enterprise AI governance platform can improve the consistency and traceability of control management by connecting the control library with the AI inventory, risk register, system cards, evidence repositories, change-management workflows, and assurance records.

The platform should support the governance process rather than dictate it. Automation cannot compensate for ambiguous control requirements, weak risk assessments, or unclear decision authority.

Useful platform capabilities include:

- Centralized control definitions and version history.
- Stable control identifiers and framework mappings.
- System-level applicability assignments.
- Links between risks and selected controls.
- Control ownership and accountability records.
- Implementation status tracking.
- Evidence references and retention metadata.
- Scheduled and event-driven review workflows.
- Exception and corrective-action management.
- Control-testing records and results.
- Management dashboards and escalation notifications.
- Historical reporting and audit trails.

Integration should reduce duplicate data entry and inconsistent records.

For example, when a new AI system is registered, its risk classification and system attributes may determine which control requirements need an applicability assessment. The system owner can then document implementation status and provide evidence references through the governance workflow.

However, automated applicability assignment should not be treated as a final decision where professional judgment, legal interpretation, or risk acceptance is required.

The platform should also distinguish between an automated status update and an approved governance decision.

For example, a workflow may identify that all required evidence fields have been populated. That does not necessarily mean the evidence is sufficient or that the control is effective. The appropriate reviewer must still evaluate the evidence where required.

Platform integration should preserve access controls, confidentiality, record integrity, and traceability. AI governance evidence may contain sensitive architecture details, security configurations, personal data, or confidential provider information.

### 57. Automating Control Monitoring and Evidence Collection

Automation can reduce the operational burden of collecting evidence and monitoring controls, particularly when controls operate frequently or generate large volumes of records.

Potential automation opportunities include:

- Collecting configuration data from AI platforms.
- Recording deployed model and system versions.
- Monitoring completion of required assessments.
- Detecting overdue control reviews.
- Identifying systems without assigned owners.
- Recording approval workflow outcomes.
- Collecting selected access and tool-invocation logs.
- Tracking control exceptions and expiry dates.
- Identifying changes that require reassessment.
- Generating control status reports from validated source records.

Automation is particularly valuable when the evidence source is reliable, structured, and available through controlled interfaces.

For example, a deployment pipeline can record the approved model version, required evaluation status, approver, and deployment timestamp. This creates a stronger audit trail than relying exclusively on manually maintained spreadsheets.

Nevertheless, automated evidence collection introduces its own risks.

The collection mechanism may omit relevant events, use incorrect mappings, fail silently, or capture data from an environment that does not correspond to the system under assessment.

The organization should establish controls over the evidence-collection process itself.

These may include source validation, access restrictions, timestamp integrity, monitoring of collection failures, reconciliation against authoritative inventories, and periodic verification that collected records remain complete.

Automation should also distinguish between missing evidence and evidence that demonstrates a control failure.

If a monitoring integration stops collecting records, the organization should not automatically mark the control as failed in every operational sense, but it must recognize that the inability to substantiate effectiveness is itself an assurance concern.

Automated status reporting should therefore reflect both the control's observed condition and the reliability of the evidence supporting that condition.

### 58. Managing AI Control Library Metrics and Key Performance Indicators

Control library metrics should provide management with insight into the quality, coverage, implementation, and effectiveness of the overall control environment.

Metrics should be selected to support specific management decisions rather than simply increase the number of reported indicators.

Useful categories include the following.

**Library quality metrics**

- Percentage of controls with complete required metadata.
- Percentage of controls with assigned owners.
- Percentage of controls with defined evidence and test criteria.
- Number of duplicate or conflicting control definitions.
- Percentage of framework mappings reviewed within the required period.

**Implementation metrics**

- Percentage of applicable controls implemented.
- Percentage of controls with approved implementation plans.
- Number of overdue control implementation actions.
- Percentage of systems with documented control applicability decisions.
- Percentage of inherited controls with current supporting evidence.

**Assurance metrics**

- Percentage of planned control tests completed.
- Percentage of tested controls rated effective.
- Number of significant control deficiencies.
- Percentage of findings supported by complete evidence.
- Percentage of corrective actions independently validated.

**Sustainability metrics**

- Number of repeated findings.
- Frequency of recurring control exceptions.
- Number of expired exceptions.
- Average time required to update controls following material changes in requirements.
- Percentage of control changes completed within defined governance timeframes.

Each metric should have a documented definition, data source, owner, reporting frequency, threshold, and intended management use.

For example, a high implementation percentage may appear favorable but could be misleading if many controls are incorrectly classified as not applicable or are marked implemented without sufficient evidence.

Management should interpret implementation metrics alongside control-testing results, exception trends, and residual-risk information.

Metrics should also avoid encouraging behavior that improves reported performance without improving actual risk management. A reduction in the number of open findings may indicate successful remediation, but it may also reflect weak detection or premature closure.

A balanced metric set should combine quantitative indicators with qualitative review and risk-based interpretation.

### 59. Establishing Control Library Maturity Levels

A control library maturity model can help an organization evaluate how consistently its control-management capabilities operate and identify priorities for improvement.

Maturity should reflect the quality of governance and assurance, not merely the number of controls documented or the sophistication of the software used.

An illustrative five-level model is shown below.

| Level                | Maturity description             | Typical characteristics                                                                                                          |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 — Initial    | Informal and reactive            | Controls are created inconsistently, ownership is unclear, and evidence is often collected only when requested                   |
| Level 2 — Defined    | Documented and repeatable        | Standard control records exist, responsibilities are assigned, and basic applicability and evidence requirements are established |
| Level 3 — Managed    | Governed and measured            | Formal approval, testing, exception management, and reporting processes operate across the defined scope                         |
| Level 4 — Integrated | Enterprise-wide and risk-aligned | Controls are connected to inventories, risk registers, change management, and shared enterprise services                         |
| Level 5 — Optimized  | Continuously improved            | Monitoring, assurance results, incident lessons, and emerging risks systematically inform control redesign and prioritization    |

The model is illustrative and should be adapted to the organization's governance methodology.

A maturity assessment should examine multiple dimensions, including control design quality, ownership, implementation coverage, evidence reliability, testing discipline, management oversight, and continual improvement.

An organization may have a highly automated evidence platform but still operate at a low maturity level if control objectives are poorly defined or management does not act on significant deficiencies.

Similarly, a smaller organization may have effective controls and reliable assurance without implementing a sophisticated governance platform.

Maturity ratings should therefore be evidence-based and supported by defined assessment criteria.

The purpose of maturity measurement is to identify meaningful capability gaps, establish realistic improvement objectives, and track progress over time.

### 60. Integrating Control Deficiencies with Corrective Action Management

Control deficiencies identified through testing, monitoring, incidents, regulatory reviews, or management assessments should be connected to a formal corrective-action process.

A control finding should not be considered resolved simply because an action has been assigned or a technical change has been completed.

The organization must establish whether the corrective action addresses the underlying deficiency and whether the resulting control operates effectively.

A corrective-action record should include:

- Finding identifier and associated control.
- Affected systems and business processes.
- Description of the observed deficiency.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Risk and impact assessment.
- Required corrective action.
- Accountable action owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards, where required.
- Required remediation evidence.
- Validation and closure criteria.
- Approval and closure records.

For example, a test may reveal that an AI deployment pipeline permits the release of a model version without the required evaluation approval.

The immediate corrective action may be to enforce the approval check. Root-cause analysis may additionally identify weak change governance, an alternative deployment path, inadequate testing of workflow controls, or unclear ownership.

The organization should address these underlying conditions rather than merely update the main pipeline while leaving the bypass mechanism available.

Corrective actions should be prioritized according to risk, and significant findings should be escalated to the appropriate governance authority.

Where remediation is delayed, management should assess whether temporary restrictions or additional safeguards are necessary and whether the remaining risk can be tolerated within the organization's approved boundaries.

Closure should require evidence that the agreed criteria have been met. For material deficiencies, independent validation may be necessary.

### 61. Conducting Root-Cause Analysis for Recurring Control Failures

Recurring control failures often indicate that the original remediation addressed the symptom rather than the underlying cause.

Root-cause analysis examines why the control failed and identifies the conditions that allowed the failure to occur or remain undetected.

Potential causes include:

- Inadequate control design.
- Incomplete implementation.
- Unclear ownership or decision authority.
- Insufficient staff competence.
- Poor integration between governance workflows and technical systems.
- Inadequate resources.
- Incorrect risk classification.
- Weak monitoring or evidence collection.
- Excessive dependence on manual processes.
- Inadequate provider oversight.
- Conflicting internal requirements.
- Poor change-management discipline.

For example, repeated failures to complete AI system reviews may initially appear to be an accountability problem. Investigation may reveal that system owners are not notified when reviews become due, the governance platform lacks reliable ownership data, and review requirements are unclear for systems using shared services.

Simply instructing owners to complete reviews may not resolve these weaknesses.

A more effective response may require correcting inventory records, automating review notifications, clarifying ownership, and defining the evidence required for review completion.

Root-cause analysis should distinguish between the immediate cause, contributing factors, and systemic conditions.

The analysis should be documented in proportion to the severity and recurrence of the issue.

Recurring findings should be aggregated across systems and business units to identify common patterns. A single root cause may affect numerous controls, while several apparently unrelated findings may originate from the same governance weakness.

The control library should be reviewed when findings indicate that existing control definitions or implementation guidance are inadequate.

### 62. Integrating the Control Library with Management Review

Management review provides a formal mechanism for evaluating whether the AI control environment remains suitable, adequate, and effective in the context of the organization's objectives and risk exposure.

Control library reporting should support management decisions rather than simply present activity statistics.

Management review inputs may include:

- Control implementation and effectiveness results.
- Significant control deficiencies.
- AI risk-register changes.
- Overdue remediation actions.
- Repeated control exceptions.
- Material incidents and near misses.
- Changes in legal and regulatory requirements.
- Changes in AI technology and provider dependencies.
- Results of internal and external assessments.
- Resource constraints and capability gaps.
- Control library quality and maturity.
- Recommendations for control redesign or prioritization.

Management should evaluate whether the control environment continues to address material risks and whether the organization has sufficient resources and authority to operate required controls.

For example, repeated findings across several AI systems may indicate that a control is difficult to implement consistently. Management may need to approve an architecture change, allocate engineering resources, revise implementation guidance, or reconsider the use of a particular provider.

Management review should produce documented decisions, assigned actions, accountable owners, and completion expectations.

It should also distinguish between a control that is ineffective and a risk that has been formally accepted. Acceptance of residual risk does not transform an ineffective control into an effective one.

The control library and associated reporting should preserve this distinction so that management receives an accurate view of both control performance and remaining exposure.

### 63. Responding to Regulatory and Standards Changes

AI governance requirements evolve as legislation, standards, regulatory interpretations, and contractual expectations change.

The control library should include a defined process for assessing whether new or amended requirements affect existing controls, system implementations, evidence expectations, or assurance procedures.

The process should include:

1. Identifying the authoritative source and relevant change.
2. Determining the applicability of the requirement.
3. Assessing the affected governance objectives and controls.
4. Identifying gaps in existing coverage.
5. Determining whether new controls or revisions are required.
6. Assessing affected AI systems and business processes.
7. Assigning implementation responsibilities.
8. Updating mappings and documentation.
9. Revising test procedures and evidence requirements.
10. Verifying implementation within the applicable timeframe.

The organization should distinguish between binding obligations, voluntary standards, supervisory expectations, and internal policy requirements.

These sources may have different legal status, scope, and effective dates. Mapping them into one control library should not erase those differences.

For example, an external framework may recommend a particular governance practice, while an applicable legal obligation may impose a specific requirement on a defined category of AI system. The organization should preserve the distinction in its requirement mapping and compliance assessment.

A change-management process should also consider transition periods, implementation deadlines, jurisdictional scope, and any conditions that determine when a requirement applies.

The control library should be updated only after the organization has established the relevance and interpretation of the change. Where legal interpretation is uncertain, the matter should be escalated to qualified legal or compliance personnel.

### 64. Integrating the AI Control Library with Enterprise Assurance

The AI control library should form part of the organization's broader assurance architecture, connecting first-line control operation, second-line oversight, and independent assurance activities.

The precise allocation of responsibilities depends on the organization's operating model, but the general principle is to separate the operation of controls from oversight and independent evaluation.

**First line — operational ownership:** Business units, system owners, engineering teams, and other operational functions implement and operate controls and maintain required evidence.

**Second line — risk and compliance oversight:** AI governance, risk management, compliance, security, and privacy functions establish requirements, challenge implementation, monitor exposure, and assess the quality of control management within their mandates.

**Third line — independent assurance:** Internal audit provides independent assurance regarding governance, risk management, and control effectiveness, subject to its approved mandate and professional standards.

The control library supports these functions by providing common control definitions, stable identifiers, risk mappings, and testing criteria.

However, common terminology does not eliminate the need for role clarity.

A second-line function may perform oversight testing without replacing the first line's responsibility for operating the control. Internal audit should retain appropriate independence from the control design and implementation decisions it evaluates.

Enterprise assurance planning should also consider reliance on previous assessments, shared controls, external reports, and provider assurance evidence.

Reliance should be based on the scope, quality, relevance, and timing of the available evidence. An external report covering a limited period or a specific service boundary may not be sufficient to establish that all controls required by a consuming AI system are effective.

The organization should document assurance coverage, material gaps, reliance decisions, and areas requiring additional testing.

### 65. Evaluating Control Library Effectiveness Through Outcomes

A mature control library should be evaluated by the extent to which it supports effective risk management, not solely by the number of controls it contains.

The organization should examine whether the library contributes to measurable improvements in control reliability, risk visibility, incident prevention, response capability, and governance consistency.

Relevant outcome indicators may include:

- Reduction in repeated control deficiencies.
- Improved coverage of high-risk AI systems.
- Fewer unauthorized or unapproved deployments.
- Faster identification of material control failures.
- Improved remediation timeliness.
- Reduced recurrence of similar incidents.
- More reliable evidence for compliance assessments.
- Better alignment between risk treatment decisions and actual control performance.
- Fewer inconsistent or conflicting control requirements.
- Improved consistency across business units and shared platforms.

Outcome analysis should consider other factors that may influence the results.

For example, an increase in reported AI security incidents may reflect improved detection rather than worsening security. A reduction in findings may reflect effective remediation, but it may also indicate reduced testing coverage.

The organization should interpret outcomes alongside testing results, monitoring coverage, incident data, system changes, and the quality of the assurance process.

It should also identify controls that create substantial administrative effort without providing meaningful risk reduction. Such controls may need redesign, consolidation, or replacement, provided that required obligations and risk-treatment objectives remain satisfied.

The purpose of continuous improvement is not simply to expand the control library. It is to maintain a coherent, proportionate, and effective set of requirements that reflects the organization's actual AI risk environment.

### 66. Conducting Periodic Control Library Reviews

Periodic reviews help ensure that the library remains relevant, complete, and aligned with the organization's AI systems, risk profile, and governance obligations.

A review should evaluate both individual controls and the overall architecture of the library.

Individual control reviews may examine whether the statement remains clear, the owner is correct, the applicability criteria remain valid, and the implementation and testing requirements are still appropriate.

Enterprise-level reviews may examine whether the library adequately covers new AI technologies, shared platforms, emerging threats, changed business processes, and revised external requirements.

Review triggers may include:

- A scheduled governance review.
- A significant AI incident.
- A material change in AI architecture.
- Introduction of agentic or other new AI capabilities.
- A change in applicable legal or regulatory requirements.
- Repeated control failures.
- Significant audit findings.
- A major provider or contractual change.
- Evidence of duplicated or conflicting controls.
- Changes to organizational risk appetite.

Review results should be documented and include decisions to retain, revise, add, consolidate, or retire controls.

Where a control is retired, its historical definition and relationships to prior assessments should be preserved. Where a control is revised materially, the organization should determine whether affected systems require reassessment or renewed approval.

The organization should also review whether control mappings remain accurate and whether implementation records reflect the current control version.

A periodic review should not be treated as effective merely because a review meeting occurred. The review must evaluate relevant evidence, identify necessary changes, and assign responsibility for implementing approved decisions.

### 67. Building an Enterprise AI Control Library Implementation Roadmap

An implementation roadmap provides a structured approach for establishing or improving the control library across the organization.

The roadmap should reflect the organization's current maturity, AI portfolio, risk exposure, available resources, and mandatory obligations.

A practical roadmap may be organized into five phases.

**Phase 1 — Establish the foundation**

Define the governance mandate, scope, domain structure, ownership model, control-record standard, and approval process.

Identify existing enterprise controls that can be reused and establish the relationship between the AI control library, AI inventory, risk register, system cards, and assurance processes.

**Phase 2 — Develop the priority control set**

Identify material AI risks and applicable requirements. Create or adapt the initial control set, prioritizing mandatory obligations and controls addressing high-risk systems, sensitive data, external exposure, and autonomous actions.

Define applicability criteria, evidence expectations, and minimum testing requirements.

**Phase 3 — Implement and integrate**

Assign controls to systems and processes, document implementation status, establish ownership, and connect controls to risk records and evidence repositories.

Integrate the control library with existing change management, incident response, supplier-risk, and enterprise-security processes where appropriate.

**Phase 4 — Test and validate**

Conduct design, implementation, and operating effectiveness assessments. Record deficiencies, evaluate residual risk, validate remediation, and establish reporting for management oversight.

**Phase 5 — Optimize and maintain**

Use testing results, incident lessons, changes in requirements, and recurring findings to improve control quality, simplify duplicated requirements, automate suitable evidence collection, and strengthen enterprise assurance.

Each phase should have defined deliverables, accountable owners, completion criteria, dependencies, and governance approval where required.

The roadmap should not assume that every control must be fully automated before the library can provide value. A well-governed manual control with clear ownership and reliable evidence may be appropriate, while an automated control with poor design or inadequate monitoring may provide limited assurance.

Implementation progress should be measured through verified outcomes, not simply the number of controls documented or assigned.

### 68. Common AI Control Library Failure Patterns

Organizations can establish extensive control libraries and still fail to achieve effective governance when the library is disconnected from operational reality.

Common failure patterns include the following.

**68.1 Checklist-driven control design**

Controls are copied from external frameworks without assessing applicability, implementation feasibility, or the organization's actual AI risks.

**68.2 Unclear control ownership**

Controls have named owners but no clear accountability for execution, evidence, remediation, or escalation.

**68.3 Inadequate testability**

Control statements use broad language without defining observable requirements or acceptance criteria.

**68.4 Mapping without substantive coverage**

Controls are mapped to numerous external requirements without sufficient analysis to demonstrate that their scope and implementation satisfy those requirements.

**68.5 False implementation confidence**

A control is marked implemented because a policy or technical feature exists, even though its operation and coverage have not been verified.

**68.6 Excessive manual administration**

The library relies on disconnected spreadsheets and repeated evidence requests, increasing the likelihood of stale records and inconsistent reporting.

**68.7 Weak exception governance**

Exceptions are renewed repeatedly without meaningful reassessment of risk or a credible remediation plan.

**68.8 Fragmented assurance**

Risk, compliance, security, and audit functions use different control identifiers, testing criteria, and evidence definitions, making enterprise-wide conclusions difficult.

**68.9 Overreliance on inherited controls**

The organization assumes that shared platforms or provider reports cover system-specific requirements without verifying scope and limitations.

**68.10 Failure to learn from findings**

Recurring control deficiencies are remediated individually without investigating common root causes or revising inadequate control definitions.

These patterns should be assessed during maturity reviews and internal assurance activities.

The corrective response should address the underlying governance weakness rather than merely expand the control inventory.

### 69. Defining Success Criteria for an AI Control Library

An AI control library should be considered successful when it provides a reliable mechanism for translating governance requirements into operational controls and supporting defensible assurance conclusions.

Success criteria should reflect the organization's objectives and risk profile, but typically include the following:

- Material AI risks have corresponding control-treatment relationships.
- Control statements are clear, testable, and appropriately scoped.
- Applicable controls have accountable owners.
- Implementation and evidence expectations are defined.
- Framework mappings are supported by documented analysis.
- Control applicability decisions are justified and traceable.
- Control testing evaluates design, implementation, and operation.
- Deficiencies are classified and remediated according to risk.
- Exceptions and residual-risk decisions are properly governed.
- Shared controls and inherited assurance are evaluated within their boundaries.
- Management receives reliable reporting on control coverage and effectiveness.
- Control changes preserve historical traceability.
- Lessons from incidents, audits, and changing requirements inform continual improvement.

The organization should periodically verify these criteria using documented evidence.

For example, a library may contain all required metadata and appear complete, but still fail to provide meaningful assurance if its control tests do not examine actual system behavior or if findings are routinely closed without validation.

Success therefore requires alignment between the library's documented requirements and the controls operating in the real environment.

### 70. Part 4 Core Principle

An enterprise AI control library is a governed component of the organization's AI risk-management and assurance architecture. Its value depends on the quality of its controls, the clarity of accountability, the reliability of implementation and evidence, and the ability to identify and correct weaknesses across the AI lifecycle.

Sustainable effectiveness requires controlled lifecycle management, consistent exception handling, integration with governance platforms, risk-based metrics, independent assurance, and continual improvement informed by incidents, testing, and changing requirements.

The ultimate objective is not to maximize the number of controls or achieve a superficial completion percentage. It is to maintain a coherent, proportionate, and auditable control environment that demonstrates how material AI risks are addressed, how control effectiveness is evaluated, and how deficiencies are resolved or escalated through accountable governance decisions.
