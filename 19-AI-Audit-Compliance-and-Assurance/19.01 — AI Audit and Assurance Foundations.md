# 19.01 — AI Audit and Assurance Foundations

## Part 1 — AI Audit and Assurance Foundations, Governance, Scope, Objectives and Core Concepts

### 1. Definition of AI Audit

AI audit is a systematic and documented examination of AI-related governance, risk management, controls, processes, systems, evidence, and outcomes against defined criteria.

AI audit may examine:

* AI governance
* AI security
* AI risk management
* AI policies
* AI controls
* AI systems
* AI models
* AI data
* AI suppliers
* AI lifecycle processes
* AI compliance
* AI documentation
* AI monitoring
* AI incident management

The audit objective should define what is being examined and against which criteria.

**AI Audit ≠ General Technology Audit Automatically.**

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Audit

The purpose of an AI audit is to provide structured and evidence-based evaluation of defined AI governance, risk, control, compliance, or assurance objectives.

Typical objectives include:

* Evaluating control design
* Evaluating control implementation
* Testing operating effectiveness
* Assessing governance processes
* Assessing risk-management practices
* Evaluating compliance against defined criteria
* Identifying control weaknesses
* Identifying documentation gaps
* Evaluating evidence
* Supporting management decisions
* Providing assurance to governance bodies

The exact audit objective determines the appropriate scope and methodology.

---

### 3. AI Audit vs AI Assurance

AI audit and AI assurance are closely related but should not automatically be treated as identical.

**AI Audit** generally refers to a structured examination performed against defined criteria.

**AI Assurance** is broader and concerns providing confidence over defined subject matter for intended users.

```text id="ai19d01-01"

        |

      FINDING
        |

     CONCLUSION
        |
        v
     ASSURANCE
```

An audit can be an assurance activity, but not every assurance activity necessarily constitutes an audit.

---

### 4. AI Audit vs AI Assessment

An assessment evaluates a defined subject against specified criteria.

An audit generally requires a more formal and systematic examination, including defined scope, evidence, testing, findings, and conclusions.

Examples of assessments include:

* AI risk assessment
* AI maturity assessment
* NIST AI RMF assessment
* AI Act compliance assessment
* Control assessment

**Assessment ≠ Audit Automatically.**

The terminology should be determined by the applicable methodology and engagement objective.

---

### 5. AI Audit vs AI Inspection

An inspection generally focuses on examining a particular condition, process, asset, or evidence set.

An AI audit may encompass broader governance and control objectives.

For example:

* Inspecting an AI model's documentation is an inspection activity.
* Evaluating whether model documentation controls operate effectively may form part of an audit.

**Inspection ≠ Complete Audit.**

---

### 6. AI Audit vs AI Review

A review may involve evaluating information, processes, or controls without necessarily providing the same level of testing or assurance as a formal audit.

The engagement terms should define:

* Scope
* Criteria
* Procedures
* Evidence
* Limitations
* Reporting
* Assurance level

**Audit Scope ≠ Entire AI Environment Automatically.**

### 11. AI Audit Objective

The audit objective defines what the auditor intends to determine.

Examples include:

* Whether AI governance controls are appropriately designed
* Whether required controls have been implemented
* Whether controls operate effectively
* Whether defined policy requirements are being followed
* Whether evidence supports defined compliance claims
* Whether AI risks are appropriately managed

An audit should avoid ambiguous objectives such as simply "audit AI."

Examples include:

* All AI systems
* All critical AI systems
* All AI models
* All AI suppliers
* All security incidents
* All policy exceptions
* All control tests
* All AI risk assessments

Population definition is fundamental to audit sampling and conclusions.

### 15. AI Audit Materiality

* Strategic importance
* AI criticality
* Risk exposure

Materiality methodology should be documented.

**Materiality ≠ Severity Automatically.**

### 16. AI Audit Risk

Audit risk is the possibility that an audit conclusion may be inappropriate because relevant conditions were not identified or adequately evaluated.

Potential sources include:

* Incomplete populations
* Insufficient evidence
* Sampling limitations
* Incorrect criteria
* Misinterpretation
* Undetected control failures
* Data-quality problems
* Management representations
* Supplier limitations

Audit risk should be considered when designing procedures.

### 20. AI Audit Procedure

An audit procedure is a defined activity performed to obtain and evaluate evidence.

Examples include:

* Inquiry
* Inspection
* Observation
* Recalculation
* Reperformance
* Confirmation
* Data analysis
* Configuration review
* Sampling
* Control testing

Different procedures provide different levels and types of evidence.

---

### 21. AI Inquiry

Inquiry involves obtaining information from knowledgeable personnel.

Examples:

* Interviewing AI governance owners
* Asking model owners about deployment processes
* Discussing security controls with security personnel
* Asking compliance teams about regulatory mappings

Inquiry is useful but should generally be corroborated where stronger evidence is required.

**Inquiry ≠ Independent Proof Automatically.**

---

### 22. AI Inspection

Inspection involves examining evidence, records, configurations, documents, or physical/digital artifacts.

Examples include:

* AI policies
* Model documentation
* Model cards
* System cards
* Risk assessments
* Access configurations
* Logs
* Audit trails
* Supplier reports

---

### 23. AI Observation

Observation involves directly observing a process or activity.

Examples include:

* Observing AI deployment approval
* Observing control operation
* Observing incident response
* Observing access review procedures

Observation provides evidence about observed behavior at a particular point in time.

**Observation ≠ Evidence of Continuous Operation Automatically.**

---

### 24. AI Reperformance

Reperformance involves independently executing a control or procedure to determine whether the expected result can be reproduced.

Examples:

* Reperforming an AI access review
* Recalculating a compliance metric
* Reperforming a risk calculation
* Reexecuting a control validation procedure

Reperformance can provide stronger evidence than inquiry alone.

---

### 25. AI Recalculation

Recalculation independently verifies mathematical or computational accuracy.

Examples include:

* Risk scores
* Compliance percentages
* KPI calculations
* Control effectiveness rates
* Model performance metrics

```text id="ai19d01-06"

    |
    v

    |
    v
RECALCULATION
    |
    v
COMPARE RESULTS
    |
    +---- MATCH
    |
    +---- DIFFERENCE
```

A correct calculation does not establish that the underlying data is correct.

---

### 26. AI Confirmation

Confirmation obtains information directly from an independent or appropriate source.

Examples include:

* Supplier confirmation
* System-owner confirmation
* Control-owner confirmation
* Independent evidence confirmation

The reliability of confirmation depends on the source and methodology.

---

* AI inventories
* Model registries

* Risk registers

* Policy exceptions
* Control testing results
* Supplier records

Analytics can identify anomalies, patterns, duplicates, missing records, and potential exceptions.

**Analytics ≠ Audit Conclusion Automatically.**

---

### 28. AI Audit Evidence

Audit evidence consists of information used to support audit conclusions.

Examples include:

* Documents
* Records
* System data
* Logs
* Configurations
* Interviews
* Observations
* Reperformance results
* Test results
* Third-party reports

Evidence should be evaluated for relevance, reliability, sufficiency, and appropriateness.

---

### 29. AI Evidence Relevance

Relevant evidence directly relates to the audit objective and criterion being evaluated.

For example, a current AI access-control configuration may be relevant to an access-control test.

An unrelated security policy would not necessarily demonstrate that the control operated effectively.

---

### 30. AI Evidence Reliability

Evidence reliability considers how trustworthy the source and method are.

Factors may include:

* Source authority
* Independence
* Integrity
* Directness
* Timeliness
* System-generated nature
* Corroboration

Evidence obtained directly from a controlled system may have different reliability characteristics from an unsupported management statement.

---

### 31. AI Evidence Sufficiency

Sufficiency concerns whether enough evidence has been obtained to support the intended conclusion.

More evidence is not automatically better if it is redundant or irrelevant.

The auditor should consider:

* Risk
* Materiality
* Population
* Sampling
* Evidence quality
* Contradictory evidence

**Evidence Quantity ≠ Evidence Sufficiency Automatically.**

---

### 32. AI Evidence Appropriateness

Appropriateness concerns the quality and relevance of evidence supporting the conclusion.

A large amount of weak evidence may be less useful than a smaller amount of high-quality evidence.

---

### 35. AI Audit Finding

An audit finding identifies a condition where the evidence does not fully satisfy the applicable audit criterion or where a control weakness has been identified.

A finding should normally describe:

* Condition
* Criteria
* Cause, where supported
* Effect or risk
* Evidence
* Management response

**Finding ≠ Risk Automatically.**

A finding describes an observed condition; risk analysis explains its potential significance.

---

### 36. AI Audit Observation

An observation is an identified condition that may require attention but does not necessarily represent a confirmed control failure.

Organizations should define their own classification methodology.

Possible classifications may include:

* Observation
* Opportunity for improvement
* Minor finding
* Major finding
* Critical finding

Classification should be evidence-based.

---

### 37. AI Audit Exception

An exception occurs when observed evidence differs from a defined requirement, criterion, or expected condition.

Examples include:

* Missing approval
* Expired assessment
* Incomplete evidence
* Unapproved AI system
* Control operating outside defined parameters

The significance of an exception depends on its context and applicable criteria.

---

### 38. AI Audit Nonconformity

A nonconformity is a failure to fulfill a specified requirement within the applicable audit criteria.

The term should be used according to the applicable standard or assessment methodology.

**Nonconformity ≠ Legal Violation Automatically.**

A nonconformity against an organizational policy or management-system requirement is not automatically a statutory violation.

---

### 39. AI Audit Root Cause

Root cause analysis seeks to identify the underlying reason for an observed condition.

Potential categories include:

* Process weakness
* Governance weakness
* Technology limitation
* Human error
* Inadequate training
* Supplier dependency
* Data-quality problem
* Inadequate control design

Root cause should be supported by evidence.

**Correlation ≠ Root Cause.**

**Auditor Hypothesis ≠ Confirmed Root Cause.**

---

### 40. AI Audit Recommendation

A recommendation identifies a proposed action to address an observed weakness or improve governance.

Recommendations should be:

* Relevant
* Proportionate
* Risk-informed
* Actionable
* Assigned to an appropriate owner

The auditor should avoid assuming management's operational responsibility.

---

### 41. AI Audit Corrective Action

A corrective action addresses an identified nonconformity, control weakness, or other defined condition.

Corrective action should normally identify:

* Action
* Owner
* Due date
* Root cause
* Evidence
* Validation

**Corrective Action Completion ≠ Corrective Action Effectiveness.**

---

### 42. AI Audit Follow-Up

Follow-up determines whether agreed actions have been:

* Implemented
* Supported by evidence
* Validated
* Closed appropriately

Follow-up should consider whether the action addressed the underlying condition rather than merely closing the administrative task.

---

### 43. AI Audit Independence

Audit independence means maintaining sufficient organizational and professional separation to support objective conclusions.

Potential threats include:

* Auditing one's own work
* Conflicts of interest
* Management pressure
* Undue influence
* Personal interests
* Operational ownership

The required degree of independence depends on the audit context and applicable professional framework.

---

### 44. AI Audit Objectivity

Objectivity requires conclusions to be based on evidence and defined criteria rather than predetermined outcomes.

An auditor should distinguish:

* Evidence
* Interpretation
* Professional judgment
* Assumption
* Management representation

**Objectivity ≠ Absence of Professional Judgment.**

Professional judgment remains necessary when evidence requires interpretation.

### 50. Core AI Audit and Assurance Principle

AI audit should be understood as a **structured, evidence-based, criteria-driven examination of defined AI governance, risk, control, security, compliance, or assurance subject matter within a clearly established scope and period**.

A defensible AI audit should establish:

* What is being audited
* Why it is being audited
* Which criteria apply
* What population is in scope
* What risks are relevant
* What procedures will be performed
* What evidence is required
* How evidence will be evaluated
* How findings will be classified
* How conclusions will be supported
* What limitations exist
* Who is accountable for corrective action
* How follow-up will occur

The fundamental distinctions are:

**AI Audit ≠ AI Assessment.**

**AI Audit ≠ AI Assurance Automatically.**

**Audit Scope ≠ Entire AI Environment.**

**Audit Criteria ≠ Every Applicable Requirement Automatically.**

**Inquiry ≠ Independent Proof.**

**Observation ≠ Continuous Evidence.**

**Evidence Quantity ≠ Evidence Sufficiency.**

**Evidence Existence ≠ Evidence Appropriateness.**

**Management Representation ≠ Sufficient Evidence Automatically.**

**Finding ≠ Risk Automatically.**

**Exception ≠ Material Finding Automatically.**

**Nonconformity ≠ Legal Violation Automatically.**

**Corrective Action Completion ≠ Corrective Action Effectiveness.**

**Correlation ≠ Root Cause.**

**Analytics ≠ Audit Conclusion Automatically.**

**Management Attestation ≠ Independent Assurance.**

**Automation ≠ Auditor Accountability.**

**Internal Audit Criteria ≠ Statutory Requirements Automatically.**

**Internal Control ≠ Legal Requirement Automatically.**

**Internal Audit Finding ≠ Regulatory Breach Automatically.**

15. **Regulatory requirements must not be conflated with internal controls or recommended practices.**
16. **Maturity must not be represented as compliance.**
17. **Assurance coverage must not be represented as assurance effectiveness.**
18. **Continuous monitoring must not automatically be represented as continuous assurance.**
19. **Supplier assurance must not automatically be treated as enterprise assurance.**
