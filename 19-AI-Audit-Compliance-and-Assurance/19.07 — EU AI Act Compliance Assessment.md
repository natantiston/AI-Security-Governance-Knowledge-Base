# 19.07 — EU AI Act Compliance Assessment

## Part 1 — EU AI Act Compliance Foundations, Legal Applicability, Risk Classification and Core Assessment Concepts

### 1. Definition of EU AI Act Compliance Assessment

An EU AI Act Compliance Assessment is a structured evaluation of whether an organization, AI system, general-purpose AI model, or relevant activity complies with the applicable requirements of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended.

The assessment examines the legal obligations applicable to the subject under review and evaluates whether appropriate controls, processes, documentation, and evidence demonstrate compliance.

An assessment may examine:

- Applicability of the EU AI Act
- AI system identification and classification
- Prohibited AI practices
- Provider and deployer obligations
- General-purpose AI model obligations
- Transparency requirements
- High-risk AI system requirements
- Conformity assessment
- Technical documentation
- Human oversight
- AI risk management
- Data governance
- Cybersecurity
- Post-market monitoring
- Regulatory reporting and cooperation

An assessment should be based on the applicable legal text, relevant implementing measures, authoritative guidance, and the facts of the specific AI use case.

**Compliance Assessment ≠ Certification Automatically.**

---

### 2. Purpose of EU AI Act Compliance Assessment

The purpose of an EU AI Act Compliance Assessment is to determine whether applicable legal obligations have been correctly identified, implemented, and supported by appropriate evidence.

Its purposes may include:

- Establishing whether the Regulation applies
- Identifying the organization's legal role
- Determining the relevant AI risk category
- Mapping applicable legal obligations
- Evaluating compliance controls
- Identifying regulatory gaps
- Assessing the reliability of compliance evidence
- Supporting conformity assessment activities
- Preparing for regulatory scrutiny
- Establishing corrective actions
- Supporting management accountability
- Monitoring compliance over time

The assessment should distinguish legal obligations that apply directly from recommended practices that may help an organization demonstrate or support compliance.

**Legal Applicability ≠ Organizational Preference.**

---

### 3. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment vs AI Governance Assessment

An AI governance assessment evaluates the effectiveness of governance arrangements, including accountability, policies, oversight, risk ownership, and decision-making.

An EU AI Act Compliance Assessment specifically evaluates obligations arising under the Regulation.

| Dimension           | EU AI Act Compliance Assessment                     | AI Governance Assessment                                   |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary objective   | Evaluate applicable legal obligations               | Evaluate governance effectiveness                          |
| Assessment criteria | EU AI Act and applicable related legal requirements | Governance principles, policies, frameworks and objectives |
| Scope               | Legally relevant systems, actors and activities     | Organization-defined governance scope                      |
| Main output         | Compliance conclusions and regulatory gaps          | Governance findings and improvement opportunities          |
| Legal significance  | Directly connected to applicable legal duties       | May include legal and non-legal criteria                   |

A governance assessment can support AI Act compliance, but it does not automatically establish compliance with every applicable legal requirement.

---

### 4. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment vs AI Audit

An AI audit is a structured assurance engagement that evaluates defined criteria using audit procedures, evidence, findings, and conclusions.

An EU AI Act Compliance Assessment may be conducted as part of an internal audit, compliance review, conformity assessment, or another defined evaluation activity.

An internal assessment does not automatically have the legal status of a conformity assessment under the Regulation.

The assessment method should identify:

- The assessment owner
- The applicable criteria
- The scope
- The evaluation methodology
- The evidence requirements
- The limitations
- The reporting audience
- The authority of the resulting conclusion

**Internal Compliance Assessment ≠ Statutory Conformity Assessment Automatically.**

---

### 5. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment vs Conformity Assessment

A conformity assessment is a legally defined process used to demonstrate that applicable requirements have been fulfilled.

Under the AI Act, certain AI systems must undergo the conformity assessment procedure applicable to their classification and circumstances before being placed on the market or put into service.

Depending on the relevant provisions, the procedure may involve internal control, assessment by a notified body, or other legally specified arrangements.

An internal compliance assessment can identify readiness gaps and evaluate supporting evidence. It cannot replace a legally required conformity assessment procedure.

The organization must identify the specific conformity assessment route applicable to the system.

**Internal Assessment ≠ Legally Required Conformity Assessment.**

---

### 6. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment vs Legal Opinion

A compliance assessment evaluates evidence and practices against defined requirements.

A legal opinion interprets legal questions and applies legal reasoning to the relevant facts.

An assessment may require legal interpretation when determining:

- Territorial scope
- The definition of an AI system
- Provider or deployer status
- High-risk classification
- Applicability of an exception
- Interaction with other legislation
- The effect of transitional provisions

Where a material legal question remains uncertain, the organization should obtain appropriately qualified legal advice.

An assessor should document unresolved interpretations rather than presenting an uncertain legal conclusion as established fact.

**Compliance Assessment ≠ Legal Opinion Automatically.**

---

### 7. Legal Foundation of the EU AI Act

The principal legal instrument is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, known as the Artificial Intelligence Act.

The Regulation establishes harmonized rules concerning artificial intelligence within its defined scope.

Its objectives include promoting the uptake of human-centric and trustworthy AI while protecting health, safety, fundamental rights, and other legally protected interests.

The AI Act uses a risk-based regulatory approach. Obligations depend on factors such as the type of AI activity, the role of the operator, the intended purpose, and the relevant risk classification.

The Regulation must be interpreted using its applicable legal text, including amendments that have entered into force.

Primary legal reference:

[https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng)

**Regulatory Guidance ≠ Primary Legislation.**

---

### 8. Legal Status of the EU AI Act

The AI Act is an EU regulation rather than a voluntary industry framework.

An applicable regulation creates legal obligations according to its provisions, scope, application dates, and relevant conditions.

Compliance cannot be established solely by showing alignment with a voluntary framework, certification, internal policy, or industry code.

The assessor should distinguish:

- Binding legal provisions
- Applicable delegated or implementing acts
- Legally relevant harmonized standards
- Authoritative regulatory guidance
- Voluntary codes of practice
- Industry frameworks
- Internal organizational policies

The legal status and effect of each source should be recorded in the assessment methodology.

**Framework Alignment ≠ Legal Compliance Automatically.**

---

### 9. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Scope

Assessment scope defines the boundaries of the compliance evaluation.

The scope may identify:

- Legal entities
- AI systems
- General-purpose AI models
- Business units
- Products and services
- AI use cases
- Providers and deployers
- Importers and distributors
- Suppliers and downstream customers
- Geographic markets
- Relevant legal obligations
- Assessment period
- AI lifecycle stages

The scope should be sufficiently precise to identify what was evaluated and what was excluded.

---

### 10. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Objective

The assessment objective defines what the evaluation is intended to determine.

Examples include:

- Whether the AI Act applies to a particular system
- Whether the organization has correctly identified its legal role
- Whether an AI system is correctly classified
- Whether prohibited practices are present
- Whether transparency obligations are implemented
- Whether required technical documentation exists
- Whether the relevant conformity assessment route has been followed
- Whether required post-market processes are established

The objective should be specific enough to guide evidence collection and support a defensible conclusion.

**Assessment Objective ≠ Assessment Scope.**

---

### 11. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria are the requirements against which the relevant subject matter is evaluated.

Criteria may include:

- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended
- Applicable delegated acts
- Applicable implementing acts
- Applicable harmonized standards
- Relevant product-safety legislation
- Applicable data protection legislation
- Applicable sector-specific legislation
- Legally relevant contractual requirements
- Internal controls designed to implement legal duties

The assessor should identify the legal source, relevant provision, applicability conditions, and required compliance outcome.

Internal policy requirements should be distinguished from obligations imposed directly by legislation.

**Assessment Criterion ≠ Legal Obligation Automatically.**

---

### 12. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Population

The assessment population is the collection of AI systems, models, activities, operators, or obligations considered potentially relevant to the assessment.

It may include:

- Internally developed AI systems
- Purchased AI applications
- Embedded AI features
- Generative AI tools
- AI agents
- Third-party AI services
- General-purpose AI models
- AI-enabled products
- AI systems used by employees
- AI systems supplied to customers

The population should be reconciled against available organizational records.

---

### 13. EU AI Act Risk-Based Regulatory Approach

The AI Act differentiates requirements according to the nature and risk of AI activities.

Its regulatory structure includes prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, transparency obligations, and requirements for general-purpose AI models, alongside provisions applicable across the Regulation's scope.

Not every AI system is subject to the same obligations.

The assessor should determine the applicable requirements from the actual legal provisions rather than applying a single checklist indiscriminately.

Risk-based assessment should consider:

- Intended purpose
- Potential effects on people
- Fundamental-rights implications
- Health and safety implications
- AI system classification
- Operator role
- Relevant exceptions
- Application dates
- Applicable sector legislation

**AI Act Risk Category ≠ Enterprise Risk Rating Automatically.**

---

### 14. AI System Identification

Before assessing compliance, the organization should determine whether the subject matter meets the legal definition of an AI system.

The AI Act defines an AI system by reference to a machine-based system that operates with varying levels of autonomy, may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and infers from inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

The assessment should consider the legal definition and relevant interpretive guidance.

Relevant questions include:

- What does the system do?
- How does it generate outputs?
- Does it infer how to produce those outputs?
- What is its intended purpose?
- How does it influence the environment or decisions?
- Is the component under review an AI system, a model, or a non-AI component?

The product label used by a vendor does not determine legal classification by itself.

**Product Marketing Description ≠ Legal Classification.**

---

### 15. AI System vs AI Model

An AI model is not necessarily the same as the complete AI system in which it is used.

An AI system may include:

- One or more models
- Data pipelines
- Application logic
- User interfaces
- Retrieval components
- Tools and integrations
- Infrastructure
- Monitoring
- Human interaction
- Output-processing mechanisms

A compliance assessment should identify the regulated subject matter and the relevant system boundaries.

For example, a third-party language model may be integrated into an organization's customer-support application. The model provider and the organization operating the complete application may have different obligations.

**AI Model ≠ Complete AI System Automatically.**

---

### 16. Intended Purpose

Intended purpose is central to determining how an AI system should be classified and which requirements apply.

The assessment should examine the purpose intended by the provider, as reflected in relevant instructions, technical documentation, marketing materials, and other applicable evidence.

It should also consider the actual deployment context where relevant to the applicable legal provisions.

Examples include:

- General customer support
- Recruitment candidate screening
- Educational assessment
- Medical diagnosis support
- Creditworthiness evaluation
- Biometric identification
- Critical infrastructure management

The same underlying technology may be subject to different requirements depending on its intended purpose and context of use.

**Same Technology ≠ Same Regulatory Classification.**

---

### 17. Actual Use and Foreseeable Misuse

The assessment should distinguish intended use from actual use and reasonably foreseeable misuse where the applicable legal requirements make those circumstances relevant.

An organization should not rely exclusively on a vendor's general product description if the organization configures or uses the system for a materially different purpose.

Assessment questions may include:

- How is the system actually used?
- Has its purpose changed?
- Have new users or populations been introduced?
- Is it used for decisions beyond the original design?
- Have integrations materially changed its behavior?
- Could the use affect its legal classification?

Material changes in use may require reassessment of legal role, classification, risk, and compliance obligations.

**Vendor Intended Purpose ≠ Every Customer Use Automatically.**

---

### 18. Territorial Applicability

The AI Act has defined territorial and market-related scope.

Depending on the applicable provisions, it can cover relevant providers placing AI systems or general-purpose AI models on the EU market, deployers located or established in the EU, and certain providers or deployers outside the EU where system outputs are used within the Union.

The assessment should evaluate the specific facts rather than assuming that location alone determines applicability.

Relevant factors may include:

- Provider establishment
- Deployer establishment
- Place of market placement
- Place of putting into service
- Intended market
- Location where outputs are used
- Relevant legal exceptions

Cross-border activities may require additional analysis.

**Non-EU Establishment ≠ Automatic Exemption.**

---

### 19. Operator Roles Under the EU AI Act

The AI Act establishes obligations for different categories of operators.

Relevant roles include:

- Provider
- Deployer
- Importer
- Distributor
- Product manufacturer in relevant circumstances
- Authorized representative, where applicable

An organization may occupy more than one role depending on its activities and the system involved.

For example, an organization may deploy a third-party AI system internally while also acting as a provider when it develops and supplies a separate AI system under its own name.

The assessor should determine the role for each relevant system and activity.

**Organizational Identity ≠ Single AI Act Role Automatically.**

---

### 20. Provider Identification

A provider is generally the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that develops an AI system or has an AI system developed and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark, subject to the Regulation's definitions and conditions.

Provider status can carry substantial obligations.

Assessment procedures should examine:

- Who developed or commissioned the system?
- Under whose name is it supplied?
- Who determines the relevant intended purpose?
- Who controls the technical documentation?
- Who is responsible for required conformity activities?
- Have changes affected the allocation of responsibilities?

Contractual labels should not be treated as conclusive if the actual circumstances indicate a different legal role.

**Contractual Label ≠ Provider Status Automatically.**

---

### 21. Deployer Identification

A deployer is generally a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body using an AI system under its authority, except where the system is used in the course of a personal, non-professional activity, subject to the Regulation's definitions and exceptions.

A deployer may have obligations concerning:

- Use in accordance with instructions
- Human oversight
- Input data under its control
- Monitoring
- Record retention where applicable
- Workplace information and consultation requirements where applicable
- Fundamental-rights impact assessment where applicable
- Incident handling and escalation

The exact obligations depend on the system, use case, operator role, and applicable provisions.

**Deployer Status ≠ Provider Status Automatically.**

---

### 22. Importer and Distributor Responsibilities

Importers and distributors may have obligations under the AI Act when the relevant conditions apply.

An importer generally places on the EU market an AI system bearing the name or trademark of a provider established outside the Union.

A distributor generally makes an AI system available on the Union market within the meaning of the Regulation.

Assessment procedures should determine whether these roles apply and identify their relevant obligations.

Potential assessment areas include:

- Provider identification
- Required documentation
- Conformity indicators
- Instructions and information
- Storage and transport conditions
- Corrective actions
- Cooperation with competent authorities

The applicable legal provisions and exceptions should be checked for each role.

---

### 23. Role Changes and Provider Deeming Rules

Certain changes or activities may cause an operator to assume provider responsibilities under the AI Act.

Relevant circumstances can include placing an AI system on the market or putting it into service under one's own name or trademark, making certain substantial modifications to a high-risk AI system, or changing the intended purpose of a system in a way that causes it to become high-risk, subject to the applicable provisions.

The assessor should examine:

- System modifications
- Changes in intended purpose
- Rebranding
- Integration into products
- Allocation of development responsibilities
- Changes in market placement
- Contractual and operational arrangements

The precise legal consequences must be assessed against the relevant provisions.

**System Modification ≠ Provider Status Automatically.**

---

### 24. AI Supply-Chain Responsibility

AI systems may depend on multiple organizations.

A supply chain may include:

- Foundation-model providers
- Model hosting providers
- Dataset providers
- Cloud providers
- Application developers
- System integrators
- Product manufacturers
- Distributors
- Deployers

Compliance responsibilities do not necessarily fall on a single organization.

The assessment should identify the relevant actors, their legal roles, their contractual responsibilities, and the information needed to demonstrate compliance.

Supplier documentation can support an assessment but does not automatically discharge the organization's own legal duties.

**Supplier Compliance Evidence ≠ Complete Customer Compliance.**

---

### 25. Prohibited AI Practices

Article 5 of the AI Act prohibits specified AI practices, subject to the wording, conditions, and exceptions in the applicable legal text.

The assessment should determine whether an AI system or activity falls within a prohibited practice.

The analysis may need to consider:

- The specific practice
- The system's purpose
- The affected individuals or groups
- The relevant methods
- The intended or actual effects
- Applicable legal conditions
- Any narrowly defined exception

The assessment should not treat every controversial or high-risk AI use as automatically prohibited. It must evaluate the relevant legal elements.

Primary reference: Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended.

**High Risk ≠ Prohibited Practice Automatically.**

---

### 26. Assessment of Prohibited-Practice Exposure

The organization should establish procedures to identify potentially prohibited AI activities before deployment and when material changes occur.

The assessment may examine:

- AI use-case inventories
- Business process descriptions
- Vendor capabilities
- Data sources
- Target populations
- Behavioral influence mechanisms
- Biometric functionality
- Decision-making practices
- Intended and actual outcomes

Where a use case may fall within a prohibition, it should be escalated for qualified legal and compliance review before the organization concludes that it is permitted.

A general-purpose vendor description is not sufficient to establish that a particular implementation is lawful.

---

### 27. High-Risk AI System Classification

High-risk classification is a central component of the AI Act compliance assessment.

Article 6 establishes the classification framework, including AI systems associated with specified regulated products and systems falling within the relevant use cases in Annex III.

The assessment should determine which classification route is relevant and whether the legal conditions are satisfied.

It should not classify a system as high-risk merely because the organization assigns it a high internal risk score.

Equally, an organization should not conclude that a system is outside the high-risk category without documenting the legal analysis.

**Enterprise High Risk ≠ AI Act High Risk Automatically.**

---

### 28. High-Risk Classification Under Article 6(1)

Article 6(1) addresses AI systems that are products, or safety components of products, covered by specified Union harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I, where the relevant third-party conformity assessment requirements apply.

The assessment should identify:

- The product or safety component
- The applicable product legislation
- The relationship between the AI system and the product
- The applicable conformity assessment requirements
- The relevant legal classification conditions

The AI Act's obligations for this category have a distinct application timeline.

The assessment should not apply the Annex III classification method as a substitute for analyzing Article 6(1).

---

### 29. High-Risk Classification Under Article 6(2) and Annex III

Article 6(2) concerns AI systems identified in Annex III, subject to the conditions and exceptions in the Regulation.

Annex III addresses specified areas and use cases, including certain applications relating to:

- Biometrics
- Critical infrastructure
- Education and vocational training
- Employment and worker management
- Access to essential private and public services
- Law enforcement
- Migration, asylum and border control
- Administration of justice and democratic processes

The assessment must examine the actual use case and relevant legal definitions.

A system operating in one of these broad sectors is not necessarily high-risk in every conceivable use. The specific Annex III entry and applicable conditions must be evaluated.

---

### 30. Assessment of High-Risk Exceptions

The AI Act provides specific circumstances in which certain systems falling within Annex III may not be considered high-risk under the relevant provisions.

These exceptions are conditional and should not be treated as general exemptions for an entire sector or technology.

The assessment should document:

- The potentially applicable Annex III entry
- The relevant exception
- The facts supporting the exception
- Whether the system performs profiling of natural persons where relevant
- Whether the applicable legal conditions are satisfied
- Any registration or documentation requirements that remain applicable

The conclusion should be supported by a reasoned classification record.

**Potential Exception ≠ Automatic Exemption.**

---

### 31. Biometric AI Assessment

Biometric AI requires careful analysis because the AI Act distinguishes among different biometric functions and use cases.

Assessment may need to identify whether the system performs:

- Biometric identification
- Biometric verification
- Biometric categorisation
- Emotion recognition
- Remote biometric identification
- Other biometric processing relevant to the applicable provisions

The legal consequences depend on the exact functionality, context, operator, purpose, and relevant exceptions.

The assessment should also consider other applicable legislation, including data protection law where personal data is processed.

**Biometric Functionality ≠ Single Regulatory Category.**

---

### 32. Employment and Worker-Management AI

AI systems used in employment and worker-management contexts may fall within Annex III high-risk categories when the applicable legal conditions are met.

Relevant use cases can include certain systems used for recruitment, selection, decisions affecting employment conditions, task allocation based on individual behavior or personal traits, and monitoring or evaluation of workers.

The assessment should identify:

- The employment-related purpose
- The affected individuals
- The system's influence on decisions
- The level of human involvement
- The relevant Annex III entry
- Applicable exceptions
- Additional employment and data-protection requirements

A tool used by an employer is not automatically high-risk solely because it uses AI; its actual function and legal classification must be evaluated.

---

### 33. Education and Essential-Services AI

Certain AI systems used in education, vocational training, access to essential services, or related decision-making may be high-risk under Annex III.

Assessment should examine the exact use case, including whether the system influences access, evaluation, allocation, eligibility, or other decisions identified in the relevant provisions.

Examples for analysis may include:

- Admission or access decisions
- Learning outcome evaluation
- Certain examination or assessment functions
- Eligibility for essential public services
- Creditworthiness evaluation
- Certain risk assessment or classification activities

The assessor should verify the precise legal entry rather than relying on a broad label such as "education AI" or "financial AI."

---

### 34. Critical Infrastructure AI

Certain AI systems intended as safety components in the management and operation of specified critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or the supply of water, gas, heating, or electricity may fall within the relevant Annex III high-risk category.

The assessment should examine:

- The infrastructure sector
- The system's intended purpose
- Whether it functions as a safety component
- The consequences of system failure
- The applicable Annex III entry
- The relationship with sector-specific legislation
- Relevant deployment and operational controls

Not every AI tool used by a critical infrastructure organization is automatically high-risk under this category.

**Critical Organization ≠ Every AI System Is High-Risk.**

---

### 35. Other Annex III Areas

The Annex III classification analysis may also involve certain AI systems used in law enforcement, migration and border management, asylum, administration of justice, and democratic processes.

These areas can involve significant effects on individuals and require careful interpretation of the relevant legal categories.

The assessment should document:

- The relevant authority or operator
- The intended purpose
- The affected individuals
- The specific Annex III entry
- Any applicable exceptions
- Other relevant legal constraints
- The required compliance and oversight arrangements

Sector labels alone are insufficient to determine the system's legal status.

---

### 36. Documentation of a Non-High-Risk Conclusion

When an organization concludes that an AI system is not high-risk, the conclusion should be supported by a documented classification analysis.

The record should identify:

- The system and version assessed
- The intended purpose
- The relevant legal provisions
- The Annex I and Annex III analysis, where applicable
- The reasons high-risk conditions are not met
- Any exception relied upon
- Supporting evidence
- The responsible decision-maker
- The date of assessment
- Reassessment triggers

The analysis should be revisited if the system's intended purpose, capabilities, deployment context, or applicable law changes.

**Non-High-Risk Conclusion ≠ No AI Act Obligations.**

---

### 37. Transparency Obligations Under Article 50

Article 50 establishes transparency obligations for specified AI systems and interactions.

Depending on the circumstances, these obligations may concern:

- Informing people when they interact directly with certain AI systems
- Machine-readable marking of certain AI-generated or manipulated content
- Disclosure concerning certain deepfakes
- Disclosure concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest
- Information relating to emotion-recognition systems
- Information relating to biometric categorisation systems

The assessment must identify the specific obligation, responsible operator, applicable conditions, and required implementation method.

Article 50 is not a universal rule requiring identical disclosure for every AI output.

Primary reference: Article 50 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended.

---

### 38. AI Interaction Disclosure

Certain AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons must be designed and developed so that individuals are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, subject to the applicable legal conditions and exceptions.

Assessment may examine:

- The interaction channel
- User-facing disclosures
- Timing and visibility
- Accessibility
- Disclosure persistence where relevant
- User experience
- Applicable exceptions

The disclosure should be evaluated against the legal requirement and the actual interaction design.

An internal policy stating that users should be informed does not by itself prove that the required disclosure is delivered in practice.

---

### 39. AI-Generated and Manipulated Content

Article 50 includes requirements concerning the marking of certain AI-generated or manipulated content.

The relevant obligation depends on the operator's role, the content, the system, and the applicable legal conditions.

The assessment should distinguish:

- Machine-readable marking obligations
- User-facing disclosure obligations
- Deepfake disclosure requirements
- Requirements relating to certain public-interest text
- Applicable exceptions
- Transitional provisions

The assessor should not assume that a visible label alone satisfies every relevant requirement.

The applicable marking approach and evidence should be evaluated against the legal text and relevant technical measures.

---

### 40. AI Literacy Obligations

The AI Act includes provisions relating to AI literacy.

The assessment should examine the applicable legal requirements, the organization's role, the people involved, and the relevant implementation measures.

Potential evidence may include:

- AI literacy initiatives
- Role-based learning materials
- Awareness activities
- Records of relevant training or communications
- Measures to promote appropriate AI understanding
- Governance ownership

The required approach should reflect the current legal text, including amendments and applicable guidance.

An organization should not assume that a particular training certificate, fixed number of training hours, or universal curriculum is legally mandatory unless the applicable requirement establishes it.

**AI Literacy Objective ≠ One Universal Training Requirement.**

---

### 41. General-Purpose AI Model Obligations

The AI Act establishes obligations for providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models.

The assessment should determine whether the subject is a GPAI model and identify the provider's applicable obligations.

Relevant assessment areas may include:

- Technical documentation
- Information for downstream AI system providers
- Copyright-related policies
- Publicly available information concerning training content, where required
- Cooperation with relevant authorities
- Applicable exceptions
- Code-of-practice or other compliance mechanisms where legally relevant

The assessment should distinguish obligations applicable to GPAI model providers from obligations applicable to providers or deployers of AI systems using those models.

**GPAI Model Provider ≠ Downstream Deployer Automatically.**

---

### 42. GPAI Models With Systemic Risk

The AI Act establishes additional requirements for general-purpose AI models classified as having systemic risk under the applicable provisions.

The assessment should examine the relevant legal classification and any applicable designation or threshold rules.

Potential assessment areas include:

- Model evaluation
- Adversarial testing
- Systemic-risk assessment and mitigation
- Serious incident reporting
- Cybersecurity
- Technical documentation
- Information sharing with relevant authorities

The applicable obligations depend on the legal classification and role of the organization.

An organization's internal description of a model as "frontier," "advanced," or "powerful" does not by itself establish the legal classification.

---

### 43. AI Act Obligations Across the Lifecycle

AI Act compliance should be assessed in the context of the relevant AI lifecycle.

Potential stages include:

- Concept and use-case definition
- Design and development
- Data preparation
- Testing and validation
- Conformity assessment, where required
- Market placement or putting into service
- Deployment
- Monitoring
- Change management
- Incident response
- Corrective action
- Retirement

Different legal obligations apply to different actors and lifecycle stages.

A one-time assessment may not remain sufficient when the system, intended purpose, legal classification, or applicable requirements change.

**One-Time Assessment ≠ Continuous Compliance Automatically.**

---

### 44. Compliance Obligation Mapping

Obligation mapping connects applicable legal requirements to the organization, AI system, process, control, and evidence needed to assess compliance.

A mapping may include:

| Field                   | Purpose                                                        |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Legal source            | Identifies the Regulation or other applicable legal instrument |
| Provision               | Identifies the relevant article or annex entry                 |
| Applicability rationale | Explains why the requirement applies                           |
| Responsible operator    | Identifies the relevant legal role                             |
| Compliance objective    | Defines the required outcome                                   |
| Control                 | Identifies the implementation mechanism                        |
| Evidence                | Identifies supporting records                                  |
| Assessment result       | Records the conclusion                                         |
| Gap and action          | Records deficiencies and remediation                           |

The mapping should be maintained as legal interpretations, systems, roles, and requirements evolve.

**Requirement Mapping ≠ Compliance Evidence Automatically.**

---

### 45. Legal Requirement vs Internal Control

A legal requirement defines an obligation established by applicable law.

An internal control is a mechanism implemented by an organization to address a risk or achieve a required outcome.

One legal requirement may be supported by several controls. One control may also support multiple legal requirements.

The organization should not represent an internal control design choice as an express legal mandate unless the legal source supports that interpretation.

For example, a company may choose a centralized AI approval committee as its control mechanism. The committee's specific structure should not automatically be described as a requirement of the AI Act.

**Internal Control Design ≠ Express Legal Requirement Automatically.**

---

### 46. Compliance Gap Analysis

A compliance gap analysis compares the applicable requirements with the organization's current state.

The analysis may identify:

- Requirements not implemented
- Controls not designed
- Controls not operating
- Missing documentation
- Incomplete classifications
- Unresolved legal interpretations
- Inadequate evidence
- Unassigned responsibilities
- Unaddressed transition requirements

A gap should be described in terms of the applicable criterion and the observed condition.

The organization should distinguish confirmed non-compliance from potential gaps, evidence limitations, and unresolved questions.

**Evidence Gap ≠ Legal Breach Automatically.**

---

### 47. Compliance Evidence Quality

Compliance conclusions should be supported by evidence that is relevant, reliable, sufficiently complete, and appropriate to the requirement being assessed.

Potential evidence includes:

- Technical documentation
- System configurations
- Risk assessments
- Testing records
- Approval records
- Instructions for use
- Monitoring records
- Incident reports
- Conformity documentation
- Declarations and registration records, where required
- Supplier information
- Management records

The evidence should demonstrate the required condition rather than merely assert that the organization intends to comply.

**Policy Existence ≠ Operational Compliance Automatically.**

---

### 48. Compliance Finding and Regulatory Breach

A compliance finding identifies a difference between the observed condition and the applicable assessment criterion.

A finding may indicate:

- Confirmed failure to meet an applicable requirement
- A control weakness that creates compliance exposure
- Missing or insufficient evidence
- An unresolved applicability question
- A potential breach requiring legal review

The assessment should distinguish these outcomes carefully.

A finding produced by an internal assessment is not itself a formal determination by a competent authority that a legal infringement has occurred.

The organization should document the evidence, legal basis, uncertainty, impact, and required response.

**Internal Finding ≠ Formal Regulatory Determination Automatically.**

### 49. EU AI Act Application Timeline and Transitional Assessment

The AI Act applies progressively. The assessment must use the applicable legal text and verify which provisions apply to the system and operator at the assessment date.

As of 4 October 2026, the published EU implementation timeline reflects the amendments introduced by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI.

Key milestones include:

- **1 August 2024:** The AI Act entered into force.
- **2 February 2025:** General provisions, definitions, AI literacy provisions, and prohibitions began applying, subject to the specific provisions and later amendments.
- **2 August 2025:** GPAI model obligations and specified governance provisions began applying.
- **2 August 2026:** The general application date for the main body of the Regulation, including Article 50 transparency obligations, subject to exceptions and transitional provisions.
- **2 December 2026:** Certain newly introduced prohibitions apply; a specific transitional deadline also concerns Article 50(2) marking and detection obligations for certain AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026.
- **2 December 2027:** The application date for the relevant high-risk AI system rules under Article 6(2) and Annex III.
- **2 August 2028:** The application date for the relevant high-risk AI system rules under Article 6(1) and Annex I.

These dates do not mean that every obligation begins on the same date. The assessor should verify the relevant provision, operator role, system category, transitional rule, and any later legal amendment.

### 51. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Framework

An EU AI Act compliance assessment framework establishes a traceable relationship between applicable legal obligations, AI system characteristics, organizational responsibilities, controls, assessment procedures, evidence, and compliance conclusions.

The framework should connect the legal requirements to the actual operation of each AI system rather than relying exclusively on policies, questionnaires, or general statements of compliance.

```
         |

```

### 52. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Planning

Assessment planning determines how the organization will evaluate its compliance position for a defined system, business activity, or portfolio of AI systems.

The assessment plan should establish:

- Assessment objective
- Scope and exclusions
- AI systems and use cases
- Applicable legal provisions
- Relevant operator roles
- System risk classification
- Business and regulatory context
- Assessment criteria
- Required evidence
- Testing procedures
- Stakeholder responsibilities
- Reporting and escalation arrangements
- Assessment period
- Reassessment triggers

Planning should occur early enough to identify compliance gaps before material deployment, a change of intended purpose, or another relevant lifecycle decision.

For example, an organization planning to use AI to screen job applicants should not wait until the system has been deployed to determine whether high-risk AI obligations apply.

---

### 53. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Scope

The assessment scope defines the systems, activities, entities, requirements, and lifecycle stages included in the assessment.

Potential scope components include:

- AI applications and integrated systems
- General-purpose AI models used within the system
- AI-enabled business processes
- Data acquisition and processing
- Model configuration and integration
- Human oversight
- Deployment and operational monitoring
- Third-party providers
- Technical documentation
- User information and transparency
- Incident management
- Post-market monitoring, where applicable
- Relevant geographic markets and legal entities

A system-level assessment may need to examine multiple organizational functions, including Legal, Compliance, IT, Security, Privacy, Procurement, HR, Product Management, and business operations.

The assessment should also document exclusions and explain why they do not prevent a reliable conclusion within the stated scope.

**Assessment Scope ≠ Entire Enterprise Automatically.**

---

### 54. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Population

The assessment population is the complete set of items from which assessment activities or samples are selected.

Depending on the objective, the population may consist of:

- AI systems in the enterprise inventory
- High-risk AI systems
- AI systems supplied to customers
- Deployed AI use cases
- AI-generated outputs
- System changes
- Human oversight records
- Risk assessments
- Incident records
- Transparency notices
- Supplier contracts
- Technical documentation packages
- Conformity assessment records
- Corrective actions

The population should be reconciled against relevant source systems where possible.

For example, an AI inventory may list 40 systems, while procurement records identify 12 additional AI-enabled services. The difference may indicate an inventory completeness problem requiring investigation.

A sample drawn from an incomplete population may fail to identify material compliance gaps.

---

### 55. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria define the requirements against which the organization evaluates the system or activity.

Potential criteria include:

- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended
- Applicable implementing or delegated acts
- Applicable harmonised standards
- Common specifications, where applicable
- Relevant guidance from competent authorities
- Applicable contractual commitments
- Internal policies and procedures
- Approved control objectives
- Documented implementation arrangements

The authority and applicability of each criterion should be recorded.

Legal provisions, standards, guidance, contracts, and internal policies should not be treated as interchangeable. A standard may provide a method for demonstrating conformity, while an internal policy may impose an additional organizational requirement.

The assessment should distinguish mandatory legal obligations from voluntary practices and internal commitments.

---

### 56. EU AI Act Requirement Interpretation

Requirement interpretation converts legal text into an operationally assessable obligation.

For each relevant provision, the assessor should identify:

1. The regulated subject or actor.
2. The circumstances in which the requirement applies.
3. The required action, condition, or outcome.
4. The relevant timing or frequency.
5. Any exceptions or qualifications.
6. Required records or documentation.
7. Dependencies on other legal provisions.
8. The evidence needed to assess implementation.
9. The consequences of non-compliance, where established by law.

For example, a requirement to maintain technical documentation should be translated into an assessment of the applicable documentation requirements, the responsible operator, the relevant system version, document completeness, maintenance arrangements, and availability when required.

A paraphrase of a legal requirement should preserve its legal meaning and qualifications.

**Requirement Interpretation ≠ Creating a New Legal Obligation.**

---

### 57. EU AI Act Obligation Register

An obligation register records the legal requirements relevant to an AI system, provider, deployer, or other operator.

An illustrative register may contain:

| Field                   | Purpose                                           |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Requirement ID          | Internal traceability identifier                  |
| Legal reference         | Applicable article or annex                       |
| Requirement summary     | Controlled operational interpretation             |
| Applicability rationale | Why the provision applies                         |
| Responsible operator    | Entity accountable for the obligation             |
| Control owner           | Person or function responsible for implementation |
| Evidence requirement    | Records or observations needed                    |
| Assessment method       | Inquiry, inspection, testing, or analysis         |
| Status                  | Assessment result                                 |
| Gap reference           | Link to an identified deficiency                  |
| Review date             | Date applicability was last reviewed              |

Internal identifiers should not be presented as official EU AI Act article numbers.

The register should be version-controlled so that amendments, new interpretations, and changes in system circumstances can be assessed systematically.

---

### 58. EU AI Act Requirement-to-Control Mapping

Requirement-to-control mapping connects a legal obligation to the organizational mechanisms intended to satisfy it.

```
LEGAL PROVISION
       |
       v
APPLICABILITY DECISION
       |
       v
OBLIGATION
       |
       v
CONTROL OBJECTIVE
       |
       v
CONTROL ACTIVITY
       |
       v
CONTROL OWNER
       |
       v
EVIDENCE
       |
       v
ASSESSMENT RESULT
```

For example, a high-risk AI system's applicable human oversight requirements may map to:

- Defined oversight responsibilities
- Appropriate user instructions
- Human intervention mechanisms
- Escalation procedures
- Training and competence requirements
- Operational records
- Testing of relevant oversight capabilities

One control may support several obligations, and one obligation may require several controls.

Mapping should preserve traceability without assuming that a single control automatically satisfies every requirement associated with a legal provision.

---

### 59. EU AI Act Control Objective

A control objective describes the condition that the organization intends to achieve to address an applicable obligation.

Examples include:

- AI systems are classified using documented legal criteria.
- High-risk AI systems satisfy applicable requirements before being placed on the market or put into service.
- Required technical documentation is prepared and maintained.
- Human oversight arrangements are appropriate to the system and its intended purpose.
- Applicable transparency information is provided.
- Relevant incidents and corrective actions are managed according to applicable requirements.
- Required records are retained and made available to authorized parties.

A control objective should be sufficiently precise to support testing and evaluation.

**Control Objective ≠ Control Activity.**

---

### 60. EU AI Act Control Design

Control design evaluates whether the proposed control, if implemented and operated as intended, is capable of addressing the relevant obligation and risk.

Assessment questions may include:

- Does the control address the correct legal requirement?
- Is the responsible operator correctly identified?
- Is ownership assigned?
- Is the control sufficiently precise?
- Does the control cover the applicable lifecycle stage?
- Are dependencies on suppliers or other systems addressed?
- Does the control account for applicable exceptions?
- Does the control generate appropriate evidence?
- Is the review frequency suitable?
- Are escalation and remediation mechanisms defined?

A control may exist in a policy but be inadequate for the legal obligation it is intended to address.

For example, a general statement that employees must supervise AI may not establish meaningful oversight arrangements for a high-risk AI system.

**Control Design ≠ Operating Effectiveness.**

---

### 61. EU AI Act Control Implementation

Control implementation evaluates whether the designed control has actually been established in the relevant operating environment.

Implementation evidence may include:

- Approved procedures
- Configured workflows
- Assigned control owners
- System settings
- Training records
- Completed risk assessments
- Technical documentation
- Operational instructions
- Supplier agreements
- Approved deployment records

An approved policy alone may not demonstrate that the associated workflow, system capability, or operational process has been implemented.

For example, a procedure may require human review before an AI-supported decision is finalized. Implementation testing should establish whether the relevant workflow actually provides that review opportunity.

**Policy Approval ≠ Control Implementation Automatically.**

---

### 62. EU AI Act Operating Effectiveness

Operating effectiveness evaluates whether a control operated as intended during the relevant period.

Testing may examine:

- Whether required actions occurred
- Whether actions occurred at the correct time
- Whether responsible personnel performed them
- Whether exceptions were identified
- Whether evidence was recorded
- Whether identified deficiencies were escalated
- Whether the control continued to operate after system changes

For example, if a procedure requires approval before deployment, the assessor may inspect a population of deployment records and verify that approval occurred before each sampled deployment.

A control that was implemented but repeatedly bypassed may not be operating effectively.

---

### 63. EU AI Act Compliance Control Outcome

Control outcome evaluation considers whether the control contributes to the intended compliance result.

A control may operate consistently while failing to achieve the intended result because its design is incomplete, the underlying system has changed, or an external dependency is not addressed.

Example:

```
CONTROL
   |
   v
OPERATES AS DESIGNED
   |
   v
REQUIRED CONDITION CHECKED
   |
   v
EXCEPTIONS IDENTIFIED
   |
   v
APPROPRIATE RESPONSE
   |
   v
COMPLIANCE OBJECTIVE SUPPORTED
```

The assessor should examine both the operation of the control and the relationship between its operation and the relevant legal requirement.

An effective internal control does not automatically establish complete compliance with the EU AI Act.

---

### 64. EU AI Act Preventive Controls

Preventive controls seek to prevent non-compliance before it occurs.

Examples include:

- AI use-case approval before procurement
- Legal applicability screening before deployment
- High-risk classification review
- Prohibited-practice screening
- Required documentation gates
- Supplier due diligence
- Restrictions on unauthorized system modifications
- Approval of intended-purpose changes
- Deployment authorization

Preventive controls are especially valuable when a failure could result in an unlawful market placement, prohibited use, or deployment without required safeguards.

The assessment should verify whether the control operates before the relevant decision or activity, rather than merely documenting it afterward.

---

### 65. EU AI Act Detective Controls

Detective controls identify potential non-compliance, control failures, or changes that may affect legal obligations.

Examples include:

- Periodic AI inventory reconciliation
- Monitoring of system-purpose changes
- Review of transparency disclosures
- Detection of missing documentation
- Monitoring of complaints and incidents
- Review of human oversight exceptions
- Supplier compliance monitoring
- Periodic reassessment of legal applicability
- Monitoring of regulatory changes

Detective controls should have defined alert criteria, responsible reviewers, escalation routes, and response expectations.

A monitoring mechanism that generates alerts but does not lead to investigation or corrective action may provide limited compliance value.

---

### 66. EU AI Act Corrective Controls

Corrective controls address identified deficiencies or unwanted conditions.

Examples include:

- Suspending an affected deployment
- Correcting incomplete documentation
- Revising inaccurate user instructions
- Restoring a required oversight mechanism
- Correcting system configuration
- Revising supplier arrangements
- Updating risk assessments
- Reclassifying a system when new facts justify it
- Implementing required corrective actions

Corrective action should address the identified condition and, where appropriate, its underlying cause.

The organization should also determine whether the deficiency requires additional legal review, escalation, notification, or other action under the applicable provisions.

**Corrective Action Completion ≠ Compliance Restored Automatically.**

---

### 67. EU AI Act Control Ownership

Control ownership identifies the person or function responsible for operating a control and maintaining its supporting evidence.

Potential owners include:

- AI governance
- Legal and regulatory compliance
- Product management
- AI engineering
- Information security
- Data protection
- Quality management
- Procurement
- Human resources
- Business operations
- Supplier management

Ownership should be aligned with the entity's actual legal role and responsibilities.

An organization should not assign every obligation to a central AI governance team if implementation depends on product engineering, operational teams, or other functions.

The assessment should distinguish the person accountable for the legal obligation from the individual responsible for a specific supporting control.

---

### 68. EU AI Act Segregation of Duties

Segregation of duties reduces the risk that important compliance decisions are made, approved, and reviewed by the same person without appropriate challenge.

Potential separations include:

- System development versus release approval
- Risk assessment versus independent review
- Supplier selection versus compliance approval
- Control operation versus assurance testing
- Finding remediation versus closure validation

The appropriate structure depends on organizational size, system criticality, risk, and available resources.

Smaller organizations may need compensating review mechanisms where full separation is impractical.

Segregation of duties is an organizational control principle; it should not be represented as a universal, identically worded EU AI Act requirement unless a specific applicable provision supports that conclusion.

---

### 69. EU AI Act Control Dependencies

A compliance control may depend on other controls, processes, systems, or third parties.

Examples include:

- Accurate AI inventory data
- Reliable system documentation
- Correct identification of the provider and deployer
- Procurement records
- Identity and access management
- Change management
- Supplier information
- Incident reporting processes
- Legal interpretation
- Data governance
- Record retention

If a control depends on an unreliable upstream process, its apparent effectiveness may be misleading.

For example, a classification workflow cannot reliably identify every high-risk AI system if the inventory omits systems acquired through business units or embedded in third-party services.

Dependency analysis should identify upstream failure modes and determine whether compensating controls exist.

---

### 70. EU AI Act Shared Controls

Shared controls support multiple AI systems, business units, or legal obligations.

Examples may include:

- Enterprise AI inventory management
- Legal change monitoring
- AI procurement standards
- Supplier due diligence
- Documentation repositories
- Staff training
- Incident management
- Centralized access governance
- Control testing methodology

Shared controls can reduce duplication, but their use should not obscure system-specific requirements.

A corporate policy may establish a common governance foundation while individual systems still require separate technical documentation, risk assessments, transparency measures, or other applicable controls.

**Shared Control ≠ Universal Compliance Evidence.**

---

### 71. EU AI Act System-Specific Controls

System-specific controls address the particular architecture, intended purpose, risk profile, or operational context of an AI system.

Examples may include:

- System-specific human oversight mechanisms
- Required input-data controls
- Performance monitoring
- Model change approval
- Logging configuration
- User instructions
- System-specific transparency measures
- Technical documentation
- Incident escalation criteria

The assessment should determine whether enterprise-wide controls are sufficiently precise for the individual system.

For example, a general incident procedure may need system-specific thresholds and escalation arrangements to address the consequences of a failure in a particular high-risk AI application.

---

### 72. EU AI Act Evidence Planning

Evidence planning defines the records, observations, tests, and other information needed to evaluate each applicable obligation.

Evidence requirements should be determined before substantive testing begins.

Potential evidence sources include:

- Technical documentation
- Risk-management records
- Quality-management records
- Conformity assessment documentation
- EU declarations of conformity, where applicable
- Registration records, where required
- Instructions for use
- System logs
- Testing results
- Validation records
- Human oversight records
- Training records
- Supplier agreements
- Incident records
- Post-market monitoring documentation
- Change-management records

The evidence plan should identify the evidence owner, source, relevant period, system version, collection method, and applicable requirement.

Evidence should be proportionate to the assessment objective and the significance of the obligation.

---

### 73. EU AI Act Evidence Relevance

Evidence is relevant when it directly supports the specific assertion being evaluated.

For example, an AI literacy training attendance record may support the conclusion that an employee attended training. It does not, by itself, establish that the organization has taken all measures appropriate to ensure AI literacy under the applicable legal provision.

Similarly, a supplier's statement that a product is AI Act compliant may be relevant background information, but it does not automatically establish that the customer's deployment, intended purpose, and operator responsibilities satisfy all applicable requirements.

Evidence relevance should be assessed against the precise requirement and the conclusion being considered.

**Evidence Relevance ≠ Evidence Sufficiency.**

---

### 74. EU AI Act Evidence Reliability

Evidence reliability concerns the extent to which evidence can be trusted for the intended assessment purpose.

Reliability may depend on:

- Source authenticity
- Data integrity
- Accuracy
- Completeness
- Independence
- Traceability
- Collection method
- Timestamp integrity
- Access restrictions
- System-generated versus manually prepared records

For example, a timestamped deployment record from a controlled release system may provide stronger evidence of deployment timing than an undated spreadsheet prepared retrospectively.

System-generated evidence is not automatically reliable. The assessor should consider the system's configuration, access controls, logging completeness, and potential alteration.

---

### 75. EU AI Act Evidence Sufficiency

Evidence sufficiency concerns whether enough appropriate evidence has been obtained to support the conclusion.

Sufficiency depends on factors such as:

- Requirement significance
- Risk
- Assessment objective
- Population size
- Control frequency
- Observed exceptions
- Evidence reliability
- Testing coverage
- Uncertainty
- Potential consequences of an incorrect conclusion

A single successful test may be insufficient to establish that a frequently performed control operated consistently throughout the assessment period.

Conversely, not every obligation requires the same volume or type of evidence.

The assessor should document why the evidence obtained is adequate for the conclusion reached.

---

### 76. EU AI Act Evidence Integrity and Chain of Custody

Evidence integrity means preserving evidence against unauthorized alteration, loss, or unexplained modification.

Where appropriate, evidence handling should include:

- Source identification
- Collection timestamp
- System and version identification
- Collector identity
- Access restrictions
- Version control
- Integrity checks
- Secure storage
- Retention arrangements
- Chain-of-custody records for sensitive or contested evidence

These practices can be especially important for technical logs, system configuration exports, incident records, and evidence supporting a material compliance finding.

The required level of formality should reflect the evidence's sensitivity, the assessment objective, and applicable legal or organizational requirements.

---

### 77. EU AI Act Inquiry and Corroboration

Interviews and written explanations can help establish how a process is intended to operate and identify relevant evidence sources.

However, inquiry should generally be corroborated where the conclusion is material.

For example, an AI system owner may state that a system is reviewed before every release. The assessor can corroborate that statement through release records, approval workflows, and sampled system changes.

Where corroboration is unavailable, the limitation should be documented and considered when determining the assessment result.

**Management Representation ≠ Independent Verification Automatically.**

---

### 78. EU AI Act Observation and Demonstration

Observation and demonstration allow the assessor to inspect a process or system capability directly.

Examples include:

- Observing a release approval workflow
- Demonstrating access to required documentation
- Reviewing the operation of a human intervention mechanism
- Demonstrating the retrieval of relevant logs
- Observing an incident escalation process

A demonstration establishes what was shown under the observed conditions. It does not necessarily prove that the process operated consistently in the past or will operate effectively in the future.

The assessor should identify the system version, configuration, environment, and conditions under which the demonstration occurred.

---

### 79. EU AI Act Document Inspection

Document inspection evaluates whether records contain the information required to support an obligation.

Potential inspection targets include:

- Technical documentation
- Risk assessments
- Quality-management procedures
- Instructions for use
- Declarations of conformity
- Supplier contracts
- Monitoring plans
- Training records
- Incident reports
- Corrective-action records

Inspection should assess more than the existence of a document. Relevant considerations include completeness, accuracy, approval, applicability, version, consistency, and maintenance.

A document may be present but relate to a different system version or intended purpose.

**Document Existence ≠ Compliance Demonstrated Automatically.**

---

### 80. EU AI Act Technical Testing

Technical testing evaluates system behavior, technical capabilities, or control operation against defined assessment criteria.

Depending on the system and obligation, testing may include:

- Configuration verification
- Logging verification
- Access-control testing
- Performance evaluation
- Validation review
- Robustness testing
- Security testing
- Human oversight capability testing
- Output or disclosure verification
- Change-control testing

The test method should be appropriate to the legal requirement and the system's technical characteristics.

A technical test may provide evidence for a particular obligation without establishing overall legal compliance.

For example, successful security testing does not by itself establish compliance with requirements concerning risk management, transparency, documentation, or human oversight.

---

### 81. EU AI Act Sampling Methodology

Sampling selects a subset of a population for assessment.

Sampling may be appropriate when testing every transaction, event, or record is impractical.

The methodology should define:

- Population
- Sampling unit
- Sampling period
- Selection method
- Sample size rationale
- Risk factors
- Expected exception rate, where relevant
- Evaluation criteria
- Treatment of exceptions
- Limitations on extrapolation

Risk-based selection may prioritize systems or records with higher regulatory exposure, significant changes, incidents, or previous control failures.

However, a risk-based sample should not automatically be described as statistically representative.

The assessment report should explain the implications of sampling for the conclusion.

---

### 82. EU AI Act Full-Population Testing

Full-population testing evaluates every item in a defined population using a specified procedure.

Examples include checking all records in a complete inventory for missing mandatory fields or comparing every deployment record in a defined period against a recorded approval requirement.

Full-population testing can increase coverage, but its reliability depends on:

- Population completeness
- Data accuracy
- Correct test logic
- Appropriate criteria
- Handling of exceptions
- Interpretation of results

A test applied to every record in an incomplete dataset does not provide complete population coverage.

**Full-Population Testing ≠ Elimination of Data-Quality Risk.**

---

### 83. EU AI Act Automated Compliance Testing

Automated compliance testing uses scripts, workflows, queries, or other tools to evaluate defined conditions.

Potential applications include:

- Detecting missing inventory attributes
- Identifying overdue assessments
- Checking documentation version mismatches
- Identifying unapproved deployments
- Monitoring overdue corrective actions
- Reconciling supplier records
- Detecting missing training records
- Identifying systems requiring reassessment

Automated tests should have documented logic, approved criteria, validation records, change control, and exception handling.

The assessor should determine whether the test actually evaluates the relevant legal requirement or only a proxy indicator.

For example, checking whether a document exists does not establish that its contents meet all applicable legal requirements.

---

### 84. EU AI Act Assessment Tool Validation

Assessment tools and automated tests should be validated for their intended purpose.

Validation may include:

- Functional testing
- Test-data verification
- Positive and negative test cases
- False-positive analysis
- False-negative analysis
- Logic review
- Version control
- Access control
- Change approval
- Output verification

Where an automated tool uses AI to classify evidence or interpret legal text, additional risks may include hallucination, inconsistent interpretation, model drift, and incorrect classification.

Human review should be proportionate to the significance of the resulting compliance conclusion.

**Automated Output ≠ Validated Compliance Conclusion Automatically.**

---

### 85. EU AI Act Exception Evaluation

An exception occurs when a test identifies a condition that differs from the expected result.

Examples include:

- Missing approval evidence
- Incomplete technical documentation
- An overdue assessment
- An unrecorded system change
- A missing transparency disclosure
- An unexplained classification decision
- An unaddressed monitoring alert

Each exception should be evaluated to determine whether it represents:

- A genuine control failure
- A data-quality issue
- A test-design limitation
- A false positive
- An isolated administrative error
- A potentially material compliance deficiency
- A condition requiring further investigation

An exception should not automatically be classified as a legal breach without evaluating the applicable requirement, facts, context, and evidence.

---

### 86. EU AI Act Compliance Gap Analysis

Gap analysis compares the assessed condition with the applicable requirement and the organization's intended control state.

A structured gap record should identify:

- Applicable legal provision
- Applicability rationale
- Required condition
- Observed condition
- Evidence supporting the observation
- Nature of the gap
- Affected system or operator
- Potential impact
- Immediate containment needs
- Root-cause investigation
- Proposed remediation
- Responsible owner
- Target date
- Validation method

Gap analysis should distinguish a missing control, an inadequately designed control, a control that was not implemented, an operating failure, and an evidence limitation.

These conditions may require different remediation approaches.

---

### 87. EU AI Act Compliance Finding

A compliance finding documents a substantiated difference between an applicable requirement and the observed condition.

A finding should ordinarily contain:

1. **Criterion:** The applicable requirement or approved assessment criterion.
2. **Condition:** What the evidence establishes.
3. **Evidence:** The records, tests, or observations supporting the condition.
4. **Impact:** The actual or potential consequence.
5. **Cause:** The identified or suspected reason, where established.
6. **Recommendation or required action:** The proposed response.
7. **Ownership:** The responsible party.
8. **Status:** The current remediation state.

The assessor should not overstate the evidence or attribute intent without support.

Where the legal position is uncertain, the finding should identify the uncertainty and seek appropriate legal interpretation rather than presenting an unresolved interpretation as a definitive breach.

---

### 88. EU AI Act Finding Severity

Finding severity is an internal assessment judgment used to prioritize remediation and escalation.

An organization may consider:

- Legal significance
- Potential harm
- Number of affected systems
- Duration of exposure
- Likelihood of recurrence
- Control failure extent
- Reversibility
- Potential impact on affected persons
- Regulatory exposure
- Whether the condition remains active

Illustrative internal ratings include Critical, High, Moderate, and Low.

The methodology should define each rating and explain how severity influences escalation and remediation deadlines.

These internal categories are not automatically equivalent to formal EU AI Act violation categories, statutory penalties, or regulatory determinations.

**Internal Severity Rating ≠ Legal Classification Automatically.**

---

### 89. EU AI Act Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis investigates why a compliance deficiency occurred.

Potential causes include:

- Unclear legal ownership
- Incomplete AI inventory
- Incorrect applicability interpretation
- Weak change management
- Inadequate supplier information
- Poorly designed workflows
- Insufficient staff competence
- Missing technical capabilities
- Inadequate monitoring
- Conflicting responsibilities
- Failure to reassess after material change

The assessor should distinguish the direct cause from contributing factors and underlying governance weaknesses.

For example, missing technical documentation may be the observed condition, but the underlying cause may be that procurement processes never required suppliers to provide the information needed for the organization's legal responsibilities.

Correcting only the document may not prevent recurrence.

---

### 90. EU AI Act Corrective Action Management

Corrective action management tracks how identified deficiencies are addressed.

A corrective-action record should identify:

- Finding reference
- Immediate containment
- Root cause
- Corrective action
- Responsible owner
- Required resources
- Target date
- Dependencies
- Progress status
- Closure evidence
- Effectiveness validation

Management should prioritize actions according to the significance of the deficiency, applicable legal timelines, exposure, and potential consequences.

Where an issue may involve a legally significant non-compliance condition, the organization should obtain appropriate legal advice concerning immediate obligations and required external action.

Closing an action in a tracking system does not establish that the underlying deficiency has been resolved.

---

### 91. EU AI Act Remediation Validation

Remediation validation determines whether the corrective action addressed the identified deficiency.

Validation may involve:

- Inspecting revised documentation
- Retesting the control
- Reviewing new system configurations
- Testing the relevant workflow
- Verifying approval records
- Examining a subsequent sample
- Confirming that required information is available
- Checking whether the deficiency recurred

For example, revising a deployment policy should be followed by verification that the release workflow enforces the required approval.

The validation should evaluate the original finding and any new risks introduced by the remediation.

**Action Implemented ≠ Finding Effectively Resolved Automatically.**

---

### 92. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Reporting

The assessment report communicates the scope, methodology, evidence, results, limitations, and conclusions.

A report may include:

- Executive summary
- Assessment objective
- Scope and exclusions
- Assessment period
- Applicable criteria
- AI systems and operator roles
- Classification conclusions
- Methodology
- Evidence limitations
- Findings and severity
- Unresolved legal interpretations
- Remediation plans
- Management responses
- Overall conclusion
- Required follow-up

The report should distinguish verified facts from management representations, assumptions, and unresolved questions.

It should also state whether the conclusion concerns a particular system, a defined group of controls, or a broader compliance program.

---

### 93. EU AI Act Compliance Conclusion

A compliance conclusion expresses the assessor's evaluation of the defined subject against the applicable criteria.

Possible internal conclusion categories include:

- Compliant within the assessed scope
- Compliant with observations
- Partially compliant
- Material gaps identified
- Unable to conclude
- Assessment incomplete

These are illustrative internal reporting categories, not official universal EU AI Act status labels.

A conclusion should consider the number and significance of findings, evidence quality, assessment limitations, and the nature of the obligations assessed.

A positive conclusion should not be extended to unassessed systems, untested obligations, other legal entities, or future system versions.

**Compliance Conclusion ≠ Permanent Compliance Guarantee.**

---

### 94. EU AI Act Management Representation

Management representation provides statements concerning the organization's systems, processes, responsibilities, and known conditions.

Representations may address:

- Completeness of the AI inventory
- Identification of relevant operators
- Accuracy of intended-purpose descriptions
- Disclosure of known incidents
- Status of remediation
- Identification of external suppliers
- Completeness of records provided
- Known changes affecting applicability

Representations should be documented and evaluated according to their significance.

Where a representation materially affects the conclusion, the assessor should seek corroborating evidence where reasonably available.

A representation should not replace required technical documentation, records, or testing.

---

### 95. EU AI Act Assessment Workpapers

Workpapers document the assessment work performed and support review of the conclusions.

They may include:

- Assessment plan
- Scope definition
- Legal applicability analysis
- Obligation register
- Control mapping
- Risk assessment
- Testing procedures
- Sampling rationale
- Evidence references
- Test results
- Exception evaluations
- Findings
- Management responses
- Remediation validation
- Review notes
- Conclusion rationale

Workpapers should be sufficiently detailed for an appropriately qualified reviewer to understand the work performed and the basis for material conclusions.

They should also be protected according to confidentiality, access-control, retention, and data-protection requirements.

---

### 96. EU AI Act Requirement-Control-Evidence Matrix

A requirement-control-evidence matrix provides end-to-end traceability between legal obligations, implemented controls, and assessment results.

| Legal requirement    | Control objective  | Evidence          | Test procedure       | Result          |
| -------------------- | ------------------ | ----------------- | -------------------- | --------------- |
| Applicable provision | Intended condition | Supporting record | Defined test         | Assessed result |
| Applicable provision | Intended condition | System record     | Configuration review | Assessed result |
| Applicable provision | Intended condition | Approval record   | Sample testing       | Assessed result |

The matrix should identify the applicable legal reference and retain links to the underlying evidence.

It can help identify obligations without mapped controls, controls without clear legal justification, and conclusions that lack sufficient supporting evidence.

A populated matrix does not establish compliance unless its entries are accurate, applicable, and supported.

---

### 97. EU AI Act Assessment Traceability

Assessment traceability allows each material conclusion to be followed back to the legal requirement and evidence supporting it.

```
LEGAL PROVISION
       |
       v
APPLICABILITY RATIONALE
       |
       v
ASSESSMENT CRITERION
       |
       v
CONTROL / REQUIRED CONDITION
       |
       v
TEST PROCEDURE
       |
       v
EVIDENCE
       |
       v
RESULT
       |
       v
FINDING OR CONCLUSION
```

Traceability should work in both directions.

Forward traceability establishes how a legal requirement is assessed. Reverse traceability establishes which legal obligations and conclusions depend on a particular control or evidence item.

This becomes particularly important when regulations change, systems are modified, or findings affect multiple AI use cases.

---

### 98. EU AI Act Assessment Limitations

Assessment limitations identify circumstances that prevent the assessor from obtaining or evaluating all evidence needed for a reliable conclusion.

Examples include:

- Inaccessible supplier documentation
- Missing historical logs
- Incomplete system inventory
- Unavailable technical specialists
- Inadequate sample coverage
- Unresolved legal interpretation
- Unverified system configuration
- Missing records for the relevant period
- Material changes during assessment
- Restrictions on system testing

Each limitation should be evaluated for its effect on the scope, evidence, findings, and conclusion.

The assessor should not treat unavailable evidence as proof of compliance or automatically treat every evidence gap as proof of a legal breach.

Where a conclusion cannot be supported, the report should state that limitation explicitly.

---

### 99. EU AI Act Assessment Independence and Objectivity

An assessment should be performed with sufficient objectivity to evaluate evidence fairly and identify deficiencies even when the results are inconvenient to the organization.

Safeguards may include:

- Clear assessment responsibilities
- Conflict-of-interest declarations
- Independent review of material findings
- Separation between control ownership and assurance where practicable
- Documented evidence evaluation
- Consistent assessment criteria
- Escalation of disagreements
- Protection against inappropriate interference

The appropriate degree of independence depends on the assessment's purpose and organizational structure.

An internal compliance self-assessment may be useful, but it should not automatically be described as independent assurance.

Where a formal conformity assessment requires a notified body or another designated external process, an internal review cannot substitute for that process merely because it follows a similar methodology.

---

### 100. EU AI Act Assessment Quality Review

Assessment quality review evaluates whether the assessment was properly planned, executed, documented, and concluded.

Review procedures may examine:

- Scope completeness
- Accuracy of legal references
- Applicability reasoning
- Operator-role analysis
- Classification rationale
- Evidence reliability
- Test design
- Sampling methodology
- Finding support
- Severity consistency
- Conclusion traceability
- Treatment of limitations
- Management responses
- Remediation validation

Material findings and high-impact legal interpretations should receive appropriate review by qualified personnel.

Quality review can improve the consistency and defensibility of the assessment, but it does not eliminate the possibility of error or substitute for required external conformity assessment.

---

### 101. EU AI Act Provider Obligation Assessment

Provider obligation assessment evaluates the obligations applicable to an organization acting as a provider under the Regulation.

The assessment should first establish whether the organization meets the relevant legal definition of a provider for the system and circumstances concerned.

Depending on the system category and applicable provisions, assessment areas may include:

- Risk-management processes
- Data governance requirements
- Technical documentation
- Record-keeping and logging capabilities
- Transparency and instructions
- Human oversight considerations
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity requirements
- Quality-management arrangements
- Conformity assessment
- Declaration of conformity
- Required registration
- Corrective action
- Post-market monitoring
- Cooperation with competent authorities

Not every obligation applies identically to every provider or AI system. Applicability depends on the system's classification, the operator's role, the relevant legal provisions, and applicable transitional arrangements.

---

### 102. EU AI Act Deployer Obligation Assessment

Deployer obligation assessment evaluates the requirements applicable to an organization using an AI system under its authority.

For a high-risk AI system, relevant assessment areas may include, where applicable:

- Use in accordance with instructions
- Assignment of competent human oversight
- Relevant input-data controls where applicable
- Monitoring of system operation
- Retention of automatically generated logs where required and under the deployer's control
- Workplace information and consultation obligations where relevant
- Required information to affected persons
- Cooperation with relevant actors
- Response to identified risks or incidents
- Use of relevant information from the provider

The assessment should determine which obligations apply to the specific deployer and deployment context.

A deployer should not assume that purchasing a system from a provider transfers all legal responsibility to that provider.

---

### 103. EU AI Act Importer and Distributor Assessment

Where relevant, importer and distributor assessments should examine the obligations associated with placing an AI system on the market or making it available under the applicable legal framework.

Potential assessment areas include:

- Identification of the relevant economic operator
- Verification of required documentation
- Verification of required conformity-related information
- Identification and escalation of suspected non-compliance
- Storage and transport conditions where relevant
- Cooperation with competent authorities
- Records supporting relevant checks
- Required corrective action or cooperation

The precise obligations depend on the operator's legal role, the system category, and the applicable provisions.

An organization should not assume that every entity in a distribution chain has the same obligations as the provider.

---

### 104. EU AI Act Role-Change Assessment

An organization may need to reassess its legal role when it modifies, rebrands, places on the market, or changes the intended purpose of an AI system.

The Regulation contains circumstances in which an importer, distributor, deployer, or other third party may be treated as a provider, including specified substantial modifications and certain changes involving the intended purpose of a high-risk AI system.

Assessment procedures should therefore examine:

- Changes to intended purpose
- Rebranding or marketing under another name
- Material system modifications
- Changes to model or system configuration
- Integration into a different product
- Changes in operational authority
- Changes in contractual responsibilities
- Changes to the entity placing the system on the market

The assessment should apply the actual legal conditions rather than assuming that every technical modification triggers a provider role.

**System Modification ≠ Provider Role Automatically.**

---

### 105. EU AI Act Supplier Evidence Assessment

Supplier evidence assessment evaluates whether information supplied by a vendor is sufficiently relevant, reliable, and complete for the organization's compliance needs.

Potential evidence includes:

- System descriptions
- Intended-purpose documentation
- Technical documentation where available and appropriate
- Instructions for use
- Risk and performance information
- Conformity-related records
- Security information
- Change notifications
- Incident notifications
- Support commitments
- Contractual responsibilities

The assessment should consider the supplier's legal role and the organization's own role in the supply chain.

Commercial assurances, marketing statements, certificates, and generic questionnaires should not automatically be accepted as evidence of every applicable obligation.

Where supplier information is insufficient, the organization should identify the gap, evaluate its significance, and determine whether additional evidence or a different deployment decision is required.

---

### 106. EU AI Act Third-Party Dependency Assessment

Third-party dependency assessment evaluates whether external components or services affect the organization's compliance position.

Dependencies may include:

- General-purpose AI models
- Hosted AI services
- External datasets
- Cloud infrastructure
- Model APIs
- AI development platforms
- External evaluation services
- Embedded AI components
- Managed AI operations

Assessment should determine which obligations remain with the organization and which information or activities depend on another operator.

Relevant considerations include documentation access, change notifications, service limitations, incident cooperation, logging, technical integration, and contractual rights.

A third-party component may be compliant for one purpose but unsuitable for another intended purpose or deployment context.

**Supplier Compliance ≠ Customer Compliance Automatically.**

---

### 107. EU AI Act High-Risk System Requirement Mapping

For an AI system classified as high-risk, the assessment should map the applicable requirements to the system's actual architecture, intended purpose, and operating arrangements.

Depending on the applicable provisions, the mapping may cover:

- Risk-management system
- Data and data governance
- Technical documentation
- Record-keeping
- Transparency and instructions
- Human oversight
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity
- Quality management
- Conformity assessment
- Declaration of conformity
- Registration where required
- Post-market monitoring
- Corrective action and incident-related duties

The mapping should distinguish provider requirements from deployer and other operator obligations.

It should also record applicable exceptions, the legal basis for any conclusion, and relevant transitional provisions.

A generic high-risk checklist should not be applied without considering the system's specific category and circumstances.

---

### 108. EU AI Act Risk-Management Assessment

Risk-management assessment evaluates whether the applicable risk-management requirements are addressed through a systematic process.

For high-risk AI systems, Article 9 establishes requirements for a risk-management system, subject to the Regulation's detailed conditions.

Assessment areas may include:

- Identification and analysis of known and reasonably foreseeable risks
- Evaluation of risks arising from intended use
- Consideration of reasonably foreseeable misuse
- Estimation and evaluation of relevant risks
- Adoption of appropriate risk-management measures
- Evaluation of residual risk
- Testing relevant to risk management
- Review across the system lifecycle
- Updating when relevant information or circumstances change

The assessor should examine the connection between identified risks and implemented measures.

A risk register alone is not sufficient evidence that the risk-management process is implemented or effective.

---

### 109. EU AI Act Data and Data-Governance Assessment

Where the applicable high-risk requirements include Article 10, the assessment should evaluate the relevant data-governance and data-management practices.

Depending on the system and data involved, relevant areas may include:

- Data collection and provenance
- Data preparation
- Data suitability
- Data relevance
- Data representativeness
- Data quality
- Bias examination and mitigation
- Identification of relevant data gaps
- Data governance practices
- Documentation of data characteristics

The assessment should account for the system's intended purpose and the role of data in its development and operation.

It should also distinguish requirements applicable to training, validation, and testing datasets from those relevant to operational inputs.

Data quality is not a single universal threshold; the applicable criteria and assessment method should be justified.

---

### 110. EU AI Act Technical Documentation Assessment

Technical documentation assessment evaluates whether the applicable documentation requirements are satisfied for the relevant system and legal role.

For high-risk AI systems, Article 11 and Annex IV establish relevant documentation requirements, subject to the Regulation's detailed provisions and applicable arrangements.

Assessment may consider:

- General system description
- Intended purpose
- Development and design information
- System architecture
- Data-related information
- Risk-management documentation
- Relevant performance characteristics
- Human oversight arrangements
- Testing and validation information
- Monitoring and maintenance information
- Applicable conformity-related records

The assessment should verify that the documentation corresponds to the system actually supplied or deployed and is maintained as required.

A technical document may be extensive but still fail to address a required element.

---

### 111. EU AI Act Logging and Record-Keeping Assessment

Logging and record-keeping assessment evaluates whether applicable records are generated, retained, protected, and made available as required.

For relevant high-risk AI systems, Article 12 addresses automatic recording of events, while Article 26 includes deployer-related logging responsibilities under specified conditions.

Assessment may examine:

- Whether logging functionality exists
- Which events are recorded
- Whether records are relevant to the system's purpose
- Whether timestamps and system identifiers are available
- Whether records can be retrieved
- Whether access is controlled
- Whether required retention arrangements are implemented
- Whether records support traceability and investigation

The assessor should distinguish provider logging requirements from the deployer's responsibilities and determine which records are under the deployer's control.

Not every AI system is subject to identical logging requirements, and logging obligations should be evaluated against the applicable provisions.

---

### 112. EU AI Act Transparency and Instructions Assessment

Transparency assessment evaluates whether the applicable information and instruction requirements are addressed.

For relevant high-risk AI systems, Article 13 addresses transparency and provision of information to deployers.

Assessment may examine whether the relevant information supports appropriate interpretation and use, including, where applicable:

- Intended purpose
- Relevant capabilities and limitations
- Expected performance
- Known circumstances affecting performance
- Human oversight measures
- Maintenance and care requirements
- Relevant logging capabilities
- Information necessary for deployer responsibilities

Other AI systems may be subject to different transparency obligations, including Article 50 where applicable.

The assessor should identify the correct legal basis and intended recipient for each information requirement.

**Transparency Requirement ≠ Identical Disclosure for Every AI System.**

---

### 113. EU AI Act Human Oversight Assessment

Human oversight assessment evaluates whether the applicable oversight arrangements are designed and implemented to address the risks associated with the AI system.

For relevant high-risk AI systems, Article 14 addresses human oversight.

Assessment may consider:

- Who performs oversight
- Whether the assigned person has appropriate competence and authority
- Whether limitations are understood
- Whether automation bias is addressed where relevant
- Whether outputs can be interpreted appropriately
- Whether intervention is possible
- Whether the system can be stopped where required
- Whether escalation procedures exist
- Whether oversight is practical in the operating environment

The assessment should test the actual oversight mechanism rather than relying only on a statement that a human remains responsible.

Human presence does not automatically establish meaningful oversight.

---

### 114. EU AI Act Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity Assessment

For applicable high-risk AI systems, Article 15 addresses accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.

Assessment may consider:

- Defined performance criteria
- Relevant testing results
- Resilience to errors and inconsistencies
- System behavior under expected operating conditions
- Protection against relevant vulnerabilities
- Appropriate cybersecurity measures
- Monitoring of material performance degradation
- Treatment of identified deficiencies
- Evidence supporting applicable performance claims

Testing should reflect the system's intended purpose and relevant risks.

Security testing can provide evidence for cybersecurity controls, but it should not be treated as proof of all accuracy or robustness requirements.

Likewise, a strong benchmark result does not automatically establish that the system satisfies every applicable legal requirement.

---

### 115. EU AI Act Quality-Management Assessment

For providers of high-risk AI systems subject to the relevant provisions, Article 17 addresses the quality-management system.

Assessment should evaluate the applicable requirements and their implementation rather than simply checking whether a quality policy exists.

Relevant areas may include, as applicable:

- Regulatory compliance strategy
- Design and development procedures
- Quality-control processes
- Testing and validation
- Technical documentation management
- Data-management arrangements
- Risk management
- Post-market monitoring
- Incident reporting
- Corrective action
- Records and responsibilities

The assessor should consider the provider's documented procedures, implementation evidence, and consistency with the system's actual development and supply processes.

The exact requirements depend on the applicable legal provisions and any relevant simplified or alternative arrangements.

---

### 116. EU AI Act Conformity-Related Evidence Assessment

Conformity-related evidence assessment evaluates whether the relevant records exist, are applicable, and support the claims being made.

Depending on the system and applicable procedure, records may include:

- Technical documentation
- Quality-management documentation
- Conformity assessment records
- EU declaration of conformity
- CE marking information where applicable
- Notified-body documentation where relevant
- Registration records where required
- Instructions for use
- Corrective-action records

The assessor should verify that the evidence corresponds to the correct system, provider, version, intended purpose, and applicable legal requirements.

A conformity-related document should not be accepted solely because it bears a familiar label or refers generally to the EU AI Act.

Internal compliance assessment and formal conformity assessment are different activities. The applicable conformity-assessment procedure must be determined from the Regulation and the circumstances of the system.

### 118. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Governance Framework

Compliance assessment governance establishes how an organization identifies applicable requirements, authorizes assessments, evaluates evidence, resolves legal interpretations, approves conclusions, and monitors remediation.

- Compliance ownership
- Assessment methodology
- Risk-based prioritization
- Evidence standards

- Finding escalation
- Remediation governance
- Regulatory engagement
- Reporting to senior management
- Reassessment requirements

### 119. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Roles and Responsibilities

The organization should define responsibilities for each stage of the assessment lifecycle.

Potential responsibilities include:

- **Legal:** Interpret legal provisions and advise on disputed or material questions.
- **Compliance:** Maintain obligation registers, coordinate assessments, and track compliance gaps.
- **AI Governance:** Coordinate enterprise AI policies, risk classification processes, and oversight.
- **System Owner:** Maintain accurate information about intended purpose, deployment, and operational changes.
- **Engineering:** Provide technical documentation, system configuration details, and testing evidence.
- **Information Security:** Assess relevant cybersecurity controls.
- **Privacy:** Evaluate applicable data-protection obligations.
- **Procurement:** Obtain supplier information and manage relevant contractual dependencies.
- **Internal Audit:** Independently evaluate governance, risk management, and controls within its approved mandate.
- **Senior Management:** Make decisions within its authority and provide appropriate resources.

The organization should distinguish the legal responsibilities of the relevant operator from internal job titles.

A compliance manager may coordinate an assessment without becoming the legal provider or deployer of the system.

### 121. EU AI Act Legal Interpretation Governance

Legal interpretation governance provides a controlled process for resolving questions about applicability, classification, operator roles, exceptions, and obligations.

The process should define:

- Questions requiring legal review
- Authorized interpreters
- Source hierarchy
- Documentation standards
- Escalation criteria
- Approval requirements
- Treatment of uncertainty
- Review dates
- Reassessment triggers

Material interpretations should record the relevant facts, applicable provisions, assumptions, reasoning, and conclusion.

For example, whether a particular AI system falls within a high-risk category may depend on its intended purpose, role in a regulated activity, and the conditions in Article 6 and the relevant annexes.

Where the facts are incomplete, the organization should record the uncertainty rather than treating an unresolved question as settled.

**Internal Interpretation ≠ Binding Regulatory Determination.**

### 122. EU AI Act Legal Source Management

Legal source management ensures that compliance assessments use appropriate and current legal materials.

The source register may include:

- The consolidated Regulation
- Amending legislation
- Applicable implementing acts
- Applicable delegated acts
- Harmonised standards
- Common specifications
- Official Commission guidance
- Relevant national implementation or enforcement information
- Decisions or communications from competent authorities, where relevant

The organization should record the publication date, effective date, applicability date, legal status, affected obligations, and source version where relevant.

Draft legislation, proposed standards, consultation papers, and informal commentary should not be treated as equivalent to binding law.

Legal source management should also identify where guidance clarifies an implementation approach without itself creating a new statutory obligation.

### 123. EU AI Act Regulatory Change Management

Regulatory change management evaluates whether new legislation, amendments, implementing measures, or other relevant developments affect the organization's compliance position.

A change-management process should include:

1. Identify the regulatory change.
2. Verify its legal status.
3. Determine relevant dates.
4. Identify affected provisions.
5. Map affected AI systems and operator roles.
6. Assess the impact on controls and documentation.
7. Assign remediation owners.
8. Update assessment criteria.
9. Validate implementation.
10. Record the resulting compliance position.

The process should distinguish publication, entry into force, application, and transitional dates.

A law may enter into force before all its substantive requirements become applicable.

**Regulatory Publication ≠ Immediate Application of Every Provision.**

- Corrective-action deadlines

### 125. EU AI Act Transitional Provision Assessment

Transitional provisions determine how certain requirements apply to systems, models, or activities already existing at specified points in time.

Assessment should identify:

- The relevant legal provision
- The affected system or operator
- The applicable reference date
- Whether the system was placed on the market, put into service, or otherwise covered by the provision
- Whether a substantial modification occurred
- Whether a specific transitional exception applies
- The resulting compliance deadline
- Evidence supporting the transitional conclusion

The assessor should not assume that every pre-existing AI system is exempt from later requirements.

A system's status depends on the precise wording of the relevant transitional provision and the facts of the case.

Transitional treatment should be documented and reassessed if the system changes or new legal measures affect the applicable timeline.

### 126. EU AI Act Prohibited-Practice Compliance Assessment

Prohibited-practice assessment evaluates whether an AI practice falls within a prohibition under Article 5, as applicable to the relevant facts and legal conditions.

The assessment should examine the actual practice rather than relying solely on the product name, vendor description, or internal risk rating.

Potential assessment areas include:

- The system's function and intended purpose
- The conduct being performed
- The people or groups affected
- The context of use
- The relevant statutory conditions
- Applicable exceptions or qualifications
- The operator's knowledge and control
- Technical and organizational safeguards
- Evidence of actual deployment

The assessment should be performed before deployment where possible and repeated when use cases, features, or operating conditions change.

A practice that raises serious ethical or reputational concerns is not automatically a prohibited practice under Article 5. The legal conditions must be evaluated.

### 127. EU AI Act Manipulative and Deceptive Practice Assessment

The Regulation addresses specified AI practices involving manipulation or deception under defined conditions.

Assessment should determine whether the system's operation, design, and context satisfy the elements of an applicable prohibition.

Relevant questions may include:

- Does the practice involve a subliminal, purposefully manipulative, or deceptive technique covered by the provision?
- Is there a relevant distortion of behavior?
- Is there the legally specified likelihood or occurrence of significant harm?
- Are the other conditions of the relevant provision satisfied?
- Is the system used in the context described by the legal text?
- Is a relevant exception applicable?

Evidence may include design specifications, interaction flows, testing results, user research, deployment settings, and documented intended purpose.

The assessment should not classify every persuasive recommendation, personalization feature, or inaccurate AI output as prohibited without evaluating the applicable legal criteria.

### 128. EU AI Act Vulnerability-Exploitation Assessment

Article 5 includes a prohibition addressing certain AI practices that exploit vulnerabilities associated with age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation, subject to the provision's conditions.

Assessment should identify:

- The population affected
- The vulnerability potentially being exploited
- The mechanism of exploitation
- The relevant behavior distortion
- The legally specified harm conditions
- The intended and actual use
- Applicable qualifications or exceptions

Examples for investigation may include AI-enabled targeting or decision mechanisms that take advantage of a person's vulnerability in a way covered by the provision.

The assessor should distinguish ordinary personalization from conduct satisfying the legal conditions of the prohibition.

The conclusion should be supported by evidence about the system's operation and the relevant context.

### 129. EU AI Act Social Scoring Assessment

Social-scoring assessment evaluates whether an AI practice falls within the specific prohibition established by Article 5.

The analysis should consider the scoring mechanism, the data and behavior evaluated, the context of use, and the consequences attached to the score.

Relevant questions include:

- What characteristics, behavior, or personal data are being evaluated?
- Who is being scored?
- Is the evaluation performed over time?
- What consequences follow from the score?
- Does the practice produce the type of detrimental or unfavorable treatment addressed by the provision?
- Is the treatment connected to the relevant context or unrelated contexts described in the provision?
- Are the statutory conditions satisfied?

A credit score, fraud indicator, employee performance metric, or customer rating should not automatically be classified as prohibited social scoring merely because it evaluates people.

The assessment must apply the actual legal criteria.

- Context of use

- Affected individuals
- Purpose and intended use
- Applicable prohibitions or exceptions
- Relevant high-risk classification rules
- Transparency or other applicable obligations

The legal conclusion should identify the precise function and applicable provision.

- The claimed output
- The context of deployment
- The affected population
- Whether workplace or educational use is involved
- Any applicable prohibition or exception
- Any additional transparency or high-risk requirements

### 132. EU AI Act High-Risk Classification Reassessment

High-risk classification should be reassessed when relevant facts change.

Potential triggers include:

- A new intended purpose
- A change in the decision supported by AI
- Expansion into a new sector
- Integration into a regulated product
- A change in the affected population
- A material change to system functionality
- New evidence about system use
- Changes in the applicable legal framework
- A change in the provider's classification rationale

The reassessment should determine whether the system continues to meet the applicable criteria under Article 6 and Annex I or Annex III.

Where the provider relies on a statutory exception from an Annex III high-risk classification, the applicable legal conditions and required documentation should be revisited when the system changes.

An enterprise risk rating may influence assessment priority but does not replace the legal classification analysis.

Potential assessment areas include:

- Disclosure of artificially generated or manipulated content in relevant circumstances
- Disclosure concerning deepfakes
- Disclosure concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, subject to the provision's conditions and exceptions

### 134. EU AI Act AI-Interaction Disclosure Assessment

- Does a specific exception apply?
- When and how is the disclosure provided?
- Can users access the disclosure in the actual interface?

### 135. EU AI Act Synthetic-Content Marking Assessment

### 136. EU AI Act Deepfake Disclosure Assessment

### 169. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Program Reassessment

The overall assessment program should be periodically reassessed to determine whether it remains suitable for the organization's AI portfolio and legal environment.

Reassessment may be necessary when:

- The number or type of AI systems changes materially
- The organization enters new markets
- New legal obligations become applicable
- Existing legal interpretations change
- Significant incidents occur
- Assessment quality weaknesses are identified
- New suppliers or deployment models are introduced
- The organization changes its governance structure

The review should evaluate whether the program's methodology, resourcing, competence, and evidence standards remain appropriate.

A program that was suitable for a small number of low-complexity systems may be inadequate after significant expansion into high-risk AI activities.

### 170. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Limitations and Uncertainty

Compliance assessment may involve uncertainty because of incomplete evidence, ambiguous facts, evolving technical conditions, or unresolved legal interpretation.

The assessor should distinguish:

- **Factual uncertainty:** Relevant facts are not established.
- **Evidence limitation:** Required information is unavailable or insufficient.
- **Legal uncertainty:** The applicable legal interpretation remains unresolved.
- **Technical uncertainty:** System behavior or capabilities are not adequately understood.
- **Scope limitation:** Relevant subject matter was excluded or inaccessible.
- **Future uncertainty:** Compliance may be affected by subsequent changes.

Each limitation should be documented and evaluated for its effect on the conclusion.

Uncertainty should not automatically be interpreted as non-compliance, but neither should it be ignored when the unresolved issue could materially affect the legal position.

---

### 171. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after relevant controls and risk-treatment measures have been applied.

The assessment should distinguish residual organizational risk from the legal status of compliance.

For example, a system may retain operational risk despite satisfying applicable requirements. Conversely, management may judge a residual risk acceptable under an internal methodology while an applicable legal obligation remains unsatisfied.

The assessment should identify:

- Relevant residual risk
- Supporting evidence
- Applicable legal requirement
- Existing safeguards
- Management decision
- Required additional action
- Reassessment triggers

**Risk Acceptance ≠ Legal Authorization to Breach an Applicable Requirement.**

---

### 172. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Risk Acceptance

Management may accept certain residual business risks within its authority, subject to applicable law and organizational governance.

The assessment should verify whether the proposed acceptance:

- Identifies the relevant risk
- Is supported by adequate information
- Identifies affected systems and persons
- Considers available safeguards
- Is approved by an authorized person
- Defines review conditions
- Records relevant limitations

Risk acceptance should not be used as a substitute for a mandatory legal requirement, prohibited-practice restriction, or required conformity procedure.

Where a compliance gap cannot be accepted under applicable law, the organization should determine the necessary corrective action and any required escalation.

---

### 173. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Regulatory Readiness

Regulatory readiness evaluates whether the organization can explain and substantiate its compliance position using accurate, traceable, and current records.

Potential readiness activities include:

- Maintaining an accurate AI inventory
- Documenting operator roles
- Maintaining legal applicability analyses
- Retaining classification decisions
- Mapping obligations to controls
- Preserving relevant technical documentation
- Tracking conformity-related records
- Maintaining incident and corrective-action records
- Preparing evidence indexes
- Assigning regulatory response responsibilities

Readiness should be tested through controlled exercises or evidence-retrieval tests where appropriate.

The organization should identify missing or inconsistent information before a regulatory request or incident makes the gap more consequential.

### 174. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Independent Validation

Independent validation provides additional scrutiny of material assessment conclusions, particularly where legal interpretation, classification, or potential harm is significant.

Validation may examine:

- The correctness of legal references
- Applicability reasoning
- Operator-role analysis
- Classification methodology
- Completeness of obligation mapping
- Evidence quality
- Test logic
- Finding substantiation
- Conclusion limitations
- Remediation effectiveness

The appropriate validator depends on the purpose of the assessment and the required degree of independence.

Independent validation of an internal assessment does not replace a formal conformity assessment, notified-body involvement, or other legally required procedure.

### 176. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Core Governance Principles

An effective compliance assessment program should follow these principles:

1. Use authoritative and current legal sources.
2. Determine applicability before testing compliance.
3. Identify the relevant operator and legal role.
4. Document system classification and its rationale.
5. Distinguish legal requirements from internal controls.
6. Map applicable obligations to operational processes.
7. Assess control design, implementation, and operation separately.
8. Use evidence that is relevant, reliable, and sufficient.
9. Record assumptions, uncertainty, and scope limitations.
10. Distinguish internal finding severity from legal classification.
11. Escalate material issues through defined governance channels.
12. Track corrective actions through effectiveness validation.
13. Reassess when material facts or legal requirements change.
14. Coordinate related assessments without treating them as interchangeable.
15. Preserve management accountability and appropriate assessment independence.

These principles support a repeatable and defensible assessment process. They do not eliminate the need to apply the exact legal provisions relevant to each system and operator.

## Part 4 — EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Execution, Testing Methodology, Reporting and Continuous Improvement

### 178. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Planning

Assessment planning establishes the scope, objectives, criteria, resources, methodology, and expected outputs of a compliance assessment.

The assessment plan should identify:

- The AI system and relevant deployment context
- The legal entity or entities involved
- The provider, deployer, importer, distributor, or other relevant operator roles
- The intended purpose and actual use
- The preliminary legal applicability analysis
- The applicable provisions and assessment criteria
- The systems, processes, and business units included
- The evidence required
- The assessment team and reviewers
- The testing approach
- The reporting arrangements
- The assessment period
- Known limitations and dependencies

Planning should begin with legal applicability rather than assuming that every AI system is subject to the same set of requirements.

For example, an assessment of an AI-enabled recruitment tool should establish the system's intended purpose, the relevant operator roles, and the applicable legal classification before selecting the controls to test.

**Assessment Plan Approved ≠ Compliance Established.**

---

### 179. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Scope Definition

The scope defines precisely what the assessment covers and what it excludes.

A scope statement should identify:

1. AI system or systems assessed.
2. Relevant versions and configurations.
3. Business processes and use cases.
4. Geographic and organizational boundaries.
5. Relevant legal entities and operator roles.
6. Applicable legal requirements.
7. Assessment period.
8. Technical environments examined.
9. Suppliers and external dependencies.
10. Exclusions and limitations.

The scope should be sufficiently precise to prevent conclusions from being applied to systems, versions, or deployments that were not assessed.

For example, evaluating a chatbot in a controlled testing environment does not automatically establish compliance for a production deployment that processes different data or supports materially different decisions.

Any exclusion that could affect the compliance conclusion should be documented and communicated to the appropriate stakeholders.

---

### 180. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Objectives

Assessment objectives describe the questions the assessment is intended to answer.

Potential objectives include determining whether:

- The AI Act applies to the system or activity.
- The organization has correctly identified its legal role.
- The system has been classified using the applicable criteria.
- Applicable obligations have been identified.
- Required controls and processes have been implemented.
- Required documentation is available and sufficiently complete.
- Controls operate effectively.
- Required conformity-related procedures have been completed.
- Identified gaps have been remediated.
- Monitoring and reassessment mechanisms are operating.

Objectives should be specific enough to guide evidence collection and testing.

A broad objective such as "verify AI Act compliance" should be decomposed into testable questions linked to specific legal provisions and relevant facts.

---

### 181. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria are the requirements against which evidence is evaluated.

They may include:

- Binding provisions of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended
- Applicable implementing and delegated acts
- Applicable harmonised standards or common specifications
- Relevant conformity-assessment requirements
- Applicable transitional provisions
- Internal controls designed to implement legal obligations
- Contractual commitments supporting compliance
- Approved organizational procedures

The assessor should distinguish statutory criteria from internal implementation criteria.

For example, a policy may require quarterly AI risk reviews. That frequency is an internal control requirement unless a specific applicable legal provision independently establishes it.

Where an assessment uses a standard or guidance document, the report should identify its role in the assessment and avoid presenting every recommendation in that source as a binding statutory obligation.

---

### 182. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Population and Sampling

The assessment population consists of the systems, records, transactions, controls, or other items from which assessment evidence will be selected.

The population should be defined before sampling.

Potential populations include:

- All AI systems within the assessment scope
- All systems classified as high-risk
- All relevant supplier agreements
- All applicable classification decisions
- All serious-incident records
- All corrective actions due during the period
- All records associated with a specified control
- All systems deployed by a particular business unit

Sampling should reflect the assessment objective and the consequences of an error.

A sample of supplier agreements may help assess procurement controls, but it cannot establish that the AI inventory is complete if the sampling frame excludes unknown or unregistered systems.

Sampling limitations should be recorded in the assessment workpapers and reflected in the final conclusion.

---

### 183. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Evidence Request

An evidence request communicates the information needed to evaluate a requirement or control.

Each request should specify:

- The requirement or assessment question
- The requested evidence
- The relevant system or process
- The applicable period
- The expected format
- The responsible evidence owner
- The submission deadline
- Any confidentiality or handling requirements

For example, an evidence request for a high-risk AI system may ask for the documented risk-management process, system-specific risk records, risk-treatment decisions, and evidence of review.

Requests should be sufficiently precise to avoid receiving large volumes of documents that do not answer the assessment question.

If evidence cannot be supplied, the assessor should document the reason, identify alternative evidence where appropriate, and determine whether the gap limits the assessment conclusion.

---

### 184. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Evidence Evaluation

Evidence evaluation determines whether the information obtained supports the relevant conclusion.

The assessor should consider:

- Relevance to the legal requirement
- Reliability of the source
- Completeness
- Accuracy
- Currency
- Authenticity
- Traceability
- Consistency with other evidence
- Applicability to the assessed system and version

Evidence may include policies, technical documentation, system configurations, test results, interviews, operational records, contracts, and direct observations.

A management interview may explain how a control operates, but it may not independently establish that the control was consistently performed.

Where evidence sources conflict, the assessor should investigate the discrepancy rather than selecting the source that supports the preferred conclusion.

---

### 185. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Interview Methodology

Interviews help the assessor understand how legal requirements are implemented in practice.

Interviewees may include Legal, Compliance, AI Governance, Product Management, Engineering, Information Security, Privacy, Procurement, system owners, and control owners.

A structured interview should establish:

- The interviewee's responsibilities
- The relevant process
- How decisions are made
- What evidence is generated
- How exceptions are handled
- Who approves material changes
- How deficiencies are escalated
- How the process is monitored

Interview statements should be corroborated where the assessment requires evidence of implementation or operating effectiveness.

For example, a system owner may state that users receive required disclosures. The assessor should inspect the deployed interface and relevant configuration rather than relying solely on the interview response.

---

### 186. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Walkthroughs

A walkthrough traces a requirement or control through an actual process, from initiation to completion.

A walkthrough may examine:

- AI system registration in the internal inventory
- Legal applicability review
- Risk classification
- Approval before deployment
- Technical documentation
- User disclosure
- Incident escalation
- Supplier onboarding
- Change management
- Corrective-action closure

The assessor should select a real example and trace the relevant records, decisions, approvals, and outputs.

Walkthroughs help establish whether a process is understood and implemented, but a single walkthrough may not demonstrate consistent operation over an extended period.

Where operating effectiveness is relevant, additional samples and testing may be necessary.

---

### 187. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Design Effectiveness

Design-effectiveness testing evaluates whether a control is suitably designed to address the relevant requirement or risk.

The assessor should ask:

- Is the control linked to the correct legal obligation?
- Does it address the relevant risk?
- Is ownership assigned?
- Are responsibilities clear?
- Is the control performed at the appropriate point in the lifecycle?
- Does it produce usable evidence?
- Are exceptions addressed?
- Is escalation defined?
- Does the control respond to relevant changes?

For example, a process that classifies AI systems only after production deployment may be poorly designed if classification is needed earlier to determine applicable pre-deployment requirements.

A control may be documented and approved but still fail design-effectiveness testing if it does not adequately address the applicable requirement.

---

### 188. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Operating Effectiveness

Operating-effectiveness testing determines whether a suitably designed control operated as intended during the relevant period.

Testing may include:

- Inspection of dated records
- Reperformance of calculations or decisions
- Observation of a process
- Examination of system logs
- Sampling of approvals
- Verification of configuration settings
- Comparison of recorded activity with actual deployment
- Testing of exception handling

The assessor should define the testing period, population, sample, method, and expected result.

A control that passed one design review may fail operating-effectiveness testing if personnel did not perform it consistently.

Likewise, a control may operate consistently but still be inadequate because its design does not address the legal requirement.

**Design Effectiveness ≠ Operating Effectiveness.**

---

### 189. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Reperformance

Reperformance independently repeats a control activity or assessment procedure to determine whether it produces a consistent and supportable result.

Examples include:

- Reperforming an AI Act applicability analysis
- Recalculating a compliance metric
- Rechecking a classification decision
- Reproducing a documented test
- Verifying a registration record
- Reconstructing an approval workflow
- Reperforming a corrective-action validation

The assessor should document the inputs, procedure, expected outcome, actual outcome, and any differences.

Reperformance is particularly useful where the original process involves judgment, calculations, classification logic, or system-generated results.

If the reperformed result differs from the original, the assessor should investigate the cause and determine whether the difference affects the compliance conclusion.

---

### 190. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Configuration Testing

Configuration testing examines whether system settings implement the intended technical or organizational control.

Depending on the system, testing may examine:

- User disclosure settings
- Access permissions
- Logging configuration
- Data-retention settings
- Human-review workflows
- Model or application version
- Security controls
- Output-marking mechanisms
- Deployment restrictions
- Monitoring alerts

The assessor should establish which configuration is applicable to the assessed production system and whether the evidence corresponds to the relevant period.

A screenshot of a development environment does not automatically establish the configuration of a production deployment.

Where configurations vary by environment, tenant, region, or customer, the assessment should account for those differences.

---

### 191. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Technical Testing

Technical testing evaluates relevant system characteristics using appropriate technical methods.

Depending on the legal obligation and system, this may include:

- Functional testing
- Performance testing
- Robustness testing
- Cybersecurity testing
- Bias or performance-disparity analysis
- Human-oversight testing
- Transparency testing
- Logging verification
- Monitoring validation
- Adversarial testing where appropriate

Technical testing should be linked to the applicable requirement and system risk.

A generic penetration test, model evaluation, or benchmark score should not automatically be treated as proof of compliance with every relevant AI Act obligation.

The assessment should document the test environment, method, assumptions, limitations, results, and relationship to the requirement being evaluated.

---

### 192. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Documentation Review

Documentation review evaluates whether required records exist, are accurate, are sufficiently complete, and correspond to the relevant system and legal role.

Potential documents include:

- System descriptions
- Intended-purpose statements
- Risk-management records
- Technical documentation
- Instructions for use
- Data-governance records
- Logging arrangements
- Human-oversight instructions
- Quality-management documentation
- Conformity-related records
- Registration information
- Post-market monitoring plans
- Incident records
- Corrective-action evidence

The required documents depend on the system category, the operator's role, the applicable provision, and relevant exceptions or transitions.

The assessor should avoid using a generic document checklist without first determining which items are legally applicable.

---

### 193. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Traceability Testing

Traceability testing verifies that the organization can connect a legal requirement to its implementation and supporting evidence.

For a selected obligation, the assessor should trace:

1. Authoritative legal source.
2. Applicable provision.
3. Applicability rationale.
4. Responsible legal operator.
5. Required outcome.
6. Implementing control.
7. Control owner.
8. Evidence record.
9. Test procedure.
10. Test result.
11. Finding, if applicable.
12. Remediation and validation.

A broken link may indicate an obligation-mapping gap, an ownership problem, missing evidence, or an inadequately designed control.

Traceability testing also helps identify controls that have no clear relationship to a legal obligation and requirements that have no corresponding implementation or assessment activity.

---

### 194. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Finding Identification

A finding identifies a substantiated deficiency, unresolved issue, or material limitation discovered during assessment.

A finding should include:

- A clear statement of the issue
- The relevant requirement or assessment criterion
- The expected condition
- The observed condition
- Supporting evidence
- The affected system or process
- The risk or consequence
- The likely cause, where established
- The responsible owner
- Recommended or required action
- The response and validation arrangements

The assessor should distinguish a confirmed deficiency from a hypothesis that requires further investigation.

Findings should be supported by evidence and should not overstate the legal consequences of the observed condition.

---

### 195. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Finding Criteria

A useful finding structure distinguishes the following elements:

| Element     | Assessment question                       |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Criterion   | What requirement or control should apply? |
| Condition   | What was actually observed?               |
| Evidence    | What supports the observation?            |
| Cause       | Why did the condition occur, if known?    |
| Consequence | What risk or effect may result?           |
| Action      | What needs to change?                     |
| Owner       | Who is accountable for the response?      |
| Validation  | How will remediation be verified?         |

Not every assessment finding requires a definitive root-cause conclusion at the time of initial reporting. Where the cause is uncertain, the report should say so.

The finding should identify whether the criterion is a binding legal obligation, an applicable technical requirement, an internal control, or another assessment benchmark.

This distinction prevents internal control deviations from being incorrectly reported as established statutory breaches.

---

### 196. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Finding Classification

Finding classification helps management prioritize remediation.

An internal classification model may consider:

- Potential harm to individuals
- Impact on fundamental rights
- Safety consequences
- Cybersecurity exposure
- Scope of affected systems
- Duration of the deficiency
- Likelihood of occurrence
- Legal or regulatory significance
- Evidence limitations
- Availability of compensating controls
- Immediacy of required action

The organization should define classification criteria before applying them.

Internal categories such as Critical, High, Medium, and Low are management tools. They are not automatically equivalent to formal legal categories under the AI Act.

Where a potential prohibited practice, serious incident, or other legally significant condition is identified, the organization should separately assess the applicable legal response and escalation requirements.

---

### 197. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Root-Cause Analysis

Root-cause analysis identifies the underlying factors that contributed to a deficiency.

Potential causes include:

- Incomplete AI inventory
- Incorrect legal interpretation
- Unclear accountability
- Inadequate system documentation
- Weak supplier governance
- Insufficient staff competence
- Poor change management
- Missing technical controls
- Inadequate monitoring
- Failure to escalate exceptions
- Outdated assessment criteria

Root-cause analysis should distinguish the immediate error from systemic conditions.

For example, a missing classification record may result from an individual omission, but repeated omissions may indicate that the deployment workflow does not require classification before release.

Corrective actions should address the established cause where feasible rather than only correcting the individual record.

---

### 198. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Corrective-Action Planning

Corrective-action planning defines the measures needed to address an identified deficiency.

Each action should specify:

- The issue being addressed
- The intended outcome
- The action owner
- Required resources
- Dependencies
- Target completion date
- Interim safeguards, where necessary
- Required evidence
- Validation criteria
- Escalation arrangements

Actions should be proportionate to the significance of the issue and consistent with applicable legal requirements.

For example, if an organization cannot substantiate a classification decision, an appropriate action may involve reconstructing the legal analysis, obtaining missing system information, reviewing affected deployments, and introducing a control that prevents similar documentation gaps.

An action plan should not rely solely on a promise to improve the process without defining how the improvement will be implemented and verified.

---

### 199. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Remediation Validation

Remediation validation determines whether corrective action has resolved the identified deficiency.

Validation should establish:

- Whether the action was completed
- Whether the original condition has been corrected
- Whether the underlying cause was addressed
- Whether the revised control operates effectively
- Whether affected systems were reviewed
- Whether residual issues remain
- Whether further action is necessary

Evidence may include revised records, configuration results, test outputs, approvals, updated procedures, and samples from subsequent operation.

A completed ticket or management declaration is not sufficient by itself where the assessment objective requires evidence of effective remediation.

The validator should document the test performed and the basis for closure.

---

### 200. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Closure Criteria

Assessment closure occurs when the defined assessment activities and reporting requirements have been completed to the required standard.

Closure criteria may include:

- Scope and objectives confirmed
- Applicable criteria documented
- Required evidence evaluated
- Testing completed or limitations disclosed
- Findings reviewed
- Material disagreements resolved or documented
- Management responses recorded
- Reporting approved
- Remediation owners assigned
- Follow-up arrangements established
- Workpapers retained according to policy

Closure does not necessarily mean that every finding has been remediated.

Where open findings remain, the report should state their status, required actions, ownership, deadlines, and any relevant interim safeguards.

The organization should not describe an assessment as demonstrating full compliance when material scope limitations or unresolved gaps prevent that conclusion.

---

### 201. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Workpapers

Workpapers preserve the evidence and reasoning supporting assessment conclusions.

A workpaper may include:

- Assessment objective
- Scope and criteria
- Relevant legal source
- Applicability analysis
- Evidence reference
- Population and sample
- Test procedure
- Expected result
- Actual result
- Exceptions
- Assessor conclusion
- Reviewer comments
- Resolution of comments
- Cross-references to findings

Workpapers should be sufficiently detailed for an appropriately qualified reviewer to understand what was tested and why the conclusion was reached.

They should use consistent identifiers and retain references to relevant system versions and evidence dates.

Workpapers should also be protected against unauthorized access or alteration and retained in accordance with applicable requirements and organizational policy.

---

### 202. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Quality Review

Quality review evaluates whether the assessment was performed consistently with the approved methodology and whether its conclusions are adequately supported.

Review may examine:

- Correctness of legal references
- Completeness of applicability analysis
- Operator-role identification
- Classification rationale
- Evidence sufficiency
- Test execution
- Finding accuracy
- Severity rationale
- Scope limitations
- Conclusion consistency
- Reporting clarity
- Required approvals

Review depth should reflect the significance and complexity of the assessment.

Material legal interpretations, high-risk classification decisions, and potentially serious compliance gaps may warrant additional specialist review.

Quality review should be documented, and significant reviewer comments should be resolved or disclosed before the report is finalized.

### 203. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Reporting Structure

The final report should communicate the assessment's purpose, scope, methodology, results, and limitations.

A recommended structure is:

1. Executive summary
2. Assessment objective
3. Scope and period
4. Applicable criteria
5. Systems and operator roles assessed
6. Methodology and sample
7. Key compliance conclusions
8. Material findings
9. Evidence limitations
10. Legal interpretation issues
11. Management responses
12. Corrective-action plan
13. Follow-up arrangements
14. Appendices and evidence references

The report should distinguish the assessor's conclusions from management representations and proposed actions.

The report should also identify the basis for any overall conclusion and explain whether the assessment provides full, limited, or otherwise qualified coverage of the defined scope.

The wording should not imply a regulatory approval or formal conformity result that the assessment did not establish.

---

### 204. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Executive Summary

The executive summary communicates the most important results to senior management.

It should identify:

- The systems and activities assessed
- The assessment period
- The overall conclusion
- Material legal applicability issues
- Significant findings
- Major evidence limitations
- Unresolved decisions
- Required management action
- Important deadlines
- The principal risks requiring oversight

The summary should use precise language.

For example, "No deficiencies were identified in the controls tested" is narrower and more defensible than "The organization is fully compliant with the AI Act" when the assessment covered only selected systems or requirements.

The summary should explain material limitations rather than hiding them in an appendix.

---

### 205. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Conclusion Types

An organization should define how assessment conclusions are expressed.

Possible internal conclusion categories include:

- **Compliant within the assessed scope:** Evidence supports satisfaction of the specified criteria within the defined scope and period.
- **Compliant with observations:** No identified deficiency prevents the stated conclusion, but improvement opportunities were noted.
- **Partially compliant:** Some applicable criteria are met while material deficiencies remain.
- **Non-compliant:** Evidence establishes that one or more applicable requirements are not satisfied.
- **Unable to conclude:** Evidence or scope limitations prevent a reliable conclusion.
- **Not applicable:** The documented legal analysis establishes that the specified requirement does not apply to the assessed circumstances.

These categories are illustrative internal reporting conventions, not official EU AI Act certification outcomes.

A conclusion should be supported by the evidence and limited to the assessed scope, period, and criteria.

---

### 206. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Management Response

The management response records how responsible management addresses each finding.

A response should state:

- Whether management agrees with the finding
- Any factual clarification
- The proposed corrective action
- The responsible owner
- The target date
- Required resources
- Interim safeguards
- Dependencies
- The evidence that will demonstrate completion

Where management disagrees, the response should explain the basis for the disagreement and provide supporting evidence.

The assessor should evaluate whether the response addresses the issue and the applicable legal requirement.

Management agreement does not itself validate the finding, and management disagreement does not automatically invalidate an evidence-supported conclusion.

---

### 207. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Follow-Up

Follow-up verifies whether agreed corrective actions have been implemented and whether material issues remain open.

Follow-up activities may include:

- Reviewing updated documentation
- Retesting controls
- Inspecting system configuration
- Sampling subsequent transactions or decisions
- Rechecking legal applicability
- Confirming completion of required procedures
- Evaluating residual risk
- Escalating overdue actions

Follow-up should prioritize findings according to their significance, legal implications, and the urgency of the required action.

If a remediation deadline is missed, the organization should determine whether escalation, interim measures, or other action is necessary.

Closing an assessment report should not prevent later reassessment when new information reveals a material problem.

---

### 208. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Continuous Monitoring

Continuous monitoring helps identify changes that may affect the compliance position between formal assessments.

Potential monitoring inputs include:

- New AI systems
- Changes to intended purpose
- Model or application updates

### 227. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Regulatory Source Hierarchy

### 230. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Practical Case Study

**Scenario:** A European organization deploys an AI-enabled recruitment platform to help screen applications and rank candidates for review by its recruitment team.

The organization wants to determine whether its deployment complies with the EU AI Act.

**Step 1 — Identify the system and use case**

Document the system, version, provider, intended purpose, actual deployment, affected population, and relevant decision workflow.

**Step 2 — Identify operator roles**

Determine which entity acts as provider, deployer, or another relevant operator under the actual arrangements. Do not infer the legal role solely from contract labels.

**Step 3 — Determine applicability**

Evaluate territorial scope and the applicable legal provisions using authoritative sources.

**Step 4 — Assess classification**

Recruitment and candidate-selection systems may fall within the high-risk category described in Annex III, subject to the relevant legal criteria and any applicable exception. The organization must assess the actual intended purpose and statutory conditions.

**Step 5 — Map obligations**

Identify the obligations applicable to each relevant operator and the system, including those concerning risk management, documentation, human oversight, transparency, and other applicable requirements.

**Step 6 — Collect evidence**

Obtain relevant system documentation, provider information, deployment records, human-review procedures, testing results, and governance records.

**Step 7 — Test controls**

Inspect actual workflows, sample decisions, verify relevant configurations, and evaluate whether required procedures operate as intended.

**Step 8 — Record findings**

If the organization cannot demonstrate that required human oversight arrangements operate effectively, record the specific deficiency, evidence, relevant criterion, and potential consequences.

**Step 9 — Remediate**

Assign actions, owners, deadlines, and appropriate interim measures.

**Step 10 — Validate and report**

Retest the revised arrangements, document any residual limitations, and issue a conclusion limited to the assessed scope.

The case illustrates why classification, obligation mapping, evidence collection, testing, and remediation must form one traceable process.

### 235. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Common Failure Modes

- Assigning operator roles solely from contractual terminology
- Confusing an internal risk rating with statutory classification
- Treating every Annex III system as high-risk without examining applicable exceptions
- Assuming that a policy proves operational compliance
- Treating supplier assurances as complete evidence

- Failing to assess transitional provisions
- Confusing internal controls with statutory requirements

- Treating management risk acceptance as permission to disregard mandatory law
- Making an overall compliance claim despite significant scope limitations
- Failing to reassess after material changes

The assessment methodology should explicitly address these failure modes through source verification, role analysis, evidence standards, quality review, and change management.

### 239. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Relationship to Formal Conformity Assessment

Compliance assessment and formal conformity assessment are related but distinct activities.

An internal compliance assessment evaluates whether specified obligations are satisfied within a defined scope. A formal conformity-assessment process follows the procedures applicable to the relevant system and legal category.

Depending on the applicable requirements, formal conformity assessment may involve internal control procedures, notified-body involvement, or other prescribed arrangements.

The assessor should determine:

- Whether the system is subject to a conformity-assessment requirement
- Which procedure applies
- Which entity is responsible
- What documentation and testing are required
- Whether a notified body is required
- What declarations or markings are required, where applicable
- Whether the relevant procedure has been completed

An internal audit report should not be represented as a declaration of conformity, certificate, or regulatory approval unless the relevant formal process has actually produced that outcome.

### 245. EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Final Governance Principles

A reliable assessment program should apply the following principles:

18. Distinguish internal assessment from formal conformity assessment.
19. Maintain appropriate records and regulatory readiness.
20. Improve the program using assessment results and operational experience.

These principles support consistency and defensibility but do not replace analysis of the precise requirements applicable to each system and operator.

### 246. Core EU AI Act Compliance Assessment Principle

An effective EU AI Act compliance assessment is a **structured, legally grounded, risk-based, and evidence-driven process that determines whether the relevant operator and AI system satisfy applicable requirements within a defined scope and period**.

The assessment must connect authoritative legal sources, applicability decisions, operator roles, statutory classification, obligation mapping, controls, evidence, testing, findings, remediation, and final conclusions.

Its reliability depends on accurate legal interpretation, suitable assessment methods, credible evidence, transparent limitations, and timely reassessment when the system or legal environment changes.

The complete lifecycle can be represented as follows:

```
AUTHORITATIVE LEGAL SOURCES
            |
            v
SCOPE AND APPLICABILITY
            |
            v
OPERATOR ROLE IDENTIFICATION
            |
            v
STATUTORY RISK CLASSIFICATION
            |
            v
OBLIGATION REGISTER
            |
            v
CONTROL MAPPING
            |
            v

            |
            v
DESIGN AND OPERATING TESTS
            |
            v
FINDINGS AND CONCLUSIONS
            |
            v
MANAGEMENT RESPONSE
            |
            v

            |
            v

            |
            v

            |
            v
CONTINUOUS MONITORING
            |
            v
LEGAL OR SYSTEM CHANGE
            |
            +----------------------+
                                   |
                                   v
                              REASSESSMENT
```

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- **Applicability Assessment ≠ Compliance Assessment.**
- **Compliance Assessment ≠ Formal Conformity Assessment Automatically.**
- **Internal Control ≠ Statutory Requirement Automatically.**
- **Enterprise Risk Rating ≠ Statutory AI Risk Classification.**
- **Supplier Assurance ≠ Complete Customer Compliance Assurance.**
- **Document Existence ≠ Operational Compliance.**
- **Evidence Availability ≠ Evidence Sufficiency.**
- **Design Effectiveness ≠ Operating Effectiveness.**
- **Technical Testing ≠ Complete Legal Compliance Assessment.**
- **Internal Finding Severity ≠ Formal Legal Classification.**
- **Management Risk Acceptance ≠ Permission to Disregard Mandatory Law.**
- **Corrective Action Completion ≠ Remediation Effectiveness.**
- **Internal Assessment ≠ Regulatory Approval.**
- **Compliance Maturity ≠ Legal Compliance Automatically.**
- **Previous Assessment ≠ Permanent Compliance Assurance.**
- **Compliance Assessment ≠ Guarantee of Future Compliance.**
