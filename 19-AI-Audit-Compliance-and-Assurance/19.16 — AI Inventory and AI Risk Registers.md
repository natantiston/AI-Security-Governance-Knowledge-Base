# 19.16 — AI Inventory and AI Risk Registers

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Inventory and AI Risk Register Governance

### 01. Introduction to AI Inventory and AI Risk Registers

AI inventory and AI risk registers are foundational components of an organization's AI governance and assurance program.

An **AI inventory** identifies and maintains an authoritative record of the AI systems, models, applications, services, and use cases operating within or on behalf of the organization.

An **AI risk register** records the risks associated with those AI systems and use cases, including their causes, potential consequences, assessments, existing controls, treatment plans, and accountable owners.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
AI Systems and Use Cases
          ↓
     AI Inventory
          ↓
   AI Risk Identification
          ↓
    AI Risk Register
          ↓
 Risk Treatment and Monitoring
          ↓
 Governance, Audit, and Assurance
```

The inventory establishes **what AI exists, where it is used, and who is accountable for it**.

The risk register establishes **what could go wrong, how significant the exposure is, and what the organization is doing about it**.

Together, they provide a structured foundation for AI risk management, regulatory compliance, internal audit, management oversight, and enterprise-level risk reporting.

---

### 02. Definition of an AI Inventory

An AI inventory is a controlled record of AI systems and related AI use cases that fall within an organization's defined governance scope.

Depending on the organization's architecture and operating model, the inventory may include:

- Internally developed AI models.
- Third-party AI applications.
- Commercial AI services.
- Generative AI assistants.
- Large language model applications.
- Retrieval-augmented generation systems.
- AI agents capable of using tools or executing actions.
- Predictive analytics and machine learning models.
- AI-enabled business applications.
- AI capabilities embedded in enterprise software.
- AI systems operated by suppliers on the organization's behalf.
- Experimental AI systems and proof-of-concept projects.

An AI inventory is not simply a list of software products.

It should provide enough information to understand the AI capability, its intended purpose, its operational context, its dependencies, its data, and its governance responsibilities.

For example:

```
AI Inventory Record

AI ID:
AI-APP-001

System:
Enterprise Customer Service Assistant

AI Capability:
Generative AI / Large Language Model

Business Purpose:
Customer inquiry support

Deployment:
Cloud-hosted

Data:
Customer records and product documentation

Business Owner:
Customer Service Director

Technical Owner:
AI Platform Manager

Risk Classification:
High

Lifecycle Status:
Production
```

The record gives governance stakeholders a starting point for determining which controls, assessments, approvals, and monitoring activities are required.

---

### 03. Definition of an AI Risk Register

An AI risk register is a structured record of identified AI-related risks and the information required to assess, manage, monitor, and report those risks.

It captures the relationship between a risk scenario, its assessment, the controls intended to manage it, and the decisions made by accountable stakeholders.

A risk register may contain:

- Risk identifier.
- Associated AI system or use case.
- Risk category.
- Risk statement.
- Causes and contributing conditions.
- Risk event.
- Potential consequences.
- Inherent risk assessment.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness.
- Residual risk assessment.
- Risk owner.
- Treatment decision.
- Corrective actions.
- Target completion dates.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Review dates.
- Evidence and supporting documentation.
- Current risk status.

A simplified record may look like this:

| Field            | Example                                                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID          | AI-R-001                                                 |
| AI System ID     | AI-APP-001                                               |
| Risk Scenario    | Unauthorized disclosure of customer information          |
| Primary Cause    | Inadequate access enforcement across retrieved documents |
| Likelihood       | Possible                                                 |
| Impact           | Major                                                    |
| Inherent Risk    | High                                                     |
| Existing Control | Identity-based access restrictions                       |
| Residual Risk    | Medium                                                   |
| Risk Owner       | Customer Service Director                                |
| Treatment        | Strengthen retrieval authorization and validation        |
| Status           | Treatment in progress                                    |

The risk register converts risk analysis into an accountable governance process.

A risk that is merely discussed in a meeting but not recorded, assigned, assessed, and tracked may be difficult to manage consistently or demonstrate during an audit.

### 04. AI Inventory vs. AI Risk Register

The inventory and risk register serve different but complementary purposes.

| Dimension        | AI Inventory                                                | AI Risk Register                                     |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary question | What AI exists?                                             | What could go wrong?                                 |
| Main purpose     | Establish AI visibility                                     | Manage AI exposure                                   |
| Main record      | AI system or use case                                       | Individual risk scenario                             |
| Key information  | Purpose, owner, model, data, dependencies                   | Causes, consequences, ratings, controls              |
| Accountability   | Business and technical ownership                            | Risk ownership and treatment                         |
| Lifecycle focus  | Discovery through retirement                                | Identification through closure or ongoing monitoring |
| Main output      | Authoritative AI landscape                                  | Documented AI risk exposure                          |
| Audit value      | Demonstrates inventory completeness and governance coverage | Demonstrates risk assessment and treatment           |
| Management value | Shows the scope of AI adoption                              | Shows the significance of AI-related risks           |

The inventory and register should not be treated as interchangeable.

An inventory entry does not automatically constitute a complete risk assessment. Similarly, a risk register cannot reliably demonstrate the organization's total AI exposure if the organization does not know which AI systems exist.

The intended relationship is:

```
AI Inventory
     │
     ├── AI System A
     │      ├── Risk A1
     │      ├── Risk A2
     │      └── Risk A3
     │
     ├── AI System B
     │      ├── Risk B1
     │      └── Risk B2
     │
     └── AI System C
            └── Risk C1
```

One AI system can have multiple risks, and the same risk theme can affect multiple systems.

---

### 05. Purpose of the AI Inventory

The primary purpose of the AI inventory is to establish organizational visibility and accountability over AI use.

Without a reliable inventory, management may be unable to determine:

- Which business units use AI.
- Which systems process confidential or personal information.
- Which AI applications are externally accessible.
- Which systems can make or influence consequential decisions.
- Which models or providers support critical business processes.
- Which AI systems require formal risk assessments.
- Which systems are subject to specific legal or regulatory requirements.
- Which systems have completed required approvals.
- Which systems require ongoing monitoring.
- Which AI deployments should be reviewed, restricted, or retired.

The inventory also supports planning and resource allocation.

For example, an organization with 200 registered AI use cases may need to prioritize governance reviews for systems that process sensitive personal data, execute autonomous actions, support critical business operations, or create significant potential consequences for individuals.

The inventory provides the population from which those priorities can be determined.

---

### 06. Purpose of the AI Risk Register

The AI risk register provides a controlled mechanism for understanding and managing AI-related uncertainty.

Its main purposes are to:

- Record identified AI risk scenarios.
- Establish consistent risk assessments.
- Identify accountable risk owners.
- Document existing controls and their effectiveness.
- Determine whether residual risks fall within approved risk tolerance.
- Track risk treatment decisions.
- Monitor unresolved risks and overdue actions.
- Record formal risk acceptance.
- Escalate material exposure to management.
- Support internal audit and independent assurance.
- Identify common risk themes across AI systems.
- Integrate AI risks into enterprise risk management.

The register should support decisions, not merely collect risk descriptions.

For example:

```
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Analyzed
      ↓
Risk Evaluated Against Criteria
      ↓
Treatment Decision
      ↓
Controls or Corrective Actions
      ↓
Effectiveness Validation
      ↓
Residual Risk Review
      ↓
Accept, Escalate, or Continue Treatment
```

The process should be proportionate to the significance of the risk and the organization's approved methodology.

### 07. Why AI Inventory Is Different From a Traditional IT Asset Inventory

A traditional IT asset inventory typically emphasizes hardware, software, infrastructure, configuration, ownership, and operational status.

An AI inventory may require those details, but it also needs information about the AI capability and its intended use.

Relevant AI-specific attributes can include:

- AI purpose and intended use.
- Model type and version.
- AI provider.
- Training, fine-tuning, or inference arrangements.
- Data sources and data provenance.
- Retrieval and knowledge sources.
- Human oversight arrangements.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Tools and external actions available to the AI.
- Output validation mechanisms.
- Model performance and known limitations.
- AI-specific risk classifications.
- Evaluation and testing status.
- Model and system change history.
- Applicable governance and compliance requirements.

For example, two applications may use the same underlying language model but have very different risk profiles.

```
Shared Foundation Model
          │
     ┌────┴────┐
     ↓         ↓
AI Assistant  AI Agent
     │         │
Drafts Text   Modifies CRM Records
     │         │
Human Review  Automated Execution
     │         │
Lower Action  Higher Action
Exposure      Exposure
```

The difference arises from the surrounding architecture, permissions, business purpose, data access, and level of autonomy.

Therefore, the inventory should describe the deployed AI capability in context rather than recording only the model's name.

### 08. Why AI Risk Registers Require AI-Specific Risk Information

Traditional IT risk registers often address cybersecurity, availability, confidentiality, integrity, operational resilience, and third-party risk.

AI risk management must consider these areas while also addressing risks associated with AI behavior, development, deployment, and use.

Examples include:

**AI security risks**

- Prompt injection.
- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Model or data poisoning.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Compromised model artifacts.
- Excessive agent permissions.

**AI performance and reliability risks**

- Hallucinations.
- Incorrect predictions.
- Inadequate model calibration.
- Model drift.
- Data drift.
- Inconsistent output quality.

**AI governance risks**

- Unapproved AI deployment.
- Unclear accountability.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Insufficient documentation.
- Uncontrolled model changes.
- Incomplete risk assessments.

**Privacy and data governance risks**

- Excessive collection or processing.
- Unauthorized secondary use.
- Inadequate retention controls.
- Unclear data provenance.
- Exposure of personal information.
- Inappropriate access to training or retrieval data.

**Human and business impact risks**

- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Incorrect consequential decisions.
- Overreliance on AI recommendations.
- Inadequate opportunities to challenge decisions.
- Unsafe automated actions.
- Operational disruption caused by AI failures.

A mature AI risk register should capture the actual risk scenario and its consequences rather than relying solely on generic labels such as "AI risk" or "model risk."

### 09. Defining the Scope of the AI Inventory

An organization should define what must be recorded in its AI inventory before attempting to establish completeness.

The scope should reflect the organization's AI governance policy, business operations, technology environment, legal obligations, and risk appetite.

Potential inventory categories include:

| Category                | Examples                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Internally developed AI | Custom classification or forecasting models                 |
| Third-party AI services | External generative AI APIs                                 |
| Enterprise applications | AI-enabled CRM, HR, or finance platforms                    |
| Generative AI tools     | Writing assistants and document summarization tools         |
| Retrieval-based systems | AI applications connected to internal document repositories |
| AI agents               | Systems that invoke APIs or perform business actions        |
| Embedded AI             | AI capabilities included within commercial software         |
| Experimental AI         | Pilots, prototypes, and proofs of concept                   |
| Supplier-operated AI    | AI used by outsourced service providers                     |
| Legacy AI               | Existing models still supporting business processes         |

The organization should also define the organizational boundaries covered by the inventory.

These may include:

- Corporate departments.
- Subsidiaries.
- Geographic regions.
- Cloud environments.
- On-premises infrastructure.
- Development and testing environments.
- Production systems.
- Managed service providers.
- Business processes delivered through external partners.

A scope that covers only formally approved production models may omit AI experiments, embedded capabilities, or employee use of external AI services.

Those omissions can create governance blind spots.

### 10. Defining What Qualifies as an AI Inventory Record

Organizations need consistent inclusion criteria so that different business units do not classify similar technologies differently.

The criteria may consider whether a system:

- Uses an AI model to generate predictions, recommendations, classifications, or content.
- Uses machine learning to influence a business process.
- Incorporates a third-party AI capability.
- Automates or materially influences decisions.
- Processes organizational information through an AI service.
- Executes actions based on model outputs.
- Uses AI in a customer-facing or employee-facing workflow.
- Is being evaluated for a future operational deployment.

The organization should distinguish between the AI system, the underlying model, the application, and the business use case.

For example, a single foundation model may support several applications, and each application may support multiple business processes.

```
AI Provider
    ↓
Foundation Model
    ↓
Enterprise AI Platform
    ├── HR Assistant
    ├── Finance Assistant
    └── Customer Service Agent
```

The inventory architecture should preserve these relationships without counting every component as an unrelated AI deployment.

A component may still require its own record when it has distinct ownership, risk, approval, or lifecycle requirements.

The objective is consistent governance coverage, not simply maximizing the number of inventory entries.

### 11. Shadow AI and Inventory Completeness

Shadow AI refers to AI use that occurs outside the organization's established approval, procurement, or governance processes.

It may arise when employees:

- Use public AI tools for work-related tasks.
- Upload internal documents to external AI services.
- Create unofficial AI-powered workflows.
- Connect AI applications to corporate data.
- Experiment with AI agents without formal approval.
- Enable AI capabilities within existing enterprise software without governance review.

Shadow AI creates an inventory challenge because some use may not be visible through conventional asset management or procurement records.

A discovery process may therefore combine several sources:

```
AI Discovery
     │
     ├── Procurement Records
     ├── Cloud Service Inventories
     ├── Identity and Access Logs
     ├── Network and Web Activity
     ├── Software Catalogues
     ├── Business Unit Declarations
     ├── Supplier Assessments
     └── Employee Reporting
              ↓
      Candidate AI Records
              ↓
       Validation and Triage
              ↓
       Authoritative Inventory
```

Discovery does not automatically mean that every detected tool is an approved deployment.

The organization should distinguish between detected, under-review, approved, restricted, and prohibited uses.

The inventory should record relevant unauthorized uses when needed to manage the exposure, while governance procedures determine whether those uses may continue.

### 12. AI Inventory Governance Objectives

A well-designed inventory should support several governance objectives.

**Visibility:** Establish what AI systems and use cases exist across the organization.

**Accountability:** Identify the business owner and relevant technical or operational owners.

**Risk-based oversight:** Identify systems that require enhanced assessments, approvals, or monitoring.

**Compliance:** Support identification of applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal policy obligations.

**Change management:** Track changes to models, data sources, integrations, permissions, and intended use.

**Lifecycle management:** Record AI systems from proposal and experimentation through production, suspension, and retirement.

**Auditability:** Maintain evidence that the organization identifies and governs AI within the defined scope.

**Portfolio management:** Provide management with a consolidated view of AI adoption, dependencies, and exposure.

These objectives should be reflected in the inventory data model, operating procedures, review schedule, and reporting arrangements.

An inventory that contains hundreds of records but has no ownership, validation, or update process may provide limited assurance.

---

### 13. AI Risk Register Governance Objectives

The risk register should support a controlled and repeatable approach to AI risk management.

Its objectives include:

- Establishing traceable risk identification.
- Supporting consistent assessment criteria.
- Separating inherent exposure from residual exposure.
- Linking risks to relevant controls.
- Assigning ownership and treatment responsibility.
- Recording management decisions.
- Tracking corrective actions to completion.
- Identifying risks that exceed approved tolerance.
- Escalating significant or systemic exposure.
- Supporting independent review and assurance.
- Preserving the history of material risk decisions.

A risk register should also make unresolved exposure visible.

For example, a high-risk AI agent may have an approved business purpose but still lack effective controls for limiting its actions.

The register should preserve the distinction between authorization to deploy a system and acceptance of its remaining risks.

A system's approval does not automatically establish that every associated risk has been adequately treated or formally accepted.

---

### 14. The Relationship Between AI Inventory, Risk Assessment, and Risk Register

The inventory, assessment process, and risk register are connected but distinct governance mechanisms.

The inventory identifies the AI system and its context.

The risk assessment examines specific scenarios and determines their significance.

The risk register records the resulting risk information and tracks management decisions.

```
AI Inventory Record
        ↓
Scope and Applicability Review
        ↓
Risk Identification
        ↓
Risk Analysis and Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Register Entry
        ↓
Control and Treatment Decisions
        ↓
Ongoing Monitoring and Review
```

The risk register may contain risks identified through multiple activities, including:

- Formal AI risk assessments.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy impact assessments.
- Model evaluations.
- AI red-team exercises.
- Internal audits.
- Incident investigations.
- Supplier assessments.
- Regulatory reviews.
- Business continuity assessments.

The organization should define how findings from these activities become risk register entries.

Not every observation needs to become a separate enterprise risk, but material risk scenarios should not disappear merely because they originated in a different governance process.

### 15. The Difference Between an AI System, a Use Case, and a Risk

These concepts must be distinguished to prevent inconsistent inventory design and risk reporting.

**AI system:** The deployed technical capability that uses AI to produce outputs, recommendations, predictions, or actions.

**AI use case:** The specific business purpose or process in which the AI system is used.

**AI risk:** A scenario involving uncertainty that could produce an undesirable consequence for the organization, individuals, or other affected stakeholders.

Example:

```
AI System:
Enterprise Language Model Platform
          ↓
AI Use Case:
Employee HR Policy Assistant
          ↓
Risk Scenario 1:
Incorrect interpretation of employment policy
          ↓
Risk Scenario 2:
Unauthorized disclosure of employee information
          ↓
Risk Scenario 3:
Employees rely on outdated AI-generated guidance
```

One technical platform can support multiple use cases. One use case can have multiple risk scenarios.

This distinction matters because risk can vary by use case even when the underlying model remains unchanged.

For example, using a language model to summarize public documentation creates a different exposure from using it to recommend employment decisions or process sensitive employee records.

The inventory should preserve the relationships among these objects so that the organization can assess risk at the appropriate level.

### 16. AI Inventory Identification and Unique Identifiers

Each inventory object should have a stable identifier that supports traceability across governance processes.

Example identifiers include:

```
AI System:
AI-SYS-001

AI Application:
AI-APP-014

AI Use Case:
AI-UC-027

AI Model:
AI-MDL-008

AI Risk:
AI-R-042

AI Control:
AI-CTRL-019

AI Finding:
AI-FND-011

AI Treatment Action:
AI-ACT-036
```

These identifiers are illustrative; the organization should define and standardize its own naming conventions.

A unique identifier helps prevent ambiguity when:

- A system name changes.
- A model is upgraded.
- A business owner changes.
- A use case is expanded.
- A risk is reassessed.
- A control is redesigned.
- An audit finding generates a corrective action.

The identifier should remain stable for the life of the record, while descriptive fields can change through controlled updates.

Identifiers should not be reused in a way that causes historical records to refer to a different system or risk.

### 17. Mandatory Information for an AI Inventory Record

The required fields should be defined by the organization's governance policy and inventory standard.

A foundational inventory record may include:

| Field                    | Purpose                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI ID                    | Unique record identifier                                      |
| System name              | Identifies the AI system or application                       |
| Business purpose         | Explains why the AI capability is used                        |
| Use case                 | Describes the specific business application                   |
| Business unit            | Identifies the organizational area                            |
| Business owner           | Establishes business accountability                           |
| Technical owner          | Establishes technical responsibility                          |
| AI provider              | Identifies the model or service provider                      |
| Model and version        | Identifies the relevant model where known                     |
| Deployment environment   | Records cloud, on-premises, or other hosting context          |
| Lifecycle status         | Identifies the current stage                                  |
| Data categories          | Describes the types of information processed                  |
| Data sensitivity         | Indicates the applicable classification                       |
| User population          | Identifies who can interact with the system                   |
| External exposure        | Records relevant access and connectivity                      |
| Integration dependencies | Identifies connected systems and services                     |
| Autonomy level           | Describes whether outputs require approval or trigger actions |

### 18. Mandatory Information for an AI Risk Register Entry

A risk register should contain enough information to support assessment, accountability, treatment, and monitoring.

A foundational risk record may include:

| Field                 | Purpose                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Risk ID               | Unique identifier for the risk scenario                      |
| AI ID or Use Case ID  | Links the risk to the relevant inventory object              |
| Risk title            | Provides a concise description                               |
| Risk statement        | Describes the scenario and potential consequence             |
| Risk category         | Classifies the risk                                          |
| Causes                | Records the conditions that could produce the event          |
| Risk event            | Identifies what could happen                                 |
| Consequences          | Describes potential impacts                                  |
| Inherent likelihood   | Records likelihood before relevant controls                  |
| Inherent impact       | Records consequence before relevant controls                 |
| Inherent risk rating  | Records the assessed inherent exposure                       |
| Existing controls     | Identifies relevant controls                                 |
| Control effectiveness | Records the assessed effectiveness of those controls         |
| Residual risk rating  | Records the remaining exposure                               |
| Risk owner            | Assigns accountability for the risk                          |
| Treatment decision    | Records how the risk will be managed                         |
| Treatment actions     | Identifies planned risk-reduction activities                 |
| Target dates          | Supports action tracking                                     |
| Acceptance status     | Records whether residual exposure has been formally accepted |
| Evidence              | Links supporting assessment and control evidence             |
| Review date           | Supports ongoing reassessment                                |
| Risk status           | Records the current management state                         |

The fields should be sufficiently specific to support a meaningful review.

For example, "AI security risk" is not an adequate substitute for a scenario describing unauthorized data retrieval caused by insufficient authorization enforcement.

A risk record should explain the exposure in terms that business owners, technical teams, GRC professionals, and auditors can understand.

### 19. Writing a Defensible AI Risk Statement

A risk statement should explain the relevant cause, event, and consequence.

A useful structure is:

```
Because of [cause or condition],
[AI-related risk event] may occur,
resulting in [potential consequence].
```

Example:

> Because the AI agent has access to customer records without sufficiently granular authorization enforcement, it may retrieve or modify information outside the intended user's permissions, resulting in unauthorized disclosure, data integrity issues, and potential customer or regulatory impact.

This statement identifies:

- The underlying condition.
- The AI-related event.
- The potential consequence.

Compare it with a weak entry:

> AI may cause a data breach.

The weak statement does not explain the relevant mechanism, affected capability, or likely impact.

A defensible risk statement should be specific enough to support analysis and treatment without assuming that the event has already occurred.

Where the causes or consequences are materially different, the organization may need separate risk scenarios rather than combining them into one overly broad record.

### 20. AI Risk Categories and Taxonomy

A risk taxonomy provides a consistent classification structure for AI risks across the organization.

Without a defined taxonomy, different teams may classify the same type of exposure differently, making aggregation and reporting difficult.

A practical taxonomy may include:

| Risk Category                   | Example                                              |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| AI security                     | Prompt injection leading to unauthorized tool use    |
| Data governance                 | Use of unapproved or poorly classified data          |
| Privacy                         | Disclosure of personal information                   |
| Model performance               | Inaccurate predictions or unreliable outputs         |
| Model integrity                 | Unauthorized modification of a model artifact        |
| AI safety                       | Unsafe AI-generated recommendations or actions       |
| Human oversight                 | Excessive reliance on AI-generated decisions         |
| Operational resilience          | AI service failure disrupting a critical process     |
| Third-party risk                | Dependency on an external model provider             |
| Legal and regulatory            | Failure to satisfy applicable AI obligations         |
| Ethics and fairness             | Discriminatory or inequitable outcomes               |
| Transparency and explainability | Inability to explain material AI-supported decisions |
| Intellectual property           | Unauthorized use or disclosure of protected material |
| Governance and accountability   | Unclear ownership or unapproved deployment           |

The taxonomy should support both detailed analysis and management-level reporting.

A risk may legitimately relate to multiple categories. For example, an AI-generated disclosure of personal information may involve AI security, privacy, regulatory, and reputational exposure.

The organization should define whether each record receives one primary category and optional secondary categories, or follows another consistent classification method.

### 21. Inherent Risk, Control Effectiveness, and Residual Risk

AI risk registers should distinguish the exposure associated with a scenario from the effect of controls intended to manage it.

**Inherent risk** describes the risk before considering the relevant controls, according to the organization's defined methodology.

**Control effectiveness** describes how appropriately the relevant controls are designed, implemented, and operating in practice.

**Residual risk** describes the risk remaining after the relevant controls are considered.

A simplified relationship is:

```
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Control Effectiveness Assessment
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment or Acceptance Decision
```

Example:

| Assessment Element    | Example                                                 |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scenario              | Unauthorized retrieval of confidential customer records |
| Inherent likelihood   | Likely                                                  |
| Inherent impact       | Major                                                   |
| Inherent risk         | High                                                    |
| Existing control      | Identity-based authorization                            |
| Control effectiveness | Partially effective                                     |
| Residual likelihood   | Possible                                                |
| Residual impact       | Major                                                   |
| Residual risk         | High                                                    |
| Required decision     | Additional treatment and escalation                     |

The example illustrates an important principle: implementing a control does not automatically mean that risk has been reduced to an acceptable level.

Controls must be relevant to the scenario and supported by evidence of their effectiveness.

The exact scoring method and relationship between inherent and residual risk should follow the organization's approved risk methodology. Organizations should not assume that residual risk can always be calculated by mechanically subtracting a control score from an inherent risk score.

### 22. Risk Assessment Methodology and Rating Criteria

An organization should establish consistent criteria for assessing AI risk.

A qualitative methodology may use likelihood and impact scales, supported by defined descriptions.

Example likelihood scale:

| Level              | Description                                                           |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Rare           | The scenario is highly unlikely within the defined assessment context |
| 2 — Unlikely       | The scenario could occur but is not expected                          |
| 3 — Possible       | The scenario could reasonably occur                                   |
| 4 — Likely         | The scenario is expected to occur under relevant conditions           |
| 5 — Almost Certain | The scenario is expected to occur frequently or repeatedly            |

Example impact scale:

| Level        | Description                                                                                               |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Minor    | Limited, readily manageable consequences                                                                  |
| 2 — Moderate | Noticeable consequences requiring routine remediation                                                     |
| 3 — Major    | Material business, security, privacy, or stakeholder impact                                               |
| 4 — Severe   | Significant disruption, harm, or regulatory exposure                                                      |
| 5 — Critical | Extremely serious consequences affecting critical operations, rights, safety, or organizational viability |

These scales are illustrative and must be tailored to the organization's context.

Impact criteria should consider more than direct financial loss. Depending on the use case, the assessment may need to address human harm, privacy, operational disruption, legal obligations, discrimination, and consequences for affected individuals.

The methodology should also specify:

- How ratings are assigned.
- How multiple impact dimensions are considered.
- How uncertainty is recorded.
- How control effectiveness is evaluated.
- How risk ratings are combined.
- What thresholds require escalation.
- Which risks require formal acceptance.
- How reassessment is triggered.

Consistency is essential for meaningful portfolio reporting.

### 23. Risk Ownership and Accountability

Every material AI risk should have an identifiable accountable owner.

Risk ownership should not be confused with the responsibility for implementing a particular technical control.

For example:

| Role                      | Typical Responsibility                                            |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business owner            | Accountable for the business purpose and consequences of AI use   |
| Risk owner                | Accountable for monitoring and managing the assigned risk         |
| System owner              | Responsible for the system's operational lifecycle                |
| Technical owner           | Responsible for relevant technical implementation and maintenance |
| Control owner             | Responsible for operating or maintaining a specific control       |
| AI governance function    | Defines governance requirements and coordinates oversight         |
| GRC function              | Supports risk methodology, reporting, and challenge               |
| Legal or privacy function | Advises on relevant legal, regulatory, and privacy obligations    |
| Internal audit            | Independently evaluates governance and control effectiveness      |

The exact allocation depends on the organization's operating model.

A single person may hold multiple roles in a smaller organization, but accountability should remain explicit.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority, information, and organizational support to make decisions or escalate issues.

Assigning a risk to an individual who cannot influence the relevant business process or secure the necessary remediation is unlikely to produce effective risk management.

### 24. AI Inventory Lifecycle Status

The inventory should represent the lifecycle state of each AI system or use case.

A possible lifecycle model is:

```
Proposed
   ↓
Under Assessment
   ↓
Approved for Development
   ↓
In Development
   ↓
Testing and Validation
   ↓
Approved for Deployment
   ↓
Production
   ↓
Suspended / Restricted
   ↓
Retired
```

Not every organization will require every status, and the sequence may vary by deployment model.

The inventory should distinguish between technical existence and governance authorization.

For example, an AI tool may be detected in use before it has received formal approval. Its record should reflect both its actual state and its governance status rather than incorrectly classifying it as approved.

Lifecycle status should be updated when material changes occur.

Relevant events include:

- Approval or rejection of a proposed use case.
- Completion of testing.
- Production deployment.
- A material model or architecture change.
- Suspension following an incident.
- Restriction of a business use case.
- Replacement by another system.
- Retirement and decommissioning.

Lifecycle accuracy supports reliable reporting and helps prevent unapproved or retired systems from being treated as active, approved deployments.

### 25. AI Risk Register Lifecycle Status

Risk records also require defined lifecycle statuses.

A possible workflow is:

```
Identified
    ↓
Under Assessment
    ↓
Assessed
    ↓
Treatment Required
    ↓
Treatment in Progress
    ↓
Awaiting Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Reviewed
    ↓
Accepted / Monitored / Further Treatment
```

A separate closure process may be used when a risk scenario is no longer relevant, has been eliminated, or has been superseded under the organization's methodology.

The organization should define what each status means and what evidence is required to move between statuses.

For example, a risk should not be marked as treated merely because a corrective action has been reported as complete.

Where validation is required, the organization should obtain evidence that the relevant control or remediation is implemented and effective.

Similarly, an accepted risk should remain subject to any monitoring, review, and expiration conditions required by the organization's risk acceptance policy.

A clear status model helps management distinguish between risks that have been identified, risks being treated, risks awaiting validation, and risks that remain open.

### 26. Inventory Completeness and Data Quality

An AI inventory is only useful when its coverage and records are sufficiently complete and reliable.

Inventory quality should be evaluated across several dimensions.

**Completeness:** Are all in-scope AI systems and use cases recorded?

**Accuracy:** Do the records reflect the actual deployed environment?

**Consistency:** Are fields and classifications applied uniformly?

**Timeliness:** Are changes recorded within the required period?

**Uniqueness:** Are duplicate records identified and resolved?

**Traceability:** Can records be linked to authoritative sources and supporting evidence?

**Ownership:** Does each relevant record have an accountable owner?

**Validity:** Do field values comply with the organization's data standards?

A simple completeness measure might be:

```
Inventory Completeness
=
Confirmed In-Scope AI Systems Recorded
÷
Total Confirmed In-Scope AI Systems
× 100
```

For example, if an organization identifies 120 in-scope AI systems and confirms that 108 have valid inventory records, the measured completeness is 90%.

This metric is meaningful only if the denominator is credible. An organization cannot reliably claim 100% completeness merely because every record in its existing inventory has been filled in.

The organization must also have reasonable evidence that the inventory population includes the AI systems that actually exist.

---

### 27. AI Risk Register Data Quality

The risk register should be subject to its own quality controls.

Potential quality checks include:

- Every material risk has a unique identifier.
- Every risk is linked to a relevant AI system or use case where applicable.
- Risk statements identify a meaningful scenario.
- Required assessment fields are completed.
- Ratings follow the approved methodology.
- The risk owner is identified.
- Existing controls are linked or described.
- Treatment actions have accountable owners and target dates.
- Risk acceptance records contain the required approvals.
- Review dates are maintained.
- Evidence links remain accessible.
- Closed or superseded records retain appropriate history.

A register can contain many entries and still be unreliable if risks are duplicated, poorly described, incorrectly rated, or left without accountable owners.

For example:

```
Weak Record
-----------
Risk: AI compliance risk
Rating: High
Owner: GRC
Action: Improve controls

Problems:
- No specific scenario
- No identified consequences
- No clear accountable risk owner
- No defined control gap
- No measurable treatment outcome
```

A stronger record describes the scenario, assessment rationale, affected system, relevant controls, accountable owner, and specific treatment objective.

The goal is not simply to populate fields. It is to create records that support consistent decisions and effective oversight.

### 28. Inventory and Risk Register Review Triggers

Periodic reviews are necessary, but calendar-based reviews alone may not be sufficient for dynamic AI environments.

An inventory or risk record may require reassessment when a material change occurs.

Potential triggers include:

- Introduction of a new AI model.
- Change in model version or provider.
- Change in intended purpose.
- Expansion to a new business process.
- Connection to a new data source.
- Access to more sensitive information.
- Addition of tool-use or autonomous capabilities.
- Changes to permissions or external interfaces.
- Significant model performance degradation.
- A security or privacy incident.
- A material audit finding.
- A change in applicable legal requirements.
- A new supplier or critical dependency.
- A significant change in user population or deployment scale.
- A change in control effectiveness.
- Expiration of a risk acceptance decision.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
Material Change or Trigger
          ↓
Determine Affected Records
          ↓
Update Inventory
          ↓
Reassess Relevant Risks
          ↓
Review Controls and Approvals
          ↓
Record Decisions and Evidence
```

The organization should define which changes require a full reassessment and which require a targeted review.

The objective is to prevent previously valid inventory and risk records from becoming misleading as the AI environment changes.

### 29. Governance Reporting and Key Metrics

Inventory and risk register data can support operational, management, and board-level reporting.

Useful inventory metrics may include:

- Total registered AI systems and use cases.
- AI systems by business unit.
- AI systems by lifecycle status.
- AI systems by risk classification.
- AI systems using third-party models.
- AI systems processing sensitive information.
- AI systems with autonomous action capabilities.
- Percentage of records with assigned business owners.
- Percentage of records reviewed within the required period.
- Identified but unregistered AI uses.

Useful risk register metrics may include:

- Total open AI risks.
- Risks by severity and category.
- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks exceeding approved tolerance.
- Risks without assigned owners.
- Overdue treatment actions.
- Risks awaiting control validation.
- Risk acceptances approaching expiration.
- Repeated risk themes across systems.
- Risks associated with material incidents or audit findings.

Metrics should be defined carefully.

For example, a reduction in the number of registered AI risks does not necessarily mean that the organization has become safer. The reduction may result from genuine risk treatment, incomplete identification, changed classification practices, or inappropriate closure of records.

Metrics should therefore be interpreted alongside evidence, trends, and governance context.

### 30. Part 1 Core Principle

The central principle of AI inventory and AI risk register governance is:

> **An organization must maintain a sufficiently complete and reliable record of its AI systems and use cases, link that inventory to meaningful risk scenarios, and establish accountable processes for assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting AI risk.**

The inventory establishes the population and context of AI use.

The risk register establishes the documented exposure and the management decisions associated with that exposure.

The relationship is:

```
Discover AI
    ↓
Register AI
    ↓
Establish Ownership and Context
    ↓
Identify Risk Scenarios
    ↓
Assess Risk
    ↓
Document Controls and Treatment
    ↓
Monitor and Reassess
    ↓
Report and Provide Assurance
```

A mature governance program does not treat these records as static spreadsheets. It maintains them as controlled governance assets that evolve with the organization's AI systems, business processes, risks, controls, and obligations.

Their value ultimately depends on completeness, data quality, traceability, clear accountability, and their practical use in organizational decision-making.
