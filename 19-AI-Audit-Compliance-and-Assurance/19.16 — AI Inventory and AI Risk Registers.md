# 19.16 — AI Inventory and AI Risk Registers

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Inventory and AI Risk Register Governance

### 01. Introduction to AI Inventory and AI Risk Registers

AI inventory and AI risk registers are foundational components of an organization's AI governance and assurance program.

An **AI inventory** identifies and maintains an authoritative record of the AI systems, models, applications, services, and use cases operating within or on behalf of the organization.

An **AI risk register** records the risks associated with those AI systems and use cases, including their causes, potential consequences, assessments, existing controls, treatment plans, and accountable owners.

The relationship can be represented as:

```
AI Systems and Use Cases
          ↓
     AI Inventory
          ↓
   AI Risk Identification
          ↓
    AI Risk Register
          ↓
 Risk Treatment and Monitoring
          ↓
 Governance, Audit, and Assurance
```

The inventory establishes **what AI exists, where it is used, and who is accountable for it**.

The risk register establishes **what could go wrong, how significant the exposure is, and what the organization is doing about it**.

Together, they provide a structured foundation for AI risk management, regulatory compliance, internal audit, management oversight, and enterprise-level risk reporting.

---

### 02. Definition of an AI Inventory

An AI inventory is a controlled record of AI systems and related AI use cases that fall within an organization's defined governance scope.

Depending on the organization's architecture and operating model, the inventory may include:

- Internally developed AI models.
- Third-party AI applications.
- Commercial AI services.
- Generative AI assistants.
- Large language model applications.
- Retrieval-augmented generation systems.
- AI agents capable of using tools or executing actions.
- Predictive analytics and machine learning models.
- AI-enabled business applications.
- AI capabilities embedded in enterprise software.
- AI systems operated by suppliers on the organization's behalf.
- Experimental AI systems and proof-of-concept projects.

An AI inventory is not simply a list of software products.

It should provide enough information to understand the AI capability, its intended purpose, its operational context, its dependencies, its data, and its governance responsibilities.

For example:

```
AI Inventory Record

AI ID:
AI-APP-001

System:
Enterprise Customer Service Assistant

AI Capability:
Generative AI / Large Language Model

Business Purpose:
Customer inquiry support

Deployment:
Cloud-hosted

Data:
Customer records and product documentation

Business Owner:
Customer Service Director

Technical Owner:
AI Platform Manager

Risk Classification:
High

Lifecycle Status:
Production
```

The record gives governance stakeholders a starting point for determining which controls, assessments, approvals, and monitoring activities are required.

---

### 03. Definition of an AI Risk Register

An AI risk register is a structured record of identified AI-related risks and the information required to assess, manage, monitor, and report those risks.

It captures the relationship between a risk scenario, its assessment, the controls intended to manage it, and the decisions made by accountable stakeholders.

A risk register may contain:

- Risk identifier.
- Associated AI system or use case.
- Risk category.
- Risk statement.
- Causes and contributing conditions.
- Risk event.
- Potential consequences.
- Inherent risk assessment.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness.
- Residual risk assessment.
- Risk owner.
- Treatment decision.
- Corrective actions.
- Target completion dates.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Review dates.
- Evidence and supporting documentation.
- Current risk status.

A simplified record may look like this:

| Field            | Example                                                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID          | AI-R-001                                                 |
| AI System ID     | AI-APP-001                                               |
| Risk Scenario    | Unauthorized disclosure of customer information          |
| Primary Cause    | Inadequate access enforcement across retrieved documents |
| Likelihood       | Possible                                                 |
| Impact           | Major                                                    |
| Inherent Risk    | High                                                     |
| Existing Control | Identity-based access restrictions                       |
| Residual Risk    | Medium                                                   |
| Risk Owner       | Customer Service Director                                |
| Treatment        | Strengthen retrieval authorization and validation        |
| Status           | Treatment in progress                                    |

The risk register converts risk analysis into an accountable governance process.

A risk that is merely discussed in a meeting but not recorded, assigned, assessed, and tracked may be difficult to manage consistently or demonstrate during an audit.

### 04. AI Inventory vs. AI Risk Register

The inventory and risk register serve different but complementary purposes.

| Dimension        | AI Inventory                                                | AI Risk Register                                     |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary question | What AI exists?                                             | What could go wrong?                                 |
| Main purpose     | Establish AI visibility                                     | Manage AI exposure                                   |
| Main record      | AI system or use case                                       | Individual risk scenario                             |
| Key information  | Purpose, owner, model, data, dependencies                   | Causes, consequences, ratings, controls              |
| Accountability   | Business and technical ownership                            | Risk ownership and treatment                         |
| Lifecycle focus  | Discovery through retirement                                | Identification through closure or ongoing monitoring |
| Main output      | Authoritative AI landscape                                  | Documented AI risk exposure                          |
| Audit value      | Demonstrates inventory completeness and governance coverage | Demonstrates risk assessment and treatment           |
| Management value | Shows the scope of AI adoption                              | Shows the significance of AI-related risks           |

The inventory and register should not be treated as interchangeable.

An inventory entry does not automatically constitute a complete risk assessment. Similarly, a risk register cannot reliably demonstrate the organization's total AI exposure if the organization does not know which AI systems exist.

The intended relationship is:

```
AI Inventory
     │
     ├── AI System A
     │      ├── Risk A1
     │      ├── Risk A2
     │      └── Risk A3
     │
     ├── AI System B
     │      ├── Risk B1
     │      └── Risk B2
     │
     └── AI System C
            └── Risk C1
```

One AI system can have multiple risks, and the same risk theme can affect multiple systems.

---

### 05. Purpose of the AI Inventory

The primary purpose of the AI inventory is to establish organizational visibility and accountability over AI use.

Without a reliable inventory, management may be unable to determine:

- Which business units use AI.
- Which systems process confidential or personal information.
- Which AI applications are externally accessible.
- Which systems can make or influence consequential decisions.
- Which models or providers support critical business processes.
- Which AI systems require formal risk assessments.
- Which systems are subject to specific legal or regulatory requirements.
- Which systems have completed required approvals.
- Which systems require ongoing monitoring.
- Which AI deployments should be reviewed, restricted, or retired.

The inventory also supports planning and resource allocation.

For example, an organization with 200 registered AI use cases may need to prioritize governance reviews for systems that process sensitive personal data, execute autonomous actions, support critical business operations, or create significant potential consequences for individuals.

The inventory provides the population from which those priorities can be determined.

---

### 06. Purpose of the AI Risk Register

The AI risk register provides a controlled mechanism for understanding and managing AI-related uncertainty.

Its main purposes are to:

- Record identified AI risk scenarios.
- Establish consistent risk assessments.
- Identify accountable risk owners.
- Document existing controls and their effectiveness.
- Determine whether residual risks fall within approved risk tolerance.
- Track risk treatment decisions.
- Monitor unresolved risks and overdue actions.
- Record formal risk acceptance.
- Escalate material exposure to management.
- Support internal audit and independent assurance.
- Identify common risk themes across AI systems.
- Integrate AI risks into enterprise risk management.

The register should support decisions, not merely collect risk descriptions.

For example:

```
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Analyzed
      ↓
Risk Evaluated Against Criteria
      ↓
Treatment Decision
      ↓
Controls or Corrective Actions
      ↓
Effectiveness Validation
      ↓
Residual Risk Review
      ↓
Accept, Escalate, or Continue Treatment
```

The process should be proportionate to the significance of the risk and the organization's approved methodology.

### 07. Why AI Inventory Is Different From a Traditional IT Asset Inventory

A traditional IT asset inventory typically emphasizes hardware, software, infrastructure, configuration, ownership, and operational status.

An AI inventory may require those details, but it also needs information about the AI capability and its intended use.

Relevant AI-specific attributes can include:

- AI purpose and intended use.
- Model type and version.
- AI provider.
- Training, fine-tuning, or inference arrangements.
- Data sources and data provenance.
- Retrieval and knowledge sources.
- Human oversight arrangements.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Tools and external actions available to the AI.
- Output validation mechanisms.
- Model performance and known limitations.
- AI-specific risk classifications.
- Evaluation and testing status.
- Model and system change history.
- Applicable governance and compliance requirements.

For example, two applications may use the same underlying language model but have very different risk profiles.

```
Shared Foundation Model
          │
     ┌────┴────┐
     ↓         ↓
AI Assistant  AI Agent
     │         │
Drafts Text   Modifies CRM Records
     │         │
Human Review  Automated Execution
     │         │
Lower Action  Higher Action
Exposure      Exposure
```

The difference arises from the surrounding architecture, permissions, business purpose, data access, and level of autonomy.

Therefore, the inventory should describe the deployed AI capability in context rather than recording only the model's name.

### 08. Why AI Risk Registers Require AI-Specific Risk Information

Traditional IT risk registers often address cybersecurity, availability, confidentiality, integrity, operational resilience, and third-party risk.

AI risk management must consider these areas while also addressing risks associated with AI behavior, development, deployment, and use.

Examples include:

**AI security risks**

- Prompt injection.
- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Model or data poisoning.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Compromised model artifacts.
- Excessive agent permissions.

**AI performance and reliability risks**

- Hallucinations.
- Incorrect predictions.
- Inadequate model calibration.
- Model drift.
- Data drift.
- Inconsistent output quality.

**AI governance risks**

- Unapproved AI deployment.
- Unclear accountability.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Insufficient documentation.
- Uncontrolled model changes.
- Incomplete risk assessments.

**Privacy and data governance risks**

- Excessive collection or processing.
- Unauthorized secondary use.
- Inadequate retention controls.
- Unclear data provenance.
- Exposure of personal information.
- Inappropriate access to training or retrieval data.

**Human and business impact risks**

- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Incorrect consequential decisions.
- Overreliance on AI recommendations.
- Inadequate opportunities to challenge decisions.
- Unsafe automated actions.
- Operational disruption caused by AI failures.

A mature AI risk register should capture the actual risk scenario and its consequences rather than relying solely on generic labels such as "AI risk" or "model risk."

### 09. Defining the Scope of the AI Inventory

An organization should define what must be recorded in its AI inventory before attempting to establish completeness.

The scope should reflect the organization's AI governance policy, business operations, technology environment, legal obligations, and risk appetite.

Potential inventory categories include:

| Category                | Examples                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Internally developed AI | Custom classification or forecasting models                 |
| Third-party AI services | External generative AI APIs                                 |
| Enterprise applications | AI-enabled CRM, HR, or finance platforms                    |
| Generative AI tools     | Writing assistants and document summarization tools         |
| Retrieval-based systems | AI applications connected to internal document repositories |
| AI agents               | Systems that invoke APIs or perform business actions        |
| Embedded AI             | AI capabilities included within commercial software         |
| Experimental AI         | Pilots, prototypes, and proofs of concept                   |
| Supplier-operated AI    | AI used by outsourced service providers                     |
| Legacy AI               | Existing models still supporting business processes         |

The organization should also define the organizational boundaries covered by the inventory.

These may include:

- Corporate departments.
- Subsidiaries.
- Geographic regions.
- Cloud environments.
- On-premises infrastructure.
- Development and testing environments.
- Production systems.
- Managed service providers.
- Business processes delivered through external partners.

A scope that covers only formally approved production models may omit AI experiments, embedded capabilities, or employee use of external AI services.

Those omissions can create governance blind spots.

### 10. Defining What Qualifies as an AI Inventory Record

Organizations need consistent inclusion criteria so that different business units do not classify similar technologies differently.

The criteria may consider whether a system:

- Uses an AI model to generate predictions, recommendations, classifications, or content.
- Uses machine learning to influence a business process.
- Incorporates a third-party AI capability.
- Automates or materially influences decisions.
- Processes organizational information through an AI service.
- Executes actions based on model outputs.
- Uses AI in a customer-facing or employee-facing workflow.
- Is being evaluated for a future operational deployment.

The organization should distinguish between the AI system, the underlying model, the application, and the business use case.

For example, a single foundation model may support several applications, and each application may support multiple business processes.

```
AI Provider
    ↓
Foundation Model
    ↓
Enterprise AI Platform
    ├── HR Assistant
    ├── Finance Assistant
    └── Customer Service Agent
```

The inventory architecture should preserve these relationships without counting every component as an unrelated AI deployment.

A component may still require its own record when it has distinct ownership, risk, approval, or lifecycle requirements.

The objective is consistent governance coverage, not simply maximizing the number of inventory entries.

### 11. Shadow AI and Inventory Completeness

Shadow AI refers to AI use that occurs outside the organization's established approval, procurement, or governance processes.

It may arise when employees:

- Use public AI tools for work-related tasks.
- Upload internal documents to external AI services.
- Create unofficial AI-powered workflows.
- Connect AI applications to corporate data.
- Experiment with AI agents without formal approval.
- Enable AI capabilities within existing enterprise software without governance review.

Shadow AI creates an inventory challenge because some use may not be visible through conventional asset management or procurement records.

A discovery process may therefore combine several sources:

```
AI Discovery
     │
     ├── Procurement Records
     ├── Cloud Service Inventories
     ├── Identity and Access Logs
     ├── Network and Web Activity
     ├── Software Catalogues
     ├── Business Unit Declarations
     ├── Supplier Assessments
     └── Employee Reporting
              ↓
      Candidate AI Records
              ↓
       Validation and Triage
              ↓
       Authoritative Inventory
```

Discovery does not automatically mean that every detected tool is an approved deployment.

The organization should distinguish between detected, under-review, approved, restricted, and prohibited uses.

The inventory should record relevant unauthorized uses when needed to manage the exposure, while governance procedures determine whether those uses may continue.

### 12. AI Inventory Governance Objectives

A well-designed inventory should support several governance objectives.

**Visibility:** Establish what AI systems and use cases exist across the organization.

**Accountability:** Identify the business owner and relevant technical or operational owners.

**Risk-based oversight:** Identify systems that require enhanced assessments, approvals, or monitoring.

**Compliance:** Support identification of applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal policy obligations.

**Change management:** Track changes to models, data sources, integrations, permissions, and intended use.

**Lifecycle management:** Record AI systems from proposal and experimentation through production, suspension, and retirement.

**Auditability:** Maintain evidence that the organization identifies and governs AI within the defined scope.

**Portfolio management:** Provide management with a consolidated view of AI adoption, dependencies, and exposure.

These objectives should be reflected in the inventory data model, operating procedures, review schedule, and reporting arrangements.

An inventory that contains hundreds of records but has no ownership, validation, or update process may provide limited assurance.

---

### 13. AI Risk Register Governance Objectives

The risk register should support a controlled and repeatable approach to AI risk management.

Its objectives include:

- Establishing traceable risk identification.
- Supporting consistent assessment criteria.
- Separating inherent exposure from residual exposure.
- Linking risks to relevant controls.
- Assigning ownership and treatment responsibility.
- Recording management decisions.
- Tracking corrective actions to completion.
- Identifying risks that exceed approved tolerance.
- Escalating significant or systemic exposure.
- Supporting independent review and assurance.
- Preserving the history of material risk decisions.

A risk register should also make unresolved exposure visible.

For example, a high-risk AI agent may have an approved business purpose but still lack effective controls for limiting its actions.

The register should preserve the distinction between authorization to deploy a system and acceptance of its remaining risks.

A system's approval does not automatically establish that every associated risk has been adequately treated or formally accepted.

---

### 14. The Relationship Between AI Inventory, Risk Assessment, and Risk Register

The inventory, assessment process, and risk register are connected but distinct governance mechanisms.

The inventory identifies the AI system and its context.

The risk assessment examines specific scenarios and determines their significance.

The risk register records the resulting risk information and tracks management decisions.

```
AI Inventory Record
        ↓
Scope and Applicability Review
        ↓
Risk Identification
        ↓
Risk Analysis and Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Register Entry
        ↓
Control and Treatment Decisions
        ↓
Ongoing Monitoring and Review
```

The risk register may contain risks identified through multiple activities, including:

- Formal AI risk assessments.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy impact assessments.
- Model evaluations.
- AI red-team exercises.
- Internal audits.
- Incident investigations.
- Supplier assessments.
- Regulatory reviews.
- Business continuity assessments.

The organization should define how findings from these activities become risk register entries.

Not every observation needs to become a separate enterprise risk, but material risk scenarios should not disappear merely because they originated in a different governance process.

### 15. The Difference Between an AI System, a Use Case, and a Risk

These concepts must be distinguished to prevent inconsistent inventory design and risk reporting.

**AI system:** The deployed technical capability that uses AI to produce outputs, recommendations, predictions, or actions.

**AI use case:** The specific business purpose or process in which the AI system is used.

**AI risk:** A scenario involving uncertainty that could produce an undesirable consequence for the organization, individuals, or other affected stakeholders.

Example:

```
AI System:
Enterprise Language Model Platform
          ↓
AI Use Case:
Employee HR Policy Assistant
          ↓
Risk Scenario 1:
Incorrect interpretation of employment policy
          ↓
Risk Scenario 2:
Unauthorized disclosure of employee information
          ↓
Risk Scenario 3:
Employees rely on outdated AI-generated guidance
```

One technical platform can support multiple use cases. One use case can have multiple risk scenarios.

This distinction matters because risk can vary by use case even when the underlying model remains unchanged.

For example, using a language model to summarize public documentation creates a different exposure from using it to recommend employment decisions or process sensitive employee records.

The inventory should preserve the relationships among these objects so that the organization can assess risk at the appropriate level.

### 16. AI Inventory Identification and Unique Identifiers

Each inventory object should have a stable identifier that supports traceability across governance processes.

Example identifiers include:

```
AI System:
AI-SYS-001

AI Application:
AI-APP-014

AI Use Case:
AI-UC-027

AI Model:
AI-MDL-008

AI Risk:
AI-R-042

AI Control:
AI-CTRL-019

AI Finding:
AI-FND-011

AI Treatment Action:
AI-ACT-036
```

These identifiers are illustrative; the organization should define and standardize its own naming conventions.

A unique identifier helps prevent ambiguity when:

- A system name changes.
- A model is upgraded.
- A business owner changes.
- A use case is expanded.
- A risk is reassessed.
- A control is redesigned.
- An audit finding generates a corrective action.

The identifier should remain stable for the life of the record, while descriptive fields can change through controlled updates.

Identifiers should not be reused in a way that causes historical records to refer to a different system or risk.

### 17. Mandatory Information for an AI Inventory Record

The required fields should be defined by the organization's governance policy and inventory standard.

A foundational inventory record may include:

| Field                    | Purpose                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI ID                    | Unique record identifier                                      |
| System name              | Identifies the AI system or application                       |
| Business purpose         | Explains why the AI capability is used                        |
| Use case                 | Describes the specific business application                   |
| Business unit            | Identifies the organizational area                            |
| Business owner           | Establishes business accountability                           |
| Technical owner          | Establishes technical responsibility                          |
| AI provider              | Identifies the model or service provider                      |
| Model and version        | Identifies the relevant model where known                     |
| Deployment environment   | Records cloud, on-premises, or other hosting context          |
| Lifecycle status         | Identifies the current stage                                  |
| Data categories          | Describes the types of information processed                  |
| Data sensitivity         | Indicates the applicable classification                       |
| User population          | Identifies who can interact with the system                   |
| External exposure        | Records relevant access and connectivity                      |
| Integration dependencies | Identifies connected systems and services                     |

### 18. Mandatory Information for an AI Risk Register Entry

A risk register should contain enough information to support assessment, accountability, treatment, and monitoring.

A foundational risk record may include:

| Field                 | Purpose                                                      |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Risk ID               | Unique identifier for the risk scenario                      |
| AI ID or Use Case ID  | Links the risk to the relevant inventory object              |
| Risk title            | Provides a concise description                               |
| Risk statement        | Describes the scenario and potential consequence             |
| Risk category         | Classifies the risk                                          |
| Causes                | Records the conditions that could produce the event          |
| Risk event            | Identifies what could happen                                 |
| Consequences          | Describes potential impacts                                  |
| Inherent likelihood   | Records likelihood before relevant controls                  |
| Inherent impact       | Records consequence before relevant controls                 |
| Inherent risk rating  | Records the assessed inherent exposure                       |
| Existing controls     | Identifies relevant controls                                 |
| Control effectiveness | Records the assessed effectiveness of those controls         |
| Residual risk rating  | Records the remaining exposure                               |
| Risk owner            | Assigns accountability for the risk                          |
| Treatment decision    | Records how the risk will be managed                         |
| Treatment actions     | Identifies planned risk-reduction activities                 |
| Target dates          | Supports action tracking                                     |
| Acceptance status     | Records whether residual exposure has been formally accepted |
| Evidence              | Links supporting assessment and control evidence             |
| Review date           | Supports ongoing reassessment                                |
| Risk status           | Records the current management state                         |

The fields should be sufficiently specific to support a meaningful review.

For example, "AI security risk" is not an adequate substitute for a scenario describing unauthorized data retrieval caused by insufficient authorization enforcement.

A risk record should explain the exposure in terms that business owners, technical teams, GRC professionals, and auditors can understand.

### 19. Writing a Defensible AI Risk Statement

A risk statement should explain the relevant cause, event, and consequence.

A useful structure is:

```
Because of [cause or condition],
[AI-related risk event] may occur,
resulting in [potential consequence].
```

Example:

> Because the AI agent has access to customer records without sufficiently granular authorization enforcement, it may retrieve or modify information outside the intended user's permissions, resulting in unauthorized disclosure, data integrity issues, and potential customer or regulatory impact.

This statement identifies:

- The underlying condition.
- The AI-related event.
- The potential consequence.

Compare it with a weak entry:

> AI may cause a data breach.

The weak statement does not explain the relevant mechanism, affected capability, or likely impact.

A defensible risk statement should be specific enough to support analysis and treatment without assuming that the event has already occurred.

Where the causes or consequences are materially different, the organization may need separate risk scenarios rather than combining them into one overly broad record.

### 20. AI Risk Categories and Taxonomy

A risk taxonomy provides a consistent classification structure for AI risks across the organization.

Without a defined taxonomy, different teams may classify the same type of exposure differently, making aggregation and reporting difficult.

A practical taxonomy may include:

| Risk Category                   | Example                                              |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| AI security                     | Prompt injection leading to unauthorized tool use    |
| Data governance                 | Use of unapproved or poorly classified data          |
| Privacy                         | Disclosure of personal information                   |
| Model performance               | Inaccurate predictions or unreliable outputs         |
| Model integrity                 | Unauthorized modification of a model artifact        |
| AI safety                       | Unsafe AI-generated recommendations or actions       |
| Human oversight                 | Excessive reliance on AI-generated decisions         |
| Operational resilience          | AI service failure disrupting a critical process     |
| Third-party risk                | Dependency on an external model provider             |
| Legal and regulatory            | Failure to satisfy applicable AI obligations         |
| Ethics and fairness             | Discriminatory or inequitable outcomes               |
| Transparency and explainability | Inability to explain material AI-supported decisions |
| Intellectual property           | Unauthorized use or disclosure of protected material |
| Governance and accountability   | Unclear ownership or unapproved deployment           |

The taxonomy should support both detailed analysis and management-level reporting.

A risk may legitimately relate to multiple categories. For example, an AI-generated disclosure of personal information may involve AI security, privacy, regulatory, and reputational exposure.

The organization should define whether each record receives one primary category and optional secondary categories, or follows another consistent classification method.

### 21. Inherent Risk, Control Effectiveness, and Residual Risk

AI risk registers should distinguish the exposure associated with a scenario from the effect of controls intended to manage it.

**Inherent risk** describes the risk before considering the relevant controls, according to the organization's defined methodology.

**Control effectiveness** describes how appropriately the relevant controls are designed, implemented, and operating in practice.

**Residual risk** describes the risk remaining after the relevant controls are considered.

A simplified relationship is:

```
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Control Effectiveness Assessment
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment or Acceptance Decision
```

Example:

| Assessment Element    | Example                                                 |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scenario              | Unauthorized retrieval of confidential customer records |
| Inherent likelihood   | Likely                                                  |
| Inherent impact       | Major                                                   |
| Inherent risk         | High                                                    |
| Existing control      | Identity-based authorization                            |
| Control effectiveness | Partially effective                                     |
| Residual likelihood   | Possible                                                |
| Residual impact       | Major                                                   |
| Residual risk         | High                                                    |
| Required decision     | Additional treatment and escalation                     |

The example illustrates an important principle: implementing a control does not automatically mean that risk has been reduced to an acceptable level.

Controls must be relevant to the scenario and supported by evidence of their effectiveness.

The exact scoring method and relationship between inherent and residual risk should follow the organization's approved risk methodology. Organizations should not assume that residual risk can always be calculated by mechanically subtracting a control score from an inherent risk score.

### 22. Risk Assessment Methodology and Rating Criteria

An organization should establish consistent criteria for assessing AI risk.

A qualitative methodology may use likelihood and impact scales, supported by defined descriptions.

Example likelihood scale:

| Level              | Description                                                           |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Rare           | The scenario is highly unlikely within the defined assessment context |
| 2 — Unlikely       | The scenario could occur but is not expected                          |
| 3 — Possible       | The scenario could reasonably occur                                   |
| 4 — Likely         | The scenario is expected to occur under relevant conditions           |
| 5 — Almost Certain | The scenario is expected to occur frequently or repeatedly            |

Example impact scale:

| Level        | Description                                                                                               |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — Minor    | Limited, readily manageable consequences                                                                  |
| 2 — Moderate | Noticeable consequences requiring routine remediation                                                     |
| 3 — Major    | Material business, security, privacy, or stakeholder impact                                               |
| 4 — Severe   | Significant disruption, harm, or regulatory exposure                                                      |
| 5 — Critical | Extremely serious consequences affecting critical operations, rights, safety, or organizational viability |

These scales are illustrative and must be tailored to the organization's context.

Impact criteria should consider more than direct financial loss. Depending on the use case, the assessment may need to address human harm, privacy, operational disruption, legal obligations, discrimination, and consequences for affected individuals.

The methodology should also specify:

- How ratings are assigned.
- How multiple impact dimensions are considered.
- How uncertainty is recorded.
- How control effectiveness is evaluated.
- How risk ratings are combined.
- What thresholds require escalation.
- Which risks require formal acceptance.
- How reassessment is triggered.

Consistency is essential for meaningful portfolio reporting.

### 23. Risk Ownership and Accountability

Every material AI risk should have an identifiable accountable owner.

Risk ownership should not be confused with the responsibility for implementing a particular technical control.

For example:

| Role                      | Typical Responsibility                                            |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business owner            | Accountable for the business purpose and consequences of AI use   |
| Risk owner                | Accountable for monitoring and managing the assigned risk         |
| System owner              | Responsible for the system's operational lifecycle                |
| Technical owner           | Responsible for relevant technical implementation and maintenance |
| Control owner             | Responsible for operating or maintaining a specific control       |
| AI governance function    | Defines governance requirements and coordinates oversight         |
| GRC function              | Supports risk methodology, reporting, and challenge               |
| Legal or privacy function | Advises on relevant legal, regulatory, and privacy obligations    |
| Internal audit            | Independently evaluates governance and control effectiveness      |

The exact allocation depends on the organization's operating model.

A single person may hold multiple roles in a smaller organization, but accountability should remain explicit.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority, information, and organizational support to make decisions or escalate issues.

Assigning a risk to an individual who cannot influence the relevant business process or secure the necessary remediation is unlikely to produce effective risk management.

### 24. AI Inventory Lifecycle Status

The inventory should represent the lifecycle state of each AI system or use case.

A possible lifecycle model is:

```
Proposed
   ↓
Under Assessment
   ↓
Approved for Development
   ↓
In Development
   ↓
Testing and Validation
   ↓
Approved for Deployment
   ↓
Production
   ↓
Suspended / Restricted
   ↓
Retired
```

Not every organization will require every status, and the sequence may vary by deployment model.

The inventory should distinguish between technical existence and governance authorization.

For example, an AI tool may be detected in use before it has received formal approval. Its record should reflect both its actual state and its governance status rather than incorrectly classifying it as approved.

Lifecycle status should be updated when material changes occur.

Relevant events include:

- Approval or rejection of a proposed use case.
- Completion of testing.
- Production deployment.
- A material model or architecture change.
- Suspension following an incident.
- Restriction of a business use case.
- Replacement by another system.
- Retirement and decommissioning.

Lifecycle accuracy supports reliable reporting and helps prevent unapproved or retired systems from being treated as active, approved deployments.

### 25. AI Risk Register Lifecycle Status

Risk records also require defined lifecycle statuses.

A possible workflow is:

```
Identified
    ↓
Under Assessment
    ↓
Assessed
    ↓
Treatment Required
    ↓
Treatment in Progress
    ↓
Awaiting Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Reviewed
    ↓
Accepted / Monitored / Further Treatment
```

A separate closure process may be used when a risk scenario is no longer relevant, has been eliminated, or has been superseded under the organization's methodology.

The organization should define what each status means and what evidence is required to move between statuses.

For example, a risk should not be marked as treated merely because a corrective action has been reported as complete.

Where validation is required, the organization should obtain evidence that the relevant control or remediation is implemented and effective.

Similarly, an accepted risk should remain subject to any monitoring, review, and expiration conditions required by the organization's risk acceptance policy.

A clear status model helps management distinguish between risks that have been identified, risks being treated, risks awaiting validation, and risks that remain open.

### 26. Inventory Completeness and Data Quality

An AI inventory is only useful when its coverage and records are sufficiently complete and reliable.

Inventory quality should be evaluated across several dimensions.

**Completeness:** Are all in-scope AI systems and use cases recorded?

**Accuracy:** Do the records reflect the actual deployed environment?

**Consistency:** Are fields and classifications applied uniformly?

**Timeliness:** Are changes recorded within the required period?

**Uniqueness:** Are duplicate records identified and resolved?

**Traceability:** Can records be linked to authoritative sources and supporting evidence?

**Ownership:** Does each relevant record have an accountable owner?

**Validity:** Do field values comply with the organization's data standards?

A simple completeness measure might be:

```
Inventory Completeness
=
Confirmed In-Scope AI Systems Recorded
÷
Total Confirmed In-Scope AI Systems
× 100
```

For example, if an organization identifies 120 in-scope AI systems and confirms that 108 have valid inventory records, the measured completeness is 90%.

This metric is meaningful only if the denominator is credible. An organization cannot reliably claim 100% completeness merely because every record in its existing inventory has been filled in.

The organization must also have reasonable evidence that the inventory population includes the AI systems that actually exist.

### 27. AI Risk Register Data Quality

The risk register should be subject to its own quality controls.

Potential quality checks include:

- Every material risk has a unique identifier.
- Every risk is linked to a relevant AI system or use case where applicable.
- Risk statements identify a meaningful scenario.
- Required assessment fields are completed.
- Ratings follow the approved methodology.
- The risk owner is identified.
- Existing controls are linked or described.
- Treatment actions have accountable owners and target dates.
- Risk acceptance records contain the required approvals.
- Review dates are maintained.
- Evidence links remain accessible.
- Closed or superseded records retain appropriate history.

A register can contain many entries and still be unreliable if risks are duplicated, poorly described, incorrectly rated, or left without accountable owners.

For example:

```
Weak Record
-----------
Risk: AI compliance risk
Rating: High
Owner: GRC
Action: Improve controls

Problems:
- No specific scenario
- No identified consequences
- No clear accountable risk owner
- No defined control gap
- No measurable treatment outcome
```

A stronger record describes the scenario, assessment rationale, affected system, relevant controls, accountable owner, and specific treatment objective.

The goal is not simply to populate fields. It is to create records that support consistent decisions and effective oversight.

Periodic reviews are necessary, but calendar-based reviews alone may not be sufficient for dynamic AI environments.

An inventory or risk record may require reassessment when a material change occurs.

Potential triggers include:

- Change in model version or provider.
- Change in intended purpose.
- Expansion to a new business process.
- Connection to a new data source.
- Access to more sensitive information.

- Significant model performance degradation.
- A security or privacy incident.
- A material audit finding.
- A change in applicable legal requirements.
- A new supplier or critical dependency.
- A significant change in user population or deployment scale.
- A change in control effectiveness.
- Expiration of a risk acceptance decision.

The relationship can be represented as:

```

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓

          ↓
```

The organization should define which changes require a full reassessment and which require a targeted review.

The objective is to prevent previously valid inventory and risk records from becoming misleading as the AI environment changes.

### 30. Part 1 Core Principle

The central principle of AI inventory and AI risk register governance is:

> **An organization must maintain a sufficiently complete and reliable record of its AI systems and use cases, link that inventory to meaningful risk scenarios, and establish accountable processes for assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting AI risk.**

The inventory establishes the population and context of AI use.

The risk register establishes the documented exposure and the management decisions associated with that exposure.

The relationship is:

```
Discover AI
    ↓
Register AI
    ↓
Establish Ownership and Context
    ↓
Identify Risk Scenarios
    ↓
Assess Risk
    ↓
Document Controls and Treatment
    ↓
Monitor and Reassess
    ↓
Report and Provide Assurance
```

A mature governance program does not treat these records as static spreadsheets. It maintains them as controlled governance assets that evolve with the organization's AI systems, business processes, risks, controls, and obligations.

Their value ultimately depends on completeness, data quality, traceability, clear accountability, and their practical use in organizational decision-making.

## Part 2 — AI Inventory Architecture, Risk Register Design, and Governance Integration

### 31. Establishing a Standardized AI Inventory Data Model

An AI inventory data model defines how AI systems, models, applications, use cases, business owners, technical dependencies, and governance records are represented and related.

A standardized data model prevents different business units from maintaining incompatible inventory structures.

For example, one department might record an AI tool as a software application, while another records the same capability as a model and a third records it as a business process. Without defined relationships and identification rules, the organization may overcount or overlook AI deployments.

A standardized data model should distinguish between the primary objects being governed.

| Data Object      | Purpose                                                     | Example                                        |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| AI system        | Represents the deployed AI capability                       | Customer Service Assistant                     |
| AI model         | Represents the model used by the system                     | Enterprise language model, version 4           |
| AI application   | Represents the software interface or application            | Customer Support Portal                        |
| AI use case      | Represents the business purpose                             | Drafting responses to customer inquiries       |
| AI provider      | Identifies the organization supplying a model or service    | External AI provider                           |
| Data asset       | Identifies relevant information sources                     | Customer knowledge repository                  |
| AI risk          | Represents an individual risk scenario                      | Unauthorized retrieval of customer information |
| AI control       | Represents a measure used to manage risk                    | Identity-based retrieval authorization         |
| AI assessment    | Records a formal assessment activity                        | AI security and privacy assessment             |
| AI finding       | Records an identified control deficiency or assurance issue | Missing authorization validation               |
| Treatment action | Tracks remediation or risk-reduction work                   | Implement document-level authorization checks  |

These objects should be linked through stable identifiers and documented relationships.

A standardized data model should also define mandatory attributes, permitted values, ownership, data validation rules, and update responsibilities.

The objective is to make AI governance information consistent enough to support enterprise reporting without losing the technical and business context needed for individual assessments.

### 32. Designing the AI Inventory Around Governed Objects

The inventory should not assume that every organization has a simple one-to-one relationship between a model and an AI system.

A single AI system may use several models. A model may support multiple systems. One application may contain several AI capabilities, and one use case may depend on multiple applications or services.

A practical object relationship model may look like this:

```
Business Process
      |
      v
   AI Use Case
      |
      v
   AI System
      |
      +------------------+
      |                  |
      v                  v
  AI Model A          AI Model B
      |                  |
      +---------+--------+
                |
                v
        External Dependencies
                |
                v
       Data Sources and Tools
```

Consider an enterprise AI assistant that uses a foundation model, retrieves documents from an internal knowledge platform, and invokes an external service to retrieve customer information.

The inventory may need to represent the assistant as the governed system, the foundation model as a dependency, the document retrieval capability as a component, and the customer service workflow as the use case.

This structure allows the organization to answer different questions without creating duplicate records:

- Which AI systems depend on a particular model?
- Which business processes depend on a particular AI system?
- Which systems access sensitive information?
- Which use cases depend on a specific supplier?
- Which risks may be affected by a model or platform change?

The data model should be proportionate to the organization's size and complexity. A smaller organization may maintain fewer object types, provided that its records preserve the relationships necessary for effective risk management and assurance.

### 33. Defining Canonical Identifiers and Record Relationships

A canonical identifier is the authoritative identifier used to refer to a specific governed object across systems and processes.

For AI governance, identifiers should distinguish among inventory records, risk scenarios, controls, findings, and treatment actions.

An illustrative identifier structure is:

| Record Type      | Example Identifier |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| AI system        | AI-SYS-001         |
| AI use case      | AI-UC-014          |
| AI model         | AI-MDL-007         |
| AI risk          | AI-R-026           |
| AI control       | AI-CTRL-018        |
| AI assessment    | AI-ASMT-009        |
| AI finding       | AI-FND-012         |
| Treatment action | AI-ACT-031         |

The organization should establish a naming convention and prevent identifiers from being reused after records are retired or closed.

Relationships should be maintained through these identifiers rather than relying exclusively on names.

For example, the name of an AI application may change following a rebranding exercise. Its stable identifier should continue to connect the application to historical risk assessments, audit findings, and approved treatment decisions.

A relationship record might contain:

```
AI System ID: AI-SYS-001
Use Case ID: AI-UC-014
Risk ID: AI-R-026
Control ID: AI-CTRL-018
Finding ID: AI-FND-012
Treatment Action ID: AI-ACT-031
```

This relationship structure supports traceability from the governed system to the risk, control, assurance finding, and corrective action.

Where records are maintained in separate platforms, integration rules should specify how identifiers are synchronized and how broken or ambiguous relationships are detected.

### 34. Separating System-Level Records From Business Use Cases

An AI system and its business use cases should not automatically be treated as the same governance object.

The system describes the technical capability. The use case describes how that capability is applied to a business purpose.

This distinction becomes important when the same AI capability is used for activities with different levels of risk.

For example:

```
AI System: Enterprise Language Model Platform
                    |
        +-----------+-----------+
        |           |           |
        v           v           v
    Use Case A  Use Case B  Use Case C
        |           |           |
        v           v           v
   Public FAQ    HR Policy   Employee
   Drafting      Guidance    Evaluation
```

The underlying platform may be the same, but the consequences of an incorrect output, unauthorized disclosure, or inappropriate reliance may differ considerably.

The public FAQ use case might require content accuracy and disclosure controls. HR policy guidance may require stronger privacy, currency, and human review controls. Employee evaluation may require additional scrutiny of fairness, decision-making impact, and applicable legal requirements.

The inventory should therefore support separate use-case records when business purposes, affected stakeholders, data processing, risk classifications, or approval requirements differ materially.

System-level information can be maintained centrally and referenced by multiple use cases.

This prevents duplicated technical information while preserving the context needed for use-case-specific risk assessment.

### 35. Establishing Inventory Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

An inventory policy should define which AI systems and use cases must be recorded, including how exceptions are handled.

Without explicit criteria, inventory completeness becomes difficult to assess because different departments may interpret the scope differently.

Potential inclusion criteria include:

- AI systems developed internally.
- AI capabilities purchased from external providers.
- AI features embedded in enterprise applications.
- AI services processing organizational or customer information.
- AI systems supporting material business decisions.
- AI agents that invoke tools or execute actions.
- AI pilots that have access to sensitive data or production environments.
- AI services operated by suppliers on behalf of the organization.
- Experimental systems that may create material security, privacy, or operational exposure.

Potential exclusions might include technologies that fall outside the organization's defined AI scope or activities that are not actually used by the organization. Any exclusion should be based on documented criteria rather than an informal assumption.

A scope decision can be represented as:

```
Candidate Technology or Use Case
              |
              v
       AI Scope Screening
              |
       +------+------+
       |             |
       v             v
   In Scope       Out of Scope
       |             |
       v             v
 Create or Update   Record Rationale
 Inventory Record   Where Required
       |
       v
 Determine Governance Requirements
```

An exclusion decision should not automatically mean that the activity is risk-free. For example, a technology excluded from the formal AI inventory might still be subject to cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, or acceptable-use requirements.

The organization should retain sufficient evidence to explain material scope decisions and reassess them when the underlying circumstances change.

### 36. Designing the AI Risk Register Data Model

The risk register data model should support the complete lifecycle of an AI risk, from identification through assessment, treatment, monitoring, and closure or continued acceptance.

A risk record should be structured around the scenario rather than around a general system description.

A practical model may include the following groups of fields:

| Field Group       | Representative Information                                |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identification    | Risk ID, title, linked AI system, linked use case         |
| Classification    | Primary category, secondary categories, risk domain       |
| Scenario          | Causes, risk event, consequences, affected stakeholders   |
| Assessment        | Likelihood, impact, inherent rating, assessment rationale |
| Controls          | Existing controls, control owners, control effectiveness  |
| Residual exposure | Residual likelihood, impact, rating, uncertainty          |
| Treatment         | Treatment strategy, planned actions, resources            |
| Accountability    | Risk owner, action owners, approving authority            |
| Acceptance        | Decision, rationale, approver, conditions, expiry         |
| Monitoring        | Indicators, review frequency, trigger conditions          |
| Evidence          | Assessment records, test results, supporting documents    |
| Workflow          | Status, creation date, last update, review date           |

The model should also distinguish between the current state and the historical record.

For example, a risk may initially be assessed as high, reduced to medium after control improvements, and later reassessed as high following a change in the AI system's permissions.

The register should preserve enough history to explain those changes.

A data model that stores only the latest rating may support current reporting but provide inadequate evidence for audit, management review, or investigation of earlier decisions.

### 37. Defining Risk Assessment Scales and Rating Rules

A standardized assessment method is necessary to compare AI risks across systems and business units.

The organization should document the meaning of each rating and the rules used to combine likelihood and impact.

A common qualitative approach uses a likelihood-impact matrix.

Illustrative matrix:

| Likelihood / Impact | Minor  | Moderate | Major  | Severe   | Critical |
| ------------------- | ------ | -------- | ------ | -------- | -------- |
| Almost Certain      | Medium | High     | High   | Critical | Critical |
| Likely              | Medium | Medium   | High   | Critical | Critical |
| Possible            | Low    | Medium   | High   | High     | Critical |
| Unlikely            | Low    | Low      | Medium | High     | High     |
| Rare                | Low    | Low      | Medium | Medium   | High     |

This matrix is an example, not a universal AI risk standard. The organization should define its own approved matrix, thresholds, and escalation requirements.

AI risk assessment should also account for the type of potential harm. For some use cases, the effect on an individual's rights or safety may be more important than direct financial loss.

The assessment process should address:

- The relevant time horizon.
- The operating environment.
- The population potentially affected.
- The likelihood of the scenario under realistic conditions.
- The severity and scale of consequences.
- Existing control effectiveness.
- Uncertainty and limitations in available evidence.
- Dependencies that may increase exposure.
- Applicable risk tolerance and escalation thresholds.

Rating rules should be applied consistently, but consistency does not require pretending that every risk can be measured precisely.

Where quantitative data is unavailable, the assessment should document its rationale and uncertainty rather than presenting subjective judgments as objective measurements.

### 38. Separating Inherent Risk, Control Effectiveness, and Residual Risk Fields

The risk register should preserve the distinction between inherent exposure, the performance of controls, and the risk remaining after those controls are considered.

Combining these concepts into one field can make risk assessments difficult to interpret.

Consider an AI agent that can retrieve confidential records and initiate customer account changes.

Its inherent exposure may be high because of the sensitivity of the data and the potential consequences of unauthorized actions.

The organization may implement identity verification, least-privilege permissions, transaction limits, human approval, logging, and monitoring.

However, the existence of these controls does not automatically establish that the remaining risk is low.

The assessment must consider whether the controls are appropriately designed, correctly implemented, and operating effectively.

```
Inherent Risk Assessment
          |
          v
Identify Relevant Controls
          |
          v
Evaluate Control Design
          |
          v
Test Implementation and Operation
          |
          v
Determine Residual Exposure
          |
          v
Compare Against Risk Tolerance
          |
     +----+----+
     |         |
     v         v
Within       Outside
Tolerance    Tolerance
     |         |
     v         v
Monitor      Treat or Escalate
```

The register should retain the rationale for the inherent and residual ratings.

Where controls are only planned and have not yet been implemented, they should not be treated as effective existing controls when calculating the current residual exposure.

Similarly, a control that has been implemented but not validated may require an explicit qualification in the assessment.

### 39. Defining Risk Treatment Strategies

Risk treatment specifies how the organization intends to respond to an assessed risk.

The selected strategy should reflect the nature of the risk, the organization's risk tolerance, applicable obligations, and the feasibility of reducing exposure.

Common treatment strategies include:

| Strategy          | Description                                                                        | AI Example                                                     |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid             | Discontinue or do not undertake the activity creating the exposure                 | Reject an AI use case that cannot meet required safeguards     |
| Mitigate          | Implement measures that reduce likelihood or impact                                | Restrict an AI agent's permissions and validate its actions    |
| Transfer or share | Allocate defined aspects of exposure through contractual or other arrangements     | Establish supplier obligations and incident notification terms |
| Accept            | Formally approve continued exposure within the organization's authority and policy | Accept a documented residual risk with monitoring conditions   |

Transfer or sharing does not eliminate the organization's responsibility for its own legal obligations or business decisions.

Likewise, acceptance is not a substitute for mandatory compliance or a justification for ignoring a risk that must be addressed under applicable law or policy.

Treatment decisions should be recorded with their rationale, accountable approver, conditions, and any required follow-up.

For mitigation, the organization should identify the intended risk-reduction outcome rather than recording only a general activity such as "improve AI security."

A stronger treatment objective might specify that the AI application must enforce authorization at retrieval time, reject unauthorized requests, and demonstrate the effectiveness of the control through defined testing.

### 40. Connecting Risk Treatment Plans to Corrective Actions

A risk treatment plan describes the approach for managing risk. Corrective actions are the specific activities used to address identified control deficiencies or implement approved improvements.

The two concepts are related but should not be treated as identical.

A single treatment plan may require multiple corrective actions. Conversely, one corrective action may contribute to reducing several related risks.

For example:

```
Risk:
Unauthorized retrieval of confidential records
                 |
                 v
Treatment Objective:
Enforce authorization across AI retrieval workflows
                 |
       +---------+---------+
       |         |         |
       v         v         v
     Action 1  Action 2  Action 3
     Review    Implement Test access
     access    authorization enforcement
     model     enforcement
       |         |         |
       +---------+---------+
                 |
                 v
       Validate Effectiveness
                 |
                 v
       Reassess Residual Risk
```

A treatment action should ordinarily have:

- A unique action identifier.
- A clear description of the required outcome.
- A linked risk or finding.
- An accountable action owner.
- A target completion date.
- A priority or severity.
- Dependencies and required resources.
- Completion evidence.
- Validation requirements.
- Current status.

The register should distinguish between action completion and risk reduction.

An action can be administratively complete while the risk remains above tolerance. For example, a new authorization mechanism may have been deployed but may not adequately cover every retrieval pathway.

Where validation identifies a continuing control gap, the organization should retain or reopen the relevant treatment activity according to its procedures and reassess the associated risk.

### 41. Risk Acceptance Records and Expiration Controls

Risk acceptance is a governance decision to tolerate a defined level of residual exposure.

It should not be treated as an informal statement that a risk is understood or that remediation is inconvenient.

A risk acceptance record should identify:

| Field                   | Purpose                                           |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID                 | Identifies the accepted risk                      |
| Residual rating         | Records the exposure being accepted               |
| Acceptance rationale    | Explains why acceptance is appropriate            |
| Decision authority      | Identifies the authorized approver                |
| Decision date           | Records when the decision was made                |
| Acceptance period       | Defines how long the decision remains valid       |
| Conditions              | Specifies required safeguards or limitations      |
| Monitoring requirements | Defines ongoing oversight                         |
| Reassessment triggers   | Identifies events that invalidate the assumptions |
| Expiration date         | Establishes when renewed review is required       |
| Supporting evidence     | Preserves the assessment and decision rationale   |

Acceptance authority should reflect the risk's severity and the organization's governance structure.

For example, a business owner may be authorized to accept certain medium risks but lack authority to accept a critical risk that requires executive escalation.

The organization should also distinguish risk acceptance from legal or regulatory authorization. Internal approval does not override mandatory requirements.

When an acceptance expires, the risk should be reassessed, reapproved where permitted, or placed under further treatment. The record should not silently remain valid because no one reviewed it.

### 42. Linking AI Controls to Risk Scenarios

Controls should be linked to the risk scenarios they are intended to manage.

This relationship helps determine whether the control framework adequately addresses the organization's actual AI exposure.

For example:

| Risk Scenario                                                   | Relevant Control Examples                                                     |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prompt injection causes unauthorized tool use                   | Tool authorization, input handling, action validation, restricted execution   |
| Sensitive information is disclosed                              | Data classification, access enforcement, output filtering, data-loss controls |
| Model output produces an incorrect consequential recommendation | Validation, human review, performance monitoring, documented limitations      |
| Model integrity is compromised                                  | Artifact integrity checks, access control, provenance verification            |
| AI service failure disrupts a critical process                  | Resilience arrangements, fallback procedures, recovery testing                |
| Supplier changes a model without adequate notice                | Contractual change notification, supplier monitoring, reassessment triggers   |

A control should not be linked merely because it belongs to a general cybersecurity or compliance framework.

The organization should be able to explain how the control reduces the likelihood or impact of the particular risk.

For each material risk, the register should identify whether the relevant controls are:

- Designed and implemented.
- Operating effectively.
- Partially effective.
- Ineffective.
- Not yet implemented.
- Not yet assessed.

The exact status vocabulary should be standardized.

This linkage supports control gap analysis, risk-based testing, remediation prioritization, and assurance reporting.

### 43. Managing Common and Systemic AI Risks

Some AI risks affect individual systems, while others arise from shared models, platforms, providers, controls, or governance processes.

A systemic risk can affect many inventory records at once.

For example, if multiple applications rely on a shared AI gateway that does not enforce adequate access restrictions, the exposure may extend across several business units.

The organization should avoid recording the same underlying systemic issue as unrelated risks without preserving their common cause.

An illustrative structure is:

```
Shared AI Platform Risk
          |
     +----+----+
     |         |
     v         v
 System A   System B
     |         |
     v         v
 Business   Business
 Process A  Process B
```

A common risk record can describe the shared exposure, while linked system-specific records describe local consequences or additional control gaps.

The chosen structure should allow management to understand both the enterprise-wide exposure and the risks affecting individual systems.

Systemic risk indicators may include:

- Repeated findings across multiple AI applications.
- Shared dependency on one model provider.
- Common weaknesses in identity or access management.
- Reuse of an unvalidated model component.
- Widespread absence of AI-specific monitoring.
- Similar incidents across several business units.
- Common deficiencies in supplier assurance.

Systemic risks may require centralized treatment, coordinated remediation, or enterprise-level escalation rather than isolated corrective actions within individual teams.

### 44. Standardizing Risk Register Workflow and Statuses

A controlled workflow prevents risk records from moving between statuses without the required assessment, evidence, or approval.

A possible workflow is:

```
Identified
    ↓
Under Assessment
    ↓
Assessed
    ↓
Treatment Decision
    ↓
Treatment in Progress
    ↓
Awaiting Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Review
    ↓
Accepted / Monitored / Further Treatment
```

The organization should define entry and exit criteria for each status.

For example:

| Status                | Minimum Governance Expectation                      |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Identified            | A potential risk scenario has been recorded         |
| Under Assessment      | Relevant information is being gathered and analyzed |
| Assessed              | The risk evaluation and rationale are documented    |
| Treatment in Progress | Approved treatment activities are underway          |
| Awaiting Validation   | Evidence is available for review or testing         |
| Monitored             | The risk is subject to defined ongoing oversight    |
| Accepted              | Acceptance has been authorized and documented       |
| Closed                | Closure criteria have been satisfied and recorded   |

The organization may use additional statuses to represent rejected, duplicate, superseded, or out-of-scope records.

Closure should not be used simply because an action was completed or a risk has become inconvenient to track.

A closed record should retain sufficient history to explain the closure decision and preserve traceability to any successor records.

### 45. Implementing Data Quality and Validation Rules

Data quality controls should be built into the inventory and risk register operating model rather than relying entirely on manual review.

Validation rules may include:

- Every active AI system has a unique identifier.
- Every material AI system has a designated business owner.
- Every material risk has an accountable risk owner.
- Every risk record uses an approved risk category.
- Risk ratings conform to the approved assessment method.
- Treatment actions have target dates and owners.
- Accepted risks contain the required authorization and expiration information.
- Retired systems have an appropriate lifecycle status.
- Referential links point to valid inventory, control, assessment, or finding records.
- Mandatory fields are completed before a record reaches the relevant approval stage.
- Material changes are recorded in the change history.

Example validation logic:

```
IF Risk Status = "Accepted"
THEN require:
    Acceptance Authority
    Acceptance Rationale
    Decision Date
    Expiration Date
    Residual Risk Rating
```

Another example:

```
IF AI Lifecycle Status = "Production"
AND Risk Classification requires formal assessment
THEN require:
    Assessment Record
    Business Owner
    Technical Owner
    Risk Review Date
```

These rules are illustrative. Actual requirements should follow the organization's governance policy and applicable obligations.

Automated validation can identify missing fields, broken links, expired decisions, and overdue reviews. However, syntactic completeness does not establish substantive accuracy.

A record may pass every automated field check while still containing an incorrect risk assessment or an ineffective control description. Periodic substantive review remains necessary.

### 46. Integrating AI Risk Registers With Enterprise Risk Management

AI risk registers should connect to the organization's broader enterprise risk management (ERM) process.

AI-related risks may affect existing enterprise risk categories such as cybersecurity, operational resilience, privacy, legal compliance, third-party risk, financial performance, and reputation.

Integration allows AI-specific risks to be escalated and aggregated using established enterprise governance mechanisms.

For example:

```
AI Risk Register
       |
       v
AI Governance and Risk Function
       |
       v
Enterprise Risk Management
       |
       +------------------+
       |                  |
       v                  v
Risk Committee      Executive Management
       |                  |
       +---------+--------+
                 |
                 v
        Enterprise Risk Reporting
```

Integration should preserve sufficient AI-specific detail.

A broad enterprise risk such as "technology and cyber risk" may be appropriate for executive reporting, but it should not replace detailed records describing prompt injection, unauthorized model access, data leakage, or unsafe autonomous actions.

The organization should define:

- Which AI risks require enterprise escalation.
- How AI risk ratings map to enterprise risk categories.
- How material AI risks are included in corporate risk reporting.
- How risk appetite and tolerance are applied.
- How systemic risks are reported.
- How AI-related incidents influence enterprise assessments.
- How disagreements between business and risk functions are resolved.

The objective is to make AI risk part of the organization's established decision-making process without losing the technical specificity required for effective treatment.

### 47. Integrating Inventory and Risk Records With Other Governance Artifacts

The AI inventory and risk register should function as connected components of the broader AI governance evidence environment.

Relevant artifacts may include:

- AI system cards.
- Model cards.

- Privacy impact assessments.
- Security assessments.
- Data protection records.
- Supplier assessments.
- AI control libraries.
- Testing and evaluation reports.

- Incident records.
- Internal audit findings.
- Corrective action plans.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Management review records.

For example, a system card may describe the AI system's intended purpose, architecture, limitations, and operational context. The inventory should identify the governed system and link to that card.

A model card may describe a model's intended use, evaluation results, limitations, and performance characteristics. The inventory may reference the model card where the model is used.

Instead, it should identify the authoritative source and reference it from the relevant inventory, risk, or assurance record.

### 48. Building AI Portfolio Risk Reporting

Portfolio risk reporting aggregates information across AI systems and use cases to provide a consolidated view of organizational exposure.

It should support management questions such as:

- How many AI systems are in production?
- Which business units have the highest concentration of AI risk?
- Which AI systems have high or critical residual risk?
- Which systems depend on the same external provider?
- Which material risks exceed approved tolerance?
- Which treatment actions are overdue?
- Which risk categories appear most frequently?
- Which systems have not been reviewed within the required period?
- Which systemic control deficiencies affect multiple applications?

Useful reporting dimensions include business unit, system criticality, lifecycle status, provider, data sensitivity, risk category, residual rating, and treatment status.

An illustrative portfolio view might be:

| Portfolio Indicator                         | Illustrative Result |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Registered AI systems                       | 120                 |
| Production AI systems                       | 75                  |
| Systems with completed risk assessments     | 68                  |
| Systems with high or critical residual risk | 14                  |
| Open AI risks                               | 96                  |
| Overdue treatment actions                   | 11                  |
| Accepted risks approaching expiration       | 5                   |

These figures are illustrative and should not be interpreted as actual organizational data.

Portfolio reports should define their population, reporting period, data sources, and calculation rules. For example, the number of open risks should distinguish unique risk records from the number of affected systems.

Management should also be able to drill down from aggregated indicators to the underlying records and evidence.

This enables decision-makers to investigate significant trends instead of relying exclusively on summary metrics.

### 49. Establishing an Operating Model for Inventory and Risk Register Administration

An AI inventory and risk register require an operating model that defines who maintains the records, who validates them, and who makes decisions.

The responsibilities may be distributed across business units, technology teams, AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, procurement, enterprise risk management, and internal audit.

A possible responsibility model is:

| Activity                        | Primary Responsibility                                 | Supporting Functions               |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Discover AI use                 | Business and technology teams                          | Procurement, IT, security          |
| Create inventory records        | Designated inventory administrator or system owner     | AI governance                      |
| Validate inventory completeness | AI governance or designated oversight function         | Business units, IT                 |
| Identify and assess AI risks    | Risk owner and relevant specialists                    | GRC, security, privacy             |
| Implement controls              | Control owner or technical team                        | System owner                       |
| Execute treatment actions       | Assigned action owner                                  | Risk owner, technical teams        |
| Approve risk acceptance         | Authorized decision-maker                              | Risk management, legal as required |
| Validate remediation            | Designated independent or qualified reviewer           | Control owner, assurance teams     |
| Report portfolio exposure       | AI governance and risk functions                       | Enterprise risk management         |
| Provide independent assurance   | Internal audit or other independent assurance function | Relevant stakeholders              |

The exact structure depends on the organization's size, risk profile, and governance arrangements.

Smaller organizations may combine administrative responsibilities, while larger organizations may use centralized platforms and distributed business-unit ownership.

Regardless of structure, the operating model should establish:

- Record ownership.
- Update responsibilities.
- Approval authority.
- Data quality expectations.
- Review frequency.
- Escalation criteria.
- Evidence retention.
- Access permissions.
- Separation of duties where appropriate.
- Independent validation requirements for material decisions.

Administrative ownership of the register does not automatically make the administrator accountable for the underlying business risk.

That accountability should remain with the formally designated risk owner and authorized management structure.

### 50. Part 2 Core Principle

The central principle of AI inventory architecture and risk register design is:

> **AI governance records must use standardized data structures, stable identifiers, clear relationships, defined assessment rules, and controlled workflows so that AI systems, risk scenarios, controls, decisions, and assurance evidence remain traceable throughout their lifecycle.**

A mature inventory and risk register should not operate as two isolated spreadsheets.

They should form part of a connected governance environment in which:

```
AI Systems and Use Cases
          ↓
Standardized Inventory Records
          ↓
Risk Scenarios and Assessments
          ↓
Controls and Treatment Actions
          ↓
Validation and Risk Decisions
          ↓
Enterprise Risk Reporting
          ↓
Audit and Assurance Evidence
```

Standardization enables consistent reporting. Traceability supports defensible decisions. Data quality improves reliability. Clear ownership establishes accountability. Controlled workflows ensure that risks and treatment actions remain visible until their defined closure or ongoing management criteria have been satisfied.

The effectiveness of the overall process depends not on the number of fields or records maintained, but on whether the information is accurate, connected, current, and used to make and evidence meaningful governance decisions.

## Part 3 — Risk Assessment Operations, Monitoring, Escalation, and Evidence Management

### 51. Establishing a Repeatable AI Risk Assessment Workflow

An AI risk register is effective only when the organization follows a repeatable process for identifying, analyzing, evaluating, treating, and monitoring risk.

The workflow should define the activities, required inputs, decision criteria, accountable roles, and evidence expected at each stage.

A standardized process reduces inconsistent assessments across business units and helps ensure that material AI risks receive appropriate attention.

A practical workflow is:

```
AI System or Use Case Identified
              |
              v
     Confirm Assessment Scope
              |
              v
      Identify Risk Scenarios
              |
              v
     Analyze Causes and Impacts
              |
              v
      Determine Inherent Risk
              |
              v
   Evaluate Existing Controls
              |
              v
      Determine Residual Risk
              |
              v
 Compare With Risk Tolerance
              |
              v
      Decide Risk Treatment
              |
              v
     Implement and Validate
              |
              v
       Monitor and Reassess
```

Each stage should have defined entry and exit criteria.

For example, an assessment should not be marked complete merely because a workshop has taken place. The organization should have a documented scope, meaningful risk scenarios, assessment rationale, relevant control information, assigned owners, and recorded decisions.

The required depth should be proportionate to the AI system's intended purpose, complexity, deployment context, and potential consequences.

A low-impact internal productivity tool may require a relatively lightweight assessment. An AI system influencing employment decisions, processing sensitive personal information, or executing consequential actions may require more detailed analysis and stronger approval requirements.

The workflow should therefore establish a common minimum standard while allowing additional assessment depth where justified.

### 52. Determining When an AI Risk Assessment Is Required

The organization should define which AI systems and use cases require formal risk assessment and which triggers require reassessment.

Assessment requirements should not depend solely on whether an AI system was developed internally or purchased from a supplier. Both internal and external AI capabilities can introduce material risks.

Potential assessment triggers include:

- Introduction of a new AI system or use case.
- Movement from experimentation into production.
- A material change in intended purpose.
- Introduction of a new model or provider.
- Access to new categories of data.
- Expansion of system permissions or autonomy.
- Integration with a critical business process.
- Processing of sensitive or personal information.
- Significant changes to affected users or stakeholders.
- A material security, privacy, safety, or reliability incident.
- A significant control deficiency.
- A change in applicable legal or regulatory requirements.
- A reassessment required by an expiring risk acceptance.

A trigger-based assessment model can be represented as:

```
New System / Material Change / Incident
                    |
                    v
         Governance Trigger Review
                    |
                    v
        Determine Assessment Scope
                    |
          +---------+---------+
          |                   |
          v                   v
   Full Assessment      Targeted Review
          |                   |
          +---------+---------+
                    |
                    v
       Update Risk Register and Evidence
```

The organization should document the rationale when a trigger results in a targeted review rather than a full reassessment.

A targeted review may be appropriate when a change has a limited and well-understood effect. A substantial architectural change, a new consequential use case, or a serious incident may require a broader reassessment.

The decision should be based on the change's potential effect on risk, not merely on the administrative convenience of the assessment team.

### 53. Identifying AI Risk Scenarios Systematically

Risk identification should examine how the AI system operates, what it can access, what decisions or actions it influences, and how it may fail or be misused.

A systematic approach considers both intended operation and reasonably foreseeable misuse, failure, and environmental change.

For a generative AI application, risk identification may examine:

| Assessment Area | Questions                                                                   |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Intended use    | What business purpose is the AI intended to support?                        |
| Inputs          | What information can users or connected systems provide?                    |
| Data sources    | Which repositories, databases, or external services can the AI access?      |
| Processing      | What model, retrieval, orchestration, or decision logic is involved?        |
| Outputs         | What information, predictions, recommendations, or actions can be produced? |
| Permissions     | Which resources and operations are available to the system?                 |
| Human oversight | Who reviews or approves consequential outputs and actions?                  |
| Dependencies    | Which models, providers, APIs, and infrastructure components are required?  |
| Failure modes   | How could the system produce incorrect, unsafe, or unauthorized outcomes?   |
| Monitoring      | How would the organization detect a failure or emerging risk?               |

Risk identification should involve stakeholders with relevant knowledge of the business process, technical architecture, data, security, privacy, and applicable obligations.

For example, a cybersecurity specialist may identify an excessive-permission risk in an AI agent, while a business owner may identify the consequences of incorrect customer account changes.

Neither perspective alone necessarily captures the complete scenario.

The objective is to establish risk scenarios that are sufficiently specific to support assessment, control selection, and treatment.

### 54. Using Cause–Event–Consequence Analysis

Cause–event–consequence analysis provides a structured way to describe an AI risk scenario.

The method distinguishes the conditions that create exposure, the event that may occur, and the consequences that may follow.

Consider an AI application connected to a confidential document repository.

```
Cause
Insufficient authorization enforcement
across retrieved documents
              |
              v
Risk Event
The AI retrieves information that the
requesting user is not authorized to access
              |
              v
Consequences
Confidentiality breach
Privacy exposure
Contractual or regulatory consequences
Loss of stakeholder trust
```

This structure improves the quality of risk records because it connects the scenario to the controls needed to manage it.

For example:

- A cause may be addressed through secure configuration or architecture changes.
- The event may be prevented or detected through authorization checks and monitoring.
- The consequences may be limited through data minimization, containment, incident response, and recovery arrangements.

The analysis should avoid presenting every possible consequence as certain. It should record plausible impacts relevant to the actual system and operating context.

Where a scenario contains several materially different events or consequences, separate risk records may be appropriate.

However, splitting a single scenario into numerous minor records can also create unnecessary administrative overhead. The organization should use a consistent rule for determining when distinct scenarios warrant separate entries.

### 55. Assessing AI Risk Across the System Lifecycle

AI risks can emerge at different stages of the system lifecycle. The risk register should not focus exclusively on production operation.

A lifecycle-oriented assessment may consider the following stages:

| Lifecycle Stage   | Example Risk                                                      |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Planning          | Intended use is poorly defined or inadequately justified          |
| Procurement       | Supplier capabilities or limitations are not adequately evaluated |
| Data preparation  | Data quality, provenance, or authorization is insufficient        |
| Development       | Model or application design introduces weaknesses                 |
| Testing           | Material failure modes are not identified before deployment       |
| Deployment        | Production configuration differs from the approved design         |
| Operation         | Model behavior or external conditions change                      |
| Maintenance       | Updates introduce new vulnerabilities or performance problems     |
| Incident response | AI-specific failure modes are not adequately addressed            |
| Retirement        | Access, data, credentials, or dependencies remain active          |

Lifecycle assessment helps identify risks that may otherwise be missed when the register is limited to current operational threats.

For example, a model may perform adequately in testing but experience reduced reliability after the underlying data distribution changes. Another system may be retired while its service credentials remain active, leaving an unnecessary access path.

The inventory and risk register should therefore capture relevant lifecycle transitions and reassessment requirements.

Risk ownership may also change across the lifecycle. A project owner may coordinate development-stage risks, while an operational business owner assumes accountability after deployment. Such transitions should be documented rather than left implicit.

### 56. Evaluating Control Design and Operating Effectiveness

Control assessment should determine whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively for the risks they are intended to address.

These are related but distinct questions.

**Design effectiveness:** Would the control, if implemented and followed, adequately address the relevant risk?

**Implementation:** Has the control been put into operation as intended?

**Operating effectiveness:** Does the control function consistently and reliably under relevant operating conditions?

Consider a control intended to prevent an AI agent from modifying customer accounts without authorization.

The control may be described in a policy, but that alone does not demonstrate that it has been implemented.

A technical implementation may exist, but it may fail to validate some action pathways.

Even a correctly implemented control may be ineffective if monitoring is absent or if exceptions routinely bypass it.

A control evaluation may therefore examine:

```
Control Requirement
        |
        v
Design Review
        |
        v
Implementation Verification
        |
        v
Operating Effectiveness Testing
        |
        v
Document Result and Limitations
        |
        v
Update Risk Assessment
```

Relevant evidence may include configuration records, test results, access reviews, execution logs, exception records, monitoring results, and documented approvals.

The required evidence should reflect the nature of the control. A policy review and a technical penetration test provide different types of assurance and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Where control effectiveness is uncertain, the risk register should preserve that uncertainty instead of assuming the control is fully effective.

### 57. Distinguishing Control Deficiencies From AI Risk Scenarios

A control deficiency and a risk scenario are related but distinct records.

A **risk scenario** describes an undesirable event and its potential consequences.

A **control deficiency** identifies a weakness in the design, implementation, or operation of a control intended to manage exposure.

For example:

| Element               | Example                                                                      |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk scenario         | An AI agent modifies customer records without appropriate authorization      |
| Expected control      | Enforce authorization before each consequential action                       |
| Control deficiency    | The authorization check is applied to only some action pathways              |
| Potential consequence | Unauthorized data modification and operational disruption                    |
| Treatment action      | Implement centralized authorization enforcement and test all action pathways |

A single control deficiency may contribute to several risks. Conversely, a single risk may be affected by multiple control deficiencies.

The register should preserve those relationships.

An audit finding may be recorded in an assurance system, while the associated risk is maintained in the risk register and the remediation is tracked through a corrective action system.

The records should reference each other so that the organization can trace the full sequence from identified weakness to risk exposure and remediation.

A control deficiency should not automatically be treated as a separate enterprise risk if it does not represent a distinct scenario. However, material deficiencies should be assessed to determine whether they reveal previously unidentified or underestimated risks.

### 58. Establishing Evidence Requirements for Risk Assessments

Risk assessments should be supported by evidence appropriate to the significance and nature of the risk.

Evidence enables reviewers to understand how conclusions were reached and whether the assessment reflects the actual operating environment.

Potential evidence includes:

- AI system architecture diagrams.
- Model and system documentation.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Data classification records.
- Supplier documentation.
- Security assessment results.
- Privacy assessment records.
- Model evaluation results.
- AI red-team reports.
- Access-control configurations.
- Logging and monitoring evidence.
- Incident records.
- Control testing results.
- Human oversight procedures.
- Approval and risk acceptance records.
- Treatment validation results.

Evidence should be linked to the relevant inventory or risk record using stable references or controlled repository links.

The organization should also distinguish between evidence that establishes a requirement and evidence that demonstrates implementation.

For example, a security policy may establish that access restrictions are required. A configuration export may demonstrate that a particular restriction is configured. Testing may provide further evidence that the restriction operates effectively.

These sources support different conclusions.

A risk assessment should document material evidence limitations. If the assessor cannot inspect the model provider's internal training process, that limitation should be recorded where relevant rather than silently interpreted as evidence of either effective or ineffective control.

Evidence requirements should be proportionate to the exposure, but material risk decisions should remain sufficiently documented to support independent review.

### 59. Maintaining Evidence Integrity and Traceability

Evidence management should ensure that supporting records remain identifiable, accessible, and connected to the decisions they support.

A useful evidence record may contain:

| Field                | Purpose                                               |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Evidence ID          | Unique evidence reference                             |
| Related AI ID        | Identifies the relevant AI system                     |
| Related Risk ID      | Identifies the associated risk scenario               |
| Evidence description | Explains what the evidence demonstrates               |
| Source               | Identifies the system, document, or responsible party |
| Collection date      | Records when the evidence was obtained                |
| Period covered       | Defines the period represented by the evidence        |
| Reviewer             | Identifies the person who reviewed the evidence       |
| Result               | Records the conclusion or relevant observation        |
| Limitations          | Documents scope or reliability constraints            |
| Storage reference    | Links to the controlled evidence location             |

The organization should apply appropriate access restrictions and retention requirements to evidence, especially where it contains personal information, confidential business data, or security-sensitive configurations.

Where evidence changes, the record should preserve the relevant version or historical reference when required for auditability.

For example, a screenshot of an access configuration may demonstrate the configuration at a particular point in time but may not establish that the control operated consistently over the preceding six months.

Evidence should therefore be evaluated in relation to the claim being made.

Traceability requires more than attaching documents. Reviewers should be able to determine which requirement, risk, control, or decision each item supports.

### 60. Managing Risk Acceptance and Escalation Thresholds

Organizations should define explicit thresholds for escalating risks and approving acceptance.

The escalation process should reflect residual exposure, applicable obligations, risk appetite, and the authority of the decision-maker.

An illustrative model is:

```
Residual Risk Assessed
          |
          v
Compare With Approved Tolerance
          |
     +----+----+
     |         |
     v         v
Within       Exceeds
Tolerance    Tolerance
     |         |
     v         v
Authorized   Treatment or
Monitoring   Escalation
     |         |
     v         v
Periodic     Authorized
Review       Decision
                |
                v
        Documented Outcome
```

The organization should distinguish between:

- Risks that can be managed within delegated authority.
- Risks that require additional treatment.
- Risks requiring management or committee escalation.
- Risks that cannot be accepted because of mandatory requirements.
- Risks that require suspension or restriction of the affected AI capability.

Acceptance should be supported by a documented rationale, the assessed residual exposure, required approvals, conditions, and a review or expiration date where applicable.

Escalation should occur promptly when exposure exceeds defined thresholds or when material incidents undermine the assumptions supporting an earlier decision.

A previously accepted risk may require renewed approval after a significant change, even if its recorded rating has not yet changed.

The decision-making process should preserve the distinction between an assessment result and the management decision made in response to that result.

### 61. Managing Risk Treatment Dependencies and Overdue Actions

AI risk treatment may depend on activities owned by different teams or suppliers.

For example, reducing the risk of unauthorized retrieval may require changes to the AI application, identity platform, document repository, monitoring system, and supplier configuration.

The treatment plan should identify dependencies that could prevent the intended outcome from being achieved.

An action record should establish:

- The required outcome.
- The responsible action owner.
- The target completion date.
- Dependencies and prerequisites.
- The resources required.
- The evidence needed to demonstrate completion.
- The validation approach.
- The escalation process for delays.

Overdue actions should be evaluated according to their effect on risk rather than managed solely as administrative exceptions.

For example, a delayed documentation improvement may have a different immediate consequence from a delayed control that prevents an AI agent from executing unauthorized financial transactions.

The organization should prioritize overdue actions according to risk severity, exposure, compensating controls, and applicable obligations.

Where a deadline cannot be met, the responsible owner should document the reason, interim safeguards, revised target date, and any required approval.

A revised date does not itself reduce risk. The register should continue to reflect the current exposure until the relevant treatment is implemented and validated.

### 62. Monitoring Residual Risk and Emerging Changes

Residual risk should be monitored after treatment because controls and operating conditions can change.

Monitoring should consider whether the assumptions underlying the risk assessment remain valid.

Relevant indicators may include:

- Changes in model performance.
- Increases in unauthorized access attempts.
- AI-generated outputs rejected by human reviewers.
- Unusual tool invocation patterns.
- Changes in data quality or distribution.
- Increased incident frequency.
- Changes in supplier reliability.
- Control exceptions.
- Overdue reviews or remediation actions.
- Changes in the AI system's permissions or autonomy.

Monitoring arrangements should specify the indicator, data source, accountable owner, review frequency, thresholds, and escalation actions.

For example, an AI system that produces recommendations for customer service representatives may be monitored for error rates, exception rates, and the frequency of human overrides.

An AI agent that can execute actions may require additional monitoring of tool calls, authorization failures, unusual transaction patterns, and attempts to access restricted resources.

Monitoring should be aligned with the actual risk scenario. A general availability metric will not necessarily detect a privacy or authorization failure.

When a threshold is exceeded, the organization should define whether the response requires investigation, reassessment, treatment, restriction, or suspension.

### 63. Applying Risk-Based Review Frequency

The organization should define review frequencies based on risk, system lifecycle, applicable obligations, and the nature of the controls.

A uniform annual review may be insufficient for a high-impact AI system that changes frequently. Conversely, a low-risk, stable system may not require the same review intensity as a critical autonomous application.

An illustrative approach is:

| Risk Profile | Possible Review Approach                                                                                                          |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low          | Periodic review and material-change reassessment                                                                                  |
| Moderate     | Scheduled review with defined change triggers                                                                                     |
| High         | More frequent review, stronger evidence requirements, and active monitoring                                                       |
| Critical     | Enhanced oversight, defined escalation thresholds, and potentially continuous or near-real-time monitoring of relevant indicators |

These categories are illustrative, not mandatory universal intervals.

The organization should establish actual frequencies in its policies and ensure that they meet applicable requirements.

Review frequency should also consider factors beyond the risk rating, including:

- Rate of model or application change.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Sensitivity of the data.
- Scale of deployment.
- Number of affected individuals.
- Criticality of the supported business process.
- Supplier transparency and dependency.
- History of incidents or control deficiencies.

A review should assess whether the inventory remains accurate, the risk scenario remains relevant, controls remain effective, and the recorded treatment decision remains appropriate.

### 64. Managing AI Incidents and Risk Register Updates

An AI incident may reveal that an existing risk was underestimated, an assumed control was ineffective, or an important risk scenario was missing.

Incident management should therefore connect to the inventory and risk register.

For example:

```
AI Incident Detected
         |
         v
Containment and Investigation
         |
         v
Identify Affected Systems and Use Cases
         |
         v
Determine Related Risk Records
         |
         v
Reassess Causes, Impact, and Controls
         |
         v
Create or Update Risk Entries
         |
         v
Track Corrective Actions
         |
         v
Validate and Report
```

The organization should consider whether an incident requires:

- A new risk record.
- An updated likelihood or impact assessment.
- A revised control effectiveness conclusion.
- A new treatment action.
- Reassessment of related systems using the same model or platform.
- Review of a previously accepted risk.
- Escalation to management or relevant governance bodies.
- Updates to monitoring thresholds and incident procedures.

An incident should not automatically result in a new risk record if the relevant scenario is already adequately represented. In that case, the existing record may need updated evidence, ratings, or treatment actions.

However, the incident should be linked to the relevant risk record so that repeated events and trends remain visible.

The objective is to ensure that operational experience improves the organization's risk understanding rather than remaining isolated within incident management.

### 65. Detecting Systemic Risk Through Portfolio Analysis

Portfolio-level analysis can reveal risk patterns that are not apparent when each AI system is reviewed independently.

For example, several AI applications may exhibit the same authorization weakness because they use a shared retrieval component.

Each application may have a separate risk record, but the common dependency may indicate an enterprise-level control problem.

Portfolio analysis can examine:

- Repeated risk categories across business units.
- Common control deficiencies.
- Shared provider dependencies.
- Multiple systems with overdue assessments.
- Concentrations of high residual risk.
- Repeated incidents with similar causes.
- Common treatment actions that remain incomplete.
- AI systems operating without clear ownership.
- Repeated exceptions to the same governance requirement.

A simple analytical sequence is:

```
Aggregate Inventory and Risk Data
              |
              v
       Identify Risk Patterns
              |
              v
      Validate Common Causes
              |
              v
     Determine Systemic Exposure
              |
              v
     Assign Enterprise Ownership
              |
              v
    Coordinate Treatment and Reporting
```

Portfolio analysis should not assume that similar risk labels prove a common underlying cause. The organization should validate whether the systems share relevant architecture, dependencies, control processes, or operating conditions.

Where a common cause is confirmed, centralized remediation may be more effective than addressing each instance independently.

This analysis can also identify opportunities to standardize controls and reduce duplicated assessment effort.

### 66. Integrating AI Inventory and Risk Data With Internal Audit

Internal audit may use inventory and risk register data to understand the AI environment, select audit subjects, and evaluate whether governance activities are operating as intended.

The inventory can help define the population of AI systems and use cases that may be relevant to the audit universe.

The risk register can help identify areas of higher exposure, unresolved treatment actions, systemic weaknesses, and risks that have been accepted by management.

A possible relationship is:

```
AI Inventory
      |
      v
Define AI Audit Universe
      |
      v
Risk-Based Audit Planning
      |
      v
Select Systems, Use Cases, and Controls
      |
      v
Perform Independent Testing
      |
      v

      |
      v

      |

```

The inventory and risk register should support audit planning, but they should not dictate audit conclusions.

Internal audit should independently evaluate the reliability of the underlying information and the effectiveness of the relevant controls.

For example, a system classified as low risk by management may still warrant audit attention if the classification process is unreliable or the system has significant unrecorded dependencies.

Audit findings should be linked to relevant risk records where appropriate. The organization should preserve the distinction between management's risk assessment and internal audit's independent observations.

### 67. Managing Changes to Inventory and Risk Records

Changes to inventory and risk records should be controlled sufficiently to preserve accuracy and historical traceability.

Material changes may affect risk classification, ownership, approval status, control assumptions, or treatment decisions.

Examples include:

- A change in intended purpose.
- A new model version.
- A change in supplier.
- A new data source.
- An expansion of access permissions.
- A change in risk ownership.
- A revised residual risk rating.
- A new acceptance decision.
- Closure of a treatment action.
- Retirement of an AI system.

The organization should record the previous value where necessary, the new value, the date, the person or process making the change, and the rationale for material decisions.

A controlled change record may include:

| Field                | Example                                 |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Record ID            | AI-SYS-001                              |
| Changed Field        | Autonomy Level                          |
| Previous Value       | Human-approved execution                |
| New Value            | Limited automated execution             |
| Change Date          | Recorded in the governance system       |
| Change Rationale     | Approved process automation enhancement |
| Risk Review Required | Yes                                     |
| Approval Reference   | Linked change approval                  |
| Related Risk Record  | AI-R-026                                |

The example illustrates the information that may be required; it does not prescribe a specific date format or approval process.

Changes that affect the system's risk profile should trigger the appropriate reassessment before the updated capability is treated as governed under the previous assessment.

The inventory should remain a reliable record of the actual system, while the change history preserves the evidence needed to understand how that state evolved.

### 68. Ensuring Consistency Between Inventory, Risk, and Control Records

The inventory, risk register, and control library should use consistent terminology and compatible identifiers.

Inconsistent records can create misleading reports and prevent effective traceability.

For example, a system may be recorded as "Customer Support AI" in the inventory, "Support Assistant" in the risk register, and "Customer Bot" in the audit repository. If those records cannot be reliably linked, the organization may overlook relevant risks or duplicate treatment actions.

Consistency can be improved through:

- Standard naming conventions.
- Stable identifiers.
- Approved taxonomies.
- Controlled status values.
- Defined ownership fields.
- Common risk assessment criteria.
- Reference-data validation.
- Automated reconciliation.
- Periodic record quality reviews.

A reconciliation process may identify:

```
Inventory Records Without Risk Links
Risk Records Without Valid System Links
Controls Without Assigned Owners
Findings Without Related Risk Records
Treatment Actions Without Parent Records
Expired Risk Acceptances
Retired Systems With Open Dependencies
```

Not every inventory entry must have a separately documented risk for every conceivable scenario. The organization should define the assessment and linkage requirements applicable to each system and use case.

However, missing relationships should be explainable rather than treated as harmless data inconsistencies.

A reliable governance environment makes it possible to trace material AI risks from the system and use case through controls, findings, decisions, and treatment outcomes.

### 69. Establishing Management Review and Escalation Reporting

Management review should convert inventory and risk register information into decisions about exposure, priorities, resources, and accountability.

Reports should emphasize information that requires management attention rather than presenting an undifferentiated list of every record.

Relevant management topics include:

- High and critical residual risks.
- Risks exceeding approved tolerance.
- Material incidents and emerging threats.
- Systemic control deficiencies.
- Overdue corrective actions.
- Unresolved ownership or accountability issues.
- Supplier and platform concentration.
- Significant changes to the AI portfolio.
- Expiring risk acceptances.
- Resource constraints affecting risk treatment.
- Repeated failures to meet governance requirements.

A management review should record material decisions, assigned actions, responsible owners, and required follow-up.

For example, management may decide to restrict an AI agent's permissions until a critical authorization weakness is corrected and independently validated.

That decision should be reflected in the relevant system status, risk treatment plan, and supporting evidence.

Management review is not a substitute for operational monitoring or technical control testing. It provides an oversight mechanism for decisions that require management authority, cross-functional coordination, or acceptance of defined exposure.

### 70. Part 3 Core Principle

The central principle of AI risk assessment operations is:

> **AI inventory and risk register governance must operate as a continuous, evidence-based process in which material risks are reassessed when conditions change, controls are evaluated rather than merely assumed effective, and decisions remain traceable through monitoring, escalation, treatment, and assurance.**

The operational relationship is:

```
Identify and Assess Risk
          |
          v
Evaluate Control Effectiveness
          |
          v
Determine Residual Exposure
          |
          v
Treat, Accept, or Escalate
          |
          v
Validate Evidence and Outcomes
          |
          v
Monitor Indicators and Changes
          |
          v
Reassess and Report
```

A mature process does not consider a risk permanently resolved merely because a control was implemented, a treatment action was marked complete, or a management approval was recorded.

It requires evidence that the relevant decisions remain appropriate under actual operating conditions.

Consistent assessment methods, reliable evidence, explicit ownership, controlled changes, and effective escalation allow the organization to maintain a defensible understanding of its AI exposure throughout the lifecycle of its systems and use cases.

## Part 4 — Assurance, Implementation, Maturity, and Continuous Improvement

### 71. Establishing an Implementation Roadmap for AI Inventory and Risk Registers

An AI inventory and AI risk register should be implemented through a controlled governance roadmap rather than as isolated documentation exercises. Organizations that attempt to capture every AI-related asset and risk simultaneously may encounter incomplete records, inconsistent classifications, unclear ownership, and resistance from business units.

The implementation roadmap should establish a minimum viable inventory, introduce a repeatable risk assessment process, integrate existing governance records, and progressively strengthen assurance and automation.

A practical implementation sequence is:

| Phase                            | Primary activities                                                                                         | Expected outcome                     |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Phase 1 — Mobilization           | Assign executive sponsorship, define scope, establish governance roles                                     | Approved implementation mandate      |
| Phase 2 — Discovery              | Identify AI systems, providers, use cases, embedded AI capabilities, and shadow AI                         | Initial AI inventory                 |
| Phase 3 — Classification         | Categorize systems by business purpose, deployment model, impact, and risk                                 | Consistently classified AI portfolio |
| Phase 4 — Risk assessment        | Identify risk scenarios, assess inherent risk, evaluate controls, determine residual risk                  | Populated AI risk register           |
| Phase 5 — Treatment              | Assign actions, owners, deadlines, and acceptance authorities                                              | Approved risk treatment plans        |
| Phase 6 — Integration            | Connect inventory and risk records to enterprise risk, security, privacy, procurement, and audit processes | Integrated governance environment    |
| Phase 7 — Assurance              | Test completeness, validate evidence, inspect approvals, and assess control effectiveness                  | Defensible governance records        |
| Phase 8 — Continuous improvement | Monitor changes, incidents, overdue actions, and emerging AI risks                                         | Sustainable governance lifecycle     |

The roadmap should be proportionate to organizational size, AI adoption, regulatory exposure, and risk appetite. A smaller organization may initially use a controlled register and documented review workflow, while a large enterprise may require integrated governance tooling, automated discovery, and portfolio-level reporting.

**Implementation principle:** Establish reliable ownership, identifiers, and minimum data-quality rules before pursuing extensive automation. Automating an inconsistent inventory can increase the speed at which inaccurate governance information is distributed.

### 72. Defining Minimum Viable Inventory and Risk Register Requirements

A minimum viable inventory is the smallest controlled dataset that allows an organization to identify its AI systems, understand their business use, assign accountability, and determine which systems require further assessment.

A minimum viable risk register must similarly capture the risks that matter for governance decisions, including their causes, consequences, ratings, controls, treatment plans, and acceptance status.

The minimum viable inventory should normally include:

- Unique system or use-case identifier.
- AI system or application name.
- Business purpose and accountable business owner.
- Technical owner or responsible service provider.
- AI capability or model category.
- Deployment or service arrangement.
- Data categories and relevant information flows.
- Business process and affected stakeholders.
- Preliminary risk classification.
- Lifecycle status and last review date.

The minimum viable risk register should normally include:

- Unique risk identifier and linked system or use case.
- Risk scenario, including cause, event, and consequence.
- Relevant AI risk category.
- Inherent risk rating and assessment rationale.
- Existing controls and control-effectiveness assessment.
- Residual risk rating and supporting rationale.
- Risk owner and treatment decision.
- Mitigation actions, accountable owners, and due dates.
- Acceptance authority, approval evidence, and review or expiry date where applicable.

These fields are a practical baseline, not a universal mandatory schema. Applicable laws, sector requirements, contractual obligations, and internal policies may require additional attributes.

The organization should distinguish between fields that are mandatory at initial registration and those that become mandatory before deployment or approval. For example, an AI system discovered during procurement may have incomplete technical details. The record should identify those gaps, assign responsibility for resolving them, and prevent missing information from being mistaken for a completed assessment.

| Governance condition                             | Required handling                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| System identified but ownership unknown          | Assign a discovery or remediation owner and escalate unresolved accountability          |
| System registered but risk assessment incomplete | Mark assessment status explicitly and apply applicable deployment restrictions          |
| Risk assessed but controls lack evidence         | Record the evidence gap and avoid assuming that control effectiveness is demonstrated   |
| Residual risk exceeds the acceptance threshold   | Escalate for treatment, redesign, restriction, or authorized acceptance where permitted |
| Risk accepted temporarily                        | Record approval scope, conditions, expiry, and reassessment requirements                |

A minimum viable dataset should not become a permanent excuse for incomplete governance. It should provide a controlled starting point with clear requirements for progressive completion.

### 73. Establishing Inventory and Risk Register Service-Level Expectations

An AI governance process requires defined timelines for registration, assessment, review, and escalation. Without operational expectations, business teams may register AI systems but leave risk records incomplete for extended periods.

Service-level expectations should reflect the risk and lifecycle stage of the AI use case rather than applying identical deadlines to every record.

Examples of measurable expectations include:

| Process                | Example expectation                                                          | Governance purpose                               |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Initial registration   | Register a proposed AI use case before formal deployment approval            | Prevent ungoverned production use                |
| Ownership assignment   | Assign accountable ownership before assessment completion                    | Establish responsibility                         |
| High-risk assessment   | Complete assessment before the relevant authorization decision               | Inform risk-based approval                       |
| Material change review | Reassess before a material change is released, where practicable             | Prevent obsolete assessments                     |
| Incident-driven review | Initiate reassessment according to incident severity and response procedures | Incorporate incident learning                    |
| Risk treatment         | Track actions against approved deadlines                                     | Prevent unresolved risks from becoming invisible |
| Periodic review        | Review according to risk tier and applicable obligations                     | Maintain current records                         |
| Acceptance expiry      | Escalate before temporary acceptance expires                                 | Prevent indefinite exceptions                    |

The specific timelines should be approved by the organization and aligned with its operational model. These examples are not universal regulatory deadlines.

Service-level performance should be measured using more than average completion time. An organization might meet its average assessment target while leaving a small number of critical systems unassessed. Reporting should therefore include overdue counts, severity, age, business impact, and exception status.

For example, a governance dashboard could report that 94% of assessments were completed within the target period while separately identifying three high-impact use cases that exceeded the deadline. The latter information may be more important for executive decisions than the overall percentage.

Service-level breaches should trigger documented handling, which may include escalation, additional resources, temporary restrictions, revised priorities, or an approved exception. A deadline extension should not automatically change the underlying risk rating.

### 74. Validating Inventory Completeness Through Independent Evidence

An inventory's completeness cannot be demonstrated solely by confirming that all entries in the inventory contain the required fields. That procedure tests record quality, not whether all relevant AI systems have been identified.

Completeness assurance requires comparison with independent evidence sources that can reveal missing or undisclosed AI use.

Potential evidence sources include:

- Procurement records for AI-enabled products and services.
- Software-as-a-service and cloud-service inventories.
- Identity and access management records.
- Cloud marketplace subscriptions and AI service configurations.
- Software asset management and application portfolios.
- Vendor assessments and third-party risk records.
- Data protection impact assessments and privacy reviews.
- Security architecture reviews and change-management records.
- AI development repositories and model registries.
- Business unit attestations and employee reporting channels.
- Security monitoring, incident reports, and approved AI usage telemetry.

The relevance of each source depends on the organization's architecture, contractual environment, and technical visibility.

A completeness validation procedure may follow this sequence:

1. Define the population of systems, suppliers, applications, and business processes expected to be within scope.
2. Obtain relevant records from independent operational and governance sources.
3. Match those records against the AI inventory using identifiers, supplier names, application names, and other reliable attributes.
4. Investigate unmatched records to determine whether they represent AI systems, duplicate records, false positives, or out-of-scope items.
5. Register newly identified AI systems and initiate the required classification and risk assessment.
6. Document the population tested, matching method, exceptions, and remediation decisions.
7. Repeat the procedure at an approved frequency and following material organizational or technology changes.

**Illustrative completeness test:**

Suppose a procurement review identifies 120 products that may contain AI capabilities. Of these, 85 match existing inventory records, 20 are confirmed not to use AI within the organization's licensed configuration, and 15 remain unresolved.

The organization should not report complete coverage merely because 85 records matched. The 15 unresolved products require investigation, and the 20 exclusions should have sufficient documented rationale to support their disposition.

Completeness should be reported with a defined denominator and clear treatment of unresolved records. An organization must not inflate its coverage metric by excluding unknown or unverified systems without justification.

### 75. Validating Risk Register Accuracy and Internal Consistency

Risk register assurance must establish whether recorded assessments are logically consistent, evidence-based, and aligned with the organization's approved risk methodology.

A technically complete risk entry may still be unreliable if its ratings conflict with the described scenario, controls are incorrectly mapped, or acceptance authority is insufficient.

Validation should cover at least five dimensions:

| Validation dimension     | Questions to test                                                                                         |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scenario quality         | Does the risk statement describe a plausible cause, event, and consequence?                               |
| Rating consistency       | Does the assigned rating follow the approved likelihood and impact criteria?                              |
| Control validity         | Do the listed controls address the identified scenario, and is their effectiveness supported by evidence? |
| Residual risk logic      | Is the residual rating justified by the controls and remaining exposure?                                  |
| Governance authorization | Is the treatment or acceptance decision approved by an authorized role?                                   |

Additional validation should identify duplicate risks, contradictory statuses, missing treatment owners, expired acceptance decisions, overdue actions, and records that reference retired systems.

Automated rules can detect many structural defects. For example:

- A high residual risk cannot be marked as accepted without the required approval reference.
- A risk marked as mitigated cannot retain an unresolved treatment action that is essential to the mitigation claim.
- A closed risk cannot remain linked to an active treatment plan without an explicit explanation.
- An expired risk acceptance cannot continue to display an unqualified valid status.
- A material change to an AI system should trigger the applicable reassessment workflow.

Automated validation cannot independently establish that a risk scenario is realistic or that a control operates effectively. Those judgments require appropriate evidence and qualified review.

For material risks, an independent reviewer should challenge the rationale, examine the supporting evidence, and confirm that the residual risk decision follows the approved methodology.

### 76. Designing an Audit Trail for Inventory and Risk Register Decisions

An audit trail enables an organization to reconstruct how an AI system was registered, classified, assessed, approved, changed, and ultimately retired.

The objective is not simply to preserve the latest version of a record. It is to retain sufficient historical information to explain significant governance decisions and demonstrate who performed or authorized them.

An effective audit trail should capture:

- Record creation, modification, and retirement events.
- Previous and updated values for material fields.
- User or service identity responsible for each change.
- Date and time of the event.
- Reason for the change where required.
- Assessment methodology and version used.
- Evidence references and assessment rationale.
- Risk treatment and acceptance approvals.
- Reassessment triggers and resulting decisions.
- Record closure or archival rationale.

For example, an AI use case may initially be classified as moderate risk because it supports internal knowledge retrieval. Six months later, the same use case may be connected to a customer-facing workflow and permitted to influence eligibility decisions. The historical record should demonstrate what changed, who evaluated the change, how the risk classification was revised, and which approval conditions were imposed.

A controlled history supports incident investigations, internal audit, regulatory inquiries, management reviews, and retrospective analysis.

Access to audit records should follow appropriate authorization rules. Where records contain confidential architecture information, personal data, or commercially sensitive supplier details, the organization should protect those elements without undermining the traceability required for assurance.

Retention periods should be determined according to applicable legal obligations, contractual requirements, internal retention policies, and legitimate governance needs. The organization should avoid both premature deletion of essential evidence and indiscriminate retention without a defined purpose.

### 77. Applying Three Lines and Independent Challenge to AI Risk Records

AI inventory administration and risk assessment should have clear accountability, with independent challenge proportionate to the risk.

The Three Lines Model can help clarify responsibilities, although organizations may adapt the arrangement to their size and operating structure.

| Function                                                        | Typical responsibility                                                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First line — Business and technology owners                     | Identify systems, provide accurate information, operate controls, assess and manage risks within delegated authority   |
| Second line — Risk, compliance, privacy, and security oversight | Define methodologies, establish requirements, challenge assessments, monitor exceptions, and report aggregate exposure |
| Third line — Internal audit                                     | Independently evaluate the design and effectiveness of governance, risk management, and control processes              |

The model does not require every organization to establish three separate departments. Smaller organizations may combine certain responsibilities, but they should identify conflicts of interest and introduce compensating review arrangements.

For example, the person responsible for deploying an AI system may provide essential technical evidence but should not automatically have unrestricted authority to approve every material residual risk associated with that deployment.

Independent challenge should focus on the quality of the decision rather than the volume of documentation. Reviewers should test whether the organization has identified relevant scenarios, considered affected stakeholders, evaluated controls realistically, and applied its acceptance criteria consistently.

Internal audit should not assume management's responsibility for maintaining the inventory or deciding how operational risks are treated. Its role is to provide independent assurance on the effectiveness of the governance arrangements.

The organization should also define how disagreements are resolved. Where the business owner and risk oversight function disagree on a material assessment, the issue should be escalated to the designated decision authority, with the competing rationales and supporting evidence retained.

### 78. Using Sampling and Thematic Reviews to Test Portfolio Governance

Reviewing every AI inventory record in depth may be impractical in a large portfolio. A risk-based assurance approach can combine automated validation, targeted testing, and thematic reviews.

Sampling should be designed around the assurance objective. A sample intended to test mandatory field completion differs from a sample intended to evaluate the quality of residual risk decisions.

Useful sampling dimensions include:

- Risk classification and potential impact.
- Business function and deployment context.
- AI model or service provider.
- Geographic jurisdiction and regulatory exposure.
- Lifecycle stage and recent material changes.
- Presence of accepted or overdue residual risks.
- Previous incidents or audit findings.
- Use of sensitive data or consequential decision support.

The organization may apply stratified sampling to ensure that high-impact systems receive appropriate coverage while lower-risk records are assessed proportionately.

A thematic review can examine one issue across multiple systems. Examples include whether human oversight is consistently assessed, whether third-party AI dependencies are documented, or whether residual risks are routinely understated after controls are implemented.

Thematic reviews are valuable because similar weaknesses may exist across different business units even when each individual record appears complete.

**Illustrative test:**

An organization reviews 30 AI use cases from different business functions. It discovers that 12 risk assessments identify human oversight as a control, but only four provide evidence of operating effectiveness.

The finding is not merely a set of eight incomplete records. It may indicate a systemic weakness in how the organization defines, evidences, and validates human oversight.

The resulting remediation should therefore address both the individual records and the underlying assessment procedure, control guidance, training, or evidence requirements.

Sampling limitations must be documented. A sample can provide evidence about the records examined, but the organization should not claim that every record has been validated unless the testing actually supports that conclusion.

### 79. Defining AI Inventory and Risk Register Key Performance Indicators

Key performance indicators (KPIs) measure operational performance, while key risk indicators (KRIs) provide signals about changing exposure or deteriorating governance conditions. The distinction is useful when designing an AI governance dashboard.

A high number of completed assessments may indicate strong process throughput, but it does not prove that the assessments are accurate or that residual risks are acceptable.

A balanced measurement framework should include coverage, data quality, assessment timeliness, treatment effectiveness, and risk exposure.

| Metric                                  | Type             | Example interpretation                                                                |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Inventory coverage                      | KPI/KRI          | Proportion of the defined in-scope AI population represented in the inventory         |
| Ownership completeness                  | KPI              | Proportion of records with accountable business and technical ownership assigned      |
| Assessment completion                   | KPI              | Proportion of in-scope systems with current, required risk assessments                |
| Overdue high-risk assessments           | KRI              | Number and severity of high-risk systems whose required assessments are overdue       |
| Risk treatment overdue rate             | KRI              | Proportion of open treatment actions that have passed their approved deadlines        |
| Expired acceptance count                | KRI              | Number of risk acceptances beyond their approved validity period                      |
| Evidence sufficiency                    | Assurance metric | Proportion of sampled assessments with evidence sufficient to support key conclusions |
| Material change reassessment compliance | KPI/KRI          | Proportion of identified material changes that received the required reassessment     |
| Reopened risk rate                      | KRI              | Frequency with which previously closed risks recur or prove inadequately treated      |
| Unresolved discovery exceptions         | KRI              | Number and age of potentially in-scope AI systems not yet dispositioned               |

Every metric should have a documented definition, data source, owner, calculation method, reporting frequency, and threshold or escalation rule where applicable.

For example, inventory coverage should define whether its denominator includes only confirmed AI systems or also unresolved candidates. If unresolved candidates are omitted, the reported percentage may appear stronger than the underlying evidence justifies.

Metrics should also be segmented by risk tier, business unit, deployment environment, and other relevant dimensions. Aggregated portfolio figures can conceal concentrated exposure.

The purpose of measurement is to support governance decisions. A dashboard that shows a declining overdue-action rate is useful, but decision-makers also need to know whether high-severity actions remain overdue and whether the underlying risks are increasing or decreasing.

### 80. Establishing an AI Inventory and Risk Register Maturity Model

A maturity model helps an organization evaluate the development of its inventory and risk register capabilities over time. It should assess whether governance practices are defined, consistently performed, evidenced, and improved.

A practical five-level model is:

| Level   | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                                                                           |
| ------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Ad hoc     | AI systems are identified inconsistently; risk decisions rely heavily on individuals and informal records                                 |
| Level 2 | Repeatable | Basic registers, named owners, and initial assessment procedures exist, but coverage and consistency remain limited                       |
| Level 3 | Defined    | Approved data models, taxonomies, methodologies, workflows, and review requirements are applied across the organization                   |
| Level 4 | Measured   | Data quality, coverage, treatment performance, and residual risk are monitored through defined metrics and assurance activities           |
| Level 5 | Adaptive   | Inventory and risk processes incorporate automated discovery, systematic lessons learned, portfolio analytics, and continuous improvement |

Maturity should be evaluated across distinct capability dimensions rather than assigned solely on the basis of tool sophistication.

Relevant dimensions include:

1. Inventory coverage and discovery.
2. Data model and record quality.
3. Risk identification and assessment consistency.
4. Ownership and decision rights.
5. Control mapping and evidence quality.
6. Treatment and risk acceptance.
7. Lifecycle change management.
8. Reporting and escalation.
9. Integration with enterprise governance.
10. Independent assurance and continuous improvement.

An organization may have an advanced governance platform but remain immature in risk assessment if business owners enter inconsistent ratings and controls are not independently validated.

Conversely, a smaller organization may demonstrate strong governance maturity using a simpler technical solution if its records are complete, decisions are traceable, responsibilities are clear, and assurance is effective.

The maturity model should therefore evaluate demonstrated capability and outcomes, not technology expenditure or the number of documented procedures.

### 81. Mapping AI Inventory and Risk Register Practices to Governance Frameworks

AI inventory and risk register practices can support several governance frameworks, but the relationship should be established through explicit mapping rather than assumed from the existence of a register.

Examples of relevant frameworks and governance instruments include:

| Framework or instrument           | Relevant relationship                                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ISO/IEC 42001                     | AI management system planning, risk-related processes, operational governance, performance evaluation, and continual improvement |
| ISO/IEC 23894                     | AI risk management concepts and processes                                                                                        |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework | Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage activities                                                                                      |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework      | Cybersecurity asset context, risk assessment, protection, detection, response, and recovery                                      |
| ISO/IEC 27001                     | Information security risk management, control governance, and management-system evidence                                         |
| Enterprise risk management        | Risk ownership, appetite, escalation, aggregation, and management reporting                                                      |
| Privacy governance                | Processing context, personal-data risks, impact assessments, and applicable privacy obligations                                  |
| Third-party risk management       | Supplier relationships, contractual obligations, external dependencies, and ongoing oversight                                    |
| Internal audit methodology        | Evidence traceability, control testing, finding management, and independent assurance                                            |

These relationships do not mean that every framework mandates an identically structured AI inventory or risk register. Specific requirements depend on the applicable framework edition, organizational scope, implementation choices, and relevant legal or contractual obligations.

A mapping exercise should identify:

- The relevant requirement or governance objective.
- The inventory or risk register fields that provide supporting information.
- The related policy, control, or procedure.
- The evidence needed to demonstrate implementation.
- The accountable control or process owner.
- Any gaps requiring additional governance artifacts.

For example, an inventory record may identify an AI system, its business purpose, and its data dependencies. A linked risk assessment may document the relevant failure scenarios, while a control record identifies safeguards and their evidence. Together, these artifacts can support governance assurance, but the inventory alone does not demonstrate that all management-system requirements have been fulfilled.

The organization should maintain a controlled mapping and update it when applicable framework editions, internal requirements, or regulatory obligations change.

### 82. Managing Regulatory and Contractual Evidence Requirements

AI inventory and risk register records may be used to support regulatory inquiries, contractual reviews, customer assurance requests, and internal compliance assessments. Their usefulness depends on whether the records accurately represent the system and preserve the evidence needed to substantiate decisions.

The organization should determine which evidence obligations apply to each AI use case. These may depend on the nature of the system, intended purpose, affected individuals, data processing, sector, jurisdiction, contractual commitments, and deployment arrangement.

A controlled evidence model should connect each relevant obligation to the records and documents that support it.

For example:

```
AI System / Use Case
        |
        +-- Classification and Scope Decision
        |
        +-- Applicable Requirements
        |
        +-- AI Risk Assessment
        |
        +-- Controls and Evidence
        |
        +-- Treatment and Approval Decisions
        |
        +-- Monitoring and Review History
        |
        +-- Audit and Assurance Results
```

The evidence model should identify the authoritative location of each artifact, the responsible owner, its version or effective date, and applicable access and retention controls.

Where an external party requests evidence, the organization should verify the requester's authority, confirm the scope of disclosure, and apply appropriate confidentiality and data-protection safeguards.

A register entry that states that a control exists is not equivalent to evidence that the control is implemented or effective. The organization should distinguish between policy references, design documentation, implementation records, operating evidence, and independent test results.

Regulatory classification and applicability decisions should also be documented with their rationale. If applicability is uncertain, the record should identify the uncertainty and the role responsible for obtaining a determination rather than presenting an unsupported conclusion as established fact.

### 83. Managing Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability of Governance Records

AI inventory and risk register information can itself become a sensitive governance asset. It may reveal business processes, security weaknesses, system architecture, supplier dependencies, model capabilities, personal-data use, or accepted residual risks.

The governance repository should therefore be protected according to the sensitivity and operational importance of the information it contains.

**Confidentiality controls** should include role-based access, least privilege, controlled sharing, and appropriate protection of sensitive fields. Business users may need access to their own use cases without requiring unrestricted access to every system's architecture or risk acceptance details.

**Integrity controls** should prevent unauthorized modification, preserve version history, and support review of significant changes. Approved risk ratings and acceptance decisions should not be silently overwritten.

**Availability controls** should ensure that authorized stakeholders can retrieve current records during assessments, incidents, audits, and management decisions. Backup, recovery, and continuity arrangements should reflect the importance of the repository.

Additional safeguards may include:

- Strong authentication and privileged-access controls.
- Segregation of record administration and risk acceptance.
- Encryption in transit and at rest where appropriate.
- Logging of sensitive access and modification events.
- Tested backup and restoration procedures.
- Defined recovery objectives for critical governance services.
- Retention and secure disposal procedures.
- Restrictions on exporting sensitive records to unauthorized AI tools.

The organization should also assess the risks introduced by the governance platform itself. If an external SaaS repository hosts sensitive architecture information or risk evidence, the service provider, data location, access model, and contractual protections may require assessment.

Security controls should be proportionate to the sensitivity of the records. Excessively broad access increases disclosure risk, while overly restrictive access can prevent business owners, risk functions, and auditors from performing their responsibilities.

### 84. Managing AI Inventory and Risk Register Platform Selection

Organizations may manage AI inventories and risk registers using spreadsheets, governance-risk-compliance platforms, enterprise asset management systems, model registries, or specialized AI governance tools.

No single platform is universally appropriate. Selection should be based on the organization's governance requirements, portfolio complexity, integration needs, security constraints, and ability to maintain reliable data.

| Capability              | Questions to evaluate                                                                                           |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Data model              | Can the platform distinguish AI systems, use cases, models, suppliers, risks, controls, and decisions?          |
| Relationship management | Can records be linked without duplicating authoritative information?                                            |
| Workflow                | Can it enforce registration, assessment, review, escalation, and approval processes?                            |
| Evidence management     | Can it reference or retain controlled evidence with appropriate permissions and version history?                |
| Access control          | Can it enforce least privilege and role-based authorization?                                                    |
| Reporting               | Can it produce portfolio views, overdue-action reports, and risk concentration analysis?                        |
| Integration             | Can it exchange data with procurement, security, privacy, cloud, asset, and enterprise risk systems?            |
| Auditability            | Can it preserve historical changes and approval evidence?                                                       |
| Scalability             | Can it support anticipated growth in systems, records, users, and review activity?                              |
| Portability             | Can records and evidence references be exported in a usable form without unreasonable dependency on one vendor? |

A spreadsheet may be adequate for a small, stable portfolio if ownership, access, version control, validation, and review procedures are reliable. It becomes less suitable when many users update records concurrently, complex approval workflows are required, or record relationships and auditability become difficult to maintain.

A dedicated platform can improve workflow enforcement and integration, but it cannot compensate for an unclear taxonomy, weak accountability, poor risk methodology, or inadequate evidence.

A proof of concept should test representative scenarios before procurement. These should include registering a new AI system, linking multiple use cases to one shared model, reassessing a material change, escalating a high residual risk, expiring a risk acceptance, and producing an audit-ready history.

Platform selection should be treated as a governance design decision, not merely a software purchasing exercise.

### 85. Using Automation Without Weakening Governance Accountability

Automation can reduce administrative effort and improve the timeliness of inventory updates. However, automated discovery, classification, risk scoring, and record synchronization must operate within defined governance boundaries.

Potential automation use cases include:

- Discovering AI-related services from approved cloud and procurement data.
- Identifying products that advertise or enable AI capabilities.
- Detecting changes to registered model endpoints or AI configurations.
- Creating reassessment tasks when defined triggers occur.
- Validating mandatory fields and record relationships.
- Flagging expired approvals and overdue treatment actions.
- Identifying duplicate or inconsistent records.
- Producing portfolio reports and evidence-request packages.

Automation should distinguish between detection and confirmation. A cloud resource with an AI-related label may require investigation before it is classified as an in-scope AI system. Similarly, a vendor's marketing description may not accurately represent the functionality enabled in the organization's environment.

Automated risk scoring should not be accepted without a documented methodology, validation, appropriate human oversight, and a mechanism for challenging incorrect results.

A controlled automation pattern is:

```
Operational Data Sources
          |
          v
Discovery or Change Detection
          |
          v
Validation and Record Matching
          |
          v
Human Review Where Required
          |
          v
Inventory / Risk Workflow Update
          |
          v
Audit Trail and Reporting
```

The organization should establish permissions for automated updates. Low-risk metadata changes may be suitable for automatic synchronization, while changes to risk classification, acceptance status, or approval authority may require human authorization.

Automation should also be monitored for missed detections, false positives, stale integrations, and incorrect record matching. If the discovery process fails, the organization should know which records may be affected and how the gap will be addressed.

The objective is not to eliminate human responsibility. It is to ensure that automation improves the reliability, timeliness, and scalability of governance decisions without obscuring accountability.

### 86. Integrating AI Inventory With Model, Data, and Technology Registries

An AI inventory should not become an isolated repository that duplicates all technical information held by existing enterprise systems.

Many organizations already maintain application inventories, cloud asset registers, data catalogs, model registries, software repositories, supplier records, and security configuration databases. The AI inventory should establish governed relationships with these sources wherever practical.

The key is to distinguish the authoritative source for each type of information.

| Information type     | Potential authoritative source                    | AI governance relationship                                                       |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business use case    | Business application or process catalog           | Establishes purpose, business ownership, and affected processes                  |
| AI model metadata    | Model registry or approved provider documentation | Identifies model version, lineage, and relevant technical characteristics        |
| Cloud deployment     | Cloud asset or configuration inventory            | Identifies hosting environment and deployment dependencies                       |
| Data assets          | Enterprise data catalog                           | Identifies relevant datasets, classifications, and lineage                       |
| Supplier information | Procurement or third-party risk platform          | Links provider relationships and contractual obligations                         |
| Security controls    | Security governance or control repository         | Connects safeguards, implementation evidence, and control owners                 |
| Privacy assessments  | Privacy management platform                       | Links processing purposes, personal-data risks, and impact assessments           |
| AI risk decisions    | AI risk register                                  | Maintains scenario-specific ratings, treatment decisions, and acceptance records |

The integration architecture should define identifiers, synchronization rules, update frequency, error handling, and ownership of conflicting information.

For example, if a model registry records a new model version, the AI governance process may need to determine whether the change is material to the use case, risk assessment, or approval conditions. A new model version should not automatically be treated as either harmless or unacceptable without evaluating the relevant change criteria.

Similarly, one model may support several business use cases with different risk profiles. The inventory should preserve their distinct purposes and exposure rather than assigning a single portfolio-wide risk rating to the shared model.

The principle is to maintain a connected governance view while avoiding uncontrolled duplication of source data.

### 87. Handling Shared Models, Common Controls, and Portfolio-Level Risks

A single AI model or provider may support multiple systems and business processes. A vulnerability, provider outage, model limitation, or shared control failure can therefore create risk across several use cases simultaneously.

The inventory should represent shared dependencies, while the risk register should distinguish between risks affecting the common component and risks arising in individual use cases.

Consider an organization that uses the same third-party language model for:

- Internal employee knowledge retrieval.
- Customer-service response drafting.
- Contract summarization.
- Automated categorization of support requests.

A provider-level weakness, such as inadequate protection against a class of prompt-injection attacks, may affect multiple applications. However, the consequences will differ according to each application's permissions, data access, human review, and operational role.

A useful relationship model is:

```
Shared AI Model
      |
      +-- Use Case A: Internal Knowledge Retrieval
      |       +-- Use-Case Risk A
      |
      +-- Use Case B: Customer-Service Drafting
      |       +-- Use-Case Risk B
      |
      +-- Use Case C: Contract Summarization
      |       +-- Use-Case Risk C
      |
      +-- Common Provider / Model Risk
              +-- Shared Controls
              +-- Common Treatment Actions
```

The organization should avoid two opposite mistakes.

First, duplicating an identical common risk in every use-case record can produce inflated counts and inconsistent treatment decisions. Second, recording the risk only at the provider level can conceal the different consequences and control arrangements of individual deployments.

A robust model links shared risks to affected systems and use cases, while preserving scenario-specific assessments where necessary.

Portfolio reporting should distinguish the number of risk records from the number of independent risk scenarios. It should also identify correlated exposures, common control dependencies, and the potential impact of a single failure across multiple business functions.

This approach improves prioritization because remediation of one shared dependency may reduce exposure across many use cases, although each affected use case may still require separate validation.

### 88. Measuring the Effectiveness of Risk Treatment

A risk treatment action should not be considered effective merely because its assigned task has been marked complete.

Completion confirms that an activity was reported as performed. Effectiveness requires evidence that the action achieved its intended control or risk-reduction objective.

A treatment validation process should establish:

1. The original risk scenario and baseline exposure.
2. The control or remediation objective.
3. The implementation evidence.
4. The method used to test whether the action works.
5. Any remaining limitations or dependencies.
6. The revised control-effectiveness assessment.
7. The resulting residual risk rating.
8. The approval required to close the treatment action or accept remaining exposure.

For example, a treatment action may require adding human approval before an AI-generated recommendation is executed. Evidence that the approval screen was deployed does not establish that meaningful review occurs. Validation may also need to examine reviewer competence, information presented, override capability, workflow bypasses, and actual operating records.

The risk owner should reassess the risk after material remediation and document whether the expected reduction occurred.

| Treatment result                                   | Appropriate handling                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Objective achieved and evidence sufficient         | Validate the revised assessment and close the action through the approved workflow    |
| Implementation complete but effectiveness unproven | Keep validation open and avoid claiming full risk reduction                           |
| Partial improvement                                | Document the remaining exposure and determine whether further treatment is necessary  |
| Objective not achieved                             | Reopen or extend remediation, reassess exposure, and escalate as required             |
| New risks introduced by the treatment              | Record and assess the new scenarios before declaring the overall treatment successful |

The organization should preserve the relationship between the original risk, the treatment decision, the implemented control, and the revised residual risk.

Treatment effectiveness is especially important for high-impact AI systems, where an apparently completed action may not sufficiently reduce the potential for harm.

### 89. Integrating Lessons Learned From AI Incidents and Near Misses

AI incidents and near misses provide evidence about whether the inventory, risk register, and associated controls reflect real operational conditions.

An incident may reveal an unregistered AI service, an incorrect use-case classification, an overlooked data dependency, an ineffective human review control, or an unrealistic assumption in a previous risk assessment.

Incident management should therefore include defined triggers for reviewing relevant inventory and risk records.

The review should ask:

- Was the affected AI system correctly registered?
- Did the inventory accurately describe its purpose, deployment, data, and dependencies?
- Was the incident scenario identified in the existing risk assessment?
- Were the relevant controls documented and operating?
- Did the residual risk rating reflect the actual exposure?
- Were previous warnings, near misses, or treatment actions overlooked?
- Should similar systems or use cases be reviewed for the same weakness?
- Are existing acceptance decisions still defensible?
- What changes are required to policy, methodology, training, monitoring, or controls?

A near miss can be particularly valuable because it may reveal a control weakness without producing the full adverse consequence.

For example, an AI assistant may attempt to retrieve restricted information but be prevented from doing so by an access-control mechanism. The absence of a confirmed disclosure does not necessarily mean that the system's risk assessment remains unchanged. The event may demonstrate that the attack path is plausible and that the organization should reassess access boundaries, monitoring, and misuse scenarios.

Lessons learned should be recorded as actionable improvements rather than generic statements. Each material action should have an owner, a due date, a completion criterion, and evidence of effectiveness where appropriate.

The organization should also examine whether the same failure mechanism could affect other AI systems. This portfolio-level review prevents the incident response from addressing only the immediate system while leaving a common weakness unresolved elsewhere.

### 90. Establishing Continuous Improvement and Periodic Governance Review

AI inventory and risk register governance must evolve as AI capabilities, business uses, providers, regulations, and operational conditions change.

Continuous improvement should be based on evidence from several sources, including inventory validation, risk assessments, control testing, incident investigations, internal audit, management reviews, user feedback, and changes in the external environment.

A structured improvement cycle can be expressed as:

```
Define Governance Objectives
          |
          v
Maintain Inventory and Risk Records
          |
          v
Assess Risk and Operate Controls
          |
          v
Measure Performance and Test Evidence
          |
          v
Identify Gaps and Recurring Weaknesses
          |
          v
Approve Improvement Actions
          |
          v
Validate Outcomes
          |
          +----------> Refresh Objectives and Methods
```

Periodic review should examine whether:

- The inventory scope remains appropriate.
- Discovery mechanisms identify AI use consistently.
- The data model supports current governance needs.
- Risk ratings remain consistent with the approved methodology.
- Risk owners and acceptance authorities are correctly assigned.
- Treatment actions achieve their intended outcomes.
- Material changes trigger reassessment.
- Metrics accurately represent portfolio conditions.
- Governance integrations remain reliable.
- Framework mappings and compliance assumptions remain current.
- Repeated findings are being addressed at their underlying cause.

The review should distinguish between isolated administrative defects and systemic governance weaknesses. An isolated missing field may require record correction, while repeated ownership gaps may require changes to accountability, procurement controls, or deployment approval workflows.

Improvement actions should be prioritized according to their effect on risk, compliance, decision quality, and operational reliability. The organization should not prioritize cosmetic completeness over the remediation of material risk exposure.

The results of periodic review should be documented, assigned to accountable owners, and presented to the appropriate governance body. Where improvement actions fail to produce the expected result, the organization should revisit its assumptions and revise the approach.

### 91. Common Failure Modes in AI Inventory and Risk Register Governance

Organizations can establish formal registers and still fail to achieve effective governance. The most common failure modes involve a gap between documentation and operational reality.

| Failure mode                                          | Why it is problematic                                               | Corrective governance response                                          |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Inventory limited to centrally developed models       | Misses embedded AI, third-party services, and business-led adoption | Expand discovery criteria and validate against independent sources      |
| One record per vendor with no use-case detail         | Conceals different purposes, impacts, and control arrangements      | Link providers and models to distinct business use cases                |
| Risk ratings assigned without rationale               | Prevents consistent review and meaningful challenge                 | Require scenario-based justification and approved rating criteria       |
| Controls treated as automatically effective           | Confuses control existence with demonstrated performance            | Require appropriate implementation and operating evidence               |
| Residual risk lowered after a task is marked complete | Overstates the effect of remediation                                | Validate treatment outcomes before changing the assessment              |
| Temporary acceptance left open indefinitely           | Allows exceptions to become permanent without review                | Enforce expiry, escalation, and reassessment controls                   |
| Changes are not connected to reassessment             | Causes risk records to become obsolete                              | Define material-change triggers and track reassessment completion       |
| Duplicate risk records inflate exposure counts        | Distorts reporting and can fragment ownership                       | Use scenario identifiers, relationships, and duplicate-resolution rules |
| Portfolio averages conceal critical exceptions        | Creates false confidence in aggregate performance                   | Report high-severity exceptions and overdue decisions separately        |
| Tool procurement precedes governance design           | Automates inconsistent practices and creates rework                 | Define the operating model, data model, and control requirements first  |
| Governance records are inaccessible during audits     | Weakens evidence retrieval and decision traceability                | Establish evidence ownership, access controls, and retrieval testing    |
| Inventory is treated as a one-time project            | Fails to reflect changes in AI deployment and use                   | Establish recurring discovery, review, and change-triggered updates     |

These failure modes should inform control design, assurance testing, and maturity assessments.

The organization should investigate why the failure occurred rather than simply correcting the visible record. A missing risk owner may result from an isolated oversight, but widespread missing ownership may indicate that the governance model does not define accountability adequately.

### 92. Worked Example — From AI Discovery to Residual Risk Decision

Consider an organization introducing an AI assistant that retrieves internal documents and generates answers for employees. The assistant uses a third-party model and connects to enterprise document repositories.

The organization should treat registration, assessment, control validation, and approval as connected activities.

**Step 1 — Inventory registration**

The system receives a unique identifier. The inventory records the business purpose, accountable business owner, technical owner, provider, deployment model, relevant data categories, document repositories, and lifecycle status.

**Step 2 — Use-case definition**

The organization distinguishes the employee knowledge-retrieval use case from other potential uses of the same model, such as customer-facing responses or automated personnel decisions.

**Step 3 — Risk scenario identification**

A risk scenario is recorded:

*Cause:* Inadequate authorization enforcement or retrieval filtering.

*Event:* The assistant retrieves and discloses information to an employee who is not authorized to access it.

*Consequence:* Confidential information is exposed, potentially causing legal, contractual, operational, or reputational harm.

**Step 4 — Inherent risk assessment**

The assessor evaluates the potential impact and likelihood before relying on the effectiveness of the proposed controls. The assessment rationale identifies the sensitivity of the information, the number of users, the potential scale of disclosure, and the plausibility of unauthorized retrieval.

**Step 5 — Control evaluation**

Relevant controls may include source-system access enforcement, identity-based authorization, retrieval filtering, testing for unauthorized information access, output monitoring, incident response, and appropriate human oversight.

The assessor evaluates whether these controls are implemented and whether evidence demonstrates that they work under relevant conditions.

**Step 6 — Residual risk determination**

The assessor evaluates the remaining likelihood and impact after considering validated controls. The residual rating is recorded separately from the inherent rating, with the rationale and any remaining limitations documented.

**Step 7 — Treatment decision**

If residual risk exceeds the organization's acceptance threshold, the risk owner initiates additional treatment, restricts the use case, or escalates the decision. Acceptance is permitted only through the organization's authorized process and where applicable requirements allow it.

**Step 8 — Approval and monitoring**

The organization retains the relevant assessment, control evidence, approval decision, and monitoring requirements. A material change to document access, model behavior, deployment scope, or the user population triggers reassessment according to the approved criteria.

**Step 9 — Assurance**

An independent reviewer checks that the system is represented accurately, the risk scenario is sufficiently specific, the control evidence is credible, and the residual risk decision follows the approved methodology.

**Step 10 — Continuous improvement**

Operational incidents, testing results, user feedback, and changes in the system are used to update the inventory, reassess risk, and improve the control environment.

This example demonstrates that an AI inventory is not merely a catalog and a risk register is not merely a list of concerns. Together, they provide a traceable structure for understanding AI use, evaluating exposure, assigning responsibility, and supporting governance decisions.

### 93. Practical Assurance Checklist for AI Inventory and Risk Registers

The following checklist can be used by governance teams, control owners, and assurance reviewers to assess whether inventory and risk register practices are sufficiently established.

**Inventory governance**

- The organization has approved a defined AI inventory scope.
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria cover relevant internal, third-party, and embedded AI capabilities.
- AI systems and business use cases have unique identifiers and appropriate relationships.
- Business and technical ownership are documented.
- Relevant providers, models, data categories, and dependencies are recorded.
- Lifecycle status and material changes are tracked.
- Completeness is tested against independent evidence sources.
- Missing information and unresolved discovery exceptions are assigned and monitored.

**Risk register governance**

- Risks are documented as specific, plausible scenarios.
- Risk categories and rating criteria are standardized.
- Inherent risk, control effectiveness, and residual risk are distinguished.
- Control claims are supported by appropriate evidence.
- Each material risk has an accountable owner.
- Treatment actions have owners, deadlines, and completion criteria.
- Acceptance decisions are approved by authorized roles.
- Temporary acceptances have review or expiry controls.
- Material changes and incidents trigger appropriate reassessment.
- Risk closure is supported by documented rationale.

**Assurance and continuous improvement**

- Historical changes and approvals are traceable.
- Data-quality rules and validation controls are operating.
- KPIs and KRIs have defined calculations and owners.
- Portfolio reporting highlights severe and overdue exposures.
- Independent review is proportionate to risk.
- Findings are analyzed for systemic causes.
- Remediation effectiveness is validated.
- Relevant governance framework mappings are maintained.
- Repository access, integrity, availability, and retention are controlled.
- Periodic reviews produce documented improvement actions.

The checklist is a practical assurance aid rather than a certification instrument. The organization should tailor it to its applicable obligations, risk appetite, operating model, and AI portfolio.

### 94. Final Governance Principle — Maintain a Defensible, Connected, and Living Record of AI Risk

An effective AI inventory and AI risk register form a connected governance capability that allows an organization to understand where AI is used, why it is used, who is accountable, what can go wrong, which controls address the exposure, and how remaining risk is managed.

The inventory establishes the governed population of AI systems and use cases. The risk register translates that population into explicit risk scenarios, assessment decisions, control relationships, treatment plans, and acceptance records. Evidence, workflow controls, independent challenge, and continuous monitoring make those records reliable enough to support management decisions and assurance.

The capability should be evaluated by the quality of the decisions it enables, not by the number of records created or the sophistication of the software used.

A mature approach demonstrates that:

- AI systems and use cases can be identified and traced to accountable owners.
- Risk scenarios reflect actual purposes, dependencies, and operating conditions.
- Ratings and treatment decisions follow consistent, documented criteria.
- Control effectiveness and residual risk are supported by evidence.
- Material changes and incidents produce timely reassessment.
- Management can identify concentrated, systemic, and overdue exposures.
- Auditors and authorized reviewers can reconstruct significant decisions.
- Governance weaknesses result in validated improvements.

The governing principle is straightforward: **an AI inventory must represent the organization's actual AI use, and an AI risk register must represent its defensible understanding of AI risk.** Neither is reliable when it becomes a static documentation exercise disconnected from deployment, operational evidence, accountability, and risk-based decision-making.
