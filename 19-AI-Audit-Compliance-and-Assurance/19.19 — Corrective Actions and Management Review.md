# 19.19 — Corrective Actions and Management Review

## 1. Introduction to AI Corrective Actions and Management Review

AI governance should not end when an audit finding, control weakness, incident, or risk treatment gap is identified.

The organization should have a structured mechanism for:

- Correcting identified problems.
- Addressing underlying causes.
- Preventing recurrence.
- Verifying corrective-action effectiveness.
- Escalating unresolved issues.
- Reviewing significant AI governance performance at management level.
- Feeding lessons learned into continual improvement.

Corrective action and management review therefore create an important feedback mechanism within the AI governance operating model.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
AI Audit / Assessment / Incident / Monitoring
                    ↓
              Finding or Issue
                    ↓
             Root Cause Analysis
                    ↓
            Corrective Action
                    ↓
            Implementation
                    ↓
          Effectiveness Verification
                    ↓
            Management Review
                    ↓
             Continual Improvement
```

The objective is not merely to close findings, but to improve the effectiveness and resilience of the AI governance system.

---

## 2. Definition of AI Corrective Action

An AI corrective action is an action taken to eliminate the cause of a detected nonconformity, control deficiency, failure, or other undesirable condition and to prevent its recurrence where appropriate.

Corrective action should be distinguished from simple remediation.

For example:

- **Remediation** may correct an incorrectly configured access control.
- **Corrective action** may address why the access-control configuration was repeatedly incorrect.
- **Preventive improvement** may strengthen the broader change-management process to reduce similar failures across other AI systems.

Corrective action should therefore address more than the visible symptom.

A corrective-action process should consider:

- What happened?
- What requirement or expectation was not met?
- What caused the condition?
- What was the actual or potential impact?
- What action is required?
- Who owns the action?
- When must it be completed?
- How will effectiveness be verified?

## 4. AI Finding-to-Corrective-Action Relationship

An audit finding identifies a condition that requires attention.

A corrective action defines what the organization will do in response.

These concepts should not be treated as interchangeable.

For example:

| Finding | Corrective Action |
|---|---|
| AI model approval evidence is missing | Establish mandatory approval evidence before production deployment |
| AI risk assessments are not consistently reviewed | Define review triggers and mandatory review ownership |
| Supplier AI assessments are incomplete | Implement a standardized third-party AI assessment process |
| Human oversight is not documented | Define and document human oversight responsibilities |
| AI audit evidence is inconsistent | Establish evidence requirements and centralized evidence retention |

The finding describes the observed condition.

The corrective action describes the response intended to address that condition.

This distinction supports accountability and auditability.

## 5. AI Corrective Action Objectives

Corrective actions should have clearly defined objectives.

Typical objectives include:

- Eliminate the identified cause.
- Reduce the likelihood of recurrence.
- Reduce potential impact.
- Restore compliance.
- Strengthen controls.
- Improve governance processes.
- Clarify accountability.
- Improve evidence quality.
- Address systemic weaknesses.
- Reduce residual AI risk.

The objective should be proportionate to the significance of the issue.

A minor documentation deficiency may require a limited process correction, while a systemic AI governance weakness may require changes to policies, controls, technology, training, ownership, and monitoring.

## 6. AI Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis is an important component of effective corrective action.

The organization should determine why the problem occurred rather than simply correcting the immediate condition.

Potential root causes include:

- Inadequate governance.
- Unclear ownership.
- Missing procedures.
- Poorly designed controls.
- Control implementation failures.
- Insufficient training.
- Inadequate technical capabilities.
- Weak change management.
- Incomplete AI inventory.
- Poor data quality.
- Supplier dependency.
- Inadequate testing.
- Conflicting business requirements.
- Insufficient management oversight.

A simple analysis model is:

```text
Finding
   ↓
Immediate Cause
   ↓
Underlying Cause
   ↓
Root Cause
   ↓
Corrective Action
```

The depth of analysis should be proportionate to the significance and potential consequences of the finding.

Corrective actions should be formally planned rather than handled through informal commitments.

A corrective-action plan may include:

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Action ID | Unique identifier |
| Finding ID | Related finding |
| Issue | Condition requiring correction |
| Root Cause | Identified underlying cause |
| Corrective Action | Required response |

| Dependencies | Related activities or prerequisites |
| Evidence | Required completion evidence |
| Verification | Effectiveness assessment |
| Status | Current implementation state |

This structure creates a traceable relationship between the original finding, the action taken, and the evidence demonstrating completion.

## 8. AI Corrective Action Ownership

Every corrective action should have an accountable owner.

The owner should have sufficient authority and resources to coordinate implementation.

Ownership should not be assigned only to the function that discovered the problem.

For example:

- Internal Audit may identify the finding.
- AI Governance may coordinate remediation.
- Cybersecurity may implement technical controls.
- Data Governance may correct data-management weaknesses.
- Legal or Compliance may address regulatory requirements.
- Business management may change operational processes.

A clear accountability model can therefore distinguish between:

```text
Finding Owner
      ↓
Corrective Action Owner
      ↓
Action Implementers
      ↓
Effectiveness Reviewer
```

The individual or function responsible for independently verifying effectiveness should have sufficient objectivity where independence is required.

## 9. AI Corrective Action Prioritization

Not all corrective actions require the same urgency.

Prioritization should consider factors such as:

- Finding severity.
- Residual AI risk.
- Regulatory exposure.
- Potential harm to individuals.
- Security implications.
- Privacy implications.
- Business impact.
- Number of affected AI systems.
- Recurrence potential.
- Control weakness.
- Management or board concern.

A simplified prioritization model is:

| Priority | Typical Condition | Expected Response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Material risk, major control failure, significant regulatory or safety exposure | Immediate management attention |
| High | Significant governance or control weakness | Prompt corrective action |
| Medium | Material but contained deficiency | Planned remediation |
| Low | Limited-impact deficiency | Address through normal improvement cycle |

Severity classification should be based on defined organizational criteria rather than subjective preference.

Implementation should address the approved corrective-action plan.

Activities may include:

- Updating policies.
- Revising procedures.
- Implementing technical controls.
- Changing system configurations.
- Improving monitoring.
- Reassigning responsibilities.
- Updating AI risk assessments.
- Updating AI inventory records.
- Training personnel.
- Revising supplier requirements.
- Improving documentation.
- Performing additional testing.

For example:

```text

      ↓

      ↓
Control Validation
      ↓
Effectiveness Verification
```

An action should not automatically be considered effective merely because the implementation task has been marked as completed.

## 11. AI Corrective Action Evidence

Evidence should demonstrate that the corrective action was actually implemented and achieved its intended objective.

Evidence may include:

- Updated policies.
- Approved procedures.
- System configuration records.
- Access-control reports.
- Training records.
- Risk assessments.
- AI inventory updates.
- Test results.
- Monitoring reports.
- Meeting decisions.

- Screenshots or system-generated reports where appropriate.

- Relevant.
- Authentic.
- Traceable.
- Sufficient.
- Current.
- Protected from inappropriate alteration.

The evidence requirement should be defined before closure where practical.

## 12. AI Corrective Action Effectiveness Verification

Effectiveness verification determines whether the corrective action actually addressed the underlying problem.

Verification may involve:

- Re-testing the affected control.
- Reviewing operational evidence.
- Performing a follow-up audit.
- Reassessing the AI risk.
- Testing similar AI systems for recurrence.
- Reviewing monitoring results.
- Interviewing responsible personnel.
- Examining incident recurrence.
- Performing independent validation.

For example:

```text
Finding:
AI risk assessments are inconsistently reviewed.

Action:
Implement mandatory annual and event-triggered reviews.

Verification:
Sample AI systems → Review assessment dates
                  ↓
             Check trigger events
                  ↓
             Evaluate evidence
                  ↓
          Determine effectiveness
```

The verification should establish whether the underlying weakness was adequately addressed rather than merely confirming that an administrative task was completed.

---

## 13. Part 1 Core Principle

AI corrective action should be treated as a controlled governance process that moves from **finding identification → root cause analysis → accountable action → implementation → evidence → effectiveness verification**.

The purpose is not simply to close audit findings. The purpose is to eliminate or reduce underlying weaknesses, prevent recurrence, strengthen AI controls, and provide management with reliable evidence that the AI governance system is improving.

## Part 2 — Corrective Action Governance and Issue Management

### 14. AI Corrective Action Classification

Corrective actions should be classified according to the nature and significance of the underlying issue.

Possible classifications include:

- **Control deficiency** — an expected control is missing or insufficient.
- **Control design deficiency** — the control exists but is not appropriately designed.
- **Control operating deficiency** — the control is appropriately designed but is not operating as intended.
- **Process deficiency** — an AI governance process is incomplete or ineffective.
- **Compliance deficiency** — an applicable requirement is not adequately addressed.
- **Documentation deficiency** — required evidence or records are incomplete.
- **Systemic deficiency** — the issue affects multiple AI systems, processes, or organizational functions.
- **Recurring deficiency** — a previously identified weakness has reappeared.
- **Emerging deficiency** — a developing condition may become material if not addressed.

Classification helps determine the appropriate treatment, ownership, escalation path, and verification approach.

The register should provide a reliable view of unresolved AI governance weaknesses.

It should also support management reporting and escalation.

### 16. AI Corrective Action Status

Corrective actions should have clearly defined lifecycle statuses.

A typical lifecycle is:

```text id="k6m4q1"
Identified
    ↓
Assigned
    ↓
Planned
    ↓
In Progress
    ↓
Implemented
    ↓
Pending Verification
    ↓
Verified
    ↓
Closed
```

Additional statuses may be required, such as:

- Overdue.
- Blocked.
- Deferred.
- Rejected.
- Reopened.
- Superseded.

Status definitions should be documented so that different functions interpret them consistently.

For example, **Implemented** should mean that the planned action has been put into operation, while **Verified** should mean that sufficient evidence has established that the action is effective.

### 17. AI Overdue Corrective Actions

Overdue corrective actions should be actively monitored.

An action may become overdue when:

- The target date has passed.
- Required evidence has not been provided.
- Implementation remains incomplete.
- Dependencies have delayed completion.
- Verification has not occurred within the required period.

Overdue actions should be assessed according to risk rather than treated only as administrative exceptions.

For example:

```text id="7n5h2p"
Target Date Passed
       ↓
Determine Reason
       ↓
Assess Risk Impact
       ↓
Revised Action Plan
       ↓
Management Decision
       ↓
Escalate if Required
```

Repeated delays may indicate insufficient resources, unrealistic planning, weak ownership, or a deeper governance problem.

---

### 18. AI Corrective Action Dependencies

Corrective actions may depend on other actions, projects, controls, suppliers, or technology changes.

Dependencies should therefore be documented where they could affect completion.

Examples include:

- A new AI monitoring capability depending on platform procurement.
- A privacy control depending on data classification.
- A supplier remediation depending on contractual changes.
- A model-control improvement depending on model redevelopment.
- A governance procedure depending on policy approval.

Dependency management should identify:

- Dependency owner.
- Expected completion.
- Impact of delay.
- Alternative treatment.
- Escalation requirement.

A corrective action should not be considered adequately managed simply because responsibility has been assigned if a critical dependency remains unresolved.

---

### 19. AI Corrective Action Exceptions

Organizations may encounter circumstances where a corrective action cannot be completed within the original timeframe.

An exception should be formally managed rather than handled through informal agreement.

An exception record may include:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Action | Implement AI output validation |
| Original Date | 30 June |
| Requested Extension | 30 September |
| Reason | Platform redesign |
| Risk Impact | Medium residual exposure |
| Interim Control | Additional human review |
| Approver | Authorized management |
| New Date | 30 September |
| Review Date | Monthly |

Exceptions should be:

- Justified.
- Risk assessed.
- Authorized.
- Time-bound.
- Documented.
- Monitored.

Temporary exceptions should not become permanent substitutes for corrective action.

---

### 20. AI Interim Controls

Where permanent corrective action cannot be implemented immediately, interim controls may reduce exposure while the final action is being completed.

Examples include:

- Additional human approval.
- Increased transaction review.
- Restricted system access.
- Reduced AI system scope.
- Manual validation.
- Increased monitoring.
- Temporary suspension of a high-risk function.
- Additional logging.

A useful model is:

```text id="r9a2cx"
Finding
   ↓
Permanent Corrective Action
        │
        └── Delayed
              ↓
        Interim Control
              ↓
        Reduced Exposure
              ↓
     Permanent Implementation
```

Interim controls should have an owner, defined duration, and monitoring requirements.

They should not obscure the need for permanent remediation.

---

### 21. AI Recurring Findings

A recurring finding occurs when a previously addressed weakness reappears or remains unresolved.

Recurring findings may indicate:

- Ineffective corrective action.
- Inadequate root cause analysis.
- Weak ownership.
- Insufficient control design.
- Inadequate monitoring.
- Organizational resistance.
- Failure to apply lessons learned across AI systems.

Recurring findings should generally receive additional management attention.

The organization should determine whether the issue is:

```text id="z3q6fw"
Local Problem
     ↓
Process Problem
     ↓
Systemic Problem
     ↓
Enterprise Governance Problem
```

The response should be proportionate to the scope of the underlying weakness.

---

### 22. AI Systemic Corrective Actions

Some findings cannot be effectively addressed through a single AI system.

For example, an audit may identify inconsistent documentation of human oversight across twenty AI systems.

Correcting one system would not address the underlying governance weakness.

A systemic corrective action might therefore include:

- Updating the enterprise AI governance procedure.
- Defining mandatory documentation requirements.
- Updating the AI control library.
- Revising the AI system approval process.
- Training relevant personnel.
- Updating templates.
- Testing compliance across the AI inventory.

Systemic corrective actions should consider both the affected system and the broader population potentially exposed to the same weakness.

---

### 23. AI Corrective Action Population Analysis

When a finding indicates a potentially widespread deficiency, the organization should determine the population affected.

For example:

> An audit identifies that AI risk assessments do not consistently document residual risk acceptance.

The organization should determine whether the deficiency affects:

- One AI system.
- One business unit.
- A specific AI risk category.
- Multiple AI systems.
- The entire AI governance framework.

Population analysis can be performed using:

- AI inventory records.
- Risk registers.
- Control assessments.
- Audit samples.
- System documentation.
- Governance records.

This helps determine whether corrective action should be local, functional, or enterprise-wide.

---

### 24. AI Corrective Action Root Cause Versus Symptom

A corrective action should distinguish between the visible symptom and the underlying cause.

For example:

| Symptom | Root Cause | Appropriate Action |
|---|---|---|
| Missing AI risk assessment | No mandatory assessment gate | Establish approval gate |
| Incorrect model documentation | Documentation requirements unclear | Define required documentation |
| Repeated access violations | Access review process ineffective | Redesign access review control |
| Missing supplier assessment | Third-party process excludes AI | Integrate AI into supplier assessment |
| Repeated audit findings | Prior actions addressed symptoms only | Perform systemic root cause analysis |

Treating only the symptom can result in recurring findings.

Effective corrective action should target the condition that allows the problem to occur.

### 25. AI Corrective Action Risk Reassessment

Significant corrective actions may change the underlying AI risk profile.

Risk should therefore be reassessed when the action materially changes:

- AI system architecture.
- Model behavior.
- Data processing.
- Human oversight.
- Security controls.
- Privacy controls.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Intended use.
- Operational processes.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text id="p8j4ws"
Corrective Action
       ↓
Control / Process Change
       ↓
Risk Reassessment
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Management Decision
```

This prevents corrective-action closure from being treated as separate from ongoing AI risk management.

### 26. AI Corrective Action Closure Criteria

Closure criteria should be defined before an action is formally closed.

Typical criteria include:

- The corrective action has been implemented.
- Required evidence has been collected.
- The root cause has been addressed sufficiently.
- Required controls are operating.
- Effectiveness has been verified.
- Residual risk is acceptable.
- Required management approval has been obtained.
- No unresolved dependency prevents closure.

A simple closure test is:

```text id="m0y8vh"
Implemented?
    ↓ Yes
Evidence Available?
    ↓ Yes
Effective?
    ↓ Yes
Residual Risk Acceptable?
    ↓ Yes
Authorized Closure
```

If one of the required conditions is not satisfied, the action should normally remain open or be returned for further remediation.

---

### 27. AI Corrective Action Reopening

A closed corrective action may need to be reopened when new evidence demonstrates that the issue was not effectively resolved.

Reopening triggers may include:

- Recurrence of the finding.
- Control failure.
- New incident.
- Failed effectiveness testing.
- Material AI system change.
- New regulatory requirement.
- New evidence concerning the original root cause.
- Discovery that the corrective action addressed only part of the issue.

Reopening should preserve the historical record.

The original closure decision should remain traceable, while the new action cycle is documented separately.

### 28. AI Corrective Action Management Reporting

Management reporting should provide visibility into the status and effectiveness of corrective actions.

Useful metrics may include:

- Number of open findings.
- Number of critical findings.
- Number of overdue actions.
- Average corrective-action age.
- Recurring findings.
- Actions by business unit.
- Actions by AI risk category.
- Actions awaiting verification.
- Closure rate.
- Reopening rate.
- Effectiveness failure rate.

Example:

| Metric | Management Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Open critical findings | Current high-priority exposure |
| Overdue high-risk actions | Remediation execution risk |
| Recurring findings | Potential systemic weakness |
| High reopening rate | Weak effectiveness |
| Long average action age | Remediation capacity issue |
| Low verification completion | Assurance gap |

Metrics should support decisions rather than become purely administrative reporting.

### 29. AI Corrective Action Escalation

Corrective actions should be escalated when their significance exceeds the authority or tolerance of the responsible owner.

Escalation may be triggered by:

- Critical findings.
- Significant regulatory exposure.
- Material safety concerns.
- Significant privacy or security risks.
- Repeated missed deadlines.
- Recurring findings.
- Insufficient remediation resources.
- Risk acceptance beyond delegated authority.
- Failure of interim controls.
- Management disagreement concerning closure.

Escalation should follow defined governance paths.

```text id="f6x2qa"
Action Owner
     ↓
AI Governance
     ↓
Executive Management
     ↓
Risk / Compliance Committee
     ↓
Board or Appropriate Oversight Body
```

The escalation path should reflect the organization's governance structure and delegated authorities.

---

### 30. Part 2 Core Principle

Corrective-action governance should provide a controlled mechanism for moving significant AI findings from **identification through classification, ownership, remediation, verification, escalation, and closure**.

Effective governance distinguishes symptoms from root causes, manages overdue and exceptional actions, evaluates systemic impact, and ensures that corrective actions remain connected to AI risk management and management decision-making.

## Part 3 — Management Review, AI Governance Performance, and Continual Improvement

### 31. AI Management Review

Management review provides a formal mechanism for evaluating whether the AI governance framework remains suitable, adequate, and effective.

Management review should consider whether:

- AI governance objectives remain appropriate.
- Significant AI risks remain within established appetite and tolerance.
- Controls continue to operate effectively.
- Corrective actions are progressing appropriately.
- Significant findings have been adequately addressed.
- AI governance resources remain sufficient.
- Changes in the AI environment require governance changes.
- Previous management decisions remain appropriate.
- Opportunities for continual improvement have been identified.

Management review should be evidence-based rather than limited to general discussion.
