# 19.13 — Model Documentation

## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Principles

### 1. Definition of AI Model Documentation

AI model documentation is the structured collection of technical, operational, governance, security, risk, and lifecycle information that explains how an AI model was developed, evaluated, approved, deployed, maintained, and retired.

It provides an authoritative record of the model's characteristics, intended purpose, design assumptions, data dependencies, limitations, evaluation results, security considerations, and operational requirements. It enables authorized stakeholders to understand what the model does, how it works at an appropriate level of abstraction, under which conditions it can be used, and what controls are necessary to manage its risks.

Model documentation serves multiple organizational functions:

- **Governance:** Demonstrates that model development and use are subject to defined policies, ownership, approval, and oversight.
- **Risk management:** Records model-specific risks, assumptions, limitations, and mitigation measures.
- **Security:** Documents security requirements, dependencies, access restrictions, threat considerations, and protective controls.
- **Compliance:** Provides evidence that applicable regulatory, contractual, and internal documentation requirements have been addressed.
- **Audit and assurance:** Enables independent reviewers to examine the model's development, evaluation, approval, and operational history.
- **Operational management:** Supports deployment, monitoring, troubleshooting, change management, incident investigation, and retirement.
- **Accountability:** Identifies the people, teams, and decision-making authorities responsible for the model and its lifecycle.

Model documentation is not simply a technical report produced at the end of development. It is a controlled information asset that evolves as the model, its operating environment, its intended purpose, and its associated risks change.

For example, a model initially approved to classify internal support tickets may later be adapted to prioritize customer complaints. Although the underlying model may remain unchanged, its intended purpose, affected individuals, business impact, and risk profile may change. Its documentation must reflect the revised use case and any resulting reassessment or approval requirements.

### 2. Purpose and Objectives of Model Documentation

The primary purpose of model documentation is to preserve reliable, reviewable information about an AI model throughout its lifecycle.

Organizations cannot effectively govern a model if they cannot establish which model is in use, why it was selected, how it was evaluated, what limitations were identified, who approved it, and what conditions apply to its continued operation.

A mature documentation process should achieve the following objectives.

**2.1 Establish model identity**

Documentation identifies the model through a unique identifier, name, version, owner, development or acquisition source, and lifecycle status. These attributes prevent confusion between different models, versions, environments, or deployments.

**2.2 Explain intended purpose and boundaries**

Documentation defines the business problem the model is designed to address, its intended users, expected inputs and outputs, operating conditions, and prohibited or unsupported uses.

**2.3 Preserve development and configuration decisions**

Documentation records relevant design decisions, model selection criteria, architecture choices, training approaches, configuration parameters, dependencies, and material assumptions.

**2.4 Demonstrate evaluation and approval**

Documentation preserves test methodologies, evaluation results, acceptance criteria, identified deficiencies, risk assessments, and approval decisions.

**2.5 Enable reproducibility and traceability**

Documentation provides enough information for authorized personnel to identify the model version, relevant data and software dependencies, configuration, and evaluation context associated with a particular result or deployment.

Reproducing an AI result exactly may not always be possible, particularly for nondeterministic systems, externally hosted models, or models whose underlying service changes without full customer visibility. Documentation should state such limitations rather than imply guaranteed reproducibility.

**2.6 Support operational resilience**

Documentation helps operators identify dependencies, recovery procedures, monitoring requirements, escalation paths, rollback conditions, and other measures necessary to maintain controlled operation.

**2.7 Facilitate audit and regulatory examination**

Documentation enables internal audit, external assessors, regulators, and other authorized reviewers to examine whether required governance processes and controls were implemented and whether supporting evidence exists.

The quality of model documentation should therefore be evaluated by its usefulness, accuracy, completeness, traceability, and fitness for the model's risk level—not merely by the number of pages produced.

### 3. Model Documentation Within the AI Governance Framework

Model documentation is a foundational component of an AI governance operating model. It connects technical model development with organizational accountability, risk management, security controls, compliance obligations, and operational oversight.

A governance framework establishes the rules under which AI systems are developed, acquired, deployed, and monitored. Model documentation translates those rules into model-specific records that demonstrate how they have been applied.

For example, an organizational AI policy may require human oversight for consequential decisions. Model documentation should identify the applicable oversight requirement, the roles responsible for review, the decision points requiring human intervention, and the evidence demonstrating that the oversight mechanism has been implemented and evaluated.

A model documentation framework commonly incorporates the following governance elements:

| Governance element      | Documentation contribution                                                            |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI policy and standards | Records applicable requirements and how they apply to the model                       |
| Model ownership         | Identifies accountable business and technical owners                                  |
| AI risk management      | Records identified risks, assessments, treatments, and residual risks                 |
| Security governance     | Documents security requirements, controls, dependencies, and limitations              |
| Data governance         | Records relevant data sources, provenance, quality considerations, and permitted uses |
| Model validation        | Preserves evaluation methods, results, limitations, and validation conclusions        |
| Change management       | Establishes the model's approved baseline and records material changes                |
| Compliance management   | Maps applicable obligations to documentation and supporting evidence                  |
| Internal audit          | Enables independent testing of governance and control implementation                  |
| Operational monitoring  | Connects documented performance and risk thresholds to monitoring activities          |

The governance framework should define minimum documentation requirements, mandatory approval points, ownership responsibilities, retention rules, access restrictions, and the process for handling missing or inaccurate information.

Documentation requirements should be proportionate to risk. A low-impact internal classification model may require a relatively lightweight documentation package, whereas a model supporting regulated, safety-critical, or high-impact decisions may require substantially more detailed records and formal approval.

Proportionality does not mean that low-risk models should have no documentation. It means that the depth, formality, and assurance requirements should reflect the model's characteristics, intended use, foreseeable consequences, and applicable obligations.

### 4. Distinction Between Model Documentation, Model Cards, and System Cards

Model documentation is the broader collection of information maintained about an AI model. Model cards and system cards are specific documentation artifacts that can form part of that collection.

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**Model documentation** may include architecture descriptions, development records, training and evaluation information, configuration baselines, validation reports, security assessments, risk records, change histories, operational requirements, and approval evidence.

**Model cards** are structured summaries designed to communicate essential information about a model, such as its intended use, evaluation results, performance characteristics, limitations, and relevant ethical or risk considerations. Their content and format depend on the applicable methodology or organizational standard.

**System cards** generally describe an AI system in its broader operational context, potentially including the model, surrounding services, interfaces, data flows, safeguards, deployment arrangements, and system-level limitations.

The distinction can be represented as follows:

```
AI Governance and Assurance Records
                |
                +-- Model Documentation Package
                |       |
                |       +-- Model identity and version records
                |       +-- Development and configuration records
                |       +-- Data and dependency documentation
                |       +-- Evaluation and validation evidence
                |       +-- Security and risk assessments
                |       +-- Approval and change records
                |       +-- Operational and lifecycle records
                |
                +-- Model Card
                |       |
                |       +-- Structured model summary
                |       +-- Intended use and limitations
                |       +-- Selected evaluation results
                |
                +-- System Card
                        |
                        +-- System context and architecture
                        +-- Interfaces and operational safeguards
                        +-- System-level risks and limitations
```

This structure is conceptual rather than a mandatory document hierarchy. Organizations may implement the artifacts as separate files, sections of a central repository, or linked records in a model management platform.

The key requirement is that each artifact has a defined purpose and that the information needed for governance, security, operations, and assurance remains accessible and traceable.

A model card should not be assumed to contain every technical detail needed for an audit. Likewise, a system card should not automatically be treated as evidence that every underlying model has been adequately documented.

### 5. Model Documentation Across the AI Lifecycle

Model documentation should be maintained throughout the AI lifecycle rather than created as a one-time deliverable.

The information required at each stage differs. Early development may involve assumptions, proposed architectures, data suitability assessments, and preliminary risk analysis. Later stages require evidence of implementation, evaluation, approval, deployment, and operational performance.

A lifecycle-based approach helps organizations identify when information must be created, reviewed, approved, and updated.

| Lifecycle stage                | Typical documentation requirements                                                              | Governance objective                                             |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Intake and use-case definition | Business justification, intended purpose, stakeholders, preliminary risk classification         | Establish whether the proposed use is appropriate                |
| Design and planning            | Architecture decisions, requirements, assumptions, design alternatives                          | Establish an approved design baseline                            |
| Data preparation               | Data provenance, collection methods, permitted uses, quality and representativeness assessments | Demonstrate responsible data management                          |
| Development or acquisition     | Model identity, source, development records, configuration, dependencies                        | Establish model lineage and technical accountability             |
| Testing and validation         | Test plans, datasets or dataset references, metrics, results, limitations, defects              | Demonstrate that defined acceptance criteria have been evaluated |
| Risk and security assessment   | Threat analysis, risk register, security controls, residual risks                               | Determine whether identified risks are adequately addressed      |
| Approval                       | Approval records, exceptions, unresolved issues, deployment conditions                          | Establish authorization to proceed                               |
| Deployment                     | Release identifier, environment, configuration, access requirements, rollback plan              | Preserve the approved production baseline                        |
| Monitoring and maintenance     | Performance trends, incidents, drift assessments, control tests, corrective actions             | Maintain continued oversight                                     |
| Material change                | Change request, impact assessment, regression testing, reapproval where required                | Prevent uncontrolled changes                                     |
| Retirement                     | Decommissioning decision, dependency removal, archival and retention records                    | Ensure controlled termination of use                             |

Documentation should be generated as close as practical to the activity it describes. Retrospective reconstruction increases the likelihood of missing information, inaccurate dates, incomplete decision histories, and unsupported assertions.

For example, a team that records evaluation results but fails to retain the corresponding model version, evaluation configuration, and dataset reference may be unable to establish which model was actually tested.

A lifecycle-based process therefore links documentation activities to workflow gates. A model should not automatically advance to the next stage merely because a document exists; the organization should verify that the required information is sufficiently complete, accurate, reviewed, and supported by evidence.

### 6. Core Principles of Effective Model Documentation

Effective model documentation follows a set of principles that help ensure records remain reliable, usable, and suitable for governance and assurance.

**6.1 Accuracy**

Documentation must reflect the actual model, its configuration, its capabilities, and its operating environment. Proposed designs must not be presented as implemented controls, and planned evaluations must not be represented as completed tests.

**6.2 Completeness**

Documentation should cover all information required for the model's risk level, intended use, lifecycle stage, and applicable obligations. Completeness is assessed against defined requirements, not against an arbitrary document length.

**6.3 Traceability**

Material statements should be traceable to their source, such as an approved design decision, test result, configuration record, risk assessment, or change request.

**6.4 Consistency**

Information should be consistent across the model inventory, model documentation, risk register, deployment records, validation reports, and approval systems. Conflicting model identifiers or version numbers create assurance gaps.

**6.5 Timeliness**

Records should be updated when material changes occur. Outdated documentation may be more dangerous than an explicitly incomplete record because users may rely on information that no longer describes the deployed model.

**6.6 Accountability**

Each documentation component should have an assigned owner responsible for its creation, review, maintenance, and correction.

**6.7 Proportionality**

Documentation depth and review rigor should reflect the model's risk, complexity, impact, and applicable legal or contractual requirements.

**6.8 Accessibility**

Authorized users should be able to locate and understand the information they need without unnecessary delays. Accessibility must be balanced with confidentiality, privacy, intellectual property protection, and security restrictions.

**6.9 Integrity**

Documentation should be protected against unauthorized modification, deletion, or substitution. Material changes should be attributable to authorized actors and supported by appropriate records.

**6.10 Verifiability**

Important claims should be supported by evidence where appropriate. Statements such as "the model passed security testing" should identify the relevant test, scope, date, version, results, and approval or disposition.

These principles reinforce one another. A technically accurate report that cannot be linked to the deployed model is not sufficiently traceable. A complete document that has not been updated after a significant model change is not timely. A signed approval that lacks supporting evaluation evidence may demonstrate that a decision was recorded, but it does not independently establish that the model was adequately assessed.

### 7. Model Documentation as a Controlled Information Asset

Model documentation should be managed as an organizational information asset with defined ownership, classification, access controls, retention requirements, and lifecycle management.

This approach is important because documentation may contain sensitive technical information, personal data, proprietary intellectual property, confidential business information, security architecture details, or information that could assist an attacker.

Examples include:

- Model architecture and deployment configuration.
- Training-data descriptions and sensitive dataset references.
- API endpoints, integration details, and service dependencies.
- Security testing findings and known vulnerabilities.
- Prompt templates, model behavior constraints, or sensitive system configuration.
- Evaluation weaknesses that could facilitate targeted manipulation.
- Incident reports containing personal or confidential information.
- Commercially sensitive information about externally supplied models.

Organizations should classify documentation according to its actual content and associated risks. Not every model document requires the same security classification, and classification should not be used to prevent legitimate governance or assurance access without justification.

A practical documentation asset management model includes the following controls:

| Control area         | Expected control                                                                            |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ownership            | Assign a document owner and, where necessary, a technical subject-matter owner              |
| Classification       | Apply information classification based on confidentiality, sensitivity, and business impact |
| Access management    | Grant access according to role, business need, and least-privilege principles               |
| Version control      | Maintain identifiable versions and an authoritative current record                          |
| Integrity protection | Restrict unauthorized edits and preserve change history                                     |
| Retention            | Define retention periods and legal holds where applicable                                   |
| Backup and recovery  | Protect important records against accidental loss or system failure                         |
| Disposal             | Securely dispose of records when retention obligations expire and disposal is permitted     |
| Review               | Periodically verify accuracy, relevance, ownership, and access rights                       |
| Auditability         | Record material access, modification, approval, and disposition events where appropriate    |

The authoritative repository may be a document management platform, a model registry, a GRC system, a version-controlled repository, or an integrated combination of these technologies.

The technology is less important than the control outcome: the organization must know which record is authoritative, who may change it, which version was approved, and how previous versions and decisions can be retrieved when required.

For instance, keeping an approved model evaluation report in a shared folder where anyone can overwrite the file creates a weakness in integrity and accountability. A controlled repository with version history, role-based permissions, and recorded approvals provides stronger assurance.

### 8. Model Documentation Requirements and Regulatory Context

Model documentation requirements arise from multiple sources, including legislation, regulations, technical standards, contractual commitments, organizational policies, and risk management frameworks.

The exact requirements depend on the model's intended purpose, deployment context, jurisdiction, organizational role, sector, and applicable legal classification. There is no single universal documentation checklist that is sufficient for every AI model.

A model documentation framework should distinguish between mandatory obligations, adopted standards, contractual requirements, and internal good practices.

**8.1 Regulatory requirements**

Applicable AI legislation may require specific technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, risk management, human oversight, or post-deployment monitoring information for certain categories of AI systems.

For example, the EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes specific technical documentation and record-keeping obligations for relevant categories of AI systems, including high-risk AI systems. The precise obligations depend on the applicable provisions, the organization's role, the system's classification, and the relevant implementation timeline.

An organization should not assume that every model automatically falls into the same regulatory category or that a generic internal model document satisfies a statutory requirement.

**8.2 Management system standards**

Standards such as ISO/IEC 42001 can support the establishment of documented information and management-system processes for AI governance. Their application should be mapped to the organization's chosen management-system scope and relevant requirements.

**8.3 Risk management frameworks**

Frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework can inform the documentation of context, risks, evaluation, measurement, management activities, and governance decisions. Organizations should distinguish framework guidance from legal obligations.

**8.4 Security and privacy requirements**

Information security, privacy, data protection, and sector-specific requirements may influence how model documentation is created, protected, shared, and retained. Documentation practices must not introduce unnecessary personal-data collection or expose sensitive information.

**8.5 Contractual and supplier requirements**

Organizations may need documentation from model providers, cloud platforms, data suppliers, or external development partners. Contracts should establish which technical information will be provided, how often it will be updated, what evidence may be requested, and how material changes will be communicated.

A regulatory and standards mapping should connect each applicable requirement to the relevant documentation artifact and supporting evidence.

| Requirement source                | Documentation question                                                  | Typical governance evidence                                    |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Applicable legislation            | What information must be documented or retained?                        | Obligation assessment and compliance mapping                   |
| AI management system              | What documented processes and records are required?                     | Controlled procedures, records, and review evidence            |
| Risk management framework         | How are risks identified, assessed, treated, and monitored?             | Risk assessments, treatment decisions, and monitoring records  |
| Information security requirements | How are model-related assets and records protected?                     | Security requirements, access records, and control assessments |
| Privacy obligations               | What personal data is involved, and how is it governed?                 | Data-flow documentation and applicable privacy assessments     |
| Supplier contracts                | What information must external providers supply?                        | Contract clauses, supplier documentation, and review records   |
| Internal policy                   | What minimum documentation and approvals does the organization require? | Policy mapping, checklists, and approval records               |

The compliance team should validate the applicable requirements rather than relying solely on a generic template. A template is a useful starting point, but it cannot independently determine legal applicability.

### 9. Model Documentation Versus Supporting Evidence

Model documentation and audit evidence are closely related, but they serve different purposes.

Model documentation describes the model, its development, its characteristics, its risks, its controls, and its lifecycle decisions. Supporting evidence demonstrates that particular activities occurred or that stated controls and outcomes can be substantiated.

For example, a model document may state that robustness testing was completed. Supporting evidence could include the approved test plan, test execution logs, test environment details, model version identifiers, recorded results, defect tickets, and the reviewer's conclusion.

The distinction matters because an assertion in a document is not necessarily proof that the asserted activity was performed correctly.

A useful evidence structure is:

```
Documented Claim
      |
      v
Defined Requirement or Acceptance Criterion
      |
      v
Supporting Evidence
      |
      +-- Test plan and methodology
      +-- Model and configuration identifiers
      +-- Execution records and results
      +-- Defect and exception records
      +-- Review and approval
      |
      v
Evaluation of Sufficiency
      |
      v
Recorded Conclusion and Follow-up
```

Supporting evidence should be sufficient, relevant, reliable, and attributable to the activity being evaluated.

For a high-impact model, evidence may need to be preserved in a controlled repository with restricted modification rights and reliable timestamps. For a low-risk model, a simpler evidence trail may be proportionate, provided that it satisfies the applicable requirements.

The organization should also distinguish between original evidence and derived summaries. A dashboard showing evaluation results may be useful for management review, but the underlying test records may still be necessary to verify how the results were produced.

Where evidence cannot be obtained—for example, because a third-party provider does not disclose certain internal development details—the limitation should be explicitly documented, along with its governance implications, compensating measures, and any resulting approval restrictions.

### 10. Model Documentation Governance Roles and Responsibilities

Model documentation requires clear accountability across business, technical, risk, security, compliance, and audit functions.

A frequent governance weakness occurs when a team assumes that another department owns the documentation. Technical teams may consider documentation a compliance responsibility, while compliance teams may lack the technical information needed to maintain accurate records.

The organization should therefore assign responsibilities explicitly.

| Role                                       | Primary documentation responsibility                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Business or use-case owner                 | Defines business purpose, intended use, expected outcomes, and operational constraints                 |
| Model owner                                | Accountable for the model's lifecycle records, completeness, and continued suitability                 |
| Model developer or engineering team        | Produces development, architecture, configuration, dependency, and evaluation records                  |
| Data owner or data steward                 | Documents relevant data provenance, quality, permitted uses, and governance constraints                |
| Model validator                            | Records independent evaluation methodology, findings, limitations, and validation conclusions          |
| AI risk manager                            | Ensures relevant risks, treatments, residual risks, and acceptance decisions are documented            |
| Information security team                  | Defines security documentation requirements and reviews relevant security controls and findings        |
| Privacy or data protection function        | Reviews applicable personal-data processing and related documentation obligations                      |
| Legal and compliance teams                 | Interpret applicable obligations and maintain the compliance mapping                                   |
| Change advisory or approval authority      | Reviews material changes and ensures required approvals are recorded                                   |
| Internal audit                             | Independently evaluates whether documentation controls are suitably designed and operating effectively |
| Records or information management function | Establishes retention, classification, access, archival, and disposal requirements                     |

These roles may be combined in smaller organizations, but accountability should remain clear. Combining responsibilities does not remove the need for appropriate independent review where required by policy, regulation, or risk.

Internal audit should generally assess the adequacy and effectiveness of documentation controls independently rather than become the operational owner responsible for creating or approving the records it later audits.

A responsibility assignment matrix can make the governance model operational. For each required artifact, the organization should identify who prepares it, who reviews it, who approves it, and who is responsible for maintaining it after approval.

### 11. The Model Documentation Lifecycle

A controlled model documentation lifecycle defines how records are created, reviewed, approved, updated, retained, and retired.

The lifecycle should align with the model lifecycle but should not be limited to it. Some documentation must be retained after a model is decommissioned because it supports regulatory accountability, incident investigation, contractual obligations, or future audit activities.

The main lifecycle activities are as follows.

**11.1 Initiation**

Create the documentation record when a model or AI use case enters the organization's governance process. Assign a unique identifier, owner, preliminary classification, and intended purpose.

**11.2 Collection**

Gather information from development teams, data owners, suppliers, validation teams, security assessments, and operational systems. Identify required information that is unavailable or not applicable.

**11.3 Review**

Check the accuracy, completeness, consistency, and evidence supporting the information. Review should be performed by people with appropriate competence and authority.

**11.4 Approval**

Record the relevant approval decision, approving authority, date, applicable model version, unresolved issues, and any conditions attached to approval.

**11.5 Publication and controlled access**

Make the approved documentation available to authorized stakeholders through a defined authoritative repository. Clearly distinguish approved records from working drafts.

**11.6 Maintenance**

Update records following material changes to the model, data, configuration, intended purpose, provider, deployment environment, risk profile, or applicable requirements.

**11.7 Periodic review**

Conduct risk-based reviews to verify that documentation remains accurate, current, and aligned with the deployed model and organizational requirements.

**11.8 Archival and retirement**

Preserve records according to retention rules and applicable legal or contractual obligations. Record retirement decisions and ensure that documentation remains retrievable for the required period.

A simplified workflow is:

```
Model or Use Case Registered
              |
              v
Documentation Requirements Identified
              |
              v
Information Collected and Linked to Evidence
              |
              v
Completeness and Accuracy Review
              |
              v
Risk, Security, and Compliance Review
              |
              v
Authorized Approval
              |
              v
Controlled Publication
              |
              v
Monitoring and Change Management
              |
              v
Periodic Review or Material-Change Review
              |
              v
Archival and Controlled Retirement
```

The workflow should include exception handling. If mandatory information is missing, the responsible authority should determine whether the gap can be remediated before approval, whether a time-limited exception is permitted, or whether deployment must be restricted.

An exception should identify the missing information, the reason for the gap, the associated risk, the compensating controls, the accountable risk owner, the approval authority, and the remediation deadline.

### 12. Minimum Model Documentation Baseline

Organizations should define a minimum documentation baseline for each model, then expand it according to risk, complexity, intended use, and applicable requirements.

A minimum baseline is not a universal legal checklist. It is an internal governance mechanism designed to prevent important information from being omitted.

A practical baseline may include the following categories.

| Documentation category     | Minimum information to consider                                                                             |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model identification       | Unique ID, model name, version, status, owner, provider, and relevant registry reference                    |
| Intended purpose           | Business objective, intended users, use cases, assumptions, restrictions, and prohibited uses               |
| Technical description      | Model type, relevant architecture, interfaces, major components, and dependencies                           |
| Data information           | Relevant data sources, provenance, permitted use, quality limitations, and data dependencies                |
| Development or acquisition | Development approach, supplier information, relevant design decisions, and configuration baseline           |
| Evaluation and validation  | Evaluation scope, methodology, metrics, results, acceptance criteria, and known limitations                 |
| Risk assessment            | Identified risks, risk ratings, treatments, residual risks, and risk acceptance decisions                   |
| Security and privacy       | Applicable security controls, relevant privacy considerations, known weaknesses, and restrictions           |
| Human oversight            | Required review or intervention points, responsible roles, and escalation conditions                        |
| Approval                   | Approval records, dates, decision authority, conditions, exceptions, and unresolved issues                  |
| Deployment                 | Environment, deployed version, configuration reference, operational dependencies, and rollback arrangements |
| Monitoring and maintenance | Monitoring requirements, performance thresholds, review frequency, incident procedures, and change triggers |
| Change history             | Version history, material changes, impact assessments, test results, and reapproval records                 |
| Retention and access       | Repository location, classification, access controls, retention rules, and archival requirements            |

The baseline should be adapted to the organization's operating model. For example, an organization using a third-party foundation model may be unable to obtain full training-data details or internal architecture documentation. It should record what is known, identify provider limitations, preserve available contractual and technical evidence, and assess whether the information gap creates unacceptable risk.

Similarly, a model that is never retrained but is used in a changing operational environment may still require documentation updates when its integrations, configuration, intended purpose, or surrounding controls change.

A model documentation baseline should be implemented as a controlled set of requirements, with each requirement mapped to an artifact, owner, review method, and acceptance criterion.

### 13. Common Model Documentation Deficiencies

Documentation deficiencies can undermine AI governance even when the model itself appears to operate correctly.

The most common weaknesses involve missing information, stale records, unsupported claims, inconsistent identifiers, unclear ownership, and insufficient links between documentation and the actual deployed model.

**13.1 Incomplete technical information**

The documentation describes the model's business purpose but omits material details about its version, configuration, interfaces, or dependencies.

**13.2 Unclear intended use**

The document states a broad objective without defining prohibited uses, operational boundaries, or the conditions under which the model has been evaluated.

**13.3 Unsupported evaluation claims**

The documentation states that the model is accurate, secure, fair, or robust without identifying the relevant tests, acceptance criteria, evidence, and limitations.

**13.4 Stale documentation**

The production model has changed, but the documentation still describes an earlier version or configuration.

**13.5 Inconsistent records**

The model registry, risk register, validation report, and deployment record use different identifiers or version numbers without a clear mapping.

**13.6 Missing accountability**

No individual or function is explicitly responsible for maintaining the documentation.

**13.7 Unrecorded exceptions**

Known limitations or unresolved findings are discussed informally but are not included in the controlled record or linked to an approved risk decision.

**13.8 Excessive reliance on supplier claims**

The organization copies vendor statements into internal documentation without evaluating their relevance, scope, evidence, or applicability to its own deployment.

**13.9 Poor evidence preservation**

Reports refer to test results that can no longer be located, or the evidence does not identify the model version that was evaluated.

**13.10 Inadequate access and integrity controls**

Documentation can be modified without authorization, or sensitive technical information is broadly accessible without a justified business need.

**13.11 Overly generic templates**

Templates are completed with boilerplate statements that do not describe the specific model, its actual controls, or its real limitations.

**13.12 Failure to address unavailable information**

The documentation silently omits important information that the organization could not obtain from a provider or supplier.

These deficiencies should be evaluated according to their consequences. A missing administrative field may be a minor issue for a low-impact model, whereas the absence of reliable version identification or validation evidence may prevent an organization from demonstrating that a high-impact model was appropriately assessed.

A mature governance process defines severity criteria, remediation requirements, escalation paths, and approval restrictions for significant documentation gaps.

### 14. Model Documentation Quality Assurance

Model documentation quality assurance is the process of verifying that records meet defined requirements before and after approval.

It differs from model performance testing. Performance testing evaluates the model's behavior; documentation quality assurance evaluates whether the information needed to understand, govern, operate, and assess the model is sufficiently accurate, complete, current, and traceable.

A quality assurance process should evaluate several dimensions.

| Quality dimension    | Example verification question                                                            |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness         | Are all required sections and supporting artifacts present?                              |
| Accuracy             | Do the records describe the actual model and its approved configuration?                 |
| Currency             | Does the documentation reflect the latest approved model version and material changes?   |
| Consistency          | Are identifiers and key facts consistent across related systems?                         |
| Traceability         | Can material claims be linked to supporting evidence?                                    |
| Clarity              | Can the intended audience understand the information and its limitations?                |
| Ownership            | Are document owners and reviewers identified?                                            |
| Approval             | Are required reviews and decisions recorded by authorized personnel?                     |
| Security             | Are classification, access, integrity, and retention controls appropriate?               |
| Regulatory alignment | Have applicable documentation and record-keeping requirements been mapped and addressed? |

Quality assurance may use automated checks, manual reviews, or a combination of both.

Automated checks can identify missing fields, invalid identifiers, expired review dates, broken evidence links, and inconsistent version references. Manual review remains important for assessing the quality of explanations, the adequacy of risk descriptions, the validity of conclusions, and the relevance of supporting evidence.

For example, an automated rule may verify that a validation report exists and contains a model identifier. A competent reviewer must still determine whether the report evaluates the correct use case, uses appropriate methods, addresses material risks, and supports the stated approval decision.

Quality assurance findings should be recorded and tracked through remediation. Where documentation does not meet mandatory requirements, the organization should apply its established exception, escalation, and approval procedures rather than silently treating the missing information as acceptable.

### 15. Practical Example — Documenting an AI Customer Support Classification Model

Consider an organization that deploys an AI model to classify customer support messages into operational categories and recommend routing destinations.

The model helps service teams prioritize requests and direct them to the appropriate department. It does not independently resolve customer complaints, but its classifications can influence response times and the handling of sensitive cases.

The organization creates a model documentation package covering the following areas.

**Model identity and ownership**

The package records the model identifier, version, business owner, technical owner, provider, lifecycle status, and deployment environment.

**Intended purpose and boundaries**

The model is approved to classify incoming support messages into defined categories. Its documentation specifies that the output is a routing recommendation and that certain sensitive or high-impact cases require human review.

**Data information**

The documentation identifies the relevant input sources, permitted processing purposes, data quality considerations, and applicable restrictions on personal or confidential information.

**Technical configuration**

The package records the model version, relevant configuration, interface dependencies, deployment settings, and the relationship between the approved configuration and the production deployment.

**Evaluation and validation**

The validation record describes the test methodology, representative test scenarios, classification metrics, error patterns, acceptance criteria, and known limitations. It also records how the organization evaluates misclassification risks for sensitive complaint categories.

**Security and risk management**

The package identifies relevant risks, such as unauthorized access to customer messages, manipulation of input content, inappropriate disclosure of sensitive information, and misrouting of important requests. It links these risks to the applicable controls and residual-risk decisions.

**Human oversight and operations**

The documentation defines which cases require manual review, who is responsible for escalation, how operational failures are reported, and what conditions trigger investigation or suspension.

**Approval and change history**

The package records the applicable approval decision, the model version approved, outstanding conditions, subsequent changes, regression test results, and any required reapproval.

**Monitoring and maintenance**

The organization establishes monitoring for classification quality, unusual error patterns, operational incidents, and relevant changes in incoming message characteristics. Material findings are linked to the model's risk and change records.

This example demonstrates why model documentation must connect technical information with governance decisions. A list of model parameters alone would not explain how the organization controls misclassification risks, assigns accountability, or determines whether the model remains suitable for its intended purpose.

It also illustrates why the documentation must be specific to the actual deployment. A general vendor description cannot replace evidence that the organization's selected configuration and operational controls have been assessed.

### 16. Model Documentation Maturity

Organizations can assess model documentation maturity to understand whether their processes are informal, repeatable, controlled, or continuously improved.

A maturity model provides a practical way to prioritize improvements and communicate documentation governance capabilities to management.

The following five-level model is an illustrative internal assessment framework, not a prescribed maturity scale from a particular standard.

| Level                | Maturity state                                        | Characteristics                                                                                               |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 — Ad hoc     | Documentation is inconsistent and reactive            | Records are created when requested; ownership and templates are unclear                                       |
| Level 2 — Repeatable | Basic documentation practices exist                   | Common templates and informal review processes are used by teams                                              |
| Level 3 — Defined    | Requirements and responsibilities are standardized    | Policies, templates, ownership, approval gates, and retention rules are formally established                  |
| Level 4 — Managed    | Compliance and quality are measured                   | Completeness, currency, evidence traceability, exceptions, and remediation are monitored                      |
| Level 5 — Optimized  | Documentation is integrated and continuously improved | Lifecycle workflows, registries, automated validation, change triggers, and assurance feedback are integrated |

A Level 1 organization may have technically capable development teams but still struggle to answer basic governance questions because records are scattered across personal folders and collaboration tools.

A Level 3 organization should have defined documentation requirements, clear accountability, controlled repositories, and consistent review practices.

A Level 4 organization should additionally be able to demonstrate measurable performance, such as the percentage of models with current documentation, the number of overdue reviews, the proportion of required evidence links that remain valid, and the time required to remediate significant gaps.

A Level 5 organization may integrate documentation updates with model registry changes, release pipelines, validation workflows, and GRC systems. Automation should support accountability rather than replace meaningful technical and governance review.

Maturity assessments should distinguish between the existence of a process and its effectiveness. A policy may be formally approved while teams routinely fail to follow it. Assessment should therefore combine document review, interviews, sampled record inspection, and testing of relevant workflows.

### 17. Key Performance Indicators for Model Documentation

Model documentation KPIs help management determine whether the documentation process is complete, current, efficient, and suitable for assurance activities.

Metrics should measure meaningful control outcomes rather than reward document volume or superficial completion.

Useful indicators include the following.

**17.1 Documentation completeness rate**

The percentage of in-scope models whose required documentation elements satisfy the organization's defined completeness criteria.

[\
\text{Completeness Rate} =\
\frac{\text{Models Meeting Documentation Criteria}}\
{\text{Models in Scope}} \times 100\
]

**17.2 Documentation currency rate**

The percentage of models whose documentation has been reviewed within the applicable review period and reflects the current approved model state.

**17.3 Evidence traceability rate**

The percentage of sampled material claims or control assertions that can be linked to appropriate supporting evidence.

**17.4 Overdue review rate**

The proportion of required documentation reviews that have passed their due dates without completion or an authorized exception.

**17.5 Documentation remediation time**

The average or median time taken to correct identified documentation deficiencies, reported by severity where appropriate.

**17.6 Material-change update compliance**

The percentage of sampled material model changes for which required documentation was updated and reviewed within the defined timeframe.

**17.7 Approval completeness**

The percentage of models requiring formal approval for which the applicable decision, authority, date, conditions, and model version are properly recorded.

**17.8 Significant documentation gap rate**

The proportion of models with one or more unresolved documentation gaps that exceed the organization's defined significance threshold.

An example dashboard could be structured as follows:

| KPI                        | Measurement objective                                   | Suggested management response                   |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Completeness rate          | Identify missing required information                   | Prioritize remediation by risk                  |
| Currency rate              | Identify outdated records                               | Trigger owner reviews and change-linked updates |
| Evidence traceability rate | Assess the substantiation of material claims            | Improve evidence linking and retention          |
| Overdue review rate        | Identify governance review delays                       | Escalate overdue high-risk model reviews        |
| Remediation time           | Evaluate the effectiveness of corrective action         | Address recurring bottlenecks                   |
| Change update compliance   | Verify documentation maintenance after material changes | Strengthen change-management gates              |
| Approval completeness      | Verify documented authorization                         | Investigate missing or invalid approvals        |
| Significant gap rate       | Measure unresolved material deficiencies                | Apply risk-based restrictions or escalation     |

KPIs should be interpreted in context. A high completeness rate does not necessarily mean that the documents are accurate or that the model is safe. A low remediation time is not necessarily positive if teams are closing findings without adequately correcting the underlying issue.

Metrics should therefore be complemented by qualitative review, risk-based sampling, and independent assurance where appropriate.

### 18. Integration of Model Documentation With GRC Systems

Model documentation becomes more effective when it is integrated with the organization's governance, risk, and compliance processes.

Without integration, teams may maintain separate model inventories, risk registers, validation records, security assessments, and compliance trackers that contain inconsistent information.

Integration creates relationships between these records and supports a coherent view of model risk, control implementation, approval status, and unresolved deficiencies.

Common integration points include:

- **AI inventory:** Provides the authoritative reference for model identity, ownership, purpose, and lifecycle status.
- **Risk register:** Links identified risks, risk ratings, treatment plans, residual risks, and acceptance decisions to the relevant model.
- **Control library:** Connects documentation requirements to applicable governance, security, privacy, and compliance controls.
- **Issue and remediation management:** Tracks documentation deficiencies, assigned owners, deadlines, escalation, and closure evidence.
- **Change management:** Connects model updates to impact assessments, testing, documentation revisions, and approvals.
- **Supplier risk management:** Records provider responsibilities, information limitations, contractual commitments, and supplier assurance evidence.
- **Incident management:** Links relevant incidents to affected model versions, known limitations, investigation records, and corrective actions.
- **Internal audit:** Enables auditors to identify in-scope models, select samples, retrieve documentation, and verify the operation of documentation controls.
- **Records management:** Enforces retention, archival, access, and disposition requirements.

A simplified GRC relationship model is:

```
AI Inventory
     |
     +----> Model Documentation
     |            |
     |            +----> Technical and Evaluation Evidence
     |            +----> Approval and Change Records
     |
     +----> AI Risk Register
     |            |
     |            +----> Risk Treatment
     |            +----> Residual Risk Acceptance
     |
     +----> Control Library
     |            |
     |            +----> Control Implementation Evidence
     |            +----> Testing and Assurance Results
     |
     +----> Issue and Remediation Management
                  |
                  +----> Corrective Actions
                  +----> Closure Evidence
```

Integration should preserve clear record ownership. A risk register should not become an uncontrolled duplicate of the model documentation, and a model repository should not be assumed to replace the organization's formal risk acceptance or compliance approval process.

The objective is to establish reliable relationships between authoritative records, minimize conflicting information, and make it possible to trace a governance requirement through implementation, evidence, review, and decision-making.

### 19. Documentation of Third-Party and Externally Hosted Models

Organizations frequently acquire models from external providers rather than developing them entirely in-house. Such arrangements create specific documentation challenges because the deploying organization may not have access to the provider's internal development records, training data, model architecture, or detailed evaluation results.

The organization should distinguish between information it controls directly, information supplied by the provider, information that can be independently verified, and information that remains unavailable.

**19.1 Provider documentation**

Collect relevant technical descriptions, usage restrictions, evaluation summaries, known limitations, security information, versioning details, and other documentation available under the provider's terms and applicable requirements.

**19.2 Deployment-specific documentation**

Record how the organization uses the model, including its configuration, interfaces, input and output handling, integration architecture, access controls, intended purpose, and operational safeguards.

**19.3 Information gaps**

Identify material information that the provider does not disclose. Explain why it matters, whether the gap affects risk assessment or compliance, and what alternative evidence or controls are available.

**19.4 Contractual accountability**

Where appropriate, establish contractual expectations for documentation availability, version-change notifications, incident cooperation, security information, audit rights, and retention or deletion obligations.

**19.5 Change transparency**

Record changes communicated by the provider and assess whether they affect the organization's approved use, evaluation assumptions, security posture, or risk profile.

**19.6 Independent assurance**

Evaluate whether supplier certifications, assessment reports, test summaries, or other assurances are relevant to the actual service and deployment. Such evidence should not automatically be treated as proof that the organization's specific implementation is compliant.

For example, an organization using an externally hosted language model may receive documentation about the service's general capabilities and safety limitations but no access to its full training dataset or internal model weights. Its documentation should accurately record this boundary, the provider's commitments, the organization's own integration controls, and the residual risks arising from limited transparency.

The organization should not invent unavailable technical details or represent assumptions as verified facts. It should assess whether the available information is sufficient for the intended use and whether additional safeguards, contractual protections, or deployment restrictions are necessary.

### 20. Establishing an Effective Model Documentation Program

An effective model documentation program combines policy, standardized requirements, assigned accountability, controlled repositories, lifecycle integration, quality assurance, and continuous improvement.

The objective is not to create the largest possible collection of documents. It is to ensure that the organization can establish what each model is, how it is used, what risks and limitations are known, which controls apply, who authorized its use, and what evidence supports those decisions.

A practical implementation sequence is as follows.

**Step 1 — Define the scope**

Determine which models and AI-enabled components fall within the documentation program. Establish how the organization treats internally developed models, third-party models, externally hosted services, embedded models, and material model updates.

**Step 2 — Map applicable requirements**

Identify relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, standards-based, and internal requirements. Distinguish mandatory obligations from voluntary practices and internal enhancements.

**Step 3 — Establish documentation standards**

Define the minimum documentation baseline, required artifacts, quality criteria, versioning rules, retention periods, and acceptable evidence sources.

**Step 4 — Assign ownership**

Identify the model owner and the people responsible for preparing, reviewing, approving, and maintaining each required artifact.

**Step 5 — Implement a controlled repository**

Establish authoritative storage, appropriate access controls, version history, integrity protection, retention mechanisms, and reliable retrieval.

**Step 6 — Integrate documentation into lifecycle workflows**

Make documentation requirements part of model intake, development, acquisition, validation, deployment, change management, monitoring, and retirement.

**Step 7 — Apply quality assurance**

Verify completeness, accuracy, consistency, evidence traceability, and approval status before relevant governance gates.

**Step 8 — Manage deficiencies**

Record missing information, assign remediation owners, establish deadlines, assess risk, and escalate material gaps through defined governance channels.

**Step 9 — Measure effectiveness**

Track documentation quality and process performance through risk-based KPIs, management reporting, sampled reviews, and independent assurance.

**Step 10 — Improve continuously**

Use audit findings, incidents, regulatory developments, operational feedback, and changes in AI technology to refine documentation requirements and control effectiveness.

The program should be supported by clear management sponsorship and a defined operating model. Documentation requirements that exist only in policy, without integration into actual engineering and governance workflows, are unlikely to remain consistently effective.

Ultimately, model documentation provides the information foundation for accountable AI governance. It enables organizations to connect technical implementation with risk decisions, operational controls, compliance obligations, and independent assurance while preserving an accurate record of how models are developed, approved, deployed, changed, and retired.
