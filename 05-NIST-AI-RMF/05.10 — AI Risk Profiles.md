# 05.10 — AI Risk Profiles

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Profiles

## 1. Introduction to AI Risk Profiles

An **AI Risk Profile** is a structured representation of the risks associated with a particular AI system, use case, organization, or operating environment.

Within the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, risk profiling helps an organization understand:

* What the AI system is intended to do.
* What risks may arise from its use.
* Which characteristics of trustworthy AI are most relevant.
* Which risks require treatment.
* Which controls and measurements are necessary.
* What residual risk remains after treatment.

An AI Risk Profile therefore converts a broad AI risk landscape into a structured view that can support governance and decision-making.

---

## 2. Why AI Risk Profiles Are Important

AI systems can create different types and levels of risk depending on:

* Their purpose.
* Their users.
* Their data.
* Their autonomy.
* Their environment.
* Their technology.
* Their stakeholders.
* Their potential impact.

For example, an AI tool used to summarize internal meeting notes may have relatively limited consequences, while an AI system supporting decisions about financial services, employment, healthcare, or access to important services may create substantially greater risks.

A standardized risk profile allows the organization to distinguish between these situations.

---

## 3. AI Risk Profile as a Structured Risk View

A practical AI Risk Profile can be represented as:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Business Purpose
    ↓
Context
    ↓
Stakeholders
    ↓
AI Capabilities
    ↓
Data
    ↓
Potential Benefits
    ↓
Potential Harms
    ↓
AI Risks
    ↓
Risk Rating
    ↓
Controls
    ↓
Residual Risk
```

This creates a structured relationship between the AI system and the organization's risk-management activities.

---

## 4. Risk Profile Versus Risk Assessment

An **AI Risk Profile** and an **AI Risk Assessment** are related but are not necessarily the same artifact.

### AI Risk Profile

Provides a structured view of the AI system and its risk characteristics.

### AI Risk Assessment

Evaluates specific risks in greater detail.

For example:

```text
AI Risk Profile
      ↓
Identifies:
Security Risk
Privacy Risk
Reliability Risk
Fairness Risk
Compliance Risk
      ↓
AI Risk Assessment
      ↓
Detailed evaluation of each risk
```

The profile provides context; the assessment provides deeper analysis.

---

## 5. Risk Profile Versus AI Inventory

An **AI inventory** answers:

> What AI systems do we have?

An **AI Risk Profile** answers:

> What is the risk landscape associated with this AI system?

For example:

| AI Inventory    | AI Risk Profile  |
| --------------- | ---------------- |
| System name     | System purpose   |
| Owner           | Stakeholders     |
| Provider        | Data sensitivity |
| Location        | Autonomy         |
| Status          | Potential impact |
| Technology      | Risk categories  |
| Lifecycle stage | Risk rating      |

The inventory identifies the system, while the profile provides a richer risk perspective.

---

## 6. Risk Profile and the NIST AI RMF

The AI Risk Profile can incorporate information generated across the four NIST AI RMF Functions.

| AI RMF Function | Contribution to Risk Profile           |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| GOVERN          | Accountability and governance context  |
| MAP             | System context and risk identification |
| MEASURE         | Evidence and risk measurements         |
| MANAGE          | Treatment and residual risk            |

This makes the profile a useful connecting artifact across the AI risk-management lifecycle.

---

---

## 8. AI System Identification

Every material AI system should have a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI System ID: AI-CUST-001
System Name: Customer Support Assistant
Business Unit: Customer Operations
Owner: Customer Operations Director
Technical Owner: AI Platform Team
Status: Production
Lifecycle Stage: Operational
```

A unique identifier allows the AI system to be consistently referenced across:

* GRC platforms.
* Risk registers.
* Control registers.
* Assessments.
* Audit records.
* Incident records.
* Monitoring systems.

---

## 9. Business Purpose

The profile should clearly explain why the AI system exists.

A weak description might be:

> "Customer service AI."

A stronger description would be:

> "An AI assistant that generates draft responses to customer inquiries using approved enterprise knowledge sources, with final responses reviewed by authorized customer-service personnel."

The second description provides important information about:

* Purpose.
* Users.
* Data.
* Human oversight.
* Autonomy.
* Potential risks.

---

## 10. Intended Use

The organization should explicitly document the intended use of the AI system.

For example:

> The system is intended to assist customer-service personnel in preparing responses to routine customer inquiries.

The intended use defines the context within which the AI system has been assessed.

---

## 11. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

An important governance consideration is the difference between intended and actual use.

```text
Intended Use
     ↓
Approved Behavior
     ↓
Actual User Behavior
     ↓
Potential Use-Case Expansion
```

Users may begin using an AI system for purposes that were never assessed.

For example:

> An AI assistant approved for drafting internal emails is later used to analyze confidential customer records.

This can introduce new risks and should trigger governance review.

---

## 12. Intended Users

The profile should identify who is expected to interact with the AI system.

Potential users include:

* Employees.
* Customers.
* Administrators.
* Developers.
* Analysts.
* Contractors.
* Partners.
* Automated systems.

User characteristics can affect the risk profile.

For example, an AI system used by trained security professionals may have different operational assumptions from one exposed directly to the general public.

---

## 13. Affected Parties

Not every affected person is an AI user.

The profile should therefore identify parties that may be affected by AI outputs or decisions.

Examples include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Applicants.
* Suppliers.
* Citizens.
* Patients.
* Business partners.

This distinction is important because AI impacts can extend beyond direct users.

---

## 14. AI System Context

The context section should explain the environment in which the AI operates.

Relevant information may include:

* Business process.
* Industry.
* Geographic scope.
* Regulatory environment.
* Organizational objectives.
* User population.
* Criticality.
* Decision authority.
* Operational dependencies.

Context helps determine whether a particular AI risk is material.

---

## 15. AI System Boundary

The profile should define the system boundary.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
Web Application
 ↓
AI Gateway
 ↓
Foundation Model
 ↓
Enterprise Knowledge Base
 ↓
External API
```

The risk profile should clarify which components are:

* Internally controlled.
* Third-party controlled.
* Shared.
* Outside organizational control.

---

## 16. AI Architecture

The profile may include a simplified architecture diagram.

Example:

```text
                User
                  ↓
          Customer Application
                  ↓
              AI Gateway
             ↙         ↘
       Knowledge       AI Model
          Base            ↓
             ↘          Output
               ↘          ↓
                Customer Agent
```

Architecture is relevant because risks may exist at multiple layers rather than only within the AI model.

---

## 17. AI Capabilities

The profile should identify what the AI can actually do.

Potential capabilities include:

* Generate text.
* Generate images.
* Summarize information.
* Classify data.
* Predict outcomes.
* Recommend decisions.
* Search enterprise information.
* Execute actions.
* Access APIs.
* Modify records.
* Communicate externally.

The greater the capability, the greater the potential attack and impact surface may become.

---

## 18. Level of Autonomy

Autonomy should be explicitly documented.

A simple spectrum is:

```text
Human Only
    ↓
AI Assistance
    ↓
AI Recommendation
    ↓
Human Approval
    ↓
Limited Automation
    ↓
High Automation
    ↓
Autonomous Action
```

An AI system that merely drafts content generally creates a different risk profile from an AI agent capable of independently modifying enterprise records.

---

## 19. Human Oversight

The profile should document:

* Whether humans review outputs.
* When review occurs.
* Who performs the review.
* Whether the reviewer can override the AI.
* What happens when the AI produces an unexpected result.
* Whether human intervention is mandatory.

Human oversight should be treated as part of the system design.

---

## 20. Data Characteristics

Data is a major component of an AI Risk Profile.

The organization should document:

* Data sources.
* Data types.
* Sensitivity.
* Personal information.
* Confidential information.
* Regulated information.
* Data volume.
* Data retention.
* Data ownership.
* Data location.

---

## 21. Data Classification

The profile may map AI data to the organization's existing data-classification scheme.

For example:

```text
Public
   ↓
Internal
   ↓
Confidential
   ↓
Restricted
```

The profile should identify the highest sensitivity level handled by the AI system.

---

## 22. Data Flow

The organization should understand where information moves.

Example:

```text
User Input
    ↓
Application
    ↓
AI Platform
    ↓
Model Provider
    ↓
Processing
    ↓
AI Output
    ↓
Enterprise Application
```

Questions include:

* Where is data processed?
* Who can access it?
* Is it retained?
* Is it used for training?
* Is it transmitted to third parties?
* Is it logged?

---

## 23. Model Characteristics

The profile should document relevant model characteristics.

Depending on the system, this could include:

* Model type.
* Model version.
* Provider.
* Training approach.
* Fine-tuning.
* Retrieval augmentation.
* Model size.
* Deployment model.
* Known limitations.

Not every technical detail is required for every risk profile.

---

## 24. Third-Party Dependencies

AI systems frequently depend on external providers.

The profile may identify:

* Cloud provider.
* AI platform.
* Foundation model provider.
* API provider.
* Data provider.
* Software components.
* External tools.

This allows third-party risks to be incorporated into the overall profile.

---

## 25. Regulatory Context

The profile should identify applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

Potential considerations include:

* Privacy.
* Cybersecurity.
* Consumer protection.
* Financial regulation.
* Employment.
* Healthcare.
* Industry-specific requirements.
* Geographic requirements.

Regulatory applicability should be determined based on the actual AI use case and operating environment.

---

## 26. Business Criticality

AI systems should be evaluated according to their importance to business operations.

For example:

### Low Criticality

AI used for optional employee productivity.

### Moderate Criticality

AI supporting customer-service operations.

### High Criticality

AI supporting a major operational process.

### Critical

AI embedded in a process where prolonged failure could create severe business consequences.

Business criticality can influence the required level of governance and resilience.

---

## 27. Potential Benefits

Risk profiling should not focus only on negative outcomes.

Potential benefits may include:

* Cost reduction.
* Productivity.
* Improved service.
* Faster analysis.
* Better decision support.
* Increased scalability.
* Improved accessibility.
* Innovation.

Understanding benefits helps management make balanced risk decisions.

---

## 28. Potential Harms

Potential harms may include:

* Security breaches.
* Privacy violations.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Discrimination.
* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Safety consequences.
* Reputational damage.
* Regulatory violations.

The profile should identify plausible harms rather than hypothetical risks without meaningful context.

---

## 29. Stakeholder Impact

The profile should identify how stakeholders could be affected.

For example:

| Stakeholder | Potential Benefit   | Potential Harm        |
| ----------- | ------------------- | --------------------- |
| Customer    | Faster service      | Incorrect information |
| Employee    | Reduced workload    | Overreliance          |
| Business    | Efficiency          | Operational risk      |
| Regulator   | Better transparency | Compliance concerns   |

This helps broaden the risk assessment beyond the organization itself.

---

## 30. AI Risk Categories

An AI Risk Profile can categorize identified risks.

Common categories include:

```text

Fairness

```

The taxonomy should be aligned with the organization's enterprise risk-management approach.

---

## 31. Risk Description

Each material AI risk should have a clear description.

Weak:

> "Security risk."

Stronger:

> "An attacker may manipulate AI inputs to bypass system restrictions and cause the AI application to access or disclose information outside the user's authorized scope."

A good risk description identifies:

* Threat.
* Vulnerability.
* Event.
* Consequence.

---

## 32. Risk Cause

The profile should identify why a risk could occur.

Examples:

* Poor access controls.
* Insufficient testing.
* Inadequate training data.
* Weak prompt isolation.
* Model limitations.
* Excessive autonomy.
* Vendor dependency.
* Inadequate monitoring.

Understanding the cause supports effective treatment.

---

## 33. Risk Consequence

The consequence describes what could happen if the risk materializes.

Examples:

* Confidential information disclosure.
* Incorrect customer decision.
* Financial loss.
* Regulatory penalty.
* Service disruption.
* Reputational damage.

Risk consequences should be expressed in business terms where possible.

---

## 34. Risk Likelihood

The organization should estimate the likelihood of the risk materializing.

A simple scale may be:

```text
1 — Rare
2 — Unlikely
3 — Possible
4 — Likely
5 — Almost Certain
```

The actual scale should align with the enterprise risk methodology.

---

## 35. Risk Impact

Impact may consider:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Legal.
* Regulatory.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Safety.
* Reputational.

A simple scale could be:

```text
1 — Insignificant
2 — Minor
3 — Moderate
4 — Major
5 — Severe
```

The actual definitions should be organization-specific.

---

## 36. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents exposure before considering controls.

For example:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

Inherent Risk = 20
```

This establishes the initial risk position.

---

## 37. Existing Controls

The profile should identify controls already implemented.

Examples:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Encryption.
* Data-loss prevention.
* Human review.
* Output filtering.
* Security testing.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Vendor controls.

Controls should be linked to the risks they address.

---

## 38. Control Effectiveness

The profile should distinguish between:

### Control Exists

The organization has implemented the control.

### Control Is Effective

Evidence demonstrates that the control actually reduces the relevant risk.

This distinction is critical for accurate residual-risk assessment.

---

## 39. Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk remaining after controls are considered.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Risk = High
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓
Risk = Moderate
```

The organization then determines whether the residual risk is acceptable.

---

## 40. Risk Acceptance

If residual risk remains, an authorized person may need to accept it.

The profile can document:

* Residual risk.
* Business justification.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Acceptance date.
* Review date.
* Conditions.

Risk acceptance should not be confused with eliminating the risk.

---

## 41. Risk Treatment

The profile should identify planned treatment where residual risk is unacceptable.

Treatment options include:

* Avoid.
* Mitigate.
* Transfer.
* Accept.

Treatment actions should have:

* Owner.
* Target date.
* Status.
* Validation method.

---

## 43. Risk Profile as a Living Document

An AI Risk Profile should not necessarily remain static.

It may need updating when:

* The model changes.
* The data changes.
* The AI capability expands.
* The user population changes.
* New integrations are introduced.
* Risk thresholds change.
* An incident occurs.
* Regulations change.
* The AI system changes purpose.

Therefore:

> **The AI Risk Profile should evolve with the AI system and its operating environment.**

---

## 44. Risk Profile Review Frequency

Review frequency should be risk-based.

For example:

| Risk Level | Possible Review       |
| ---------- | --------------------- |
| Low        | Annual                |
| Moderate   | Semiannual            |
| High       | Quarterly             |
| Critical   | Continuous / frequent |

These intervals are illustrative. Organizations should establish their own review requirements.

---

## 45. Risk Profile Ownership

Ownership should be clearly defined.

A typical structure is:

```text
Business Owner
      ↓
AI System Owner
      ↓
Risk Owner
      ↓
Control Owners
      ↓
GRC / Assurance
```

Different organizations may combine some responsibilities.

The important principle is that accountability must be explicit.

---

## 46. Risk Profile and Decision-Making

The AI Risk Profile should support practical decisions.

Management may use it to decide whether to:

* Approve deployment.
* Require additional controls.
* Restrict use.
* Accept residual risk.
* Escalate risk.
* Delay deployment.
* Reject the use case.
* Reassess the system.

The profile is therefore a decision-support artifact, not merely documentation.

---

## 47. Risk Profile and AI Lifecycle

The profile should follow the AI system through its lifecycle.

```text
Concept
  ↓
Design
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Operation
  ↓
Change
  ↓
Retirement
```

Risk characteristics may change at each stage.

---

## 48. Risk Profile at the Concept Stage

At the concept stage, the profile may focus on:

* Intended purpose.
* Expected benefits.
* Stakeholders.
* Potential harms.
* Regulatory context.
* Initial risk classification.

This provides an early governance decision point.

---

## 49. Risk Profile During Development

During development, additional information becomes available:

* Architecture.
* Model.
* Data sources.
* Integrations.
* Security controls.
* Testing requirements.

The profile should be updated as these characteristics become known.

---

## 50. Risk Profile Before Deployment

Before production deployment, the profile should contain sufficient information to support a formal risk decision.

Potential requirements include:

* Completed assessments.
* Testing results.
* Control status.
* Residual risk.
* Approval.
* Monitoring plan.

---

## 51. Risk Profile During Operations

Operational updates may include:

* Performance.
* Incidents.
* Model drift.
* New threats.
* Control failures.
* Changes in usage.
* Risk indicators.

This turns the profile into an operational risk-management artifact.

---

## 52. Risk Profile During Retirement

At retirement, the organization may update the profile to document:

* Retirement decision.
* Data disposition.
* Access removal.
* Model decommissioning.
* Vendor termination.
* Residual obligations.
* Lessons learned.

This ensures governance extends through the complete AI lifecycle.

---

## 53. Key Takeaways

The fundamental principles of AI Risk Profiles are:

1. An AI Risk Profile provides a structured view of AI-related risk.
2. It should describe the AI system and its operating context.
3. It should identify intended use and intended users.
4. It should identify affected stakeholders.
5. It should document data and system boundaries.
6. It should identify AI capabilities and autonomy.
7. It should consider potential benefits and harms.
8. It should identify material AI risk categories.
9. It should document inherent risk.
10. It should identify existing controls.
11. It should assess residual risk.
12. It should support risk-treatment decisions.
13. It should identify accountable owners.
14. It should support governance decisions.
15. It should be updated when material changes occur.
16. It should follow the AI system through its lifecycle.
17. It should integrate with enterprise GRC processes.
18. It should provide evidence for management and assurance.
19. It should be proportional to the AI system's risk.
20. It should remain a living representation of the AI system's risk posture.

## Part 2 — Developing and Structuring an AI Risk Profile

## 54. AI Risk Profile Development Process

Developing an AI Risk Profile should follow a structured process rather than relying on informal judgment.

A practical sequence is:

```text
Identify AI System
        ↓
Define Purpose and Context
        ↓
Identify Stakeholders
        ↓
Understand Architecture and Data
        ↓
Identify Potential Benefits and Harms
        ↓
Identify AI Risks
        ↓
Assess Inherent Risk
        ↓
Identify Controls
        ↓
Assess Residual Risk
        ↓
Define Treatment
        ↓
Approve and Maintain Profile
```

The depth of each activity should be proportional to the AI system's risk.

---

## 55. Step 1 — Identify the AI System

The first step is to establish exactly what system is being profiled.

The organization should identify:

* System name.
* Unique identifier.
* Business unit.
* Business owner.
* Technical owner.
* AI provider.
* Model or service.
* Deployment environment.
* Lifecycle stage.
* Current status.

This prevents ambiguity about which AI system the profile represents.

---

## 56. AI System Identifier

A standardized naming convention can improve governance.

For example:

```text
AI-HR-001
AI-FIN-002
AI-CUST-003
AI-SEC-004
```

The identifier can incorporate:

* AI classification.
* Business function.
* Sequential number.

The exact naming convention should follow organizational standards.

---

## 57. Step 2 — Define the Business Purpose

The profile should describe the business problem the AI system is intended to solve.

A useful structure is:

> The AI system is used to [perform activity] for [user group] in order to [business objective].

Example:

> The AI system is used to summarize customer-service interactions for authorized customer-service personnel in order to reduce manual documentation effort.

This creates a clear connection between AI capability and business purpose.

---

## 58. Step 3 — Document Intended Use

The profile should explicitly define approved use.

Example:

```text
Approved:
Summarize customer-service conversations.

Not Approved:
Make autonomous customer eligibility decisions.
```

Documenting prohibited or out-of-scope uses can help prevent **function creep**.

---

## 59. Function Creep

Function creep occurs when an AI system gradually expands beyond its originally approved purpose.

Example:

```text
Original:
Employee productivity assistant

Expansion:
Analyze employee performance

Further expansion:
Recommend disciplinary action
```

Each expansion can introduce new risks.

Material changes in purpose should trigger reassessment.

---

## 60. Step 4 — Identify Stakeholders

Stakeholder identification should consider both direct and indirect participants.

Potential stakeholders include:

* Business owner.
* AI system owner.
* Developers.
* Security team.
* Privacy team.
* Legal team.
* Compliance.
* Internal audit.
* End users.
* Customers.
* Individuals affected by outputs.
* Regulators.
* Third-party providers.

The stakeholder list should reflect the actual AI use case.

---

## 61. Stakeholder Responsibility Matrix

A RACI-style model can clarify responsibilities.

| Activity         | Business | AI Team | Security | Privacy | GRC |
| ---------------- | -------- | ------- | -------- | ------- | --- |
| Purpose          | A        | C       | C        | C       | C   |
| Architecture     | C        | A/R     | C        | C       | C   |
| Risk Assessment  | A        | R       | C        | C       | R   |
| Security Testing | C        | R       | A        | C       | C   |
| Privacy Review   | C        | C       | C        | A/R     | C   |
| Approval         | A        | C       | C        | C       | R   |
| Monitoring       | A        | R       | R        | C       | C   |

The letters represent:

* **R** — Responsible.
* **A** — Accountable.
* **C** — Consulted.

The exact responsibility model should be organization-specific.

---

## 62. Step 5 — Define the AI System Boundary

The organization should determine what components belong within the risk profile.

For example:

```text
                 AI System Boundary
┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                         │
│  Application                            │
│       ↓                                 │
│  AI Gateway                             │

│       ↓                                 │
│  Enterprise Knowledge Base              │
│                                         │

          ↓
External AI Provider
```

External dependencies should still be identified even when they are outside the organization's direct control.

---

## 63. Step 6 — Document AI Architecture

The architecture section should describe the major technical components.

Potential components include:

* User interface.
* Application.
* AI gateway.
* Model.
* Prompt layer.
* Retrieval system.
* Vector database.
* Enterprise database.
* API integrations.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Security controls.

The objective is not necessarily to document every technical detail but to identify components relevant to risk.

---

## 64. AI Architecture and Risk

Architecture directly influences the risk profile.

For example:

```text
Simple AI
User → Model → Output
```

may have fewer dependencies than:

```text
User
 ↓
Application
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Model
 ↓
RAG
 ↓
Enterprise Database
 ↓
External APIs
 ↓
Automated Actions
```

The second architecture creates additional risks involving:

* Data access.
* Authorization.
* Tool execution.
* API security.
* Agent autonomy.
* Dependency management.

---

## 65. Step 7 — Identify AI Data

The profile should identify all important data used by the AI system.

Data may include:

* Training data.
* Fine-tuning data.
* Retrieval data.
* User prompts.
* User-provided files.
* System instructions.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* Feedback data.

Each category may create different risks.

## 66. Data Ownership

The organization should determine who owns or controls relevant data.

Potential owners include:

* Business departments.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Vendors.
* Public sources.
* Third-party data providers.

Data ownership can affect:

* Authorization.
* Usage rights.
* Retention.
* Sharing.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.

## 67. Data Sensitivity

Data should be evaluated according to organizational classification.

Example:

| Data Type    | Example                 | Risk      |
| ------------ | ----------------------- | --------- |
| Public       | Public website          | Lower     |
| Internal     | Internal procedures     | Moderate  |
| Confidential | Business plans          | High      |

The table is illustrative and should align with the organization's classification model.

## 68. Data Quality

The profile should consider whether data is:

* Accurate.
* Complete.
* Relevant.
* Current.
* Representative.
* Consistent.
* Traceable.

Poor data quality can create downstream AI risks even when the model itself functions correctly.

* Compliance.

## 70. Step 8 — Identify AI Capabilities

* Recommend.
* Search.
* Summarize.
* Extract.
* Analyze.
* Execute.
* Modify.
* Communicate.

Capabilities should be evaluated against the actual implementation rather than marketing descriptions.

## 71. AI Capability Risk

| ----------------- | --------------------- |

| Tool execution    | Unauthorized action   |
| Autonomous action | Loss of human control |

## 72. Step 9 — Assess Autonomy

Autonomy should be explicitly documented.

A practical classification is:

| Level | Description                          |
| ----- | ------------------------------------ |
| 0     | No AI action                         |
| 1     | AI provides information              |
| 2     | AI recommends                        |
| 3     | Human approves AI action             |
| 4     | AI performs limited automated action |
| 5     | AI performs autonomous actions       |

These levels are illustrative.

The important point is to clearly document how much decision or operational authority the AI possesses.

## 73. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop design requires human involvement before a defined AI action occurs.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Approve / Reject
      ↓
Business Action
```

This can reduce certain risks but should not automatically be treated as a sufficient control.

The effectiveness of human oversight depends on:

* Reviewer competence.
* Review timing.
* Information available.
* Workload.
* Authority to override.
* Ability to detect AI errors.

---

## 74. Human-on-the-Loop

In a human-on-the-loop model, AI performs actions while humans supervise the system.

```text
AI Action
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Human Intervention
if required
```

This may be appropriate for certain automated processes but requires effective monitoring and intervention mechanisms.

---

## 75. Human-in-Command

A stronger governance model maintains meaningful human authority over the AI system.

Human decision-makers retain authority to:

* Approve deployment.
* Define operating boundaries.
* Override decisions.
* Suspend the system.
* Change configuration.
* Retire the system.

This supports accountability for higher-risk AI applications.

## 76. Step 10 — Identify Potential Benefits

The organization should document expected benefits.

Examples:

* Productivity improvement.
* Cost reduction.
* Faster processing.
* Improved customer experience.
* Better accessibility.
* Improved analysis.
* Reduced manual work.

Benefits should be measurable where possible.

## 77. Benefit Measurement

A benefit statement can be converted into a measurable objective.

For example:

> Reduce customer-service response preparation time.

Potential measurement:

```text

Expected Improvement:
37.5%
```

Figures are illustrative.

Benefit measurement helps determine whether AI provides sufficient value to justify its risks and costs.

A useful model is:

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

For example:

```text

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

```

## 79. Direct and Indirect Harm

AI harms may be:

### Direct

The AI itself produces the harmful output.

### Indirect

The AI output influences another system or human who produces the final harmful result.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Human Decision
      ↓

```

Risk profiling should consider the complete chain.

## 84. Step 13 — Assess Inherent Risk

## 85. Risk Scoring Is Not the Entire Assessment

A numerical score can help prioritize risks, but it should not replace professional judgment.

For example:

```text

Risk B = 12
```

Therefore, the profile should retain qualitative information alongside numerical ratings.

The organization does not understand where AI data goes.

### 5. Static profiles

The profile is never updated after deployment.

### 6. Control-centric assessment

The organization lists controls without first understanding the risk.

### 7. No residual-risk assessment

The organization does not determine whether remaining risk is acceptable.

# 05.10 — AI Risk Profiles

## Part 3 — AI Risk Profile Analysis, Risk Scenarios, and Risk Treatment

### 112. AI Risk Profile Analysis

Once an AI Risk Profile has been established, the organization should analyze the information collected to determine the actual risk exposure.

The analysis should connect:

```text
AI Context
    ↓
AI Characteristics
    ↓
Potential Harm
    ↓
Risk Scenario
    ↓
Likelihood
    ↓
Impact
    ↓
Risk Level
    ↓
Treatment
```

The objective is to move from descriptive information to a defensible risk decision.

---

### 113. Context-Driven Risk Analysis

AI risk cannot be evaluated independently from context.

The same AI capability may create different levels of risk depending on how it is used.

For example:

```text
AI Capability:
Generate recommendations

Use Case A:
Recommend internal meeting times
→ Low consequence

Use Case B:
Recommend employee disciplinary actions
→ Higher consequence

Use Case C:
Recommend medical treatment
→ Potentially very high consequence
```

Therefore, risk profiling should evaluate the **AI system in its intended operating context**, not merely the underlying technology.

---

### 114. AI Risk Scenario Development

Risk scenarios provide a practical way to translate AI characteristics into potential adverse outcomes.

A useful structure is:

> **Source or condition → AI event → consequence → affected stakeholder**

Example:

> A malicious user manipulates an AI assistant's instructions, causing the system to retrieve unauthorized internal information, resulting in disclosure of confidential business data.

This is more useful than recording only:

> Prompt injection risk.

---

### 115. Risk Scenario Components

Each material scenario should ideally identify:

| Component   | Question                          |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- |
| Source      | What could cause the event?       |
| Weakness    | What makes the event possible?    |
| Event       | What could happen?                |
| AI Behavior | How could the AI respond?         |
| Consequence | What would happen next?           |
| Stakeholder | Who could be affected?            |
| Risk        | What organizational risk results? |

This structure supports consistent analysis.

---

### 116. Threat-Based Scenarios

Threat-based scenarios consider deliberate actions against the AI system.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection.
* Data poisoning.
* Model manipulation.
* Unauthorized access.
* Model extraction.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Tool abuse.
* Credential compromise.

The organization should evaluate whether the AI architecture creates opportunities for these threats.

---

### 117. Non-Malicious AI Risk Scenarios

Not every AI risk requires an attacker.

Examples include:

* Hallucination.
* Incorrect classification.
* Data-quality problems.
* Model drift.
* Unexpected outputs.
* Excessive reliance on AI.
* Incorrect human interpretation.
* Configuration errors.

A comprehensive profile therefore considers both **malicious and non-malicious sources of risk**.

---

### 118. Misuse Scenarios

Users may intentionally or unintentionally use AI outside its approved purpose.

Examples:

```text
Approved:
Internal document summarization

Misuse:
Processing restricted customer information

Potential consequence:
Privacy or confidentiality violation
```

Misuse scenarios should be considered when defining AI controls and monitoring.

---

### 119. Abuse Scenarios

Abuse involves deliberate exploitation of AI capabilities.

Examples include:

* Using an AI system to generate malicious content.
* Attempting to bypass safeguards.
* Using AI access to retrieve unauthorized information.
* Manipulating AI outputs.
* Exploiting connected tools.

The profile should identify abuse scenarios relevant to the system's capabilities.

---

### 120. Failure Scenarios

Failure scenarios consider what happens when the AI does not behave as expected.

Examples:

* Incorrect recommendation.
* Missing information.
* Model failure.
* Service outage.
* Incorrect classification.
* Hallucinated information.
* Unexpected automated action.

Failure analysis is particularly important where AI outputs influence consequential decisions.

---

### 121. Dependency Failure

AI systems may depend on external services.

A risk scenario could be:

```text
External Model Provider Outage
          ↓
AI Application Unavailable
          ↓
Business Process Disrupted
          ↓
Operational Impact
```

The profile should therefore consider availability and resilience dependencies.

---

### 122. AI Risk Interdependencies

AI risks can interact with one another.

For example:

```text
Poor Data Quality
       ↓
Incorrect Model Output
       ↓
Human Overreliance
       ↓
Incorrect Decision
       ↓
Regulatory / Reputational Impact
```

This means individual risks should not always be evaluated in isolation.

---

### 123. Risk Cascading

A small AI failure can sometimes create a larger business impact.

Example:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Incorrect Customer Information
   ↓
Customer Complaint
   ↓
Operational Escalation
   ↓
Regulatory Investigation
   ↓
Reputational Damage
```

The profile should consider the complete chain where consequences could become material.

---

### 124. Risk Concentration

Multiple AI systems may create the same type of exposure.

For example:

```text
AI Customer Assistant ─┐
AI Employee Assistant ─┼→ Data Leakage Exposure
AI Analytics Platform ─┤
AI Agent Platform ─────┘
```

The enterprise should consider the aggregate exposure rather than examining each system independently.

---

### 125. Common Risk Across Multiple Systems

A shared infrastructure component can create systemic AI risk.

For example:

```text
Multiple AI Applications
          ↓
Shared AI Gateway
          ↓
Common Vulnerability
          ↓
Multiple Systems Exposed
```

The risk profile should identify important shared dependencies.

---

### 126. Risk Inheritance

An AI system may inherit risk from:

* Cloud infrastructure.
* Foundation models.
* Data providers.
* APIs.
* Software libraries.
* Third-party services.
* Enterprise identity systems.

Therefore, risk analysis should distinguish between:

**System-specific risk**

and

**Inherited or dependency-related risk**.

---

### 127. Risk Classification

Organizations can classify AI risks into categories.

A practical structure is:

```text
AI Risk
├── Cybersecurity
├── Privacy
├── Safety
├── Reliability
├── Fairness
├── Transparency
├── Explainability
├── Compliance
├── Operational
├── Third-Party
├── Strategic
└── Reputational
```

The taxonomy should be aligned with the enterprise risk framework.

---

### 128. Cybersecurity Risk

Cybersecurity risks may include:

* Unauthorized access.
* Prompt injection.
* Data leakage.
* Model manipulation.
* Malicious inputs.
* Insecure APIs.
* Excessive privileges.
* Compromised dependencies.

The profile should connect technical weaknesses to business consequences.

---

### 129. Privacy Risk

Privacy risks may include:

* Unauthorized personal-data processing.
* Excessive data collection.
* Data leakage.
* Improper retention.
* Inappropriate secondary use.
* Insufficient transparency.

Privacy risk should consider the entire AI data lifecycle.

---

### 130. Safety Risk

Safety risk becomes particularly important when AI can influence physical or high-consequence outcomes.

Potential examples include:

* Incorrect operational instructions.
* Unsafe recommendations.
* Failure to detect dangerous conditions.
* Inappropriate autonomous actions.

Safety considerations should be proportional to the AI system's intended use.

---

### 131. Fairness Risk

Fairness risks can occur when AI produces materially different outcomes for different groups.

Potential causes include:

* Biased data.
* Underrepresentation.
* Poor model design.
* Inappropriate thresholds.
* Proxy variables.

The organization should identify whether fairness is relevant to the particular use case.

---

### 132. Transparency Risk

Stakeholders may not understand:

* That AI is being used.
* What the AI is intended to do.
* What limitations exist.
* How outputs are used.
* Who remains accountable.

Transparency requirements should reflect the system's context and impact.

---

### 133. Explainability Risk

Explainability becomes especially important where AI outputs influence significant decisions.

The profile should consider:

* Whether explanations are possible.
* Who needs the explanation.
* What level of detail is necessary.
* Whether the explanation is technically meaningful.
* Whether users can challenge or review the result.

---

### 134. Reliability Risk

Reliability risk addresses whether the AI consistently performs as expected.

Potential indicators include:

* Error rate.
* Failure rate.
* Model drift.
* Availability.
* Inconsistent outputs.
* Unexpected behavior.

Reliability should be assessed against the system's intended operating conditions.

---

### 135. Compliance Risk

Compliance risk can result when AI use conflicts with:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Contracts.
* Internal policies.
* Industry requirements.
* Customer commitments.

The AI Risk Profile should identify relevant compliance obligations rather than assuming that generic AI governance is sufficient.

---

### 136. Operational Risk

Operational risks may include:

* Service interruption.
* Incorrect automation.
* Excessive manual intervention.
* Dependency failure.
* Poor change management.
* Inadequate support.
* Insufficient monitoring.

These risks should be evaluated against business criticality.

---

### 137. Third-Party AI Risk

Third-party AI providers can introduce risks involving:

* Data processing.
* Security.
* Availability.
* Model changes.
* Subprocessors.
* Contractual limitations.
* Vendor concentration.
* Exit difficulty.

Third-party risks should be incorporated into the AI Risk Profile rather than treated entirely separately.

---

### 138. Strategic Risk

AI can create strategic risks when:

* Business objectives are not achieved.
* AI investment does not produce expected value.
* Competitors gain an advantage.
* The organization becomes overly dependent on a provider.
* AI adoption conflicts with business strategy.

Risk profiling should therefore consider both downside exposure and strategic opportunity.

---

### 139. Reputational Risk

AI incidents can damage stakeholder trust.

Potential triggers include:

* Public AI failures.
* Privacy incidents.
* Biased outcomes.
* Harmful outputs.
* Poor transparency.
* Misuse of customer information.

Reputational impact should be considered when material.

---

### 140. Risk Scoring

Organizations may use a standardized risk-scoring methodology.

A basic model is:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

Example:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

Risk Score = 20
```

The organization can then map scores to risk categories.

---

### 141. Example Risk Matrix

| Likelihood     |   Impact | Example Rating |
| -------------- | -------: | -------------- |
| Rare           |      Low | Low            |
| Possible       | Moderate | Medium         |
| Likely         |    Major | High           |
| Almost Certain |   Severe | Critical       |

The organization should define its own formal thresholds.

---

### 142. Qualitative Risk Assessment

Not every AI risk should be reduced to a number.

Qualitative analysis may consider:

* Severity.
* Uncertainty.
* Reversibility.
* Detectability.
* Stakeholder sensitivity.
* Regulatory importance.

For certain AI risks, qualitative factors can materially influence the final decision.

---

### 143. Risk Uncertainty

AI risk assessments often involve uncertainty because:

* Model behavior can be probabilistic.
* Data may be incomplete.
* Future use may be unpredictable.
* Threats may evolve.
* Models may change.
* External dependencies may change.

The profile should document important assumptions and uncertainties.

---

### 144. Risk Assumptions

Examples include:

> Human review is assumed to occur before customer-facing output is released.

or:

> The model provider is assumed not to use customer data for model training under the applicable contractual terms.

Important assumptions should be validated rather than treated as permanent facts.

---

### 145. Risk Confidence

An organization may also record confidence in its risk assessment.

For example:

| Risk          | Assessment | Confidence |
| ------------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Data leakage  | High       | High       |

### 147. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

A practical treatment hierarchy is:

1. Eliminate the activity.
2. Reduce the risk through design.
3. Implement technical controls.
4. Implement organizational controls.
5. Introduce human oversight.
6. Monitor residual risk.
7. Accept the remaining exposure when authorized.

The strongest approach is often to address risk as early as possible in system design.

---

### 148. Secure-by-Design AI

AI risk treatment should begin during architecture and design.

For example:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Architecture Change
      ↓
Reduced Exposure
      ↓
Fewer Compensating Controls
```

Addressing risk at design time can be more effective than attempting to correct it after deployment.

### 149. Privacy-by-Design AI

Privacy risks should also be considered during system design.

Potential approaches include:

* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Access restrictions.
* Retention controls.
* Anonymization or pseudonymization where appropriate.
* Privacy-preserving architectures.

The applicable controls should reflect the organization's legal and privacy requirements.

---

### 150. Human Oversight as a Risk Treatment

Human oversight can reduce some AI risks.

However, the organization should assess whether the human actually has the ability to identify and correct AI errors.

A weak control is:

> "Human reviews AI output."

A stronger control specifies:

* Who reviews.
* What they review.
* What thresholds trigger intervention.
* What training is required.
* Whether they can override the AI.
* What evidence is recorded.

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

Risk reduced

### 172. Key Takeaways

Effective AI Risk Profile analysis should:

1. Evaluate AI within its actual context.
2. Develop realistic risk scenarios.
3. Consider malicious and non-malicious risks.
4. Consider misuse and abuse.
5. Consider system failure.
6. Consider third-party and dependency risk.
7. Identify interconnected risks.
8. Identify systemic and concentration risks.
9. Use consistent risk categories.
10. Assess likelihood and impact.
11. Document uncertainty and assumptions.
12. Consider qualitative factors alongside numerical scores.
13. Compare residual risk with appetite and tolerance.

15. Validate that treatments reduce risk.
16. Reassess risk when controls fail.

### 220. Risk-Based Tiering

Not every AI system requires the same level of profiling.

A practical model might be:

| Tier   | Profile Depth | Governance                    |
| ------ | ------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Tier 1 | Basic         | Business approval             |
| Tier 2 | Standard      | GRC review                    |
| Tier 3 | Enhanced      | Multidisciplinary review      |
| Tier 4 | Critical      | Executive / formal governance |

The organization should define the criteria for each tier.

### 221. Factors for AI Risk Tiering

Potential factors include:

* Impact on individuals.
* Data sensitivity.
* Degree of autonomy.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Number of affected users.
* Potential financial impact.
* Safety implications.
* External exposure.
* Third-party dependency.

Tiering should be risk-based rather than technology-based alone.

### 222. Proportionality Principle

A low-risk internal productivity assistant should not necessarily require the same documentation burden as a high-impact autonomous AI system.

The principle is:

> **More potential impact should generally result in stronger governance, deeper assessment, stronger controls, and greater assurance.**

This makes AI governance practical and scalable.

### 224. Common Implementation Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

**Mistake 1 — Treating the profile as paperwork**

The profile should support decisions.

**Mistake 2 — Profiling only the model**

The complete AI system should be considered.

**Mistake 3 — Ignoring users**

Human behavior can materially affect AI risk.

**Mistake 4 — Ignoring third parties**

External providers may introduce significant risk.

**Mistake 5 — Ignoring changes**

AI systems evolve rapidly.

**Mistake 6 — Relying only on numerical scores**

Context and professional judgment remain important.

**Mistake 7 — Assuming human review eliminates risk**

Human oversight must be effective and meaningful.

### 227. Final Key Takeaways

The most important principles are:

1. AI Risk Profiles should be integrated into enterprise AI governance.
2. The profile should connect the AI inventory to risk management.
3. Material AI risks should integrate with the enterprise risk register.
4. AI controls should connect to existing enterprise control frameworks.
5. AI-specific controls should be introduced where generic controls are insufficient.
6. The profile can support all four NIST AI RMF Functions.
7. It can provide evidence for AI management systems such as ISO/IEC 42001.
8. It can connect AI risks with information-security governance.
9. Third-party AI risk should be incorporated into the profile.
10. Procurement should consider AI risk before deployment.
11. Architecture should address identified risks early.
12. Testing should validate important controls and risk assumptions.
13. Monitoring should detect changes in AI risk.
14. Material changes should trigger reassessment.
15. AI incidents should update risk understanding.
16. AI agents require explicit profiling of permissions and autonomy.
17. Least privilege should apply to AI systems and agents.
18. High-risk AI may require multidisciplinary governance.
19. Risk profiles should be proportionate to the AI system's potential impact.
20. Automation can improve scalability but should not replace judgment.
21. Profiles should support auditability and evidence-based assurance.
22. Executive reporting should summarize portfolio-level AI risk.
23. The profile should remain a living governance artifact.
24. The ultimate objective is not documentation but **informed, accountable, and risk-based AI decision-making**.
