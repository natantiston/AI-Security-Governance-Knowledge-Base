# 05.11 — AI Risk Identification and Documentation

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Identification and Documentation

## 1. Introduction to AI Risk Identification

**AI Risk Identification** is the systematic process of discovering, describing, and documenting potential risks associated with an AI system throughout its lifecycle.

Within the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, risk identification is primarily supported by understanding the AI system's:

* Intended purpose.
* Context of use.
* Stakeholders.
* Data.
* Models.
* Components.
* Dependencies.
* Capabilities.
* Limitations.
* Potential benefits.
* Potential harms.

The objective is not simply to create a list of technical problems.

The objective is to understand:

> **What could go wrong, why could it happen, who could be affected, and what consequences could result?**

---

## 2. Why AI Risk Identification Is Important

AI systems can introduce risks that may not exist in conventional software systems or may amplify existing risks.

For example, a traditional application may return a deterministic database value, while an AI system may:

* Generate incorrect information.
* Produce different outputs for similar inputs.
* Infer sensitive information.
* Reflect limitations in training data.
* Be manipulated through adversarial inputs.
* Make recommendations that users incorrectly treat as authoritative.
* Interact with external systems.

Therefore, AI risk identification must consider both **technology risk** and **human, organizational, societal, and business consequences**.

---

## 3. AI Risk Identification as a Continuous Activity

AI risk identification should not occur only before deployment.

A practical lifecycle is:

```text
Concept
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

Risks can emerge or change at every stage.

For example:

```text
Development Risk
      ↓
New Data Source
      ↓
New Model Capability
      ↓
New User Population
      ↓
New Operational Risk
```

Therefore, organizations should treat risk identification as a **continuous governance activity**.

---

## 4. AI Risk Identification Versus AI Risk Assessment

These terms should not be confused.

### Risk Identification

Determines:

> What risks exist or could exist?

### Risk Assessment

Determines:

> How significant are those risks?

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Risk Identification
        ↓
Risk Analysis
        ↓
Risk Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Assessment
```

For example:

**Identification:**

> The AI system could disclose confidential information.

**Assessment:**

> Likelihood = Possible
> Impact = Major
> Overall Risk = High

Identification comes before meaningful assessment.

---

## 5. AI Risk Identification Versus Risk Treatment

Risk identification determines what could go wrong.

Risk treatment determines what the organization will do about it.

```text
Identify
   ↓
Assess
   ↓
Prioritize
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
```

For example:

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI data disclosure

Treatment:
Access control
+
Data filtering
+
Output validation
+
Monitoring
```

The organization should avoid selecting controls before adequately understanding the underlying risk.

---

## 6. What Constitutes an AI Risk?

An AI risk can be understood as the possibility that an AI-related event, condition, behavior, or failure could produce an undesirable consequence.

Examples include:

* Security compromise.
* Privacy violation.
* Incorrect decision.
* Harmful output.
* Discrimination.
* Operational disruption.
* Regulatory non-compliance.
* Loss of stakeholder trust.

The term **AI risk** should therefore be interpreted broadly.

---

## 7. AI Risk Is Not Limited to the AI Model

One of the most important principles in AI risk identification is:

> **The AI model is only one component of the overall AI system.**

A complete AI system may include:

```text
Users
  ↓
Application
  ↓
AI Interface
  ↓
Prompt / Instruction Layer
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Retrieval System
  ↓
Enterprise Data
  ↓
External APIs
  ↓
Automated Actions
```

A weakness anywhere in this chain can create AI-related risk.

---

## 8. AI System Risk Surface

The **AI risk surface** is the collection of components, interactions, data, users, and conditions through which risk may arise.

It may include:

* Model.
* Data.
* Application.
* APIs.
* Users.
* Administrators.
* Vendors.
* Infrastructure.
* Interfaces.
* Tools.
* External systems.
* Business processes.

The larger and more interconnected the system, the more comprehensive the risk identification should be.

---

## 9. Risk Identification Starts With Context

Before identifying specific risks, the organization should understand the context.

Important questions include:

1. What does the AI system do?
2. Why is it being used?
3. Who uses it?
4. Who is affected?
5. What decisions does it influence?
6. What data does it process?
7. What systems does it interact with?
8. How autonomous is it?
9. What happens if it fails?
10. What happens if it is misused?

Context prevents generic risk identification.

---

## 10. Intended Purpose

The intended purpose establishes the boundary for risk identification.

For example:

> An AI system is intended to summarize internal customer-service interactions.

Relevant risks may include:

* Incorrect summaries.
* Confidentiality exposure.
* Privacy concerns.
* Unauthorized access.
* User overreliance.

However, if the same system is later used to automatically determine customer eligibility, additional risks may emerge.

---

## 11. Intended Use

The organization should document how the AI system is expected to be used.

Example:

```text
Intended:
Assist customer-service employees.

Not intended:
Make autonomous customer eligibility decisions.
```

This distinction is important because the risk profile depends heavily on the actual use case.

---

## 12. Unintended Use

AI systems can be used in ways that were never anticipated during design.

Examples:

* Employees entering sensitive information into a public AI tool.
* Using a productivity assistant for employment decisions.
* Using an AI chatbot to provide regulated advice.
* Connecting an AI agent to systems beyond its original scope.

These situations should be considered during risk identification.

---

## 13. Misuse and Abuse

Risk identification should consider both accidental misuse and deliberate abuse.

### Misuse

The system is used incorrectly or outside its approved purpose.

### Abuse

Someone intentionally exploits the system's capabilities for harmful purposes.

Examples include:

* Attempting to bypass safeguards.
* Manipulating prompts.
* Extracting sensitive information.
* Using unauthorized tools.
* Creating harmful automated actions.

---

## 14. AI Risk Sources

Risk sources can originate from multiple areas.

A practical model is:

```text
Technology
Data
People
Process
Third Parties
Environment
Governance
Threats
```

Each category can generate different risks.

---

## 15. Technology Risk Sources

Technology-related sources may include:

* Model limitations.
* Software vulnerabilities.
* Insecure APIs.
* Weak authentication.
* Excessive privileges.
* Poor configuration.
* Insecure integrations.
* Model drift.
* Infrastructure failures.

Technology risk identification should consider both the AI model and surrounding technology.

---

## 16. Data Risk Sources

Data-related risks may originate from:

* Poor-quality data.
* Incomplete data.
* Biased data.
* Unauthorized data.
* Sensitive information.
* Incorrect labels.
* Outdated information.
* Poor provenance.
* Excessive data collection.

Data quality and data governance are therefore fundamental to AI risk management.

---

## 17. People Risk Sources

Human behavior can introduce significant AI risk.

Examples include:

* Overreliance on AI.
* Lack of training.
* Misinterpretation of outputs.
* Unauthorized use.
* Poor review.
* Excessive trust.
* Deliberate misuse.
* Inadequate escalation.

The organization should not assume that humans automatically reduce AI risk.

---

## 18. Process Risk Sources

Weak processes may introduce risks through:

* Poor change management.
* Inadequate testing.
* Weak approval processes.
* Missing documentation.
* Inadequate incident response.
* Poor vendor management.
* Insufficient monitoring.

AI governance therefore needs to integrate with existing enterprise processes.

---

## 19. Third-Party Risk Sources

External providers may introduce risks through:

* Model changes.
* Data processing.
* Subprocessors.
* Service outages.
* Security weaknesses.
* Contractual limitations.
* Lack of transparency.
* Vendor concentration.

Third-party AI dependencies should be included in risk identification.

---

## 20. Environmental and Contextual Risk Sources

AI risk can also arise from the environment in which the system operates.

Examples include:

* Regulatory changes.
* New threat patterns.
* Changing customer behavior.
* New business requirements.
* Changes in market conditions.
* Changes in geopolitical or operational conditions.

A risk that was acceptable previously may become material when the environment changes.

---

## 21. Governance Risk Sources

Weak governance can create risks such as:

* Unclear accountability.
* Missing policies.
* Unapproved AI use.
* Inadequate risk ownership.
* Weak oversight.
* Poor escalation.
* Lack of monitoring.

Governance risk is particularly important because it can allow multiple other risks to remain uncontrolled.

---

## 22. Threat-Based Risk Identification

Threat-based analysis asks:

> Who or what could intentionally cause harm to the AI system?

Potential threat actors include:

* External attackers.
* Malicious insiders.
* Compromised accounts.
* Third-party attackers.
* Fraudsters.
* Malicious users.

The organization should identify realistic threats based on the system's exposure and capabilities.

---

## 23. Vulnerability-Based Risk Identification

Vulnerability-based analysis asks:

> What weaknesses could allow an unwanted event to occur?

Examples include:

* Weak access controls.
* Poor input validation.
* Excessive permissions.
* Insecure APIs.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Poor data segregation.
* Inadequate testing.

The combination of threat and vulnerability can produce a realistic risk scenario.

---

## 24. Event-Based Risk Identification

An event-based approach asks:

> What could actually happen?

Examples:

* Confidential data is disclosed.
* AI output is manipulated.
* The AI produces incorrect information.
* An unauthorized user accesses the AI.
* An automated action is executed incorrectly.

This approach helps convert abstract concerns into actionable risks.

---

## 25. Consequence-Based Risk Identification

The organization should then ask:

> What happens if the event occurs?

Potential consequences include:

* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Privacy harm.
* Security compromise.
* Regulatory action.
* Customer harm.
* Reputational damage.

Risk identification becomes stronger when consequences are clearly documented.

---

A practical risk chain can be represented as:

```text

       ↓

       ↓

      ↓

      ↓
```


This structure is useful for documenting material AI risks.

Existing documentation can reveal potential risks.

## 32. AI Risk Identification and Documentation

Risk identification and documentation should be treated as closely connected activities.

The process is:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Describe
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Record
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Approve
```

A risk that exists only in someone's discussion but is not documented can easily be forgotten or inconsistently managed.

---

## 33. Characteristics of Good AI Risk Documentation

Good documentation should be:

### Specific

Clearly identify the risk.

### Contextual

Explain how the risk applies to the AI system.

### Traceable

Connect the risk to evidence.

### Actionable

Allow a treatment decision.

### Understandable

Be clear to both technical and non-technical stakeholders.

### Current

Reflect the current system state.

---

## 34. Weak Versus Strong Risk Statements

### Weak

> AI security risk.

### Better

> Unauthorized users may access AI-generated information.

### Stronger

> An authenticated user with excessive permissions may use the AI assistant to retrieve confidential customer information outside their authorized business function, resulting in unauthorized disclosure.

The stronger statement provides a basis for control design and assessment.

---

## 35. Risk Statement Structure

A useful risk statement can follow:

> **Because of [cause or weakness], [risk event] could occur, resulting in [consequence].**

Example:

> Because AI application permissions are broader than the user's business role, an unauthorized retrieval request could expose confidential customer information, resulting in privacy and regulatory impact.

This structure promotes consistency.

## 37. Risk ID

Every material AI risk should have a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI-RISK-001
AI-RISK-002
AI-RISK-003
```

The identifier enables traceability across:

* Risk profiles.
* Risk registers.
* Controls.
* Assessments.
* Incidents.
* Remediation.
* Audit evidence.

---

## 38. Risk Title

The risk title should be concise but meaningful.

Weak:

> Security.

Better:

> Unauthorized AI Data Access.

Stronger:

> Unauthorized Retrieval of Confidential Customer Data Through AI Assistant.

The title should allow someone to understand the core issue quickly.

---

## 39. Risk Description

The description should explain:

* What could happen.
* Why it could happen.
* What could be affected.
* What consequences could result.

A good description should avoid unnecessary technical terminology unless it improves precision.

---

## 40. Risk Category

The organization should classify the risk consistently.

For example:

```text
Primary Category:
Cybersecurity

Secondary Categories:
Privacy
Compliance
Reputational
```

Multiple categories may be appropriate when one risk produces several consequences.

---

## 41. Risk Owner

Every material risk should have a designated owner.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is appropriately:

* Assessed.
* Managed.
* Monitored.
* Escalated.
* Accepted or treated.

The risk owner does not necessarily need to be the person who implements the controls.

---

## 42. Control Owner Versus Risk Owner

These roles should be distinguished.

### Risk Owner

Accountable for the risk.

### Control Owner

Responsible for operating or maintaining a specific control.

Example:

```text
Risk Owner:
Head of Customer Operations

Control Owner:
Security Operations Manager
```

This separation is important for GRC accountability.

---

## 43. Risk Evidence

Risk documentation should be supported by evidence where appropriate.

Evidence may include:

* Architecture diagrams.
* Security test reports.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Configuration records.
* Logs.
* Vendor documentation.
* Incident records.
* Assessment results.

Evidence strengthens the defensibility of the risk assessment.

---

## 44. Assumptions

AI risk documentation should identify important assumptions.

Examples:

> Human approval is required before external communication.

> The model provider does not use enterprise prompts for training under the applicable service configuration.

> AI output is not used as the sole basis for high-impact decisions.

Assumptions should be validated periodically.

---

## 46. Risk Identification and the NIST AI RMF

Within the NIST AI RMF, risk identification is strongly associated with understanding context and risks through the **MAP Function**, while risk measurement and management provide additional information for evaluating and responding to those risks.

A practical relationship is:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
Establish accountability
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
Identify context and risks
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
Evaluate with evidence
   ↓
MANAGE
   ↓
Prioritize and treat
```

This demonstrates that risk identification is not a standalone activity.

---

## 47. Risk Identification and the AI Lifecycle

Risk identification should be repeated throughout the lifecycle.

### Concept

Identify strategic and high-level risks.

### Design

Identify architecture, data, security, privacy, and operational risks.

### Development

Identify implementation and model risks.

### Testing

Identify risks revealed by testing.

### Deployment

Identify operational and user risks.

### Operation

Identify emerging risks.

### Change

Identify new or modified risks.

### Retirement

Identify data, dependency, and residual obligations.

---

## 48. Key Takeaways

The foundations of AI Risk Identification and Documentation are:

1. AI risk identification determines what could go wrong.
2. Risk identification should precede detailed risk assessment.
3. AI risk is broader than model risk.
4. The complete AI system should be considered.
5. Context is essential to meaningful risk identification.
6. Intended use and unintended use should both be considered.
7. Misuse and deliberate abuse should be considered.
8. Technology, data, people, processes, and third parties can all create risk.
9. Threats and vulnerabilities should be analyzed together.
10. Risk scenarios should describe realistic paths to harm.
11. Consequences should be documented in meaningful business or stakeholder terms.
12. Multidisciplinary participation improves risk identification.
13. Risk statements should be specific and actionable.
14. Every material risk should have a unique identifier.
15. Risk ownership should be explicit.
16. Control ownership should be distinguished from risk ownership.
17. Important assumptions should be documented and validated.
18. Risk records should be supported by evidence.
19. Risk documentation should provide traceability from risk through controls and evidence.
20. AI risk identification should remain continuous throughout the AI lifecycle.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Identification Methods, Techniques, and Risk Scenario Development

### 49. Structured AI Risk Identification

A mature AI governance program should use a structured methodology rather than relying on informal brainstorming.

A practical process is:

```text
Define AI System
      ↓
Understand Context
      ↓
Identify Assets and Stakeholders
      ↓
Identify Threats and Conditions
      ↓
Identify Vulnerabilities and Limitations
      ↓
Develop Risk Scenarios
      ↓
Identify Potential Consequences
      ↓
Document Risks
      ↓
Validate and Review
```

This creates consistency across AI systems and business units.

---

### 50. Asset-Based Risk Identification

Risk identification should begin by identifying what needs to be protected or preserved.

AI-related assets may include:

* Training data.
* Inference data.
* Personal information.
* Intellectual property.
* Model weights.
* Prompts.
* System instructions.
* Knowledge bases.
* Credentials.
* API keys.
* Business processes.
* AI-generated outputs.
* Customer trust.

Different assets create different risk scenarios.

---

### 51. Stakeholder-Based Risk Identification

Stakeholder analysis asks:

> Who could be affected by the AI system?

Potential stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Suppliers.
* Business partners.
* Management.
* Regulators.
* Members of the public.

Stakeholders should be considered both as **users** and as **potentially affected parties**.

---

### 52. Direct and Indirect Stakeholders

Some stakeholders interact directly with the AI system.

Others may never interact with it but may still be affected.

Example:

```text
AI Recruitment System
       ↓
HR Employee → Direct User
       ↓
Applicant → Directly Affected
       ↓
Applicant's Family → Potentially Indirectly Affected
```

The scope of stakeholder analysis should be proportionate to the potential impact.

---

### 53. Threat Modeling for AI Systems

Threat modeling provides a structured way to identify security-related AI risks.

The process may consider:

```text
Assets
   ↓
Threat Actors
   ↓
Attack Surface
   ↓
Threats
   ↓
Vulnerabilities
   ↓
Attack Paths
   ↓
Consequences
```

AI threat modeling should cover both traditional cybersecurity threats and AI-specific attack techniques.

---

### 54. AI Attack Surface

An AI system's attack surface may include:

* User interfaces.
* APIs.
* Prompts.
* System instructions.
* Retrieval systems.
* Model endpoints.
* Plugins.
* Tools.
* Connected applications.
* Data stores.
* Administrative interfaces.

Every exposed interface can represent a potential entry point.

---

### 55. Prompt Injection Risk Identification

Prompt injection should be considered when an AI system processes untrusted instructions or content.

A simplified scenario is:

```text
Untrusted Input
      ↓
Prompt Manipulation
      ↓
AI Interprets Malicious Instruction
      ↓
Unauthorized Behavior
      ↓
Potential Data / System Impact
```

Risk identification should consider both direct and indirect prompt injection.

---

### 56. Data Poisoning Risk

Where models are trained, fine-tuned, or otherwise influenced by external data, malicious or poor-quality data can introduce risk.

Potential consequences include:

* Incorrect outputs.
* Biased behavior.
* Hidden malicious behavior.
* Reduced model reliability.

The organization should understand how data enters the AI lifecycle.

---

### 57. Sensitive Information Disclosure

AI systems may expose sensitive information through:

* Prompts.
* Outputs.
* Logs.
* Retrieval systems.
* Training data.
* Integrations.
* Model behavior.

Risk identification should determine whether sensitive information can enter or leave the AI environment.

---

### 58. Model Extraction

Some AI services may expose enough functionality for an attacker to attempt to reproduce or infer aspects of a model.

Potential impacts include:

* Intellectual-property loss.
* Competitive disadvantage.
* Security exposure.

The applicability of this risk depends on the model architecture, exposure, and service design.

---

### 59. Adversarial Input Risk

Adversarial inputs may be intentionally crafted to cause unexpected AI behavior.

Potential outcomes include:

* Misclassification.
* Incorrect recommendations.
* Evasion of detection.
* Unsafe outputs.

Risk identification should consider adversarial manipulation where the AI system is used in security-sensitive or high-impact contexts.

---

### 60. Model Inversion and Information Inference

AI systems may create risks where sensitive information can be inferred from model behavior or outputs.

The organization should consider whether:

* Sensitive information is present in model-related data.
* Outputs could reveal protected information.
* Attackers could infer information through repeated queries.

This is particularly relevant where AI systems process sensitive datasets.

---

### 61. Hallucination Risk

Hallucination occurs when an AI system produces information that appears plausible but is incorrect or unsupported.

Potential consequences include:

* Incorrect business decisions.
* Incorrect customer information.
* Misleading reports.
* Incorrect technical recommendations.
* Reputational harm.

The severity depends heavily on the use case.

---

### 62. Overreliance Risk

Even when an AI system is designed as an assistant, users may treat its outputs as authoritative.

Example:

```text
AI Output
    ↓
User Assumes Correctness
    ↓
No Verification
    ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

Risk identification should therefore consider **human reliance**, not only model accuracy.

---

### 63. Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when people place excessive trust in automated or AI-generated recommendations.

Risk increases when:

* The AI appears highly confident.
* Users lack expertise.
* Human review is superficial.
* Workload encourages rapid acceptance.

Human oversight controls should address this risk where relevant.

---

### 64. Human-in-the-Loop Risk

The presence of a human reviewer does not automatically eliminate AI risk.

The organization should ask:

* Does the reviewer understand the AI's limitations?
* Does the reviewer have enough time?
* Can the reviewer override the AI?
* Is the reviewer trained?
* Are decisions documented?
* Are overrides monitored?

A nominal human review may provide little actual risk reduction.

---

### 65. Human-on-the-Loop Risk

In some architectures, humans supervise AI operations but do not review every individual decision.

Example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Multiple Automated Decisions
   ↓
Human Monitoring
   ↓
Intervention When Threshold Breached
```

Risk identification should determine whether this level of oversight is appropriate for the AI system.

---

### 66. Human-in-Command

For higher-impact AI systems, the organization may require humans to retain ultimate decision authority.

This can be particularly important where AI outputs could materially affect:

* Individuals.
* Safety.
* Employment.
* Financial outcomes.
* Access to important services.

The appropriate level of human control should be based on risk.

---

### 67. Fairness Risk Identification

Fairness-related risks should be identified when AI outcomes may differ across groups.

Potential sources include:

* Historical bias.
* Data imbalance.
* Proxy variables.
* Sampling problems.
* Model design.
* Threshold selection.
* Human interpretation.

The analysis should consider the context rather than assuming that every AI system has identical fairness requirements.

---

### 68. Bias in Data

Data may contain:

* Historical discrimination.
* Missing populations.
* Measurement errors.
* Unequal representation.
* Incorrect labels.

The presence of bias in source data does not automatically mean that the deployed AI system will produce harmful outcomes, but it creates a risk requiring assessment.

---

### 69. Bias in Model Behavior

Even when source data appears appropriate, model behavior may introduce undesirable differences.

Potential causes include:

* Model architecture.
* Optimization objectives.
* Training procedures.
* Fine-tuning.
* Prompting.
* Retrieval context.

Testing should be used to determine whether the identified concern creates a material risk.

---

### 70. Explainability Risk Identification

Organizations should ask whether stakeholders need to understand how an AI output was produced.

Relevant questions include:

* Who receives the decision?
* Who is affected?
* Is the decision consequential?
* Can the decision be challenged?
* Is an explanation required?
* Can the organization provide meaningful information about the result?

Explainability requirements should be proportionate to impact.

---

### 71. Transparency Risk Identification

Transparency risks may arise when stakeholders do not know:

* AI is being used.
* What the AI does.
* What information it processes.
* What limitations exist.
* How outputs are used.

Transparency should therefore be considered as part of risk identification where relevant.

---

### 72. Reliability Risk Identification

Reliability risk can arise from:

* Unstable outputs.
* Performance degradation.
* Model drift.
* Data drift.
* Infrastructure failures.
* Dependency failures.

The organization should identify what level of reliability is required for the business purpose.

---

### 73. Availability Risk

AI availability should be assessed where the business process depends on the AI service.

Questions include:

* What happens if the AI is unavailable?
* Is there a manual alternative?
* Is there a backup provider?
* How long can the business operate without the system?

Business-critical AI may require stronger resilience controls.

---

### 74. Model Drift

Model behavior may change as:

* Data changes.
* User behavior changes.
* The environment changes.
* The model is updated.
* External dependencies change.

Risk identification should therefore consider whether performance could degrade over time.

---

### 75. Data Drift

Data distributions may change after deployment.

Example:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Historical Customer Behavior

Production Data
      ↓
New Customer Behavior
```

If production conditions differ materially from development conditions, AI performance may decline.

---

### 76. Concept Drift

The relationship between inputs and desired outcomes may change over time.

For example:

> A fraud-detection model trained on historical fraud patterns may become less effective when attackers change their behavior.

Risk identification should consider whether the underlying business environment can change.

---

### 77. Third-Party Model Risk

When an organization uses an external model, it may not control:

* Training methods.
* Model updates.
* Data sources.
* Security architecture.
* Internal controls.

Risk identification should therefore include risks arising from limited visibility and control.

---

### 78. Model Update Risk

A provider may change a model without changing the organization's application architecture.

For example:

```text
Application
     ↓
External AI API
     ↓
Provider Changes Model
     ↓
Different AI Behavior
     ↓
New Risk
```

Contracts and technical controls should provide appropriate visibility into material changes.

---

### 79. Vendor Lock-In Risk

Dependence on one AI provider can create:

* Switching difficulty.
* Increased cost.
* Operational dependency.
* Reduced bargaining power.
* Business continuity risk.

This becomes more important when AI is deeply embedded into critical business processes.

---

### 80. Concentration Risk

An organization may use one provider for many AI systems.

Example:

```text
Business Unit A ─┐
Business Unit B ─┼→ Same AI Provider
Business Unit C ─┤
Business Unit D ─┘
```

A provider outage or security incident could therefore affect multiple business processes simultaneously.

---

### 81. Supply Chain Risk

AI supply chains may involve:

```text
Model Provider
      ↓
Cloud Provider
      ↓
AI Platform
      ↓
Application Vendor
      ↓
Enterprise
```

Risk identification should consider important dependencies across this chain.

---

### 82. Open-Source AI Risk

Open-source models and components may introduce risks involving:

* Vulnerabilities.
* Malicious modifications.
* Unclear provenance.
* Licensing.
* Unsupported dependencies.
* Model integrity.

The organization should apply appropriate due diligence.

---

### 83. AI Software Dependency Risk

AI applications may depend on:

* Libraries.
* Frameworks.
* APIs.
* Plugins.
* Vector databases.
* Orchestration platforms.

A vulnerability in one component can affect the overall AI system.

---

### 84. Integration Risk

AI systems increasingly interact with enterprise applications.

Example:

```text
AI Assistant
     ↓
CRM
     ↓
ERP
     ↓
Payment Platform
```

Each integration introduces additional trust relationships and potential failure modes.

---

### 85. Excessive Agency Risk

AI systems with autonomous capabilities may perform actions without direct human approval.

Risk identification should consider:

* What actions can the AI perform?
* What permissions does it have?
* What limits exist?
* Can actions be reversed?
* What happens if the AI makes an incorrect decision?

The more consequential the action, the stronger the controls generally need to be.

---

### 86. Tool-Use Risk

An AI agent may use external tools such as:

* Search engines.
* Databases.
* Email.
* CRM systems.
* Ticketing systems.
* Financial APIs.

Each tool should be evaluated for:

* Permission.
* Data access.
* Action capability.
* Failure behavior.
* Abuse potential.

---

### 87. Cascading Failure Risk

AI failures can propagate into connected systems.

Example:

```text
AI Incorrect Output
       ↓
Automated Workflow
       ↓
Incorrect Database Update
       ↓
Downstream System
       ↓
Customer Impact
```

Risk identification should therefore consider **downstream consequences**, not just the AI output itself.

---

### 88. Feedback Loop Risk

AI systems that learn from their own outputs or user behavior may create feedback loops.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
User Behavior
      ↓
New Data
      ↓
Model Update
      ↓
Stronger Recommendation
      ↓
More User Behavior
```

Such loops can reinforce undesirable patterns.

---

### 89. Emergent Behavior Risk

Complex AI systems may behave in ways that were not explicitly anticipated.

This is particularly relevant when systems combine:

* Multiple models.
* Agents.
* Tools.
* External data.
* Autonomous workflows.

Risk identification should consider system-level behavior rather than evaluating every component independently.

---

### 90. Boundary Failure Risk

A system may operate outside the assumptions under which it was designed.

Examples include:

* New user populations.
* New geographic regions.
* New languages.
* New data types.
* New business processes.

A change in operating boundary can invalidate parts of the original risk assessment.

---

### 91. Documentation Review Technique

One practical risk-identification technique is to review existing documentation and ask:

> What assumptions are being made?

Examples:

> Users will verify AI outputs.

> Data will remain within the approved environment.

> The model provider will maintain service availability.

> The AI will only be used for the approved purpose.

Each assumption can become a risk if it is not adequately controlled.

---

### 92. What-If Analysis

A simple **what-if analysis** can identify risks.

Examples:

> What if the model is unavailable?

> What if the model produces incorrect information?

> What if a user enters confidential information?

> What if an attacker manipulates the prompt?

> What if the vendor changes the model?

> What if the AI performs an unauthorized action?

The answers can generate risk scenarios.

---

### 93. Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis examines plausible situations in greater depth.

Example:

```text id="m7d5q8"
Scenario:
AI customer assistant receives a malicious prompt.

Condition:
User has access to sensitive customer records.

Event:
AI retrieves restricted information.

Consequence:
Unauthorized disclosure.

Potential Impact:
Privacy + Regulatory + Reputational
```

Scenario analysis is especially useful for higher-risk AI systems.

---

### 94. Threat Scenario Versus Risk Scenario

A **threat scenario** focuses primarily on the attack or threat.

Example:

> An attacker attempts prompt injection.

A **risk scenario** extends the analysis to consequences.

Example:

> An attacker successfully performs prompt injection, causing the AI assistant to disclose restricted customer information, resulting in privacy and regulatory exposure.

Risk documentation should generally capture the broader risk scenario.

---

### 95. Failure Mode Analysis

Organizations can examine individual ways the AI system could fail.

Example:

| Failure Mode             | Potential Effect   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------ |
| Incorrect classification | Wrong decision     |
| Missing data             | Incomplete output  |
| Hallucination            | False information  |
| Model unavailable        | Process disruption |
| Unauthorized access      | Data exposure      |
| Incorrect automation     | Business impact    |

This technique can be especially useful during design and testing.

---

### 96. Root Cause Analysis

When a risk or incident is identified, the organization should distinguish the immediate event from the underlying cause.

Example:

```text
Incident:
AI disclosed confidential information

Immediate Cause:
AI retrieved restricted content

Root Cause:
Weak access-control design
```

This distinction is important for effective treatment.

---

### 97. Five Whys for AI Risk

The **Five Whys** technique can be used to explore root causes.

Example:

**Why** did the AI disclose confidential information?

Because it retrieved restricted content.

**Why** could it retrieve restricted content?

Because access controls were too broad.

**Why** were access controls too broad?

Because AI permissions were not mapped to user roles.

**Why** were they not mapped?

Because the AI integration bypassed the standard IAM review.

**Why** did the review not occur?

Because AI integrations were not included in the existing architecture-review process.

The final finding may therefore be a governance-process weakness rather than simply an AI model problem.

---

### 98. Bow-Tie Risk Analysis

A bow-tie model can illustrate causes, the central risk event, controls, and consequences.

```text
Threats
  ↓
Preventive Controls
  ↓
Risk Event
  ↓
Detective / Corrective Controls
  ↓
Consequences
```

For example:

```text
Prompt Injection
      ↓
Input Filtering
      ↓
Unauthorized AI Behavior
      ↓
Monitoring + Human Escalation
      ↓
Data Exposure
```

This provides a useful visual representation of control strategy.

---

### 99. Data Flow Analysis

AI risk identification should follow data through the system.

Example:

```text
User
 ↓
Application
 ↓
AI Gateway
 ↓
Model
 ↓
Retrieval Database
 ↓
External API
 ↓
AI Output
 ↓
User
```

At each stage, ask:

* What data is present?
* Who can access it?
* What could happen to it?
* What controls apply?

---

### 100. Trust Boundary Analysis

A **trust boundary** exists where information or commands move between environments with different trust levels.

Examples:

```text
Internal Network → External AI Provider
Enterprise Data → SaaS AI Platform
User Input → AI Model
AI Agent → Financial API
```

Each boundary should be evaluated for:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Encryption.
* Data exposure.
* Validation.
* Monitoring.

---

### 101. Abuse Case Analysis

An abuse case describes how an AI capability could be intentionally misused.

Example:

> An employee intentionally uses the enterprise AI assistant to retrieve confidential information about another department.

The organization can then identify:

* Required access.
* Potential weaknesses.
* Detection mechanisms.
* Consequences.
* Preventive controls.

---

### 102. Misuse Case Analysis

A misuse case can also involve accidental behavior.

Example:

> An employee accidentally pastes customer personal information into a public generative AI service.

The organization should identify:

* Why the user did it.
* Whether policy was clear.
* Whether technical restrictions existed.
* Whether the action could be detected.
* What data could be exposed.

---

### 103. Risk Identification Through Incidents

Past incidents are valuable sources of risk information.

Sources may include:

* Security incidents.
* Privacy incidents.
* AI failures.
* Customer complaints.
* Audit findings.
* Service outages.
* Near misses.

A mature organization should feed lessons learned back into AI risk identification.

---

### 104. Near-Miss Analysis

A near miss occurs when a potentially harmful event almost occurs but does not produce material impact.

Example:

> An AI assistant attempted to access restricted data, but the request was blocked by an access-control policy.

The event may still reveal a risk requiring review.

Near misses can provide valuable early-warning information.

---

### 105. Risk Identification Through Testing

Testing can reveal previously unidentified risks.

Examples:

* Security testing.
* Red teaming.
* Adversarial testing.
* Performance testing.
* Fairness testing.
* Privacy testing.
* User acceptance testing.

A finding discovered during testing should be evaluated to determine whether the underlying risk should be added to the risk documentation.

---

### 106. Risk Identification Through Monitoring

Operational monitoring can reveal emerging risks.

Examples include:

* Increased error rates.
* Increased user overrides.
* Unusual prompt patterns.
* New attack attempts.
* Unexpected data access.
* Declining model performance.

Monitoring should therefore feed back into risk identification.

---

### 107. Risk Identification Through Regulatory Change

Changes in laws, regulations, or organizational obligations may introduce new risks.

For example:

```text
Regulatory Change
      ↓
New Requirement
      ↓
Existing AI Practice
      ↓
Compliance Gap
      ↓
New / Modified AI Risk
```

The risk-management process should include a mechanism for identifying such changes.

---

### 108. Risk Identification Through Business Change

Business transformation can also introduce new AI risks.

Examples:

* AI introduced into a new business process.
* Expansion to new customers.
* New geography.
* New product.
* New automation.
* New data source.

Business change management should therefore connect to AI governance.

---

### 109. Risk Identification Quality Review

Before finalizing an AI risk assessment, reviewers should ask:

1. Is the risk specific?
2. Is the context clear?
3. Is the cause identified?
4. Is the event realistic?
5. Are consequences documented?
6. Are affected stakeholders identified?
7. Are existing controls identified?
8. Is evidence available?
9. Is ownership assigned?
10. Is the risk traceable?

This quality check improves consistency and completeness.

---

### 110. Avoiding Duplicate Risks

Multiple stakeholders may identify similar risks.

For example:

* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Confidentiality breach.

These may represent the same underlying risk.

The organization should consolidate duplicate risks where appropriate while retaining relevant relationships to different consequences or controls.

---

### 111. Avoiding Overly Broad Risks

A risk such as:

> "AI cybersecurity risk"

is usually too broad to manage effectively.

It should be decomposed into meaningful risks such as:

* Unauthorized AI access.
* Prompt injection.
* Sensitive data disclosure.
* Model integrity compromise.
* Insecure AI integration.

Specific risks support specific controls.

---

### 112. Avoiding Excessive Risk Fragmentation

The opposite problem is creating hundreds of extremely narrow risks.

For example:

```text
Prompt Injection
Prompt Manipulation
Malicious Prompt
Instruction Override
Prompt Bypass
```

These may be different manifestations of a broader risk depending on the organization's taxonomy.

Risk documentation should balance sufficient detail with practical manageability.

---

### 113. Risk Taxonomy

A standardized AI risk taxonomy improves consistency.

A practical taxonomy may include:

```text
Cybersecurity
Privacy
Safety
Reliability
Fairness
Transparency
Explainability
Compliance
Operational
Third-Party
Strategic
Reputational
Human Factors
```

Organizations should adapt the taxonomy to their own risk environment.

---

### 114. Risk Identification Completeness

No risk-identification process can guarantee that every possible risk will be discovered.

AI systems involve uncertainty.

Therefore, the objective is to establish a **reasonable, systematic, evidence-based process** for identifying material risks.

The organization should explicitly recognize uncertainty rather than claiming complete risk visibility.

---

### 115. Risk Identification Documentation Standard

A mature organization should define minimum documentation requirements.

For example, every material AI risk should contain:

```text
Risk ID
Risk Statement
Category
Cause
Event
Consequence
Affected Stakeholders
Risk Owner
Existing Controls
Assessment
Evidence
Treatment
Review Date
```

This creates a consistent organizational baseline.

---

### 116. Key Principles

Effective AI risk identification should:

1. Begin with context.
2. Consider the entire AI system.
3. Identify assets and stakeholders.
4. Examine threats and vulnerabilities.
5. Consider malicious and non-malicious scenarios.
6. Consider intended and unintended use.
7. Analyze data flows and trust boundaries.
8. Consider human behavior.
9. Consider third-party dependencies.
10. Consider downstream consequences.
11. Use multiple identification techniques.
12. Use incidents and testing as sources of new risk information.
13. Document assumptions and uncertainty.
14. Avoid risks that are too broad to manage.
15. Avoid unnecessary duplication.
16. Establish consistent risk-taxonomy and documentation standards.
17. Maintain traceability between risks, controls, evidence, and decisions.
18. Revisit risk identification when the AI system or its environment changes.


# 05.11 — AI Risk Identification and Documentation

## Part 3 — AI Risk Documentation, Evidence, Ownership, and Governance

### 117. From Risk Discovery to Formal Documentation

Identifying an AI risk is only the beginning of the governance process.

A mature organization converts an identified concern into a formal risk record that can be:

* Reviewed.
* Assessed.
* Assigned.
* Treated.
* Monitored.
* Reported.
* Audited.

The transition can be represented as:

```text
Potential Concern
       ↓
Validated Risk
       ↓
Documented Risk Scenario
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Treatment
       ↓
Ongoing Monitoring
```

This distinction is important because not every observation automatically becomes a formally recorded enterprise risk.

---

### 118. Risk Observation Versus Formal Risk

A **risk observation** is an initial concern discovered during analysis.

A **formal risk** is a validated risk that has sufficient information to be managed through the organization's risk-management process.

Example:

**Observation:**

> The AI assistant can access customer information.

**Formal risk:**

> Because AI access permissions are broader than the user's authorized business role, the assistant could retrieve confidential customer information for unauthorized users, resulting in privacy, regulatory, and reputational impact.

The second statement is sufficiently structured for formal governance.

---

### 119. Risk Validation

Before adding a risk to the formal risk register, the organization should validate whether the scenario is:

* Relevant.
* Plausible.
* Material.
* Supported by evidence.
* Within the organization's risk scope.

Validation prevents risk registers from becoming collections of theoretical concerns with no practical relevance.

---

### 120. Risk Documentation Lifecycle

A practical documentation lifecycle is:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Capture
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Approve
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
Review
   ↓
Close / Retire
```

Risk records should therefore be treated as **living governance records**, not static documents.

---

### 121. AI Risk Record

A standardized AI risk record may contain:

| Field             | Purpose                    |
| ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| Risk ID           | Unique identifier          |
| AI System         | Identifies affected system |
| Risk Title        | Concise risk description   |
| Risk Statement    | Formal scenario            |
| Risk Category     | Classification             |
| Cause             | Source of the risk         |
| Risk Event        | What could occur           |
| Consequence       | Potential impact           |
| Stakeholders      | Who may be affected        |
| Existing Controls | Current safeguards         |
| Risk Owner        | Accountability             |
| Control Owner     | Control responsibility     |
| Risk Rating       | Significance               |
| Treatment         | Planned response           |
| Evidence          | Supporting information     |
| Status            | Current state              |
| Review Date       | Governance review          |

---

### 122. Risk Statement Quality

A good AI risk statement should answer three questions:

1. **What could happen?**
2. **Why could it happen?**
3. **What could happen as a result?**

A useful format is:

> Because of **[cause]**, **[risk event]** could occur, resulting in **[consequence]**.

Example:

> Because the AI application does not enforce data-level authorization, an AI user could retrieve information outside their approved business scope, resulting in unauthorized disclosure of confidential information.

---

### 123. Cause

The **cause** explains why the risk could occur.

Examples:

* Excessive privileges.
* Poor data segregation.
* Weak validation.
* Inadequate testing.
* Lack of user training.
* Unclear governance.
* Vendor dependency.

The cause should be sufficiently specific to support treatment.

---

### 124. Risk Event

The **risk event** is the undesirable occurrence itself.

Examples:

* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Incorrect recommendation.
* Model compromise.
* AI service outage.
* Unauthorized automated action.
* Biased output.

The event should be distinct from both its cause and consequence.

---

### 125. Consequence

The consequence describes what happens if the risk event occurs.

Examples:

* Customer harm.
* Financial loss.
* Privacy violation.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Operational disruption.
* Reputational damage.

A strong risk record should distinguish the event from its impact.

---

### 126. Cause, Event, and Consequence

A useful structure is:

```text
CAUSE
Weak authorization
      ↓
EVENT
Unauthorized AI data retrieval
      ↓
CONSEQUENCE
Confidentiality and privacy impact
```

This structure improves risk analysis and control mapping.

---

### 127. Stakeholder Documentation

The risk record should identify potentially affected stakeholders.

For example:

```text
AI Customer Assistant

Users:
Customer Service Employees

Affected Parties:
Customers

Other Stakeholders:
Privacy Office
Security
Legal
Regulators
Business Management
```

Stakeholder identification helps ensure that risk impact is not evaluated solely from the organization's perspective.

---

### 128. Risk Context

The risk record should provide enough context for someone unfamiliar with the AI system to understand the scenario.

Useful contextual information includes:

* Business purpose.
* AI capability.
* User population.
* Data processed.
* System dependencies.
* Deployment environment.
* Degree of autonomy.

Without context, a risk rating can become difficult to defend.

---

### 129. AI System Identification

Each risk should be associated with the relevant AI system or service.

Example:

```text

System:
Enterprise Customer-Service Assistant

Customer Operations

Technology Owner:
Enterprise AI Platform Team
```

This allows risks to be traced back to the correct system.

### 130. AI Inventory Relationship

An AI risk-management process should ideally connect the risk record to the organization's AI inventory.

```text
     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

```

This prevents risk management from becoming disconnected from the actual AI estate.

### 131. Risk Source Documentation

The organization should document where the risk was discovered.

Possible sources include:

* Risk workshop.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Audit.
* Incident.
* Testing.
* Red team.
* User feedback.
* Vendor assessment.
* Regulatory review.

Example:

> Source: AI security assessment conducted during pre-production review.

This improves traceability.

### 132. Risk Discovery Date

The date on which a risk was first identified can be useful for governance.

Example:

```text

```

This helps organizations understand how long a risk has remained open.

### 133. Risk Status

Common risk statuses include:

* Identified.

* Open.
* Treatment Planned.
* Treatment in Progress.
* Accepted.
* Monitoring.
* Closed.
* Retired.

The organization should define clear criteria for each status.

### 134. Open Risk

An open risk remains relevant and requires ongoing management.

Example:

> AI model outputs may contain unsupported factual statements, and additional validation controls are still being implemented.

The risk should remain visible until the organization's treatment decision is complete.

---

### 135. Accepted Risk

Risk acceptance means the organization consciously decides to retain the risk under defined conditions.

It should not mean:

> Nobody wants to deal with the risk.

A proper acceptance decision should include:

* Risk description.
* Risk rating.
* Business justification.
* Acceptance authority.
* Duration.
* Conditions.
* Review date.

### 136. Risk Acceptance Authority

Not every employee should be allowed to accept every risk.


The exact authority structure should be defined by organizational governance.

### 137. Risk Escalation

AI risks should be escalated when they exceed predefined thresholds.

Triggers may include:

* High impact.
* High likelihood.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Significant customer harm.
* Safety concerns.
* Material financial exposure.
* Major security implications.

Escalation ensures that significant risks receive appropriate management attention.

---

### 138. Risk Evidence

Evidence supports the credibility of the risk record.

Examples include:

* Architecture diagrams.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Security test results.
* Model evaluations.
* Configuration records.
* Access-control reviews.
* Incident reports.
* Logs.
* Vendor documentation.
* User testing.
* Audit findings.

Evidence should be traceable and appropriately protected.

---

### 139. Evidence Versus Assertion

An unsupported statement such as:

> The AI system is secure.

is not sufficient evidence.

A stronger record might reference:

> Security assessment completed on 15 August 2026, including authentication, authorization, API security, and prompt-injection testing.

The second statement can be independently verified.

---

### 140. Evidence Quality

Evidence should ideally be:

* Relevant.
* Accurate.
* Current.
* Complete.
* Traceable.
* Protected from unauthorized modification.

The quality of evidence affects confidence in the risk assessment.

### 141. Risk Documentation and Auditability

### 142. Risk Ownership

Risk ownership should be assigned based on business accountability.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority to:

* Make decisions.
* Allocate resources.
* Accept or escalate risk.
* Require treatment.
* Monitor progress.

Assigning a risk to someone without decision-making authority creates weak accountability.

---

### 143. AI Business Owner

The AI business owner typically understands:

* Why the AI system exists.
* What business process it supports.
* Who depends on it.
* What business consequences may result from failure.

The business owner is therefore often an important risk owner or risk stakeholder.

---

### 144. AI Technical Owner

The technical owner is responsible for technical aspects such as:

* Architecture.
* Configuration.
* Integration.
* Model deployment.
* Technical controls.
* Technical remediation.

The technical owner may manage controls without necessarily accepting the business risk.

---

### 145. Security Risk Ownership

Cybersecurity-related AI risks may involve:

* CISO organization.
* Security architecture.
* Security operations.
* Application security.
* Identity and access management.

However, security teams should not automatically become the owner of every AI cybersecurity risk.

The business should retain accountability for business consequences.

---

### 146. Privacy Risk Ownership

Where AI processes personal information, privacy stakeholders may participate in:

* Privacy impact assessment.
* Data-protection analysis.
* Lawful-use assessment.
* Data minimization.
* Retention analysis.

Privacy specialists may provide expertise while business ownership remains explicit.

---

### 147. Legal and Compliance Participation

Legal and compliance functions may identify risks associated with:

* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Intellectual property.
* Consumer protection.
* Sector-specific requirements.

Their involvement is particularly important where AI use creates significant legal or regulatory exposure.

* Corrective retraining.

### 155. Compensating Controls

A compensating control can reduce risk when the preferred control cannot be implemented.

Example:

> A system cannot provide automated output validation, so mandatory human review is implemented before high-impact decisions.

The organization should document why the compensating control is appropriate.

---

### 156. Control Effectiveness

The existence of a control does not automatically mean that the risk is adequately managed.

The organization should ask:

1. Does the control exist?
2. Is it properly designed?
3. Is it implemented?
4. Does it operate consistently?
5. Does it reduce the relevant risk?

This distinction is fundamental to GRC.

### 161. Risk Exceptions

* Why.
* Risk created.
* Compensating controls.
* Expiration date.
* Approval authority.

Exceptions should not become permanent substitutes for risk treatment.

### 162. Risk Acceptance Versus Exception

These concepts are related but different.

### Risk Acceptance

The organization consciously accepts a risk.

### Exception

The organization temporarily permits deviation from a requirement or control.

For example:

```text
Risk Acceptance:
"We accept the residual AI availability risk."

Exception:
"We temporarily permit this AI system to operate without the standard backup control until 30 September."
```

## Part 4 — Practical AI Risk Identification Framework and Governance Application

### 172. End-to-End AI Risk Identification Process

A practical enterprise AI risk-identification process should connect business context, technical analysis, stakeholder impact, documentation, and governance decisions.

A complete workflow can be represented as:

```text
AI System Identification
        ↓
Business Context
        ↓
Stakeholder Identification
        ↓
AI Lifecycle Analysis
        ↓
Asset and Data Identification
        ↓
Threat and Vulnerability Analysis
        ↓
Risk Scenario Development
        ↓
Consequence Analysis
        ↓
Risk Documentation
        ↓
Validation
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Control Mapping
        ↓
Treatment
        ↓
Monitoring and Review
```

This creates a repeatable process that can be applied across different AI systems.

---

### 173. Step 1 — Identify the AI System

The organization should first establish exactly which AI system is being assessed.

Document:

* System name.
* System owner.
* Business owner.
* Technology owner.
* AI capability.
* Model or provider.
* Deployment environment.
* Business process.
* User population.
* Data processed.

Example:

```text
AI System:
Enterprise Customer-Service Assistant

Purpose:
Assist customer-service employees

AI Capability:
Generative AI

Primary Users:
Customer-service agents

Data:
Customer interactions and approved knowledge content
```

Without clearly defining the system, risk identification can become too broad.

---

### 174. Step 2 — Define the Business Purpose

The organization should document why the AI system exists.

For example:

> The system assists customer-service employees by summarizing customer interactions and retrieving approved service information.

The purpose establishes the context against which risk is evaluated.

---

### 175. Step 3 — Define Intended Use

The organization should identify approved use cases.

Example:

```text
Approved:
Customer-service assistance
Information retrieval
Interaction summarization

Not Approved:
Autonomous customer decisions
Unauthorized employee monitoring
Independent regulatory determinations
```

This provides a boundary for governance.

---

### 176. Step 4 — Identify Users

Risk can vary significantly depending on who uses the AI system.

Identify:

* Employees.
* Administrators.
* Developers.
* Customers.
* Contractors.
* External partners.

Consider different permission levels and capabilities.

---

### 177. Step 5 — Identify Affected Parties

The organization should also identify people who may be affected even if they never use the AI system.

For example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Employee User
   ↓
Customer Decision
   ↓
Customer
```

The customer is therefore a stakeholder even though the customer may never interact directly with the AI.

---

### 178. Step 6 — Identify Data

Document the major categories of data involved.

Examples:

* Public information.
* Internal information.
* Confidential information.
* Personal information.
* Financial information.
* Authentication information.
* Intellectual property.
* Operational information.

Data classification is an important input into AI risk identification.

---

### 179. Step 7 — Map Data Flows

Document how data moves through the AI system.

Example:

```text

    ↓
Enterprise Application
    ↓
AI Gateway
    ↓
External Model
    ↓
Retrieval System
    ↓
AI Response
    ↓
User
```

At each stage, identify:

* Data exposure.
* Access.
* Processing.
* Storage.
* Transmission.
* Logging.

---

### 180. Step 8 — Identify AI Components

The risk assessment should include the entire technology stack.

Potential components include:

* Foundation model.
* Fine-tuned model.
* AI application.
* AI gateway.
* Prompt-management layer.
* Retrieval-augmented generation system.
* Vector database.
* Plugins.
* APIs.
* Agents.
* Monitoring tools.

The organization should avoid treating the model as the entire system.

---

### 181. Step 9 — Identify External Dependencies

Identify important external dependencies.

Examples:

```text
Cloud Provider
Model Provider
Data Provider
SaaS Platform
Identity Provider
External API
```

For each dependency, consider:

* Availability.
* Security.
* Data protection.
* Contractual obligations.
* Change management.
* Concentration risk.

---

### 182. Step 10 — Identify Assets

Document the assets that require protection.

Examples:

```text

       ↓
Business Information
       ↓
AI Model
       ↓
Prompts
       ↓
System Instructions
       ↓
Credentials
       ↓
AI Outputs
```

Asset identification helps establish what could be harmed.

---

### 183. Step 11 — Identify Threat Actors

Where relevant, identify potential threat actors.

Examples:

* External attackers.
* Malicious insiders.
* Compromised users.
* Fraudsters.
* Unauthorized third parties.
* Malicious application users.

Threat identification should remain realistic and context-specific.

---

### 184. Step 12 — Identify Vulnerabilities and Weaknesses

Identify conditions that could allow an undesirable event to occur.

Examples:

* Excessive privileges.
* Weak authentication.
* Inadequate authorization.
* Poor input validation.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Weak data segregation.
* Insufficient testing.
* Poor vendor oversight.

---

### 185. Step 13 — Identify AI-Specific Failure Modes

AI systems require consideration of failure modes that may not be prominent in traditional applications.

Examples include:

* Hallucination.
* Bias.
* Model drift.
* Data drift.
* Prompt injection.
* Unsafe outputs.
* Excessive autonomy.
* Inconsistent responses.
* Incorrect reasoning.
* Unexpected behavior.

The organization should determine which are relevant to the specific AI system.

---

### 186. Step 14 — Develop Risk Scenarios

Combine the information into realistic scenarios.

Example:

```text

Excessive AI permissions

        ↓

Event:
AI retrieves restricted information

        ↓

Consequence:
Confidential information is disclosed

        ↓

Impact:
Privacy + Regulatory + Reputational
```

This creates a risk scenario that can be assessed and treated.

---

### 187. Step 15 — Identify Existing Controls

Before defining new treatment, document controls that already exist.

Examples:

* IAM.
* RBAC.
* DLP.
* Encryption.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Human approval.
* Output validation.
* Security testing.
* Vendor assessments.

This prevents unnecessary duplication of controls.

---

### 188. Step 16 — Determine Inherent Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effectiveness of existing controls.

A simple example:

```text
Likelihood: High
Impact: High

Inherent Risk: High
```

The organization's methodology should define how likelihood and impact are calculated.

---

### 189. Step 17 — Evaluate Control Effectiveness

The organization should determine whether existing controls actually reduce the identified risk.

Consider:

* Design effectiveness.
* Implementation.
* Operating effectiveness.
* Coverage.
* Frequency.
* Evidence.

A control should not automatically be considered effective simply because it exists in a policy.

---

### 190. Step 18 — Determine Residual Risk

Residual risk is the risk remaining after considering existing controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
High

Existing Controls
     ↓
RBAC
DLP
Monitoring
Human Review

Residual Risk
     ↓
Medium
```

Residual risk should be documented and evaluated against organizational risk appetite and tolerance.

---

### 191. Step 19 — Assign the Risk Owner

The risk should have one clearly accountable owner.

The owner should have enough authority to:

* Make decisions.
* Approve treatment.
* Allocate resources.
* Escalate risk.
* Accept residual risk where authorized.

---

### 192. Step 20 — Define Risk Treatment

Possible treatment options include:

### Avoid

Stop or do not deploy the AI use case.

### Mitigate

Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact.

### Transfer

Shift part of the risk through contractual or insurance arrangements where appropriate.

### Accept

Consciously retain the risk within approved thresholds.

These options should be selected based on risk and business context.

---

### 193. Risk Avoidance

Avoidance may be appropriate when the potential consequences cannot be adequately controlled.

Example:

> The organization decides not to use a generative AI system for a high-impact decision because adequate safeguards cannot be established.

Avoidance is a legitimate risk-management decision.

---

### 194. Risk Mitigation

Mitigation is often the most common treatment approach.

Example:

```text
Risk:
AI Data Disclosure

Mitigation:
Least Privilege
+
DLP
+

+
Monitoring
+
Human Review
```

The objective is to reduce the risk to an acceptable level.

---

### 195. Risk Transfer

Some risk may be transferred or shared through:

* Contracts.
* Service-level agreements.
* Supplier obligations.
* Insurance.

However, transferring responsibility contractually does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying accountability.

---

### 196. Risk Acceptance

Acceptance should occur only when:

* The risk is understood.
* The risk is within the appropriate authority.
* The business justification is documented.
* Required conditions are defined.
* Review dates are established.

Acceptance should be an informed governance decision.

### Risk Indicator

Example:

Performance information can become risk information when it indicates increased exposure.

### 222. Common Mistakes in AI Risk Identification

Organizations should avoid:

### Mistake 1 — Treating AI risk as only cybersecurity risk

AI risk includes security but can also involve privacy, fairness, reliability, transparency, safety, compliance, and operational consequences.

### Mistake 2 — Assessing only the model

The application, data, users, integrations, and dependencies must also be considered.

### Mistake 3 — Using vague risk statements

"AI risk" is not sufficiently actionable.

### Mistake 4 — Ignoring human behavior

Users can create or amplify AI risks.

### Mistake 5 — Ignoring third parties

External models and platforms introduce dependencies.

### Mistake 6 — Assuming human review eliminates risk

Human oversight must be meaningful and effective.

### Mistake 7 — Documenting risks only once

AI risk is dynamic.

### Mistake 8 — Treating controls as proof of zero risk

Controls reduce risk; they do not necessarily eliminate it.

### 224. Practical GRC Interview Answer

A concise professional explanation could be:

> "I approach AI risk identification by first understanding the AI system's purpose, users, data, architecture, dependencies, and lifecycle context. Each material risk is assigned an owner, supported by evidence, mapped to existing controls, assessed for inherent and residual risk, and connected to a treatment plan. ### 227. Final Key Principles

The essential principles of **AI Risk Identification and Documentation** are:

1. Identify the AI system and establish its context.
2. Understand intended and unintended use.
3. Identify users and affected stakeholders.
4. Identify data, assets, components, and dependencies.
5. Analyze threats, vulnerabilities, limitations, and failure modes.
6. Consider cybersecurity, privacy, fairness, reliability, transparency, safety, operational, legal, and strategic risks where applicable.
7. Develop realistic risk scenarios.
8. Clearly separate cause, event, and consequence.
9. Document material risks consistently.
10. Assign accountable risk ownership.
11. Identify and evaluate existing controls.
12. Distinguish inherent risk from residual risk.
13. Define evidence requirements.
14. Establish treatment actions and accountability.
15. Escalate risks according to defined thresholds.
16. Document risk acceptance and exceptions formally.
17. Integrate AI risks with enterprise GRC processes.
18. Connect risks with controls, evidence, testing, and audit.
19. Reassess risks when AI systems or their environments change.
20. Use incidents, testing, monitoring, and lessons learned to continuously improve risk identification.
21. Treat AI risk documentation as a living governance record.
22. Use risk information to support informed management decisions rather than simply producing documentation.
23. Apply the NIST AI RMF as a continuous risk-management cycle across the AI lifecycle.
24. Maintain sufficient evidence to demonstrate that identified risks are understood, owned, treated, and monitored.
