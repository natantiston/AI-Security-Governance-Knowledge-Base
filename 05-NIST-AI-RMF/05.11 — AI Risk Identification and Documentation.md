# 05.11 — AI Risk Identification and Documentation

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Identification and Documentation

## 1. Introduction to AI Risk Identification

**AI Risk Identification** is the systematic process of discovering, describing, and documenting potential risks associated with an AI system throughout its lifecycle.

Within the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, risk identification is primarily supported by understanding the AI system's:

* Intended purpose.
* Context of use.
* Stakeholders.
* Data.
* Models.
* Components.
* Dependencies.
* Capabilities.
* Limitations.
* Potential benefits.
* Potential harms.

The objective is not simply to create a list of technical problems.

The objective is to understand:

> **What could go wrong, why could it happen, who could be affected, and what consequences could result?**

---

## 2. Why AI Risk Identification Is Important

AI systems can introduce risks that may not exist in conventional software systems or may amplify existing risks.

For example, a traditional application may return a deterministic database value, while an AI system may:

* Generate incorrect information.
* Produce different outputs for similar inputs.
* Infer sensitive information.
* Reflect limitations in training data.
* Be manipulated through adversarial inputs.
* Make recommendations that users incorrectly treat as authoritative.
* Interact with external systems.

Therefore, AI risk identification must consider both **technology risk** and **human, organizational, societal, and business consequences**.

---

## 3. AI Risk Identification as a Continuous Activity

AI risk identification should not occur only before deployment.

A practical lifecycle is:

```text
Concept
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

Risks can emerge or change at every stage.

For example:

```text
Development Risk
      ↓
New Data Source
      ↓
New Model Capability
      ↓
New User Population
      ↓
New Operational Risk
```

Therefore, organizations should treat risk identification as a **continuous governance activity**.

---

## 4. AI Risk Identification Versus AI Risk Assessment

These terms should not be confused.

### Risk Identification

Determines:

> What risks exist or could exist?

### Risk Assessment

Determines:

> How significant are those risks?

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Risk Identification
        ↓
Risk Analysis
        ↓
Risk Evaluation
        ↓
Risk Assessment
```

For example:

**Identification:**

> The AI system could disclose confidential information.

**Assessment:**

> Likelihood = Possible
> Impact = Major
> Overall Risk = High

Identification comes before meaningful assessment.

---

## 5. AI Risk Identification Versus Risk Treatment

Risk identification determines what could go wrong.

Risk treatment determines what the organization will do about it.

```text
Identify
   ↓
Assess
   ↓
Prioritize
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
```

For example:

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI data disclosure

Treatment:
Access control
+
Data filtering
+
Output validation
+
Monitoring
```

The organization should avoid selecting controls before adequately understanding the underlying risk.

---

## 6. What Constitutes an AI Risk?

An AI risk can be understood as the possibility that an AI-related event, condition, behavior, or failure could produce an undesirable consequence.

Examples include:

* Security compromise.
* Privacy violation.
* Incorrect decision.
* Harmful output.
* Discrimination.
* Operational disruption.
* Regulatory non-compliance.
* Loss of stakeholder trust.

The term **AI risk** should therefore be interpreted broadly.

---

## 7. AI Risk Is Not Limited to the AI Model

One of the most important principles in AI risk identification is:

> **The AI model is only one component of the overall AI system.**

A complete AI system may include:

```text
Users
  ↓
Application
  ↓
AI Interface
  ↓
Prompt / Instruction Layer
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Retrieval System
  ↓
Enterprise Data
  ↓
External APIs
  ↓
Automated Actions
```

A weakness anywhere in this chain can create AI-related risk.

---

## 8. AI System Risk Surface

The **AI risk surface** is the collection of components, interactions, data, users, and conditions through which risk may arise.

It may include:

* Model.
* Data.
* Application.
* APIs.
* Users.
* Administrators.
* Vendors.
* Infrastructure.
* Interfaces.
* Tools.
* External systems.
* Business processes.

The larger and more interconnected the system, the more comprehensive the risk identification should be.

---

## 9. Risk Identification Starts With Context

Before identifying specific risks, the organization should understand the context.

Important questions include:

1. What does the AI system do?
2. Why is it being used?
3. Who uses it?
4. Who is affected?
5. What decisions does it influence?
6. What data does it process?
7. What systems does it interact with?
8. How autonomous is it?
9. What happens if it fails?
10. What happens if it is misused?

Context prevents generic risk identification.

---

## 10. Intended Purpose

The intended purpose establishes the boundary for risk identification.

For example:

> An AI system is intended to summarize internal customer-service interactions.

Relevant risks may include:

* Incorrect summaries.
* Confidentiality exposure.
* Privacy concerns.
* Unauthorized access.
* User overreliance.

However, if the same system is later used to automatically determine customer eligibility, additional risks may emerge.

---

## 11. Intended Use

The organization should document how the AI system is expected to be used.

Example:

```text
Intended:
Assist customer-service employees.

Not intended:
Make autonomous customer eligibility decisions.
```

This distinction is important because the risk profile depends heavily on the actual use case.

---

## 12. Unintended Use

AI systems can be used in ways that were never anticipated during design.

Examples:

* Employees entering sensitive information into a public AI tool.
* Using a productivity assistant for employment decisions.
* Using an AI chatbot to provide regulated advice.
* Connecting an AI agent to systems beyond its original scope.

These situations should be considered during risk identification.

---

## 13. Misuse and Abuse

Risk identification should consider both accidental misuse and deliberate abuse.

### Misuse

The system is used incorrectly or outside its approved purpose.

### Abuse

Someone intentionally exploits the system's capabilities for harmful purposes.

Examples include:

* Attempting to bypass safeguards.
* Manipulating prompts.
* Extracting sensitive information.
* Using unauthorized tools.
* Creating harmful automated actions.

---

## 14. AI Risk Sources

Risk sources can originate from multiple areas.

A practical model is:

```text
Technology
Data
People
Process
Third Parties
Environment
Governance
Threats
```

Each category can generate different risks.

---

## 15. Technology Risk Sources

Technology-related sources may include:

* Model limitations.
* Software vulnerabilities.
* Insecure APIs.
* Weak authentication.
* Excessive privileges.
* Poor configuration.
* Insecure integrations.
* Model drift.
* Infrastructure failures.

Technology risk identification should consider both the AI model and surrounding technology.

---

## 16. Data Risk Sources

Data-related risks may originate from:

* Poor-quality data.
* Incomplete data.
* Biased data.
* Unauthorized data.
* Sensitive information.
* Incorrect labels.
* Outdated information.
* Poor provenance.
* Excessive data collection.

Data quality and data governance are therefore fundamental to AI risk management.

---

## 17. People Risk Sources

Human behavior can introduce significant AI risk.

Examples include:

* Overreliance on AI.
* Lack of training.
* Misinterpretation of outputs.
* Unauthorized use.
* Poor review.
* Excessive trust.
* Deliberate misuse.
* Inadequate escalation.

The organization should not assume that humans automatically reduce AI risk.

---

## 18. Process Risk Sources

Weak processes may introduce risks through:

* Poor change management.
* Inadequate testing.
* Weak approval processes.
* Missing documentation.
* Inadequate incident response.
* Poor vendor management.
* Insufficient monitoring.

AI governance therefore needs to integrate with existing enterprise processes.

---

## 19. Third-Party Risk Sources

External providers may introduce risks through:

* Model changes.
* Data processing.
* Subprocessors.
* Service outages.
* Security weaknesses.
* Contractual limitations.
* Lack of transparency.
* Vendor concentration.

Third-party AI dependencies should be included in risk identification.

---

## 20. Environmental and Contextual Risk Sources

AI risk can also arise from the environment in which the system operates.

Examples include:

* Regulatory changes.
* New threat patterns.
* Changing customer behavior.
* New business requirements.
* Changes in market conditions.
* Changes in geopolitical or operational conditions.

A risk that was acceptable previously may become material when the environment changes.

---

## 21. Governance Risk Sources

Weak governance can create risks such as:

* Unclear accountability.
* Missing policies.
* Unapproved AI use.
* Inadequate risk ownership.
* Weak oversight.
* Poor escalation.
* Lack of monitoring.

Governance risk is particularly important because it can allow multiple other risks to remain uncontrolled.

---

## 22. Threat-Based Risk Identification

Threat-based analysis asks:

> Who or what could intentionally cause harm to the AI system?

Potential threat actors include:

* External attackers.
* Malicious insiders.
* Compromised accounts.
* Third-party attackers.
* Fraudsters.
* Malicious users.

The organization should identify realistic threats based on the system's exposure and capabilities.

---

## 23. Vulnerability-Based Risk Identification

Vulnerability-based analysis asks:

> What weaknesses could allow an unwanted event to occur?

Examples include:

* Weak access controls.
* Poor input validation.
* Excessive permissions.
* Insecure APIs.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Poor data segregation.
* Inadequate testing.

The combination of threat and vulnerability can produce a realistic risk scenario.

---

## 24. Event-Based Risk Identification

An event-based approach asks:

> What could actually happen?

Examples:

* Confidential data is disclosed.
* AI output is manipulated.
* The AI produces incorrect information.
* An unauthorized user accesses the AI.
* An automated action is executed incorrectly.

This approach helps convert abstract concerns into actionable risks.

---

## 25. Consequence-Based Risk Identification

The organization should then ask:

> What happens if the event occurs?

Potential consequences include:

* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Privacy harm.
* Security compromise.
* Regulatory action.
* Customer harm.
* Reputational damage.

Risk identification becomes stronger when consequences are clearly documented.

---

## 32. AI Risk Identification and Documentation

Risk identification and documentation should be treated as closely connected activities.

The process is:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Describe
   ↓
Analyze
   ↓
Record
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Approve
```

A risk that exists only in someone's discussion but is not documented can easily be forgotten or inconsistently managed.

---

## 33. Characteristics of Good AI Risk Documentation

Good documentation should be:

### Specific

Clearly identify the risk.

### Contextual

Explain how the risk applies to the AI system.

### Traceable

Connect the risk to evidence.

### Actionable

Allow a treatment decision.

### Understandable

Be clear to both technical and non-technical stakeholders.

### Current

Reflect the current system state.

---

## 34. Weak Versus Strong Risk Statements

### Weak

> AI security risk.

### Better

> Unauthorized users may access AI-generated information.

### Stronger

> An authenticated user with excessive permissions may use the AI assistant to retrieve confidential customer information outside their authorized business function, resulting in unauthorized disclosure.

The stronger statement provides a basis for control design and assessment.

---

## 35. Risk Statement Structure

A useful risk statement can follow:

> **Because of [cause or weakness], [risk event] could occur, resulting in [consequence].**

Example:

> Because AI application permissions are broader than the user's business role, an unauthorized retrieval request could expose confidential customer information, resulting in privacy and regulatory impact.

This structure promotes consistency.

## 37. Risk ID

Every material AI risk should have a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI-RISK-001
AI-RISK-002
AI-RISK-003
```

The identifier enables traceability across:

* Risk profiles.
* Risk registers.
* Controls.
* Assessments.
* Incidents.
* Remediation.
* Audit evidence.

---

## 38. Risk Title

The risk title should be concise but meaningful.

Weak:

> Security.

Better:

> Unauthorized AI Data Access.

Stronger:

> Unauthorized Retrieval of Confidential Customer Data Through AI Assistant.

The title should allow someone to understand the core issue quickly.

---

## 39. Risk Description

The description should explain:

* What could happen.
* Why it could happen.
* What could be affected.
* What consequences could result.

A good description should avoid unnecessary technical terminology unless it improves precision.

---

## 40. Risk Category

The organization should classify the risk consistently.

For example:

```text
Primary Category:
Cybersecurity

Secondary Categories:
Privacy
Compliance
Reputational
```

Multiple categories may be appropriate when one risk produces several consequences.

---

## 41. Risk Owner

Every material risk should have a designated owner.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is appropriately:

* Assessed.
* Managed.
* Monitored.
* Escalated.
* Accepted or treated.

The risk owner does not necessarily need to be the person who implements the controls.

---

## 42. Control Owner Versus Risk Owner

These roles should be distinguished.

### Risk Owner

Accountable for the risk.

### Control Owner

Responsible for operating or maintaining a specific control.

Example:

```text
Risk Owner:
Head of Customer Operations

Control Owner:
Security Operations Manager
```

This separation is important for GRC accountability.

---

## 43. Risk Evidence

Risk documentation should be supported by evidence where appropriate.

Evidence may include:

* Architecture diagrams.
* Security test reports.
* Data-flow diagrams.
* Configuration records.
* Logs.
* Vendor documentation.
* Incident records.
* Assessment results.

Evidence strengthens the defensibility of the risk assessment.

---

## 44. Assumptions

AI risk documentation should identify important assumptions.

Examples:

> Human approval is required before external communication.

> The model provider does not use enterprise prompts for training under the applicable service configuration.

> AI output is not used as the sole basis for high-impact decisions.

Assumptions should be validated periodically.

---

---

## 46. Risk Identification and the NIST AI RMF

Within the NIST AI RMF, risk identification is strongly associated with understanding context and risks through the **MAP Function**, while risk measurement and management provide additional information for evaluating and responding to those risks.

A practical relationship is:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
Establish accountability
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
Identify context and risks
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
Evaluate with evidence
   ↓
MANAGE
   ↓
Prioritize and treat
```

This demonstrates that risk identification is not a standalone activity.

---

## 47. Risk Identification and the AI Lifecycle

Risk identification should be repeated throughout the lifecycle.

### Concept

Identify strategic and high-level risks.

### Design

Identify architecture, data, security, privacy, and operational risks.

### Development

Identify implementation and model risks.

### Testing

Identify risks revealed by testing.

### Deployment

Identify operational and user risks.

### Operation

Identify emerging risks.

### Change

Identify new or modified risks.

### Retirement

Identify data, dependency, and residual obligations.

---

## 48. Key Takeaways

The foundations of AI Risk Identification and Documentation are:

1. AI risk identification determines what could go wrong.
2. Risk identification should precede detailed risk assessment.
3. AI risk is broader than model risk.
4. The complete AI system should be considered.
5. Context is essential to meaningful risk identification.
6. Intended use and unintended use should both be considered.
7. Misuse and deliberate abuse should be considered.
8. Technology, data, people, processes, and third parties can all create risk.
9. Threats and vulnerabilities should be analyzed together.
10. Risk scenarios should describe realistic paths to harm.
11. Consequences should be documented in meaningful business or stakeholder terms.
12. Multidisciplinary participation improves risk identification.
13. Risk statements should be specific and actionable.
14. Every material risk should have a unique identifier.
15. Risk ownership should be explicit.
16. Control ownership should be distinguished from risk ownership.
17. Important assumptions should be documented and validated.
18. Risk records should be supported by evidence.
19. Risk documentation should provide traceability from risk through controls and evidence.
20. AI risk identification should remain continuous throughout the AI lifecycle.
