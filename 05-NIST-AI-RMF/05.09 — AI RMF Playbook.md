# 05.09 — AI RMF Playbook

## Part 1 — Introduction to the NIST AI RMF Playbook

## 1. Introduction

The **NIST AI RMF Playbook** is a practical resource designed to help organizations operationalize the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The AI RMF establishes the high-level structure through the four Functions:

* **GOVERN**
* **MAP**
* **MEASURE**
* **MANAGE**

The Playbook provides more practical suggestions for implementing those Functions, Categories, and Subcategories.

A useful distinction is:

```text
NIST AI RMF
    ↓
Defines the risk-management framework
    ↓
AI RMF Playbook
    ↓
Provides practical implementation suggestions
```

The Playbook therefore helps organizations move from **"what should we accomplish?"** toward **"what practical activities could we perform?"**

---

## 2. Purpose of the AI RMF Playbook

The primary purpose of the Playbook is to support organizations in putting AI risk-management principles into practice.

It can help organizations:

* Identify AI risks.
* Understand AI system context.
* Establish governance.
* Evaluate trustworthy AI characteristics.
* Develop measurement activities.
* Manage identified risks.
* Improve AI governance maturity.
* Connect AI risk management with organizational processes.

The Playbook is particularly useful for organizations that understand the AI RMF conceptually but need more practical implementation guidance.

---

## 3. Relationship Between the AI RMF and the Playbook

The AI RMF and Playbook should not be treated as two separate frameworks.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
AI RMF
Framework Structure
        ↓
GOVERN
MAP
MEASURE
MANAGE
        ↓
Categories
        ↓
Subcategories
        ↓
AI RMF Playbook
Practical Actions
        ↓
Organizational Implementation
```

The AI RMF establishes the structure, while the Playbook provides examples of actions that organizations may consider.

---

## 4. The Playbook Is Not a Checklist

One of the most important concepts is that the Playbook should not be interpreted as a mandatory checklist.

Organizations should determine:

* Which actions are relevant.
* Which actions are already implemented.
* Which actions require modification.
* Which actions are unnecessary.
* Which additional actions are required because of organizational circumstances.

Therefore:

> **The Playbook provides suggested actions rather than a universal mandatory implementation sequence.**

This allows organizations to apply risk-based judgment.

---

## 5. Risk-Based Use of the Playbook

The Playbook should be applied proportionately.

For example:

```text
Low-Risk AI
    ↓
Limited Assessment
    ↓
Basic Controls
    ↓
Periodic Monitoring
```

Whereas:

```text
High-Impact AI
    ↓
Detailed Risk Assessment
    ↓
Enhanced Controls
    ↓
Comprehensive Testing
    ↓
Continuous Monitoring
    ↓
Executive Oversight
```

The appropriate level of implementation depends on the AI system's context and risk.

---

## 6. Playbook Structure

The Playbook is organized around the four AI RMF Functions:

### GOVERN

Establish organizational governance and accountability.

### MAP

Understand context, risks, impacts, and stakeholders.

### MEASURE

Analyze, assess, and monitor AI risks.

### MANAGE

Prioritize, treat, and continuously manage AI risks.

This maintains consistency with the core AI RMF.

---

## 7. GOVERN in the Playbook

The GOVERN Function establishes the organizational foundation for AI risk management.

Practical activities may include:

* Establishing AI governance policies.
* Defining roles and responsibilities.
* Establishing accountability.
* Defining risk-management processes.
* Establishing communication mechanisms.
* Providing AI risk-management training.
* Managing third-party relationships.
* Establishing documentation requirements.

A simplified model is:

```text
Policy
  ↓
Roles
  ↓
Responsibilities
  ↓
Processes
  ↓
Oversight
  ↓
Accountability
```

## 11. Accountability

AI governance requires clear accountability.

```text

    ↓
```

Accountability should not disappear simply because an AI system is provided by a third party.

The organization remains responsible for managing the risks associated with its use of the AI system.

# MAP Practical Actions

## 14. MAP Function in the Playbook

The MAP Function helps organizations understand:

* What the AI system is.
* Why it exists.
* Who uses it.
* Who may be affected.
* What data it processes.
* What impacts could occur.
* What risks may arise.

The central principle is:

> **Risk cannot be properly evaluated without understanding context.**

---

## 15. Define the AI System Context

Organizations should document:

* Intended purpose.
* Intended users.
* Operating environment.
* Business process.
* Data sources.
* Technical architecture.
* Dependencies.
* Stakeholders.
* Potential impacts.

A context record could be structured as:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Purpose
    ↓
Users
    ↓
Data
    ↓
Environment
    ↓
Dependencies
    ↓
Potential Impacts
```

---

## 16. Identify Stakeholders

Stakeholder identification should extend beyond the AI development team.

Potential stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Suppliers.
* Regulators.
* Management.
* Data subjects.
* Communities.
* Business partners.

Different stakeholders may experience different benefits and harms.

---

## 17. Identify Intended Use

The organization should clearly define how the AI system is intended to be used.

For example:

> "The system generates draft customer-service responses for review by trained customer-service personnel."

This is substantially different from:

> "The system autonomously communicates with customers."

The difference can materially change the risk profile.

---

## 18. Identify Unintended Use

Organizations should also consider how the AI system could be used outside its intended purpose.

Examples include:

* Employees using confidential data with public AI tools.
* Users relying on AI outputs without verification.
* Using a model for decisions for which it was never validated.
* Connecting an AI system to unauthorized systems.

Unintended use can create significant governance gaps.

---

## 19. Identify AI System Dependencies

The Playbook approach encourages organizations to understand dependencies.

Examples:

```text
AI Application
     ↓
Foundation Model
     ↓
Cloud Platform
     ↓
External API
     ↓
Data Provider
```

A risk assessment should consider the dependencies that could affect the AI system.

---

## 20. AI Data Context

AI risk management should consider:

* Data origin.
* Data quality.
* Data sensitivity.
* Data representativeness.
* Data completeness.
* Data processing.
* Data retention.
* Data access.

Poor data can create risks even when the underlying AI model is technically sophisticated.

---

## 21. AI Impact Assessment

An AI impact assessment should consider potential consequences for:

* Individuals.
* Groups.
* Organizations.
* Society.
* The environment.

Potential impact areas include:

* Privacy.
* Security.
* Safety.
* Financial harm.
* Discrimination.
* Reputation.
* Operational disruption.

---

## 22. AI Risk Identification

A practical risk identification exercise can ask:

1. What can go wrong?
2. Why could it happen?
3. Who could be affected?
4. How severe could the impact be?
5. How likely is it?
6. Can the impact be detected?
7. Can the impact be reversed?
8. What controls already exist?

This provides the foundation for an AI risk register.

---

# MEASURE Practical Actions

## 24. MEASURE Function in the Playbook

The MEASURE Function focuses on generating evidence about AI risk.

Activities may include:

* Testing.
* Evaluation.
* Validation.
* Metrics.
* Monitoring.
* Performance assessment.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Bias assessment.

The objective is to determine whether identified risks are actually being controlled.

---

## 25. Define AI Measurement Objectives

Measurement should begin with the question:

> **What do we need to know about this AI system to make an informed risk decision?**

For example:

```text
Risk:
Incorrect AI decisions

Question:
How frequently does the system make errors?

Measurement:
Error rate

Threshold:
Organizationally defined tolerance

Action:
Investigation when threshold is exceeded
```

## 33. Establish Measurement Thresholds

Measurement becomes more actionable when thresholds are defined.

Example:

| Metric                     | Target | Escalation Threshold |
| -------------------------- | -----: | -------------------: |
| Availability               | ≥99.9% |                 <99% |
| Error Rate                 |    ≤2% |                  >5% |
| False Positive Rate        |    ≤3% |                  >6% |
| Critical Security Findings |      0 |                   >0 |

These values are illustrative and should not be treated as universal requirements.

# MANAGE Practical Actions

## 34. MANAGE Function in the Playbook

The MANAGE Function translates risk information into action.

Activities may include:

* Prioritizing risks.
* Selecting treatments.
* Implementing controls.
* Accepting residual risks.
* Escalating risks.
* Monitoring treatment effectiveness.
* Updating risk decisions.

---

## 35. Prioritize AI Risks

Not every AI risk requires the same response.

Prioritization can consider:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Security implications.
* Safety implications.
* Risk appetite.

---

## 36. AI Risk Treatment Options

Common treatment strategies include:

### Avoid

Do not deploy or discontinue the AI use case.

### Mitigate

Implement additional controls.

### Transfer

Transfer certain risks contractually or through appropriate arrangements.

### Accept

Accept residual risk when it falls within authorized tolerance.

---

## 37. Risk Treatment Example

```text
Risk:
Sensitive information disclosure

Current Risk:
High

Treatment:
DLP + Access Control + Output Filtering

Expected Residual Risk:
Moderate

Decision:
Accept with continuous monitoring
```

The example illustrates the treatment process rather than prescribing a universal control set.

---

## 38. AI Risk Prioritization Matrix

A simple model may use:

| Impact | Likelihood | Priority |
| ------ | ---------- | -------- |
| High   | High       | Critical |
| High   | Medium     | High     |
| Medium | High       | High     |
| Medium | Medium     | Moderate |

---

## 39. AI Risk Treatment Plans

A treatment plan should identify:

* Risk.
* Treatment.
* Control.
* Owner.
* Due date.
* Required resources.
* Expected residual risk.
* Validation method.

Example:

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI access

Treatment:
Implement RBAC and MFA

Owner:
IAM Team

Due:
Defined remediation deadline

Evidence:
Access review + authentication logs

Residual Risk:
Moderate
```

---

## 40. Residual Risk Management

Risk treatment does not necessarily eliminate all risk.

Therefore:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Acceptance / Further Treatment
```

Residual risk should be compared with the organization's risk appetite and tolerance.

---

## 41. AI Risk Escalation

Risks should be escalated when they exceed authorized tolerance.

Examples:

* Critical security exposure.
* Significant privacy impact.
* Severe safety concern.
* Material regulatory exposure.
* Unacceptable residual risk.
* Major AI incident.

Escalation procedures should be defined before a serious event occurs.

---

Potential incidents include:

The incident should feed lessons learned back into risk management.

## 52. AI Governance Stage Gates

Organizations may establish governance gates.

### Gate 1 — Use Case Approval

Is the AI use case acceptable?

### Gate 2 — Design Review

Have security, privacy, and risk requirements been addressed?

### Gate 3 — Pre-Production Assessment

Has the system passed required testing?

### Gate 4 — Production Approval

Are residual risks acceptable?

### Gate 5 — Operational Review

Does ongoing performance remain within tolerance?

## 65. Key Takeaways

The key concepts of the NIST AI RMF Playbook are:

1. The Playbook provides **practical implementation guidance** for the AI RMF.
2. It is organized around **GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE**.
3. It should not be treated as a rigid checklist.
4. Organizations should select activities based on **risk and context**.
5. AI governance should begin with a clear **AI inventory and scope**.
6. AI systems should be classified according to their potential risk and impact.
7. The Playbook can help translate Subcategories into practical organizational actions.
8. Actions should be connected to **risks, controls, owners, and evidence**.
9. Existing GRC, security, privacy, and compliance controls should be reused where appropriate.
10. AI risks should be managed throughout the AI lifecycle.
11. Measurement should generate evidence for management decisions.
12. Risk treatment should address both inherent and residual risk.
13. High-risk AI systems require stronger governance and assurance.
14. AI governance should integrate with enterprise risk management.
15. Continuous monitoring and reassessment are essential for changing AI environments.
16. The Playbook is most effective when adapted to the organization's specific AI ecosystem.
17. The ultimate objective is not Playbook completion; it is **effective and accountable AI risk management**.
