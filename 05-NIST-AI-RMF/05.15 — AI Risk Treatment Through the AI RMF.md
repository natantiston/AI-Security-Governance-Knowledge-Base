# 05.15 — AI Risk Treatment Through the AI RMF

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Treatment

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment is the process of determining and implementing appropriate actions to modify AI-related risk.

Risk treatment answers a fundamental governance question:

> **Once an AI risk has been identified, assessed, and prioritized, what should the organization do about it?**

The treatment process converts risk assessment into action.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
AI Risk Identified
        ↓
Risk Assessed
        ↓
Risk Prioritized
        ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
        ↓
Treatment Implemented
        ↓
Residual Risk Assessed
        ↓
Management Decision
```

Risk treatment is therefore a central component of operational AI governance.

---

### 2. Risk Treatment Within the NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI RMF organizes AI risk management around four functions:

```text
GOVERN
MAP
MEASURE
MANAGE
```

The **MANAGE** function is particularly relevant to risk treatment because it focuses on allocating resources to risks that have been mapped and measured.

Risk treatment should therefore be understood as part of an ongoing risk management process rather than as an isolated activity.

---

### 3. Purpose of AI Risk Treatment

The primary objectives of AI risk treatment are to:

* Reduce unacceptable AI risk.
* Prevent foreseeable harm.
* Strengthen controls.
* Align residual risk with organizational risk appetite.
* Protect stakeholders.
* Support regulatory and contractual obligations.
* Enable responsible AI deployment.
* Improve resilience.
* Maintain trustworthy AI outcomes.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every AI risk.

In practice, some level of residual risk will normally remain.

---

### 4. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

Risk treatment requires understanding the difference between **inherent risk** and **residual risk**.

**Inherent risk** is the level of risk before considering the effect of controls.

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after controls and treatment measures are considered.

A simplified model is:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Existing Controls
      ↓
Control Effectiveness
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Risk treatment primarily seeks to reduce residual risk to an acceptable level.

---

### 5. Example of Inherent and Residual Risk

Consider an AI system that processes sensitive customer information.

Before controls:

```text
Likelihood: High
Impact: High

Inherent Risk:
Critical
```

The organization implements:

* Encryption.
* Access controls.
* Data minimization.
* Monitoring.
* Human oversight.

After evaluating the controls:

```text
Residual Risk:
Moderate
```

The treatment decision then determines whether moderate residual risk is acceptable.

---

### 6. Risk Treatment Is Not the Same as Risk Assessment

Risk assessment determines:

> **How significant is the risk?**

Risk treatment determines:

> **What should we do about it?**

For example:

```text
Assessment:
Unauthorized access risk = High

Treatment:
Implement MFA and least privilege
```

The two activities are closely related but serve different purposes.

---

### 7. Risk Treatment Is Not the Same as Control Implementation

A control is a mechanism used to manage risk.

Risk treatment is the broader management decision that may involve:

* Implementing controls.
* Changing the AI system.
* Restricting the use case.
* Transferring certain risks.
* Accepting residual risk.
* Avoiding the activity.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Treatment
    ├── Controls
    ├── Process Changes
    ├── Technical Changes
    ├── Contractual Measures
    ├── Usage Restrictions
    └── Risk Acceptance
```

---

### 8. Risk Treatment as a Management Decision

Treatment should not automatically be delegated to technical teams.

A high-risk AI decision may affect:

* Business operations.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal obligations.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Corporate reputation.

Management therefore needs appropriate visibility into significant treatment decisions.

---

### 9. Risk Treatment Principles

A mature AI risk treatment process should follow several principles:

1. **Risk-based** — treatment should reflect the significance of the risk.
2. **Proportionate** — controls should be appropriate to the exposure.
3. **Context-aware** — treatment should consider the AI system's intended purpose and environment.
4. **Evidence-based** — treatment decisions should use available evidence.
5. **Accountable** — ownership should be clearly assigned.
6. **Documented** — decisions and rationale should be recorded.
7. **Continuous** — treatment should be reassessed as conditions change.

---

### 10. Risk Treatment and Trustworthy AI

Risk treatment supports the characteristics of trustworthy AI.

Depending on the use case, treatment may improve:

* Validity and reliability.
* Safety.
* Security and resilience.
* Accountability and transparency.
* Explainability and interpretability.
* Privacy enhancement.
* Fairness with harmful bias managed.

The treatment strategy should therefore reflect which trustworthy AI characteristics are relevant to the system.

---

### 11. Risk Treatment and the AI Lifecycle

Risk treatment should occur throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text
Planning
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

A treatment that is appropriate during development may not be sufficient during production.

---

### 12. Treatment During AI Design

Early design decisions can eliminate or reduce risk before the system is built.

Examples include:

* Choosing a less risky use case.
* Limiting data collection.
* Reducing system autonomy.
* Designing human approval.
* Selecting appropriate model architectures.
* Establishing security boundaries.

Early treatment is often more effective than attempting to correct problems after deployment.

---

### 13. Treatment During Development

During development, treatment may include:

* Secure coding.
* Dataset validation.
* Data-quality controls.
* Model testing.
* Privacy protections.
* Access controls.
* Security testing.
* Bias evaluation.
* Documentation.

These controls reduce the likelihood that unacceptable risk reaches production.

---

### 14. Treatment During Testing

Testing provides evidence about whether implemented controls are effective.

Testing may include:

* Functional testing.
* Security testing.
* Robustness testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Privacy testing.
* Bias testing.
* Performance testing.
* Human-factors testing.

A treatment should not be considered complete simply because a control has been implemented.

---

### 15. Treatment Before Deployment

Before production deployment, organizations may establish risk gates.

For example:

```text
Risk Assessment Complete
        ↓
Treatment Implemented
        ↓
Controls Tested
        ↓
Residual Risk Assessed
        ↓
Risk Acceptance / Approval
        ↓
Production Deployment
```

This prevents unresolved high-priority risks from automatically moving into production.

---

### 16. Treatment During Operation

Production treatment may involve:

* Continuous monitoring.
* Incident response.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Access reviews.
* Security monitoring.
* Drift detection.
* Human oversight.
* Periodic reassessment.

Operational treatment is particularly important because AI behavior and environmental conditions can change over time.

---

### 17. Treatment During AI Change

Changes can create new risks.

Examples include:

* New model versions.
* New training data.
* New users.
* New integrations.
* Increased autonomy.
* New geographic deployment.
* New business processes.

Significant changes should trigger a risk treatment review.

---

### 18. Treatment During AI Retirement

Risk treatment continues through retirement.

Organizations may need to:

* Disable AI services.
* Remove credentials.
* Revoke access.
* Retire integrations.
* Securely dispose of data where appropriate.
* Preserve required records.
* Confirm that no hidden dependencies remain.

An AI system should not simply be abandoned when it is no longer actively used.

---

### 19. Risk Treatment Options

Organizations generally have several broad treatment options:

```text
Avoid
Reduce
Transfer
Accept
```

In AI environments, additional practical strategies may include:

```text
Restrict
Redesign
Replace
Delay
Decommission
```

These options can be combined.

---

### 20. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means choosing not to undertake an activity that creates unacceptable risk.

For example:

```text
High-Risk AI Use Case
        ↓
Risk Cannot Be Adequately Controlled
        ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

Avoidance can occur by:

* Canceling the use case.
* Removing a high-risk capability.
* Choosing a different business process.
* Selecting a safer technical approach.

---

### 21. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction attempts to decrease either:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Or both.

For example:

```text
Before:
High Likelihood + High Impact

Treatment:
Access Control
+
Human Approval
+
Monitoring

After:
Lower Likelihood + Lower Potential Impact
```

Risk reduction is often the most common treatment strategy.

---

### 22. Risk Transfer

Risk transfer moves some financial or contractual consequences to another party.

Examples include:

* Insurance.
* Vendor contracts.
* Indemnification.
* Service-level agreements.
* Outsourcing arrangements.

However, risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's responsibility.

---

### 23. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means deliberately deciding that the residual risk is acceptable.

Acceptance may be appropriate where:

* Risk is within appetite.
* Controls are proportionate.
* Further treatment is not reasonably justified.
* Business benefits justify the remaining exposure.

Acceptance must be an explicit decision rather than an assumption.

---

### 24. Risk Restriction

An organization can reduce risk by restricting how an AI system is used.

Examples:

```text
General Access
      ↓
Approved Users Only
```

or:

```text
Autonomous Execution
      ↓
Recommendation Only
```

Restriction can preserve useful functionality while reducing exposure.

---

### 25. Risk Redesign

Redesign changes the AI system or process to reduce risk.

Examples include:

* Removing autonomous actions.
* Adding approval gates.
* Reducing model permissions.
* Changing data sources.
* Separating sensitive workloads.
* Introducing deterministic validation.

Redesign can sometimes achieve greater risk reduction than adding controls around an inherently risky architecture.

---

### 26. Risk Replacement

An organization may replace a high-risk AI approach with another technology or process.

For example:

```text
Highly Autonomous AI
        ↓
Rule-Based Workflow
```

If the business objective can be achieved with significantly lower risk, replacement may be appropriate.

---

### 27. Risk Delay

Sometimes deployment should be postponed until sufficient controls are available.

For example:

```text
AI System Ready
      ↓
Critical Control Missing
      ↓
Deployment Delayed
      ↓
Control Implemented
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
Deployment
```

Delay can be an appropriate treatment when risk cannot currently be reduced to an acceptable level.

---

### 28. Risk Decommissioning

An existing AI system may need to be removed when:

* Risk becomes unacceptable.
* Controls fail.
* Business requirements change.
* Regulatory obligations change.
* A safer replacement becomes available.
* The system no longer provides sufficient value.

Decommissioning can therefore be a legitimate risk treatment.

---

### 29. Selecting the Appropriate Treatment

Treatment selection should consider:

* Risk level.
* Risk appetite.
* Business objectives.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Technical feasibility.
* Cost.
* Time.
* Control effectiveness.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Reversibility.
* Availability of alternatives.

No single treatment is appropriate for every AI risk.

---

### 30. Treatment Cost Versus Risk Reduction

Organizations may compare:

```text
Treatment Cost
        vs.
Expected Risk Reduction
```

For example:

```text
Control Cost:
€50,000

Expected Risk Reduction:
Significant

Business Criticality:
High
```

The investment may be justified.

However, financial efficiency should not override mandatory legal, safety, or security requirements.

---

### 31. Cost of Inaction

Treatment decisions should also consider the cost of not acting.

Potential consequences include:

* Financial losses.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Customer harm.
* Security incidents.
* Operational disruption.
* Reputational damage.
* Loss of trust.

A treatment that appears expensive may be reasonable when compared with the potential consequences of unmanaged risk.

---

### 32. Risk Treatment Prioritization

Not all treatment actions can be performed simultaneously.

Organizations should prioritize treatment based on:

```text
Risk Priority
+
Potential Harm
+
Regulatory Urgency
+
Risk Velocity
+
Control Effectiveness
+
Business Criticality
```

Critical risks should generally receive accelerated attention.

---

### 33. Treatment Planning

A treatment plan should define:

* Risk being treated.
* Treatment objective.
* Selected treatment strategy.
* Required controls.
* Responsible owner.
* Resources.
* Dependencies.
* Target date.
* Validation method.
* Target residual risk.

---

### 34. Example Treatment Plan

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI access

Priority:
High

Treatment:
Reduce

Actions:
Implement MFA
Review privileges
Enable logging
Perform access recertification

Risk Owner:
Business Owner

Control Owner:
Security Team

Target:
Residual Risk = Moderate

Validation:
Security testing and access review
```

---

### 35. Treatment Ownership

Every treatment action should have a clear owner.

A useful distinction is:

```text
Risk Owner
    ↓
Accountable for Risk

Treatment Owner
    ↓
Accountable for Implementing Action

Control Owner
    ↓
Responsible for Operating Control
```

One person may perform multiple roles, but the responsibilities should remain clear.

---

### 36. Treatment Dependencies

AI risk treatment may depend on other functions.

Examples:

```text
AI Treatment
   ↓
Identity Team
   +
Security Team
   +
Privacy Team
   +
Application Team
   +
Vendor
```

Dependencies should be documented because delays in one area can leave the AI risk unresolved.

---

### 37. Treatment Milestones

Large treatment programs should use milestones.

Example:

```text
Milestone 1:
Design Control

Milestone 2:
Implement Control

Milestone 3:
Test Control

Milestone 4:
Validate Effectiveness

Milestone 5:
Reassess Risk
```

This provides management visibility.

---

### 38. Treatment Evidence

Evidence should demonstrate that treatment was actually implemented.

Examples include:

* Configuration records.
* Test results.
* Approval records.
* Access review reports.
* Monitoring evidence.
* Updated procedures.
* Contractual amendments.
* Training records.

Evidence should support the conclusion that risk has been reduced.

---

### 39. Treatment Effectiveness

Treatment effectiveness asks:

> **Did the treatment actually reduce the intended risk?**

For example:

```text
Control:
MFA

Expected Effect:
Reduce unauthorized access

Evidence:
Access testing

Result:
Effective
```

The risk can then be reassessed.

---

### 40. Control Failure

If a treatment control fails, the organization should not assume that the original risk rating remains valid.

For example:

```text
Expected Control:
Human Approval

Finding:
Approval process bypassed

Result:
Control Ineffective

Action:
Reassess Residual Risk
```

The priority may need to increase.

---

### 41. Treatment and Residual Risk

After treatment:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Treatment
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

The organization must then compare residual risk against appetite and tolerance.

---

### 42. Target Residual Risk

Treatment planning should establish the desired residual risk.

Example:

```text
Current:
Critical

Treatment:
Strong access controls
+
Human approval
+
Monitoring

Target:
Moderate
```

This provides a measurable treatment objective.

---

### 43. Treatment Completion

Treatment should not be marked complete simply because an action was assigned.

A stronger completion model is:

```text
Action Assigned
      ↓
Action Implemented
      ↓
Control Tested
      ↓
Effectiveness Confirmed
      ↓
Risk Reassessed
      ↓
Treatment Closed
```

This provides stronger governance assurance.

---

### 44. Treatment Closure

Treatment closure should document:

* What was implemented.
* Evidence.
* Testing results.
* Residual risk.
* Approval.
* Remaining issues.
* Monitoring requirements.

A closed treatment should still remain subject to ongoing monitoring where appropriate.

---

### 45. Compensating Controls

When the preferred control cannot be implemented immediately, a compensating control may be used.

Example:

```text
Preferred Control:
Automated transaction validation

Unavailable

Compensating Control:
Mandatory human review
+
Daily reconciliation
```

Compensating controls should be formally evaluated and approved.

---

### 46. Temporary Controls

Temporary controls may be appropriate during remediation.

For example:

```text
Permanent Control:
Automated AI security gateway

Implementation:
3 months

Temporary Control:
Manual review
+
Restricted access
```

Temporary controls should have an expiration or review date.

---

### 47. Treatment and Defense in Depth

AI risk treatment should avoid relying on one control where consequences are significant.

A defense-in-depth approach may include:

```text
Identity
   ↓
Access Control
   ↓
Input Validation
   ↓
Model Guardrails
   ↓
Output Validation
   ↓
Human Oversight
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Incident Response
```

Multiple layers can reduce the probability that a single control failure results in harm.

---

### 48. Preventive, Detective, and Corrective Treatment

Controls can be categorized as:

**Preventive**

Designed to stop an unwanted event.

**Detective**

Designed to identify an event.

**Corrective**

Designed to restore or reduce consequences after an event.

A mature treatment strategy may use all three.

---

### 49. Example of Layered AI Treatment

For prompt injection:

```text
Preventive:
Input filtering

Detective:
Behavior monitoring

Corrective:
Session termination
+
Incident response
```

This creates multiple opportunities to manage the risk.

---

### 50. Risk Treatment as Continuous Improvement

AI risk treatment should be treated as an iterative process:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Assess
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
Learn
   ↓
Improve
```

AI systems, threats, data, users, and business requirements can change continuously.

Effective treatment therefore requires ongoing review rather than a one-time remediation exercise.

# 05.15 — AI Risk Treatment Through the AI RMF

## Part 2 — AI Risk Treatment Strategies and Control Design

### 51. Treatment Strategy Selection

Selecting a treatment strategy requires the organization to determine how the identified AI risk should be modified.

The basic decision structure is:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Assess Risk
   ↓
Compare With Risk Appetite
   ↓
Select Treatment Strategy
   ↓
Implement Treatment
   ↓
Validate Effectiveness
   ↓
Reassess Residual Risk
```

The treatment decision should be based on the characteristics of the AI system, the potential consequences, available controls, and organizational objectives.

---

### 52. Treatment Strategy Decision Tree

A practical decision tree can be structured as:

```text
Is the AI activity necessary?
        │
       No
        ↓
     Avoid

       Yes
        ↓
Can the risk be adequately reduced?
        │
       Yes
        ↓
     Reduce

        │
       No
        ↓
Can the activity be redesigned or restricted?
        │
       Yes
        ↓
Redesign / Restrict

        │
       No
        ↓
Can the remaining risk be formally accepted?
        │
       Yes
        ↓
     Accept

        │
       No
        ↓
Do Not Deploy / Suspend / Decommission
```

This type of structure supports consistent decision-making.

---

### 53. Avoidance as a Treatment Strategy

Risk avoidance removes the source of risk by deciding not to perform the activity.

Examples include:

* Not deploying a particular AI use case.
* Removing a high-risk feature.
* Avoiding sensitive data processing.
* Choosing a non-AI solution.
* Cancelling a high-risk integration.

Avoidance is especially relevant when the potential harm cannot be adequately controlled.

---

### 54. Avoidance Does Not Always Mean Abandoning AI

An organization may avoid a specific AI implementation while still pursuing the underlying business objective.

For example:

```text
High-Risk Approach:
Fully autonomous hiring decision

        ↓

Alternative:
AI-assisted candidate analysis
+
Human decision
```

The organization avoids the higher-risk implementation rather than abandoning the business objective entirely.

---

### 55. Risk Reduction Through Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop unwanted events before they occur.

Examples include:

* Identity verification.
* Access controls.
* Least privilege.
* Data filtering.
* Input validation.
* Model guardrails.
* Segmentation.
* Approval requirements.
* Usage restrictions.

Preventive controls are particularly important where AI risks can materialize rapidly.

---

### 56. Risk Reduction Through Detective Controls

Detective controls identify undesirable behavior or conditions.

Examples include:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security logging.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Drift detection.
* Anomaly detection.
* Output review.
* Audit trails.
* User reporting.

Detective controls provide visibility into emerging problems.

---

### 57. Risk Reduction Through Corrective Controls

Corrective controls reduce consequences after a problem occurs.

Examples include:

* Incident response.
* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Transaction reversal.
* Data recovery.
* System isolation.
* Model replacement.

Corrective controls are particularly important where prevention cannot guarantee complete protection.

---

### 58. Layered Treatment

A mature AI risk treatment strategy generally combines different control types.

```text
Prevent
   ↓
Detect
   ↓
Respond
   ↓
Recover
```

For example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
Least Privilege
   ↓
Activity Monitoring
   ↓
Human Intervention
   ↓
Automatic Shutdown
   ↓
Incident Response
```

This provides defense in depth.

---

### 59. Treatment Through AI System Architecture

Risk can often be reduced through architectural decisions.

Examples include:

* Network segmentation.
* Isolated execution environments.
* Restricted APIs.
* Separate production and development environments.
* Controlled model gateways.
* Secure secrets management.
* Sandboxing.
* Policy enforcement layers.

Architectural controls can reduce the blast radius of AI failures.

---

### 60. Reducing AI Autonomy

Autonomy is an important treatment consideration.

An organization can reduce risk by moving from:

```text
Fully Autonomous
        ↓
Semi-Autonomous
        ↓
Human Approved
        ↓
Recommendation Only
```

Reducing autonomy can be particularly useful where AI actions could create significant consequences.

---

### 61. Human Approval as a Treatment

Human approval can be used for high-impact AI actions.

Examples:

* Financial transactions.
* Customer account changes.
* Security actions.
* Employment decisions.
* Access changes.
* High-impact operational decisions.

However, human approval should be meaningful and timely.

---

### 62. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop model requires the human to review and approve an AI-generated action before execution.

```text
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Approve / Reject
      ↓
Action
```

This can reduce the likelihood of harmful automated decisions.

---

### 63. Human-on-the-Loop

In a human-on-the-loop model, the AI can act automatically while a human supervises the system.

```text
AI
 ↓
Automated Action
 ↓
Human Monitoring
 ↓
Intervention if Required
```

This may be appropriate for lower-risk actions but may be insufficient for high-impact decisions where intervention must occur before the action.

---

### 64. Human-in-Command

For highly consequential systems, the organization may retain explicit human authority over whether the AI system operates.

The human can:

* Start the system.
* Stop the system.
* Restrict capabilities.
* Override decisions.
* Disable the system.

This provides stronger governance over autonomous capabilities.

---

### 65. Treatment Through Least Privilege

AI systems should receive only the permissions required for their intended purpose.

Example:

```text
AI Agent
    ↓
Needs:
Read customer ticket

Does NOT Need:
Delete customer account
Transfer funds
Modify identity records
```

Reducing unnecessary privileges limits potential harm.

---

### 66. Privilege Segmentation

Different AI functions can be separated.

```text
AI Component A
→ Read Only

AI Component B
→ Limited Write

AI Component C
→ Administrative Action
```

High-risk privileges can therefore be isolated and subjected to stronger controls.

---

### 67. Treatment Through Data Minimization

Data minimization reduces risk by limiting the amount of information processed.

Instead of:

```text
Entire Customer Database
```

the AI system may receive:

```text
Only Required Customer Attributes
```

This can reduce:

* Privacy exposure.
* Security exposure.
* Unnecessary data processing.
* Potential consequences of a breach.

---

### 68. Treatment Through Data Segmentation

Sensitive information can be separated from general AI processing.

For example:

```text
General AI Environment
        │
        ├── Public Data
        └── Internal Data

Restricted Environment
        │
        └── Highly Sensitive Data
```

The most sensitive information can therefore receive stronger controls.

---

### 69. Treatment Through Data Access Policies

Organizations can define:

* Who can provide data to AI.
* Which AI systems can access it.
* Which data types are permitted.
* Where data may be processed.
* How long data can be retained.

This converts data governance principles into operational controls.

---

### 70. Treatment Through Input Controls

AI systems can validate inputs before processing.

Controls may identify:

* Sensitive information.
* Malicious prompts.
* Unauthorized instructions.
* Unsupported formats.
* Excessive requests.
* Potential injection attempts.

Input controls can reduce both security and reliability risks.

---

### 71. Prompt Injection Treatment

Potential controls include:

```text
Input Filtering
      ↓
Instruction Separation
      ↓
Tool Permission Restrictions
      ↓
Output Validation
      ↓
Human Approval
      ↓
Monitoring
```

No single control should be assumed to eliminate prompt injection risk completely.

---

### 72. Treatment Through Output Controls

AI outputs may require validation before being presented or acted upon.

Examples include:

* Content filtering.
* Policy validation.
* Schema validation.
* Human review.
* Business-rule validation.
* Security inspection.

Output controls are particularly important where AI results trigger downstream actions.

---

### 73. Output Validation

For an AI system generating financial transactions:

```text
AI Generates Transaction
        ↓
Business Rules
        ↓
Transaction Validation
        ↓
Human Approval
        ↓
Execution
```

The AI output is therefore not treated as automatically trustworthy.

---

### 74. Treatment Through Business Rules

Deterministic business rules can constrain AI behavior.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation:
Refund €5,000

Business Rule:
AI cannot approve refunds > €500

Result:
Human approval required
```

This limits AI authority regardless of what the model recommends.

---

### 75. Treatment Through Guardrails

AI guardrails can enforce organizational policies around:

* Content.
* Access.
* Data.
* Tools.
* Actions.
* Output.
* Usage.

Guardrails can operate at multiple layers.

```text
User
 ↓
Application Guardrail
 ↓
AI Model
 ↓
Output Guardrail
 ↓
Business Control
```

---

### 76. Treatment Through Monitoring

Monitoring provides ongoing visibility into AI risk.

Potential monitoring areas include:

* Model performance.
* Security events.
* Access.
* Data usage.
* AI actions.
* Errors.
* Drift.
* Policy violations.
* User complaints.

Monitoring should be proportionate to risk.

---

### 77. Continuous Monitoring for High-Risk AI

High-risk AI systems may require more frequent or continuous monitoring.

For example:

```text
High-Risk AI Agent
       ↓
Continuous Telemetry
       ↓
Anomaly Detection
       ↓
Alert
       ↓
Human Investigation
```

The objective is to detect material changes before they produce significant harm.

---

### 78. Treatment Through Logging

AI systems should maintain appropriate records of significant activity.

Logs may capture:

* User.
* Timestamp.
* Model version.
* Prompt or request metadata.
* Tool invocation.
* AI action.
* Result.
* Approval.
* Error.
* Security event.

Logging supports investigation, accountability, and assurance.

---

### 79. Audit Trails

An audit trail allows the organization to reconstruct important events.

For example:

```text
User Request
   ↓
AI Decision
   ↓
Tool Call
   ↓
Human Approval
   ↓
System Action
```

This can be critical when investigating incidents or demonstrating accountability.

---

### 80. Treatment Through Model Validation

## Part 3 — Risk Treatment Implementation, Validation, Monitoring, and Governance

### 101. Treatment Implementation

Risk treatment becomes operational when approved treatment actions are implemented within the AI system, business process, or governance environment.

A treatment should move through a controlled implementation process:

```text
Treatment Decision
        ↓
Treatment Plan
        ↓
Control Design
        ↓
Implementation
        ↓
Testing
        ↓
Validation
        ↓
Residual Risk Reassessment
```

Implementation should be traceable to the original risk.

---

### 102. Treatment Action Register

A treatment action register can provide centralized visibility.

Recommended fields include:

| Field           | Purpose                         |
| --------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Risk ID         | Identifies the associated risk  |
| Treatment ID    | Identifies the treatment action |
| Risk Owner      | Accountable for the risk        |
| Treatment Owner | Responsible for implementation  |
| Action          | Describes required activity     |
| Priority        | Establishes urgency             |
| Target Date     | Defines expected completion     |
| Status          | Tracks progress                 |
| Evidence        | Demonstrates completion         |
| Validation      | Confirms effectiveness          |
| Residual Risk   | Records remaining exposure      |

---

### 103. Treatment Status

A standardized status model improves reporting.

Example:

```text
Not Started
     ↓
Planned
     ↓
In Progress
     ↓
Implemented
     ↓
Under Validation
     ↓
Validated
     ↓
Closed
```

A treatment should not automatically move to "Closed" simply because implementation is complete.

---

### 104. Treatment Implementation Evidence

Implementation should produce objective evidence.

Examples include:

* Configuration screenshots or records.
* Access-control reports.
* Test results.
* Change tickets.
* Updated procedures.
* Training records.
* Approval records.
* Monitoring dashboards.
* Contract amendments.

Evidence allows the organization to demonstrate that treatment was actually implemented.

---

### 105. Treatment Traceability

Each treatment should remain traceable to the original risk.

```text
Risk ID
   ↓
Risk Statement
   ↓
Treatment Decision
   ↓
Control
   ↓
Implementation
   ↓
Test
   ↓
Residual Risk
```

This prevents controls from becoming disconnected from the risks they are intended to address.

---

### 106. Control Objectives

Before implementing a control, the organization should establish what the control is intended to accomplish.

For example:

**Risk:** Unauthorized AI agent actions.

**Control objective:**

> Ensure that AI agents can execute only authorized actions within approved business boundaries.

The control can then be evaluated against that objective.

---

### 107. Control Design

A control should define:

* Objective.
* Scope.
* Owner.
* Frequency.
* Trigger.
* Procedure.
* Evidence.
* Exception process.
* Testing method.

Poorly defined controls are difficult to operate and difficult to audit.

---

### 108. Preventive Control Design

A preventive control should answer:

> **How will the organization stop the undesirable event from occurring?**

Example:

```text
AI Agent
   ↓
Authorization Check
   ↓
Approved Action?
   ├── Yes → Execute
   └── No  → Block
```

The control should operate before the potentially harmful action.

---

### 109. Detective Control Design

A detective control should answer:

> **How will the organization identify the event if it occurs?**

Example:

```text
AI Activity
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Anomaly Detected
   ↓
Alert
   ↓
Investigation
```

Detection should occur quickly enough to be useful relative to the risk velocity.

---

### 110. Corrective Control Design

A corrective control should answer:

> **What will the organization do after an unwanted event occurs?**

Examples include:

* Disable the AI capability.
* Revoke credentials.
* Roll back the model.
* Restore data.
* Reverse an action.
* Activate incident response.

---

### 111. Control Frequency

Controls may operate:

* Continuously.
* In real time.
* Daily.
* Weekly.
* Monthly.
* Quarterly.
* After a significant change.

The frequency should reflect the risk.

A high-velocity AI risk may require continuous controls rather than quarterly reviews.

---

### 112. Control Automation

Where appropriate, controls can be automated.

Examples include:

* Automatic access enforcement.
* Automated data classification.
* Automated prompt filtering.
* Automated model monitoring.
* Automated policy enforcement.
* Automatic transaction limits.

Automation can improve consistency but should itself be governed.

---

### 113. Automated Control Failure

Automated controls can fail because of:

* Configuration errors.
* Software defects.
* Integration failures.
* Incorrect assumptions.
* Model changes.
* Infrastructure outages.

Therefore, organizations should monitor the controls themselves.

---

### 114. Control Monitoring

Control monitoring should verify:

```text
Control Exists
      ↓
Control Operates
      ↓
Control Operates Correctly
      ↓
Control Reduces Risk
```

The existence of a control does not prove effectiveness.

---

### 115. Control Effectiveness

Control effectiveness can be assessed using categories such as:

```text
Effective
Partially Effective
Ineffective
Not Tested
```

Organizations may use numerical scoring where appropriate.

---

### 116. Effective Control

A control is effective when:

* It is appropriately designed.
* It operates as intended.
* It addresses the relevant risk.
* Evidence demonstrates operation.
* Testing confirms effectiveness.

---

### 117. Partially Effective Control

A control may be partially effective where:

* It covers only some scenarios.
* It has implementation gaps.
* It operates inconsistently.
* It has excessive exceptions.
* Monitoring is insufficient.

Partial effectiveness should be reflected in residual risk assessment.

---

### 118. Ineffective Control

A control may be ineffective where:

* It does not address the risk.
* It is routinely bypassed.
* It is incorrectly configured.
* It fails testing.
* It lacks operational evidence.

An ineffective control should not materially reduce the risk assessment.

---

### 119. Control Exceptions

Organizations should maintain a controlled exception process.

An exception should document:

* Control being bypassed.
* Reason.
* Duration.
* Risk impact.
* Compensating controls.
* Approval authority.
* Expiration date.

Exceptions should not become permanent undocumented practices.

---

### 120. Compensating Controls

A compensating control can be used when the primary control cannot be implemented.

Example:

```text
Primary Control:
Automated AI output validation

Unavailable

Compensating Control:
Manual expert review
```

The compensating control should provide sufficient risk reduction for the temporary condition.

---

### 121. Treatment Validation

Treatment validation determines whether the implemented treatment achieved its intended result.

The basic question is:

> **Did the treatment reduce the risk as expected?**

Validation may include:

* Testing.
* Independent review.
* Monitoring.
* Simulation.
* Audit.
* Performance analysis.
* Red-team testing.

---

### 122. Treatment Verification Versus Validation

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Verification** asks:

> Was the treatment implemented according to its requirements?

**Validation** asks:

> Does the implemented treatment actually achieve the intended risk-reduction objective?

For example:

```text
Verification:
MFA is configured.

Validation:
Unauthorized-access testing demonstrates that MFA effectively reduces the risk.
```

---

### 123. Independent Validation

High-impact AI treatments may require independent validation.

Independence helps reduce the possibility that the implementation team evaluates its own work without sufficient challenge.

Independent reviewers may include:

* Cybersecurity.
* Risk management.
* Compliance.
* Internal audit.
* Independent assurance teams.

---

### 124. Treatment Testing

Testing should be proportional to risk.

Low-risk AI systems may require:

* Basic control verification.

High-risk systems may require:

* Security testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Model validation.
* Privacy testing.
* Human-factors testing.
* Resilience testing.
* Independent review.

---

### 125. Security Treatment Testing

Security treatment testing may examine:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Privilege boundaries.
* API security.
* Prompt injection.
* Data leakage.
* Model abuse.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.

The testing should reflect realistic attack scenarios.

---

### 126. Privacy Treatment Testing

Privacy-related treatment may be tested for:

* Data minimization.
* Access restrictions.
* Retention.
* Data leakage.
* Unintended disclosure.
* Appropriate processing.
* Privacy-enhancing controls.

The objective is to determine whether the treatment actually reduces privacy exposure.

---

### 127. Reliability Treatment Testing

AI reliability controls can be tested through:

* Accuracy testing.
* Failure testing.
* Edge-case testing.
* Drift testing.
* Stress testing.
* Data-quality testing.

Testing should reflect the AI system's intended operational environment.

---

### 128. Human Oversight Testing

Human oversight should also be tested.

Questions include:

* Does the reviewer receive sufficient information?
* Can the reviewer understand the AI output?
* Can the reviewer intervene?
* Is intervention fast enough?
* Does the reviewer have authority?
* Are overrides recorded?

This determines whether human oversight is genuinely effective.

---

### 129. Treatment Validation Results

Validation should produce a clear conclusion.

Example:

```text
Treatment:
Human approval for transactions > €500

Validation:
Successful

Evidence:
Sample testing of 1,000 transactions

Result:
All transactions above threshold required approval

Conclusion:
Control effective
```

The result should feed into residual risk assessment.

---

### 130. Residual Risk Reassessment

Once treatment has been validated, residual risk should be reassessed.

```text
Original Residual Risk
        ↓
Treatment
        ↓
Validation
        ↓
New Residual Risk
```

The new assessment should use the approved organizational methodology.

---

### 131. Treatment That Does Not Reduce Risk

Sometimes treatment does not produce the expected result.

Example:

```text
Initial Risk:
High

Treatment:
Output filtering

Expected:
Moderate

Actual:
High
```

The organization should investigate why the treatment failed.

Possible responses include:

* Improve the control.
* Add additional controls.
* Redesign the system.
* Restrict functionality.
* Suspend deployment.
* Accept the risk only if authorized.

---

### 132. Treatment Effectiveness Gap

The difference between expected and actual risk reduction can be treated as a treatment effectiveness gap.

```text
Expected Residual Risk:
Moderate

Actual Residual Risk:
High

Gap:
Significant
```

This should trigger additional management attention.

---

### 133. Risk Treatment and Risk Appetite

The final residual risk should be compared with the organization's approved risk appetite.

```text
Residual Risk
      ↓
Compare with Appetite
      ↓
Within Appetite?
   /          \
 Yes           No
 ↓             ↓
Monitor      Treat / Escalate
```

Risk treatment is not complete until this decision is addressed.

---

### 134. Within Risk Appetite

If residual risk is within appetite:

```text
Treatment
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Within Appetite
   ↓
Accept / Monitor
```

Management may allow the AI system to continue operating while maintaining appropriate monitoring.

---

### 135. Outside Risk Appetite

If residual risk remains outside appetite:

```text
Treatment
   ↓
Residual Risk
   ↓
Outside Appetite
   ↓
Additional Treatment
       OR
Restrict / Suspend / Avoid
       OR
Authorized Risk Acceptance
```

The organization should not silently tolerate the exposure.

---

### 136. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should be an explicit governance decision.

The decision should document:

* Risk.
* Residual exposure.
* Business rationale.
* Applicable controls.
* Acceptance authority.
* Conditions.
* Expiration or review date.

---

### 137. Risk Acceptance Authority

Acceptance authority should correspond to the magnitude of risk.

For example:

```text
Low Risk
→ Operational Management

Moderate Risk
→ Senior Management

High Risk
→ Executive Management

Critical Risk
→ Appropriate Executive / Risk Committee
```

Actual authority levels should be established by organizational policy.

---

### 138. Conditional Risk Acceptance

Acceptance may include conditions.

Example:

> Residual risk is accepted temporarily provided that enhanced monitoring remains active and the permanent control is implemented by the agreed deadline.

This provides controlled flexibility.

---

### 139. Temporary Risk Acceptance

Temporary acceptance may be appropriate when:

* A permanent control is being developed.
* A business-critical service cannot immediately be stopped.
* A compensating control exists.
* The exposure is actively monitored.

Temporary acceptance should have an expiration date.

---

### 140. Expired Risk Acceptance

Expired acceptance should trigger review.

```text
Risk Acceptance
      ↓
Expiration Date
      ↓
Review
      ↓
Extend / Treat / Escalate / Close
```

Expired risk acceptance should not automatically continue indefinitely.

---

### 141. Risk Treatment and Exceptions

Risk acceptance and control exceptions should be linked where appropriate.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Additional Risk
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Temporary Acceptance
      ↓
Remediation
```

This creates a complete governance chain.

---

### 142. Treatment Prioritization Portfolio

Large organizations may have hundreds of AI treatment actions.

Portfolio prioritization can consider:

* Criticality.
* Regulatory deadlines.
* Customer impact.
* Risk velocity.
* Treatment dependency.
* Cost.
* Resource availability.

This helps management allocate limited resources.

---

### 143. Treatment Dependencies

Some treatment actions depend on others.

Example:

```text
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
Human Approval
        ↓
Production Deployment
```

A delay in identity architecture may therefore delay multiple AI controls.

---

### 144. Treatment Roadmap

A roadmap can organize treatment actions over time.

```text

Access Controls

Month 2
Monitoring

Month 3
Output Validation

Month 4
Independent Testing

Month 5
Risk Reassessment
```

The actual schedule should reflect risk priority and organizational capacity.

---

### 145. Quick Wins

Some treatment actions can reduce risk quickly.

Examples include:

* Disabling unused integrations.
* Restricting sensitive data.
* Enabling logging.
* Applying rate limits.
* Requiring human approval.

Quick wins can reduce immediate exposure while longer-term controls are developed.

### 146. Strategic Treatments

Other treatments require significant investment.

Examples include:

* AI governance platforms.

## Part 4 — Continuous Monitoring, Metrics, Assurance, and Practical AI Risk Treatment

### 151. Continuous Monitoring of AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment does not end when a control is implemented and validated.

AI systems operate in changing environments, and therefore the effectiveness of risk treatment can change over time.

A continuous treatment cycle is:

```text id="4xj3ay"
Risk Identified
      ↓
Treatment Implemented
      ↓
Control Validated
      ↓
Residual Risk Assessed
      ↓
Continuous Monitoring
      ↓
Change Detected
      ↓
Reassessment
      ↓
Additional Treatment
```

This creates an ongoing governance process rather than a one-time remediation activity.

---

### 152. Why Continuous Monitoring Is Necessary

AI risk can change because of:

* Model updates.
* New data.
* New users.
* New threats.
* New integrations.
* Changes in business processes.
* Changes in regulation.
* Changes in AI provider behavior.
* Changes in system autonomy.
* Changes in the operating environment.

A treatment that was effective six months ago may no longer provide adequate protection.

---

### 153. Treatment Monitoring Objectives

Monitoring should determine whether:

1. The treatment remains implemented.
2. The control continues to operate.
3. The control remains effective.
4. Risk conditions have changed.
5. New risks have emerged.
6. Residual risk remains within appetite.
7. Treatment assumptions remain valid.

---

### 154. Treatment Monitoring Indicators

Organizations can establish indicators such as:

* Control failures.
* Policy violations.
* Security alerts.
* AI incidents.
* Model drift.
* Performance degradation.
* Increased exception rates.
* Unauthorized access attempts.
* Human override frequency.
* Customer complaints.

These indicators can provide early warning of changing risk.

---

### 155. Key Risk Indicators

Key Risk Indicators, or KRIs, can provide management with information about risk exposure.

Example:

```text id="t7bjai"
KRI:
Unauthorized AI Tool Calls

Threshold:
> 10 per day

Current:
4

Status:
Within Threshold
```

Thresholds should be defined according to the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 156. Key Performance Indicators

Key Performance Indicators, or KPIs, can measure operational performance.

Examples include:

* Percentage of AI assessments completed.
* Percentage of treatments completed on time.
* Average treatment duration.
* Percentage of controls tested.
* Percentage of high-risk systems monitored.

KPIs measure activity and performance, while KRIs focus more directly on risk exposure.

---

### 157. Key Control Indicators

Key Control Indicators can help determine whether critical controls are operating.

Examples include:

* MFA coverage.
* Privileged-account review completion.
* Logging coverage.
* Human approval compliance.
* Model monitoring coverage.
* Data-loss prevention coverage.

---

### 158. Treatment Thresholds

Thresholds can trigger predefined actions.

Example:

```text id="s7i3j5"

Normal

Amber:
Management Review

Red:
Immediate Escalation
```

This allows organizations to respond consistently when risk indicators deteriorate.

---

### 159. Risk Trend Monitoring

Risk should be monitored not only by its current level but also by its direction.

Example:

```text id="a1udk9"
High Risk
↓
Moderate
↓
Low
```

represents improving exposure.

Conversely:

```text id="e6y0c8"
Low
↓
Moderate
↓
High
```

indicates increasing exposure.

### 160. Treatment Trend

Treatment progress can also be monitored.

Example:

```text id="sl1h0g"

50

After Treatment:
35

Later:
20
```

A declining number of unresolved high-priority risks may indicate improving risk management.

However, the number of risks alone should not be used as the only measure of effectiveness.

### 161. Risk Treatment Aging

Treatment aging measures how long a risk treatment remains unresolved.

Example:

| Priority |      Age | Management Concern |
| -------- | -------: | ------------------ |
| Critical |   5 days | Immediate          |
| High     |  45 days | Elevated           |
| Moderate | 120 days | Monitor            |

### 162. Treatment SLA

```text id="4ufqsa"
Accelerated remediation

Planned remediation

Low:
Routine remediation
```

Treatment SLAs should reflect risk rather than arbitrary deadlines.

### 163. Overdue Treatment

An overdue treatment is not automatically equivalent to a risk incident.

However, it should trigger review where:

* The risk remains outside appetite.
* The deadline was tied to regulatory requirements.
* Compensating controls have expired.
* The risk has increased.
* Management approval has expired.

### 164. Treatment Escalation

A treatment may require escalation when:

* The due date is missed.
* The risk increases.
* Controls fail.
* Residual risk exceeds appetite.
* A regulatory deadline approaches.
* Resources are insufficient.
* Dependencies prevent completion.

Escalation should follow the organization's governance structure.

### 165. AI Incident as a Treatment Trigger

An AI incident can trigger immediate reassessment.

* Unauthorized AI action.
* Security compromise.
* Harmful model output.
* Significant customer complaint.
* Regulatory inquiry.

The incident may reveal that existing treatment is inadequate.

* Governance failure.

### 169. Near-Miss Events

```text id="q9cwhb"
   ↓
```

### 170. Control Failure Versus AI Failure

These should be distinguished.

**Control failure**

The mechanism intended to prevent, detect, or respond to the failure does not work.

### 173. Data Drift

* Increased false positives.

* Unexpected system behavior.

### 250. Final Principle of AI Risk Treatment

AI risk treatment should ultimately answer four questions:

```text id="3z2p3d"
1. What risk are we trying to reduce?

2. What treatment will modify that risk?

3. How do we know the treatment works?

4. What residual risk remains after treatment?
```

The objective is not simply to implement more controls.

The objective is to make **deliberate, evidence-based decisions that bring AI risk to a level consistent with the organization's risk appetite, obligations, business objectives, and responsibility to affected stakeholders.**
