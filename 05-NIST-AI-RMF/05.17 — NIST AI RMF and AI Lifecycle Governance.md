# 05.17 — NIST AI RMF and AI Lifecycle Governance

## Part 1 — Applying GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE Across the AI Lifecycle

### 1. Introduction to NIST AI RMF and AI Lifecycle Governance

AI governance should not be treated as a single approval event performed before an AI system enters production.

AI systems evolve throughout their lifecycle, and risk can change at every stage.

A lifecycle-based governance model can be represented as:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Design
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Testing
 ↓
Validation
 ↓
Deployment
 ↓
Operation
 ↓
Monitoring
 ↓
Change
 ↓
Retirement
```

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a useful structure for managing AI risk across these lifecycle activities through four core functions:

```text
GOVERN
MAP
MEASURE
MANAGE
```

These functions should operate throughout the lifecycle rather than being treated as isolated sequential steps.

---

### 2. AI Lifecycle Governance

AI lifecycle governance is the process of establishing oversight, accountability, risk management, controls, and decision-making throughout the existence of an AI system.

The objective is to ensure that:

* AI use is authorized.
* Risks are identified.
* Controls are implemented.
* Performance is evaluated.
* Changes are governed.
* Incidents are addressed.
* Residual risks remain understood.
* Retirement is controlled.

---

### 3. Lifecycle Governance Principle

A fundamental principle is:

> AI risk management begins before development and continues until the AI system is formally retired.

This means governance should address both technical and organizational activities.

---

### 4. AI Lifecycle Stages

A practical AI lifecycle can be divided into:

1. Idea and use-case identification.
2. Business and risk justification.
3. Design.
4. Development.
5. Testing.
6. Validation.
7. Deployment.
8. Production operation.
9. Monitoring.
10. Change and revalidation.
11. Retirement.

Different risks become more prominent at different stages.

---

### 5. GOVERN Across the Lifecycle

The GOVERN function establishes the organizational context in which AI risk is managed.

It addresses areas such as:

* Policies.
* Accountability.
* Roles.
* Risk appetite.
* Legal and regulatory requirements.
* Organizational values.
* Stakeholder responsibilities.
* Governance processes.
* Risk culture.

GOVERN should therefore be present across every lifecycle stage.

---

### 6. MAP Across the Lifecycle

The MAP function establishes the context for the AI system and identifies relevant risks.

It considers:

* Intended purpose.
* Intended users.
* Stakeholders.
* Operating environment.
* Data.
* Potential impacts.
* Threats.
* Dependencies.
* Assumptions.
* Constraints.

MAP provides the contextual understanding necessary for meaningful risk management.

---

### 7. MEASURE Across the Lifecycle

The MEASURE function evaluates and analyzes AI risks using appropriate methods and evidence.

Measurement may include:

* Testing.
* Metrics.
* Performance evaluation.
* Security testing.
* Privacy assessment.
* Fairness assessment.
* Robustness evaluation.
* Monitoring.
* Validation.
* Independent assessment.

MEASURE provides evidence about whether identified risks and controls are behaving as expected.

---

### 8. MANAGE Across the Lifecycle

The MANAGE function prioritizes and responds to identified AI risks.

Possible responses include:

* Mitigation.
* Risk reduction.
* Risk avoidance.
* Risk transfer.
* Risk acceptance.
* Increased monitoring.
* Restriction.
* Suspension.
* Retirement.

MANAGE connects risk analysis with practical decisions.

---

### 9. The Four Functions Are Interdependent

The four functions should not be understood as a simple linear sequence.

```text
        GOVERN
       ↙      ↘
    MAP  ↔  MEASURE
       ↘      ↙
        MANAGE
```

Governance establishes the context.

Mapping establishes the risk context.

Measurement produces evidence.

Management determines and implements responses.

The results can then influence governance and subsequent mapping activities.

---

### 10. Lifecycle Governance Model

A simplified model is:

```text
                 GOVERN
                   ↓
IDEA → DESIGN → DEVELOPMENT → TESTING
 ↓       ↓          ↓             ↓
MAP     MAP        MAP           MAP
 ↓       ↓          ↓             ↓
MEASURE MEASURE    MEASURE       MEASURE
 ↓       ↓          ↓             ↓
MANAGE  MANAGE     MANAGE        MANAGE

        ↓

VALIDATION → DEPLOYMENT → OPERATION
     ↓            ↓            ↓
    MAP          MAP          MAP
    MEASURE      MEASURE      MEASURE
    MANAGE       MANAGE       MANAGE

        ↓

MONITORING → CHANGE → RETIREMENT
     ↓          ↓          ↓
    MAP        MAP        MAP
    MEASURE    MEASURE    MEASURE
    MANAGE     MANAGE     MANAGE
```

This demonstrates that risk management continues throughout the lifecycle.

---

### 11. Idea Stage

The idea stage is where an organization identifies a potential AI use case.

Questions may include:

* What problem is AI intended to solve?
* Why is AI being considered?
* What business objective is involved?
* Who will use the system?
* Who could be affected?
* What decisions will the AI influence?
* What information will it process?

Governance should begin before technical development.

---

### 12. GOVERN at the Idea Stage

At the idea stage, GOVERN may establish:

* AI policy requirements.
* Approval authority.
* Risk classification requirements.
* Responsible AI principles.
* Security requirements.
* Privacy requirements.
* Legal review requirements.
* Acceptable-use boundaries.

This determines whether the proposed use case is permissible within the organization's governance framework.

---

### 13. MAP at the Idea Stage

MAP should establish the initial context.

For example:

```text
Business Problem
      ↓
AI Use Case
      ↓
Intended Purpose
      ↓
Affected Stakeholders
      ↓
Potential Benefits
      ↓
Potential Risks
```

The organization should understand the problem before selecting an AI solution.

---

### 14. MEASURE at the Idea Stage

Measurement at this stage may be preliminary.

The organization can evaluate:

* Potential impact.
* Initial risk level.
* Feasibility.
* Data availability.
* Security concerns.
* Privacy concerns.
* Expected performance requirements.

Detailed model testing may not yet be possible.

---

### 15. MANAGE at the Idea Stage

Management decisions may include:

* Approve exploration.
* Request additional analysis.
* Restrict the use case.
* Require additional safeguards.
* Reject the use case.

A use case should not automatically proceed simply because AI technology is technically available.

---

### 16. Use-Case Approval

A formal AI use-case approval process can establish whether the proposed application is:

* Aligned with business objectives.
* Permitted by policy.
* Legally acceptable.
* Technically feasible.
* Risk-manageable.
* Appropriately governed.

This creates an early governance checkpoint.

---

### 17. Risk Classification at the Idea Stage

AI use cases can be classified according to factors such as:

* Potential harm.
* Business criticality.
* Data sensitivity.
* Degree of automation.
* Level of autonomy.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Security impact.

The classification should influence the depth of governance required.

---

### 18. Design Stage

During design, the organization determines how the AI system will operate.

Design decisions may include:

* Architecture.
* Model selection.
* Data sources.
* Interfaces.
* Human oversight.
* Security controls.
* Access controls.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Failure handling.

Risk treatment should influence architecture rather than being added after development.

---

### 19. GOVERN at the Design Stage

Governance should define design expectations such as:

* Security-by-design.
* Privacy-by-design.
* Human oversight.
* Documentation.
* Accountability.
* Approved technologies.
* Architecture standards.
* Risk-based control requirements.

These expectations should be translated into technical and operational requirements.

---

### 20. MAP at the Design Stage

MAP should identify how the proposed architecture changes the risk context.

Questions may include:

* What data enters the system?
* Where is data processed?
* Which external services are involved?
* What systems can the AI access?
* What actions can the AI perform?
* What happens when the AI fails?
* Who can override the AI?

This provides the foundation for architectural risk analysis.

---

### 21. MEASURE at the Design Stage

Measurement may include:

* Threat modeling.
* Privacy analysis.
* Security architecture assessment.
* Data-quality assessment.
* Model feasibility analysis.
* Performance requirements.
* Control requirements.

The objective is to determine whether the proposed design can satisfy the organization's risk requirements.

---

### 22. MANAGE at the Design Stage

Risk management decisions may result in:

* Architectural controls.
* Restricted access.
* Reduced AI autonomy.
* Additional human oversight.
* Data minimization.
* Network segmentation.
* Secure AI gateways.
* Additional monitoring.

Design is therefore an important opportunity to reduce risk before implementation.

---

### 23. Development Stage

During development, the AI system is constructed and configured.

Activities may include:

* Data preparation.
* Model development.
* Prompt development.
* Application development.
* Integration.
* Configuration.
* Security implementation.
* Guardrail implementation.

Governance requirements should remain active during development.

---

### 24. GOVERN During Development

Governance may require:

* Approved development processes.
* Secure coding practices.
* Access controls.
* Separation of duties.
* Version control.
* Documentation.
* Change management.
* Developer training.

Development activities should remain traceable.

---

### 25. MAP During Development

The organization should continue identifying risks as implementation details become clearer.

New risks may emerge because of:

* Actual data characteristics.
* Model limitations.
* Integration dependencies.
* Security weaknesses.
* New capabilities.
* Unexpected use cases.

The initial risk assessment should therefore not be considered permanently complete.

---

### 26. MEASURE During Development

Measurement may include:

* Data-quality testing.
* Model evaluation.
* Security testing.
* Robustness testing.
* Bias testing.
* Performance testing.
* Adversarial testing.

Results should be documented and compared against defined requirements.

---

### 27. MANAGE During Development

Development findings may result in:

* Code changes.
* Model changes.
* Data changes.
* Additional controls.
* Restricted functionality.
* Additional testing.
* Change in intended use.

Risk management should remain iterative.

---

### 28. Testing Stage

Testing determines whether the AI system behaves according to its intended requirements and whether relevant controls operate effectively.

Testing can cover:

* Functional behavior.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Performance.
* Robustness.
* Reliability.
* Fairness.
* Safety.
* Human oversight.

---

### 29. GOVERN During Testing

Governance should define:

* Testing requirements.
* Test ownership.
* Acceptance criteria.
* Required evidence.
* Independence requirements.
* Approval authority.

High-risk AI systems may require stronger independent testing.

---

### 30. MAP During Testing

MAP provides the context for determining what should be tested.

Testing should reflect:

* Intended use.
* Known risks.
* Stakeholder impacts.
* Threat scenarios.
* Failure scenarios.
* Regulatory requirements.

Testing should therefore be risk-based rather than simply technical.

---

### 31. MEASURE During Testing

Testing produces evidence about:

* Model performance.
* Control effectiveness.
* Failure rates.
* Security weaknesses.
* Data behavior.
* Robustness.
* Reliability.

The evidence should be documented and traceable.

---

### 32. MANAGE During Testing

Testing results may lead to:

```text
Pass
 ↓
Proceed

Conditional Pass
 ↓
Additional Controls

Fail
 ↓
Remediation

Critical Failure
 ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

This ensures testing results influence actual governance decisions.

---

### 33. Validation Stage

Validation provides additional confidence that the AI system is fit for its intended purpose and that risk controls are appropriate.

Validation may examine:

* Requirements.
* Model performance.
* Risk controls.
* Documentation.
* Testing evidence.
* Intended use.
* Residual risk.

---

### 34. Independent Validation

For higher-risk AI systems, validation may need appropriate independence from the development team.

This helps reduce the risk of self-assessment bias.

For example:

```text
Development Team
       ↓
Builds AI System

Independent Validator
       ↓
Reviews Evidence
       ↓
Challenges Results
```

---

### 35. GOVERN at Validation

Governance should determine:

* Who can validate.
* What must be validated.
* What evidence is required.
* What constitutes acceptable validation.
* Who can approve deployment.

---

### 36. MAP at Validation

Validation should confirm that the system is still being evaluated against the correct context.

Changes in:

* Use case.
* Data.
* Users.
* Model.
* Architecture.
* Business environment

may require the validation scope to be reconsidered.

---

### 37. MEASURE at Validation

Validation should evaluate available evidence against predefined criteria.

Evidence may include:

* Test results.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Performance results.
* Model documentation.
* Monitoring design.
* Control validation.

---

### 38. MANAGE at Validation

Validation findings can result in:

* Approval.
* Conditional approval.
* Additional remediation.
* Restricted deployment.
* Reassessment.
* Rejection.

The decision should be documented.

---

### 39. Deployment Stage

Deployment moves the AI system into its operational environment.

Deployment introduces additional risks because the system now interacts with:

* Real users.
* Real data.
* Production infrastructure.
* Business processes.
* External services.

Deployment should therefore be treated as a controlled governance event.

---

### 40. GOVERN at Deployment

Deployment governance may require:

* Formal approval.
* Defined system ownership.
* Production access controls.
* Monitoring activation.
* Incident procedures.
* Business continuity arrangements.
* Documentation completion.

---

### 41. MAP at Deployment

The organization should confirm that the production environment matches the assessed environment.

Differences may include:

* Different data.
* Additional users.
* Additional integrations.
* Different infrastructure.
* Increased transaction volume.
* Additional permissions.

Material differences should trigger reassessment.

---

### 42. MEASURE at Deployment

Initial production monitoring may measure:

* Availability.
* Performance.
* Error rates.
* Security events.
* User behavior.
* Model behavior.
* Control effectiveness.

This establishes an operational baseline.

---

### 43. MANAGE at Deployment

If deployment produces unexpected risk, the organization may:

* Restrict access.
* Reduce functionality.
* Increase monitoring.
* Require additional human review.
* Roll back the deployment.
* Suspend the AI system.

Deployment should therefore have defined rollback and escalation mechanisms.

---

### 44. Production Operation

During operation, the AI system becomes part of normal business activity.

Governance must continue through:

* Operational monitoring.
* Risk monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Performance monitoring.
* Incident management.
* Control testing.

---

### 45. GOVERN During Operation

Operational governance should maintain:

* Ownership.
* Accountability.
* Policy compliance.
* Risk reporting.
* Exception management.
* Periodic review.

The AI system should remain within its approved operating boundaries.

---

### 46. MAP During Operation

The organization should remain aware of changes in:

* Users.
* Data.
* Threats.
* Business processes.
* Dependencies.
* Stakeholders.
* Regulatory requirements.

Operational context can evolve significantly after deployment.

---

### 47. MEASURE During Operation

Operational measurement may include:

* Accuracy.
* Reliability.
* Availability.
* Drift.
* Security events.
* Privacy events.
* Human overrides.
* Control failures.
* User complaints.

These measurements provide evidence about continuing risk.

---

### 48. MANAGE During Operation

Operational risk responses may include:

* Control enhancement.
* Configuration changes.
* Additional training.
* Increased monitoring.
* Restricted functionality.
* Model adjustment.
* Risk acceptance.
* Suspension.

---

### 49. Lifecycle Governance as a Closed Loop

AI lifecycle governance should ultimately operate as a continuous loop:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
MANAGE
   ↓
Monitor Outcomes
   ↓
Identify Change
   ↓
Reassess
   ↓
GOVERN
```

This ensures that AI risk management remains connected to the actual behavior and changing context of the AI system throughout its lifecycle.

## Part 2 — Lifecycle Risk Management, Change Management, Monitoring, and Reassessment

### 50. AI Lifecycle Governance Is Iterative

AI lifecycle governance should not be viewed as a one-time sequence.

The system can move backward to earlier governance activities whenever new information changes the risk profile.

```text
Operate
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
New Risk Identified
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
MANAGE
   ↓
Implement Change
   ↓
Monitor Again
```

This iterative approach is particularly important for AI because models, data, users, threats, and dependencies can change over time.

---

### 51. Continuous Risk Identification

Risk identification should continue after deployment.

New risks can emerge from:

* New threats.
* New vulnerabilities.
* Model behavior.
* Data changes.
* User behavior.
* New integrations.
* New business processes.
* Regulatory developments.
* Vendor changes.

Therefore, the original risk assessment should be treated as a baseline rather than a permanent conclusion.

---

### 52. Risk Context Can Change

An AI system may remain technically unchanged while its risk context changes.

For example:

```text
Same AI Model
      ↓
New Customer Population
      ↓
Higher Impact
      ↓
Different Risk Profile
```

This demonstrates why lifecycle governance must consider the environment around the AI system, not only the model itself.

---

### 53. Context Reassessment

Context should be reassessed when there is a material change to:

* Purpose.
* Users.
* Data.
* Geography.
* Business process.
* Decision impact.
* Autonomy.
* Technology.
* Third-party dependencies.

The reassessment should determine whether existing controls remain adequate.

---

### 54. Intended Use

The intended use defines what the AI system was approved to do.

For example:

> An AI assistant is approved to summarize internal documents.

If users begin using it to make customer eligibility decisions, the system may now be operating outside its approved purpose.

This creates a governance concern.

---

### 55. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

Organizations should compare intended use with actual use.

```text
Approved Use
     ↕
Actual Use
     ↓
Identify Differences
     ↓
Risk Assessment
```

Differences may indicate:

* Scope creep.
* Shadow AI.
* Unauthorized use.
* New risk.
* Need for governance modification.

---

### 56. Use-Case Drift

Use-case drift occurs when an AI system gradually begins being used for purposes different from its originally approved purpose.

For example:

```text
Original:
Internal Productivity Assistant

Later:
Customer Recommendations

Later:
Automated Customer Decisions
```

Each expansion can introduce materially different risks.

---

### 57. Model Drift

Model drift occurs when the relationship between model behavior and the operating environment changes over time.

Potential indicators include:

* Declining accuracy.
* Increasing errors.
* Changing input distributions.
* Changing output patterns.

Model drift may require:

* Revalidation.
* Retraining.
* Configuration changes.
* Additional monitoring.

---

### 58. Data Drift

Data drift occurs when the characteristics of incoming data change.

For example:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Historical Customer Population

Production Data
      ↓
New Customer Population
```

The difference may affect model performance and risk.

---

### 59. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the underlying relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

For example, a model trained using historical purchasing behavior may become less reliable when customer behavior changes significantly.

This can create performance and decision-making risks.

---

### 60. Configuration Drift

Configuration drift occurs when system configuration changes from its approved state.

Potential changes include:

* System prompts.
* Guardrails.
* Access permissions.
* API endpoints.
* Model parameters.
* Tool permissions.
* Network configuration.

Configuration drift should be monitored for material AI systems.

---

### 61. Governance Drift

Governance drift can occur when actual AI operations gradually diverge from approved governance requirements.

Examples include:

* Documentation becoming outdated.
* Reviews no longer being performed.
* Exceptions becoming permanent.
* Ownership becoming unclear.
* Monitoring becoming incomplete.

Governance drift can be as important as technical drift.

---

### 62. Change Management

AI changes should be governed according to their potential risk impact.

Changes may include:

* New model.
* Model version update.
* New prompt.
* New data source.
* New integration.
* New tool.
* New user population.
* New geographic deployment.
* Increased autonomy.

---

### 63. Risk-Based Change Classification

Not every change requires the same level of governance.

A practical classification may be:

```text
Low Impact Change
      ↓
Standard Change Process

Moderate Impact Change
      ↓
Risk Review

High Impact Change
      ↓
Formal Assessment + Validation

Critical Change
      ↓
Executive / Governance Approval
```

The organization's methodology should define the exact criteria.

---

### 64. Minor AI Changes

Examples of potentially lower-impact changes include:

* Interface improvements.
* Non-material documentation changes.
* Cosmetic changes.
* Operational configuration changes with no material risk impact.

Even minor changes should remain traceable.

---

### 65. Material AI Changes

Material changes may alter:

* AI capabilities.
* Risk exposure.
* Data processing.
* User population.
* Autonomy.
* Decision impact.

These changes should normally trigger additional risk assessment.

---

### 66. Major Model Changes

Replacing an AI model can significantly alter:

* Performance.
* Security characteristics.
* Data requirements.
* Output behavior.
* Bias characteristics.
* Cost.
* Availability.
* Third-party dependencies.

A new model should therefore not automatically inherit the previous model's approval.

---

### 67. Model Version Control

AI systems should maintain traceability between:

```text
Model Version
+
Configuration
+
Data Version
+
Prompt / System Instructions
+
Deployment Date
+
Approval
```

This allows the organization to reconstruct what was operating at a particular point in time.

---

### 68. Change Traceability

A material AI change should be traceable from request through implementation.

```text
Change Request
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Approval
      ↓
Implementation
      ↓
Testing
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Monitoring
```

This creates an auditable lifecycle record.

---

### 69. Change Approval

Change approval should be performed by an appropriately authorized person or governance body.

Approval should consider:

* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Testing results.
* Security impact.
* Privacy impact.
* Compliance requirements.
* Residual risk.

---

### 70. Separation of Duties

For higher-risk changes, responsibilities may be separated.

For example:

```text
Developer
   ↓
Builds Change

Tester
   ↓
Tests Change

Risk / Validator
   ↓
Reviews Risk

Approver
   ↓
Authorizes Production
```

This reduces the risk of uncontrolled self-approval.

---

### 71. Emergency Changes

Emergency changes may be required to address:

* Security vulnerabilities.
* Critical AI failures.
* Major incidents.
* Provider outages.
* Unsafe behavior.

Emergency changes should still be documented and reviewed retrospectively.

---

### 72. Emergency Change Review

A simplified process is:

```text
Emergency Identified
      ↓
Immediate Risk Decision
      ↓
Emergency Change
      ↓
Stabilization
      ↓
Testing
      ↓
Post-Implementation Review
```

Emergency procedures should not become a permanent shortcut around governance.

---

### 73. Revalidation After Change

A material AI change may require revalidation.

Revalidation determines whether:

* Previous assumptions remain valid.
* Controls remain effective.
* Performance remains acceptable.
* New risks have emerged.
* Existing approval remains appropriate.

---

### 74. Change Impact Assessment

A change impact assessment can examine:

| Area        | Question                                    |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Security    | Does attack exposure change?                |
| Privacy     | Does data processing change?                |
| Performance | Does expected performance change?           |
| Safety      | Can the system create new harmful outcomes? |
| Compliance  | Do regulatory obligations change?           |
| Operations  | Does the operating model change?            |
| Governance  | Does ownership or oversight change?         |

---

### 75. AI Risk Reassessment Triggers

Risk reassessment may be triggered by:

* Material model changes.
* Significant incidents.
* New vulnerabilities.
* New regulations.
* New use cases.
* New stakeholders.
* New data.
* New providers.
* Significant performance degradation.
* Changes in risk appetite.

---

### 76. Periodic Risk Reassessment

Even without a major change, high-risk AI systems may require periodic reassessment.

For example:

```text
Initial Assessment
       ↓
Annual Review
       ↓
Risk Reassessment
       ↓
Updated Treatment
```

The review frequency should be appropriate to risk.

---

### 77. Event-Driven Reassessment

Event-driven reassessment occurs when a specific event indicates that the risk profile may have changed.

Examples:

```text
Major Incident
     ↓
Immediate Reassessment
```

or:

```text
New Regulation
     ↓
Compliance Reassessment
```

This complements periodic reviews.

---

### 78. Trigger-Based Governance

A mature governance program defines triggers in advance.

For example:

```text
IF model changes materially
THEN revalidate

IF risk exceeds appetite
THEN escalate

IF intended use changes
THEN reassess

IF critical control fails
THEN investigate

IF monitoring is unavailable
THEN activate compensating controls
```

This makes governance more predictable and consistent.

---

### 79. AI Lifecycle Gates

Governance gates can be established at important lifecycle stages.

Example:

```text
Idea
 ↓
Gate 1 — Use-Case Approval
 ↓
Design
 ↓
Gate 2 — Risk / Architecture Review
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Gate 3 — Testing
 ↓
Validation
 ↓
Gate 4 — Production Approval
 ↓
Operation
```

Gates prevent the system from progressing without required evidence.

---

### 80. Gate Criteria

Each governance gate should have clearly defined criteria.

For example:

**Production Gate**

```text
Risk Assessment Complete
Security Testing Complete
Privacy Review Complete
Validation Complete
Monitoring Enabled
Owner Assigned
Incident Process Defined
Residual Risk Approved
```

The exact criteria should depend on system risk.

---

### 81. Gate Evidence

A governance gate should be supported by evidence.

Evidence may include:

* Assessment reports.
* Test results.
* Validation reports.
* Security findings.
* Privacy assessments.
* Risk acceptance.
* Architecture documentation.
* Monitoring configuration.

Evidence allows governance decisions to be independently understood later.

---

### 82. Gate Failure

If required criteria are not met, the system should not automatically progress.

Possible outcomes include:

* Remediation.
* Conditional approval.
* Restricted deployment.
* Additional testing.
* Risk acceptance.
* Rejection.

This creates a controlled decision process.

---

### 83. Conditional Approval

Conditional approval allows deployment when specific conditions are imposed.

For example:

```text
Approved:
Limited Internal Users

Condition:
Human Review Required

Condition:
Enhanced Monitoring

Condition:
Full Validation Within 30 Days
```

Conditions should have owners and deadlines.

---

### 84. Technical Debt and AI Governance Debt

AI systems can accumulate governance debt when required activities are postponed.

Examples include:

* Missing documentation.
* Outdated risk assessments.
* Unresolved findings.
* Temporary exceptions.
* Incomplete monitoring.
* Unvalidated changes.

Governance debt should be tracked and prioritized.

---

### 85. AI Technical Debt

Technical debt may include:

* Outdated models.
* Legacy dependencies.
* Weak architecture.
* Inadequate logging.
* Manual controls.
* Unsupported components.

Technical debt can eventually become a risk-management problem.

---

### 86. Governance Technical Debt Relationship

```text
Technical Debt
      +
Governance Debt
      ↓
Increasing Operational Risk
      ↓
Higher Remediation Cost
```

# 05.17 — NIST AI RMF and AI Lifecycle Governance

## Part 3 — Roles, Responsibilities, Decision Rights, Documentation, and Assurance Across the AI Lifecycle

### 101. Lifecycle Governance Requires Clear Accountability

AI lifecycle governance depends on clearly defined accountability.

Every significant AI system should have identified individuals or functions responsible for:

* Business outcomes.
* AI system operation.
* Risk management.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Data.
* Compliance.
* Controls.
* Validation.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.

Without clear ownership, risks can remain unmanaged because each function assumes another party is responsible.

---

### 102. Business Owner

The business owner is responsible for the business purpose and expected outcomes of the AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* Defining the business objective.
* Approving intended use.
* Understanding business impact.
* Ensuring appropriate resources.
* Accepting business-related residual risk within authority.
* Participating in lifecycle decisions.

The business owner should understand not only what the AI system does, but also why the organization is using it.

---

### 103. AI System Owner

The AI system owner is responsible for the overall operation and lifecycle management of the AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining the system inventory record.
* Coordinating lifecycle activities.
* Ensuring controls are implemented.
* Maintaining documentation.
* Coordinating monitoring.
* Managing changes.
* Coordinating reassessments.
* Supporting retirement.

The AI system owner acts as an important coordination point across technical and governance functions.

---

### 104. Model Owner

Where the AI system includes a distinct model component, a model owner may be responsible for:

* Model configuration.
* Model performance.
* Model versioning.
* Model documentation.
* Model validation.
* Model monitoring.
* Model changes.

For externally provided foundation models, ownership responsibilities should be clearly allocated even when the organization does not control the underlying model.

---

### 105. Data Owner

The data owner is responsible for appropriate governance of relevant data.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data classification.
* Data access.
* Data quality.
* Data usage authorization.
* Retention.
* Data-sharing requirements.

AI risk can be significantly affected by the quality and governance of the underlying data.

---

### 106. Security Function

The security function may address:

* Threat modeling.
* Identity and access management.
* Application security.
* Infrastructure security.
* AI-specific attacks.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.
* Security testing.

Security should be integrated throughout the AI lifecycle rather than performed only before deployment.

---

### 107. Privacy Function

Where personal information is processed, privacy responsibilities may include:

* Privacy impact assessment.
* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Data protection requirements.
* Individual rights.
* Retention.
* Cross-border data considerations.

Privacy risk should be considered alongside security and broader AI risk.

---

### 108. Legal and Compliance Functions

Legal and compliance functions may evaluate:

* Applicable laws.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Intellectual property.
* Sector-specific requirements.
* Documentation obligations.
* Reporting requirements.

These requirements should be translated into practical lifecycle controls.

---

### 109. Risk Management Function

The risk function may provide:

* Risk methodology.
* Risk taxonomy.
* Risk assessment challenge.
* Risk appetite guidance.
* Risk reporting.
* Escalation.
* Risk acceptance governance.

Risk management should challenge assumptions without taking over operational ownership.

---

### 110. Internal Audit

Internal audit provides independent assurance.

It may assess:

* Governance effectiveness.
* Risk-management processes.
* Control design.
* Control effectiveness.
* Documentation.
* Compliance.
* Monitoring.

Internal audit should generally remain independent from operating the controls it later evaluates.

---

### 111. AI Governance Committee

An organization may establish an AI governance committee to oversee significant AI use cases.

Potential responsibilities include:

* Reviewing high-risk AI systems.
* Approving significant use cases.
* Reviewing risk assessments.
* Reviewing material incidents.
* Reviewing exceptions.
* Reviewing risk trends.
* Escalating significant issues.

The committee's authority should be formally defined.

---

### 112. Decision Rights

Governance requires more than assigning responsibilities.

It must also define who has the authority to make decisions.

For example:

| Decision                      | Potential Authority             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Approve low-risk use case     | Business owner                  |
| Approve high-risk use case    | AI governance committee         |
| Accept material residual risk | Authorized executive            |
| Approve production deployment | Designated authority            |
| Suspend AI system             | Defined incident authority      |
| Approve major model change    | Governance / business authority |
| Retire critical AI system     | Business / executive authority  |

Actual decision rights should be defined by the organization's governance structure.

---

### 113. Accountability Versus Responsibility

Responsibility refers to performing an activity.

Accountability refers to being answerable for the outcome.

For example:

```text id="2lqv4f"
Security Team
→ Performs Security Testing

AI System Owner
→ Accountable for Ensuring Testing Occurs
```

Both should be clearly documented.

---

### 114. RACI for AI Lifecycle Governance

A RACI model can clarify roles.

```text
R = Responsible
A = Accountable
C = Consulted
I = Informed
```

For example:

| Activity            | Business Owner | AI Owner | Security | Risk | Privacy |
| ------------------- | -------------- | -------- | -------- | ---- | ------- |
| Use-Case Approval   | A              | R        | C        | C    | C       |
| Risk Assessment     | C              | R        | C        | A    | C       |
| Security Testing    | I              | C        | A/R      | I    | I       |
| Privacy Assessment  | I              | C        | C        | C    | A/R     |
| Production Approval | A              | R        | C        | C    | C       |
| Monitoring          | A              | R        | C        | C    | C       |

The exact RACI should reflect the organization's structure.

### 115. Lifecycle Governance Forums

Governance decisions may occur through different forums.

Examples include:

* AI governance committee.
* Enterprise risk committee.
* Security architecture review board.
* Privacy review board.
* Change advisory board.
* Executive risk committee.

The organization should avoid unnecessary duplication while ensuring material risks receive appropriate review.

### 116. Escalation Paths

Escalation paths should be established before incidents occur.

Example:

```text id="f85s1z"

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

```

The escalation level should depend on severity and impact.

### 117. Risk Escalation Criteria

Escalation may be triggered by:

* Risk appetite breach.
* Critical security issue.
* Significant privacy event.
* Material AI incident.
* Repeated control failures.
* Significant regulatory exposure.
* High-impact model failure.
* Unauthorized autonomous action.

Criteria should be measurable wherever possible.

---

### 118. Human Oversight

Human oversight should be proportionate to the potential impact of the AI system.

Higher-impact AI may require:

* Human review.
* Human approval.
* Human override.
* Manual escalation.
* Independent decision-making.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely nominal.

---

### 119. Meaningful Human Oversight

A human reviewer should have:

* Sufficient information.
* Appropriate expertise.
* Adequate time.
* Authority to intervene.
* Ability to reject AI recommendations.

A human who can only approve an AI decision without meaningful opportunity to challenge it may not provide effective oversight.

---

### 120. Human Override

High-impact AI systems may require a mechanism allowing authorized personnel to override AI outputs or actions.

An override process should define:

* Who may override.
* When override is permitted.
* How it is performed.
* How it is recorded.
* How repeated overrides are analyzed.

Frequent overrides may indicate that the AI system requires reassessment.

---

### 121. Human-in-the-Loop

Human-in-the-loop means that a human participates directly in the decision or action process.

Example:

```text id="x9z5x8"
AI Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Approve / Reject / Modify
      ↓
Final Action
```

This may be appropriate for higher-impact decisions.

---

### 122. Human-on-the-Loop

Human-on-the-loop involves ongoing human supervision while the AI system operates with greater autonomy.

Example:

```text id="x0n0zi"
AI Operates
      ↓
Continuous Monitoring
      ↓
Human Intervention When Required
```

This model may be appropriate where autonomous operation is acceptable but intervention capability remains necessary.

---

### 123. Human-in-Command

Human-in-command means that humans retain ultimate authority over the AI system's operation and consequences.

This can be especially relevant to systems with significant autonomy or potential impact.

The level of human authority should correspond to the risk of the AI use case.

---

### 124. Governance of Autonomous AI

Autonomous or agentic AI may require additional governance because it can:

* Execute actions.
* Call tools.
* Access systems.
* Make decisions.
* Chain actions.
* Operate with limited direct intervention.

Governance should therefore address both model outputs and actions performed on behalf of the organization.

---

### 125. Agent Permission Governance

An AI agent's permissions should follow least privilege.

For example:

```text id="t8e9cj"

 ↓
Read Customer Record
 ↓
Generate Recommendation
```

may present lower risk than:

```text id="z2e7r8"
Agent
 ↓
Read Customer Record
 ↓
Modify Customer Record
 ↓
Approve Transaction
 ↓
Execute Payment
```

The level of authority should be justified by the approved use case.

---

### 126. Tool Access Governance

Each tool available to an AI agent should be governed.

Examples include:

* Database access.
* Email.
* File systems.
* APIs.
* Financial systems.
* Cloud infrastructure.
* Customer systems.

Tool access should be:

* Authorized.
* Limited.
* Logged.
* Monitored.
* Periodically reviewed.

---

### 127. Separation Between AI Recommendation and Action

Where appropriate, organizations can separate:

```text id="8z7vdc"
AI Recommendation
        ↓
Human / Control Validation
        ↓
Authorized Action
```

This can reduce the risk that an incorrect AI output directly produces an irreversible action.

---

### 128. Irreversible Actions

Greater governance controls may be appropriate where AI can perform irreversible or difficult-to-reverse actions.

Examples include:

* Financial transfers.
* Deleting records.
* Changing production infrastructure.
* Terminating services.
* Sending legally significant communications.

Such actions may require explicit authorization.

---

### 129. Reversibility as a Risk Factor

A useful governance consideration is:

> How easily can an AI-generated action be reversed?

```text id="6c7h1g"
Highly Reversible
→ Lower Control Requirement

Difficult to Reverse
→ Stronger Control Requirement

Irreversible
→ Highest Governance Attention
```

Reversibility can therefore influence risk treatment.

2. Are appropriately designed.

A control is design-effective when it is capable of addressing the intended risk.

* Ineffective.

Deficiencies should be assessed according to their potential impact.

* No monitoring for critical autonomous actions.
* No incident response capability.

Such weaknesses may require immediate escalation.

### 200. Final Integrated AI Lifecycle Governance Model

The complete model can be summarized as:

```text
                     GOVERN
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
     Policy        Roles          Risk Appetite
        │              │              │
        └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                       ↓
                      MAP
                       ↓
             Context + Risk + Impact
                       ↓
                    MEASURE
                       ↓
          Testing + Metrics + Evidence
                       ↓
                    MANAGE
                       ↓
       Treatment + Approval + Escalation
                       ↓
                  DEPLOY / OPERATE
                       ↓
                    MONITOR
                       ↓
             Change / Incident / Trigger
                       ↓
                  REASSESS
                       ↓
                 REVALIDATE
                       ↓
             CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT
                       ↓
                    RETIRE
                       ↓
                 LESSONS LEARNED
                       ↓
                    GOVERN
```

The central lesson of **05.17** is that NIST AI RMF should not be implemented as a checklist that is completed once before an AI system goes into production.

Instead, **GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE should form a continuous lifecycle governance mechanism**.

A mature organization establishes governance before an AI system exists, identifies its context and risks, measures those risks using evidence, manages them through appropriate controls and decisions, continuously monitors the operating environment, reassesses material changes, validates important decisions, maintains audit evidence, and eventually retires the system in a controlled manner.

The ultimate objective is not simply to demonstrate that an AI system was **approved**. It is to demonstrate that the organization can continuously answer:

> **What is this AI system doing, what could go wrong, how do we know, who is accountable, what are we doing about it, and does it remain within the organization's approved risk boundaries?**
