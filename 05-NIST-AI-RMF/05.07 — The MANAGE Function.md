# 05.07 — The MANAGE Function

## Part 1 — Introduction to the MANAGE Function

## 1. Introduction to MANAGE

The **MANAGE function** is one of the four core functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The four functions are:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
MANAGE
```

The MANAGE function focuses on **prioritizing, responding to, and managing identified and measured AI risks**.

While:

* **GOVERN** establishes organizational direction, accountability, and policies.
* **MAP** establishes context and identifies AI risks.
* **MEASURE** analyzes, evaluates, and monitors AI risks.

**MANAGE focuses on what the organization does about those risks.**

A simplified representation is:

```text
GOVERN
Establish direction
       ↓
MAP
Understand context and identify risks
       ↓
MEASURE
Evaluate and monitor risks
       ↓
MANAGE
Prioritize and respond to risks
```

MANAGE therefore represents the transition from **risk understanding to risk action**.

---

## 2. Purpose of the MANAGE Function

The fundamental purpose of MANAGE is to ensure that identified AI risks are addressed according to their:

* Likelihood.
* Potential impact.
* Severity.
* Urgency.
* Business significance.
* Regulatory significance.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Available controls.
* Available resources.

The organization should determine whether a risk should be:

* Avoided.
* Reduced.
* Controlled.
* Transferred or shared.
* Accepted.
* Restricted.
* Suspended.
* Terminated.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every AI risk.

The objective is to ensure that **AI risks are intentionally managed to an acceptable level**.

---

## 3. MANAGE as an Action-Oriented Function

The previous AI RMF functions generate information that enables management decisions.

For example:

```text
MAP
AI system creates potential privacy risk
       ↓
MEASURE
Risk evaluated and evidence collected
       ↓
MANAGE
Determine appropriate treatment
```

Suppose an AI system is found to have a significant risk of exposing confidential information.

The organization now needs to determine what to do.

Possible actions include:

* Restricting access.
* Implementing data-loss prevention.
* Limiting the information available to the model.
* Improving authentication.
* Adding monitoring.
* Restricting certain use cases.
* Requiring human approval.
* Replacing the technology.
* Suspending the system.

This demonstrates the central purpose of MANAGE:

> **Convert AI risk information into deliberate management action.**

---

## 4. MANAGE Is Not the Same as Risk Elimination

AI risk cannot always be completely eliminated.

For example, an AI system may occasionally generate an incorrect answer despite extensive testing and controls.

The organization may determine that:

* The remaining risk is understood.
* Appropriate controls are implemented.
* The residual risk is within tolerance.
* The business benefit justifies continued use.

In that situation, the organization may continue operating the AI system while monitoring the residual risk.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Exists
      ↓
Risk Assessed
      ↓
Risk Treated
      ↓
Residual Risk Remains
      ↓
Risk Compared Against Tolerance
      ↓
Management Decision
```

Effective AI risk management is therefore about **informed risk decisions**, not the unrealistic elimination of all uncertainty.

---

## 5. Inherent AI Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effect of controls or other risk treatments.

For example:

> An AI system automatically recommends whether a customer should receive a particular service.

Before controls are considered, the system may present significant risks because an incorrect recommendation could affect customers, create financial consequences, or produce regulatory concerns.

The organization should understand this underlying exposure before evaluating the effectiveness of controls.

A simplified model is:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Inherent Risk
   ↓
Controls
   ↓
Residual Risk
```

Understanding inherent risk helps management determine how much risk reduction may be required.

---

## 6. Residual AI Risk

**Residual risk** is the risk that remains after controls and other treatments have been implemented.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk:
High

Controls:
Human review
Access controls
Monitoring
Model validation

Residual Risk:
Moderate
```

The organization should then determine whether the residual risk falls within its approved risk tolerance.

If it does not, additional treatment may be required.

---

## 7. Risk Treatment

Risk treatment refers to actions taken to modify AI risk.

A practical enterprise risk-treatment model includes:

1. **Avoid the risk**
2. **Reduce or mitigate the risk**
3. **Transfer or share the risk**
4. **Accept the residual risk**

Additional operational decisions may include:

* Restricting the system.
* Suspending the system.
* Replacing the system.
* Decommissioning the system.

The selected treatment should be appropriate to the nature and significance of the AI risk.

---

## 8. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means changing or stopping an activity so that the organization no longer incurs the particular risk.

For an AI system, avoidance could mean:

> The organization decides not to use AI for a particular high-impact decision.

Other examples include:

* Not deploying the AI system.
* Removing a high-risk feature.
* Discontinuing an AI use case.
* Choosing a non-AI alternative.
* Restricting the system from a particular business process.

Risk avoidance may be appropriate when the risk cannot reasonably be reduced to an acceptable level.

---

## 9. Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation means reducing the likelihood or impact of a risk.

AI risk mitigation may include:

* Improving training data.
* Increasing testing.
* Adding human oversight.
* Restricting access.
* Implementing security controls.
* Adding monitoring.
* Improving model design.
* Restricting system functionality.
* Adding additional validation.

For example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
High Risk of Incorrect Decisions
   ↓
Human Review
+
Model Validation
+
Monitoring
   ↓
Reduced Risk
```

Mitigation is often an important component of AI risk management because many AI risks cannot be completely eliminated.

---

## 10. Risk Transfer or Sharing

Some risks may be transferred or shared with another organization or party.

Examples include:

* Contractual obligations.
* Vendor responsibilities.
* Third-party service arrangements.
* Insurance.
* Shared operational responsibilities.

However, contractual transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's overall accountability.

For example, an enterprise may use a third-party AI provider, but it may still remain responsible for determining whether the provider is appropriate for the organization's intended use.

Therefore:

> **Transferring operational responsibility does not automatically eliminate organizational risk.**

---

## 11. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance occurs when an authorized person or body deliberately decides that the remaining risk is acceptable.

A proper risk acceptance decision should be:

* Explicit.
* Informed.
* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Consistent with organizational risk appetite.
* Consistent with applicable risk tolerance.

Risk acceptance should not mean:

> "The organization has not yet addressed the risk."

Instead, it should mean:

> "The organization understands the residual risk and an authorized decision-maker has formally agreed to accept it."

---

## 12. Risk Acceptance Example

Consider an internal AI productivity assistant.

### Risk

The AI may occasionally generate inaccurate information.

### Controls

* Approved use cases.
* User verification.
* Restricted access.
* Approved knowledge sources.
* User training.

### Residual Risk

Low.

### Decision

The authorized risk owner accepts the residual risk because it is within the organization's approved tolerance.

The decision should be documented.

---

## 13. Risk Avoidance Versus Risk Acceptance

Risk avoidance and risk acceptance represent fundamentally different decisions.

### Risk Avoidance

> The organization will not perform the activity because the risk is unacceptable.

### Risk Acceptance

> The organization will continue the activity because the residual risk is within approved tolerance.

The distinction can be represented as:

```text
Risk Too High
     ↓
Avoid / Stop / Restrict

Risk Reduced to Acceptable Level
     ↓
Continue
     ↓
Accept Residual Risk
```

This distinction is important for AI governance because not every risky AI use case should automatically be rejected.

---

## 14. Risk Prioritization

Organizations usually have limited resources.

An enterprise may have:

* Hundreds of AI systems.
* Thousands of AI-related controls.
* Multiple risk findings.
* Numerous treatment actions.

It is therefore necessary to determine which risks require the greatest attention.

Risk prioritization may consider:

* Potential impact.
* Likelihood.
* Severity.
* Urgency.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory implications.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Control effectiveness.
* Risk concentration.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.

---

## 15. AI Risk Severity

Risk severity represents the significance of the consequences associated with a risk.

Potential AI consequences may include:

### Financial

* Financial losses.
* Fraud.
* Compensation.
* Increased operating costs.

### Operational

* Business disruption.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Service failures.

### Security

* Unauthorized access.
* Data disclosure.
* System compromise.

### Privacy

* Exposure of personal information.
* Inappropriate processing.

### Legal and Regulatory

### Human

* Discrimination.

* Other adverse effects.

---

## 16. AI Risk Is Multi-Dimensional

Traditional information-security risk often focuses heavily on:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.

AI risk may require a broader perspective.

Relevant dimensions can include:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Safety.
* Fairness.
* Reliability.
* Robustness.
* Transparency.
* Explainability.
* Human oversight.
* Accountability.

Therefore, an AI risk assessment should consider the characteristics that are relevant to the system's intended purpose and context.

---

## 17. Risk Interdependencies

AI risks may interact with one another.

For example:

```text
Poor Data Quality
       ↓
Model Error
       ↓
Incorrect Decision
       ↓
Customer Harm
       ↓
Regulatory Exposure
       ↓
Reputational Damage
```

A single underlying problem can therefore produce multiple risk consequences.

MANAGE should consider these relationships when determining treatment priorities.

---

## 18. Root-Cause-Based Risk Treatment

Risk treatment should address the underlying cause whenever possible.

For example:

> The AI system produces incorrect outputs.

A superficial response might be:

> Add more human review.

But further investigation may reveal:

> The training data is incomplete and poorly representative.

A stronger treatment may therefore include:

* Improving the dataset.
* Validating the data.
* Retraining the model.
* Testing the model again.
* Adding appropriate human oversight.

The objective is to address the **source of the risk**, not merely its visible symptoms.

---

## 19. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

A practical treatment hierarchy can be represented as:

```text
Eliminate / Avoid
       ↓
Reduce Likelihood
       ↓
Reduce Impact
       ↓
Add Compensating Controls
       ↓
Transfer / Share
       ↓
Accept Residual Risk
```

The organization should determine which treatment provides an appropriate balance between:

* Risk reduction.
* Business requirements.
* Cost.
* Complexity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Operational feasibility.

---

## 20. AI Risk Treatment Plan

A significant AI risk should have a documented treatment plan.

The plan may contain:

1. Risk description.
2. Risk owner.
3. Risk rating.
4. Treatment decision.
5. Treatment actions.
6. Control owner.
7. Target completion date.
8. Required resources.
9. Expected residual risk.
10. Success criteria.
11. Approval.
12. Current status.

A treatment plan converts a risk assessment into an actionable management process.

---


This provides a clear connection between:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

```

---

## 22. Risk Treatment Ownership

Every significant AI risk should have an accountable owner.

A practical structure is:

```text
Risk Owner
    ↓
Treatment Decision
    ↓
Control Owner
    ↓
Implementation
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Review
```

The **risk owner** is accountable for the risk decision.

The **control owner** may be responsible for implementing and operating a specific control.

These responsibilities should not automatically be assumed to be the same person.

---

## 23. Control Selection

Controls should be selected based on the specific risk.

For example:

### Risk

Unauthorized users access an AI system containing sensitive information.

### Potential Controls

* Identity and access management.
* Role-based access control.
* Multi-factor authentication.
* Privileged access management.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The organization should not implement controls simply because they are available.

The control should provide a reasonable response to the identified risk.

---

## 24. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to prevent an undesirable event before it occurs.

Examples include:

* Access restrictions.
* Input validation.
* Data classification.
* Approved-use policies.
* Human approval requirements.
* Secure configuration.
* Network restrictions.

For AI systems, preventive controls can reduce opportunities for misuse or harmful outcomes.

---

## 25. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify undesirable events after or while they occur.

Examples include:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security alerts.
* Data leakage detection.
* Output monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* Incident detection.
* Model behavior monitoring.

Detection is particularly important where prevention cannot completely eliminate the risk.

---

## 26. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls respond to identified problems.

Examples include:

* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data correction.
* Model retraining.
* Configuration changes.
* Incident response.
* Additional human review.
* Service restriction.

Corrective controls help reduce the duration and impact of AI-related incidents.

---

## 27. Compensating Controls

A compensating control provides an alternative risk-reduction mechanism when the preferred control cannot be fully implemented.

For example:

```text
Automated AI Decision
       ↓
Risk Limitation Identified
       ↓
Mandatory Human Approval
       ↓
Compensating Control
```

Compensating controls should themselves be assessed to determine whether they adequately address the original risk.

---

## 28. Human Oversight as a Risk Treatment

Human oversight can be an important AI risk treatment.

However, simply stating:

> "There is a human in the loop"

does not automatically demonstrate effective risk management.

The organization should determine:

* Who performs the review?
* What information is provided?
* What authority does the reviewer have?
* Can the reviewer override the AI?
* When must intervention occur?
* Is the review documented?
* Are reviewers appropriately trained?
* Is the review actually being performed?

Human oversight should therefore be treated as a control that requires governance and effectiveness evaluation.

---

## 29. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop arrangement generally requires meaningful human intervention before a consequential action.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject / Modify
       ↓
Final Decision
```

This can reduce certain risks where human judgment is necessary.

---

## 30. Human-on-the-Loop

A human-on-the-loop arrangement involves monitoring an automated process and intervening when predefined conditions occur.

Example:

```text
AI Automated Process
       ↓
Continuous Monitoring
       ↓
Threshold Exceeded
       ↓
Human Intervention
```

This may be appropriate where continuous human involvement in every individual transaction is impractical but meaningful intervention remains necessary.

---

## 31. Human Oversight Limitations

Human oversight can itself fail.

Potential problems include:

* Reviewers lack sufficient expertise.
* Reviewers are overloaded.
* AI outputs appear overly authoritative.
* Review becomes routine rubber-stamping.
* Reviewers cannot override the system.
* Important context is unavailable.
* Review requirements are unclear.

Therefore:

> **Human oversight must be designed, implemented, monitored, and evaluated like any other important control.**

---

## 32. MANAGE and Business Decision-Making

AI risk management should support informed business decisions.

The organization should balance:

```text
Business Benefit
       +
AI Risk
       +
Treatment Cost
       +
Regulatory Requirements
       +
Stakeholder Impact
       ↓
Management Decision
```

The objective is not to prevent the organization from using AI.

The objective is to ensure that AI is used in a manner consistent with the organization's:

* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Objectives.
* Obligations.
* Governance requirements.

---

## 33. Core MANAGE Principle

The central principle of the MANAGE function can be summarized as:

> **AI risks should not merely be identified and documented; they should be prioritized, assigned, treated, monitored, and subject to appropriate management decisions.**

A mature MANAGE capability connects:

```text
Risk Identification
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Prioritization
       ↓
Treatment Decision
       ↓
Control Implementation
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Management Decision
```

This establishes MANAGE as the action-oriented component of the NIST AI RMF.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Treatment and Response

## 34. Risk Treatment Strategy

AI risk treatment is the process of determining and implementing actions that modify identified risks.

A treatment strategy should consider:

* The nature of the AI system.
* The affected stakeholders.
* The potential harm.
* Likelihood and impact.
* Existing controls.
* Applicable requirements.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Available resources.
* Business objectives.

The treatment decision should be proportionate to the significance of the risk.

---

## 35. Risk Treatment Decision

A management decision can generally fall into several categories:

```text id="l4kwq5"
Avoid
  ↓
Mitigate
  ↓
Transfer / Share
  ↓
Accept
```

Additional operational decisions may include:

* Restrict.
* Suspend.
* Replace.
* Decommission.

The organization should document the rationale for significant treatment decisions.

---

## 36. Risk Avoidance Through AI Use-Case Removal

Risk avoidance can involve removing the AI use case entirely.

For example:

> An organization evaluates an AI system intended to automatically make a high-impact decision and determines that the potential harm cannot be reduced sufficiently through available controls.

The organization may decide:

```text id="xg6xj6"
AI Use Case
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Unacceptable Residual Risk
     ↓
Do Not Deploy
```

This is a legitimate risk-management decision.

---

## 37. Risk Avoidance Through Scope Reduction

Avoidance does not always require eliminating the entire AI system.

The organization may instead remove the highest-risk functionality.

For example:

```text id="wqf83h"
AI Platform
   ├── Low-risk recommendation → Continue
   ├── Medium-risk automation → Restrict
   └── High-risk autonomous action → Remove
```

This allows the organization to retain useful AI capabilities while eliminating specific unacceptable exposures.

---

## 38. Risk Mitigation Through Design

Risk mitigation should ideally begin during system design.

A secure and responsible AI design process may include:

```text id="m8t0d3"
Business Requirement
       ↓
AI Use Case
       ↓
Risk Identification
       ↓
Control Requirements
       ↓
System Design
       ↓
Testing
       ↓
Deployment
```

Designing controls early can be more effective than attempting to add them after the AI system is already operational.

* Monitoring.
* Rate limiting.
* Sandboxing.
* Secrets management.

The control selection should be based on the identified risk rather than on generic security practices alone.

Data is a major source of AI risk.

* Data classification.

```text id="v8gj1a"
      ↓

      ↓
Access Restriction
      ↓
```

## 41. Risk Mitigation Through Model Controls

Model-related risks may be treated through:

* Model monitoring.
* Model version control.
* Model rollback.
* Retraining.

The objective is to ensure that the model behaves within defined expectations.

## 42. Risk Mitigation Through Process Controls

Not all AI risks can be solved technically.

Process controls may include:

* Approval workflows.
* Segregation of duties.
* Human review.
* Change management.
* Incident management.
* Exception management.
* Periodic reassessment.
* AI system inventory requirements.

For GRC purposes, process controls are particularly important because they establish repeatable organizational behavior.

---

## 43. Risk Mitigation Through Policies

Policies establish organizational expectations and boundaries.

Examples include:

* AI governance policy.
* Generative AI acceptable-use policy.
* AI security policy.
* AI data-handling policy.
* AI development standard.
* AI third-party risk policy.
* AI incident-management policy.

A policy should not be considered sufficient by itself.

It should be supported by:

```text id="6a6x9d"
Policy
  ↓
Standard
  ↓
Procedure
  ↓
Control
  ↓
Evidence
```

---

## 44. Risk Mitigation Through Standards

Standards translate policy requirements into more specific control expectations.

For example:

### Policy

> Sensitive information must be protected when using AI.

### Standard

> Sensitive information must not be submitted to unapproved external AI services.

### Procedure

> Employees must verify the AI service against the approved AI service inventory before processing organizational information.

### Control

> Technical restrictions prevent sensitive data from being submitted to unauthorized AI platforms.

This creates a governance-to-control chain.

---

## 45. Risk Mitigation Through Human Oversight

Human oversight can be used when automated AI decisions may create significant consequences.

A practical model is:

```text id="g4wz7r"
AI Output
    ↓
Risk / Confidence Assessment
    ↓
Human Review
    ↓
Approve
Reject
Modify
Escalate
```

The degree of human oversight should be proportional to the potential consequences.

---

## 46. Meaningful Human Oversight

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than symbolic.

An effective reviewer should generally have:

* Appropriate knowledge.
* Adequate time.
* Sufficient context.
* Defined responsibilities.
* Authority to intervene.
* Ability to override or escalate where appropriate.

A process where a human merely clicks "approve" without understanding the AI output provides weak risk mitigation.

---

## 47. Risk Mitigation Through Restricted Access

Access restrictions can reduce AI risk by limiting who can:

* Use the AI system.
* Modify the model.
* Change configuration.
* Access training data.
* Access prompts.
* Access system logs.
* Execute administrative actions.

A role-based model may be:

```text id="h6ey1h"
User
  ↓
Role
  ↓
Authorized AI Function
  ↓
Approved Data
```

This follows the principle of limiting access to what is required for the intended purpose.

---

## 48. Risk Mitigation Through Data Minimization

Data minimization reduces exposure by limiting the amount of information processed.

For example, if an AI system only needs:

* Customer age range.
* Product category.
* Transaction value.

it may not need:

* Full name.
* Personal address.
* Identification number.

Reducing unnecessary data can reduce privacy and security exposure.

## 51. Risk Mitigation Through Monitoring

Monitoring helps identify changes that may require additional treatment.

## 52. Monitoring Must Have Defined Responses

* Default-deny behavior.
* Transaction limits.

The appropriate mechanism depends on the system's risk and operating context.

* What the provider controls.

## 60. Risk Treatment Through Insurance

Insurance may provide financial protection against certain consequences of AI-related incidents.

> Insurance does not eliminate the underlying AI risk.

It should therefore be considered a potential risk-transfer mechanism rather than a replacement for:

* Security controls.
* Privacy controls.
* AI governance.
* Risk monitoring.
* Incident response.

## Part 3 — Residual Risk, Risk Acceptance, Monitoring, and Escalation

## 80. Residual Risk Management

After risk treatment has been implemented, the organization must determine what risk remains.

This remaining exposure is referred to as **residual risk**.

A simplified model is:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Management Decision
```

Residual risk is important because controls rarely eliminate every possible source of AI risk.

The objective is to determine whether the remaining risk is:

* Acceptable.
* Within tolerance.
* Requires additional treatment.
* Requires escalation.
* Requires restriction or discontinuation of the AI system.

---

## 81. Residual Risk Assessment

Residual risk should be reassessed after significant controls or treatments have been implemented.

For example:

```text
Risk:
Confidential information disclosure

Inherent Risk:
High

Treatment:
DLP + Access Control + Monitoring

Residual Risk:
Moderate

Risk Tolerance:
Moderate

Decision:
Accept
```

The organization should retain evidence supporting the residual-risk assessment.

---

## 82. Residual Risk Is Not Automatically Acceptable

Implementing controls does not automatically mean that the remaining risk is acceptable.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk
High
   ↓
Controls Implemented
   ↓
Residual Risk
High
   ↓
Tolerance
Moderate
   ↓
Additional Treatment Required
```

The residual risk must be compared against an established risk appetite or tolerance.

---

## 83. Residual Risk and Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the level of variation or exposure the organization is prepared to accept for a particular risk.

For example:

```text
AI Error Rate

Target:
≤ 3%

Tolerance:
≤ 5%

Above 5%:
Management Action Required
```

The actual thresholds should be established by the organization.

The important principle is that residual risk should be evaluated against **defined organizational criteria**, rather than personal judgment alone.

---

## 84. Residual Risk and Risk Appetite

Risk appetite expresses the organization's broader willingness to accept risk.

For example:

> The organization has a very low appetite for unauthorized disclosure of customer information.

If an AI system creates a significant possibility of such disclosure, management may require stronger controls before allowing deployment.

Risk appetite therefore provides strategic direction for risk treatment.

---

## 85. Risk Appetite Versus Risk Acceptance

These concepts should not be confused.

### Risk Appetite

The organization's general willingness to accept a category of risk.

### Risk Acceptance

A specific decision to accept the remaining risk associated with a particular AI system or activity.

Example:

```text
Enterprise Risk Appetite
Low privacy-risk appetite
        ↓
Specific AI System
Moderate residual privacy risk
        ↓
Management Decision
Additional treatment required
```

Risk acceptance should therefore be consistent with the organization's broader risk appetite.

---

## 86. Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance should be performed by an appropriately authorized individual or governance body.

For example:

| Risk Level | Possible Approval Authority           |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Low        | System Owner                          |
| Moderate   | Business / Risk Owner                 |
| High       | Senior Management                     |
| Critical   | Executive Management / Risk Committee |

This is only an illustrative governance structure.

Each organization should define its own authority model.

---

## 87. Informed Risk Acceptance

A valid acceptance decision requires sufficient information.

The decision-maker should understand:

* The AI system.
* The identified risk.
* The potential consequences.
* Existing controls.
* Control effectiveness.
* Residual risk.
* Treatment alternatives.
* Business justification.
* Applicable requirements.

Risk acceptance without adequate information is weak governance.

---

---

## 89. Time-Bound Risk Acceptance

Some risk acceptances should have an expiration or review date.

For example:

> Residual risk is accepted for six months while a permanent model improvement is being developed.

This prevents temporary acceptance from becoming indefinite acceptance.

A practical model is:

```text
Risk Acceptance
      ↓

      ↓
Reassessment
      ↓
Continue / Modify / Withdraw
```

For example:

```text
     +

```

Conditional acceptance can provide additional assurance where risk remains within tolerance but requires closer oversight.

## 91. Risk Exceptions

An **exception** occurs when an AI system or process does not fully comply with an established requirement but management permits the deviation under controlled conditions.

An exception should normally document:

* Requirement not met.
* Reason for deviation.
* Risk created.
* Compensating controls.
* Residual risk.
* Owner.
* Approver.
* Expiration date.
* Review date.

Exceptions should be actively managed.

---

## 92. Risk Acceptance Versus Exception

These concepts are related but different.

### Risk Acceptance

Management accepts a particular residual risk.

### Exception

Management permits deviation from a defined requirement or control.

For example:

```text
Policy Requirement:
AI systems must undergo annual validation.

Exception:
System cannot complete validation by the required date.

Risk Acceptance:
Management accepts the temporary residual risk.

Compensating Control:
Additional monitoring implemented.
```

An exception may therefore require an associated risk decision.

---

## 93. Risk Escalation

Risks should be escalated when they exceed defined thresholds.

Triggers may include:

* Risk exceeds tolerance.
* Controls fail.
* Risk severity increases.
* Significant incident occurs.
* Regulatory exposure emerges.
* Treatment is overdue.
* Residual risk becomes unacceptable.
* Management authority is exceeded.

Escalation allows appropriate decision-makers to become involved.

## 103. Treatment Completion Rate

A simple metric is:

```text
Treatment Completion Rate =
Completed Treatment Actions
÷
Total Treatment Actions Due
× 100
```

For example:

```text
80 completed
100 due

Completion Rate = 80%
```

The metric should be interpreted alongside risk severity.

An 80% completion rate may look positive, but if the remaining 20% contains critical risks, significant exposure may remain.

## 104. Risk Aging

Risk aging measures how long a risk remains unresolved.

Example:

| Risk     |     Age | Status      |
| -------- | ------: | ----------- |

| High     | 60 days | At Risk     |
| Critical | 90 days | Overdue     |

Aging helps management identify risks that are remaining unresolved for excessive periods.

## 105. Risk Treatment Effectiveness

Treatment effectiveness should consider whether the treatment actually reduced the risk.

For example:

```text
Before Treatment
High Risk
     ↓
Treatment
     ↓
After Treatment
Moderate Risk
```

However, management should not rely only on the existence of the treatment.

Evidence should demonstrate that the control operates effectively.

## 106. Control Existence Versus Control Effectiveness

A control may exist but still be ineffective.

Example:

> Policy requires human review of high-risk AI decisions.

However:

* Reviewers do not consistently perform the review.
* Reviewers lack sufficient information.
* Reviewers cannot override AI decisions.

The control exists on paper but may not provide effective risk reduction.

This distinction is critical for GRC.

## 107. Control Testing

Control testing may evaluate:

### Design Effectiveness

Does the control appropriately address the risk?

### Operating Effectiveness

Does the control actually operate as intended?

For example:

```text
Risk:
Unauthorized AI access

Control:
Role-Based Access Control

Design Test:
Does RBAC prevent unauthorized access?

Operating Test:
Is RBAC correctly configured and consistently enforced?
```

Both perspectives are important.

* Access records.
* Validation results.
* Incident records.

* Audit results.

* Change the process.
* Accept the remaining risk.

## 111. Unintended Consequences

Risk treatment can sometimes create new risks.

For example:

* User frustration.
* Process bottlenecks.
* Reduced availability.
* New operational risks.

Therefore, treatments should be evaluated for both intended and unintended consequences.

## 132. Core Principles of Residual Risk Management

The key principles are:

1. Residual risk should be explicitly assessed.
2. Residual risk should be compared against risk tolerance.
3. Risk acceptance should be informed and authorized.
4. Significant acceptance decisions should be documented.
5. Exceptions should be controlled and reviewed.
6. Risks exceeding tolerance should be escalated.
7. Controls should be tested for effectiveness.
8. Monitoring should continue after deployment.
9. Risk indicators should trigger defined responses.
10. Significant changes should trigger reassessment.
11. Risk treatment should consider unintended consequences.
12. Risk decisions should remain aligned with business objectives and applicable requirements.

## 214. Common MANAGE Weaknesses

Organizations may struggle with:

### Weak Ownership

Nobody clearly owns the AI risk.

### Risk Register Without Action

Risks are documented but not treated.

### Paper Controls

Controls exist in policy but are not operating effectively.

### Permanent Exceptions

Temporary exceptions become indefinite.

### Uncontrolled Acceptance

Risks are accepted without appropriate authority.

### Poor Monitoring

Organizations deploy AI but do not monitor risk afterward.

### Vendor Blindness

Third-party AI risk is treated as someone else's responsibility.

## 215. Common MANAGE Anti-Patterns

### "We Have a Policy"

A policy alone does not demonstrate effective risk treatment.

### "The Vendor Handles Security"

Vendor responsibility does not automatically eliminate enterprise accountability.

### "A Human Reviews It"

Human review is only meaningful when the reviewer has sufficient authority, knowledge, and information.

### "The Risk Is Documented"

Documentation without treatment is not risk management.

### "The Control Exists"

Control existence does not prove control effectiveness.

### "The Risk Is Accepted"

Acceptance must be informed, authorized, and appropriate to the organization's risk criteria.

Implement appropriate treatment and controls.

Assign clear ownership and decision authority.

## 226. Final MANAGE Principle

The fundamental principle of MANAGE is:

> **Identifying an AI risk is not the end of risk management. The organization must decide what to do about the risk, implement appropriate treatment, evaluate the resulting residual risk, obtain appropriate acceptance or escalation, and continuously monitor whether the decision remains valid.**

MANAGE therefore provides the practical connection between:

**AI risk identification → risk treatment → control implementation → residual risk → management decision → continuous monitoring.**
