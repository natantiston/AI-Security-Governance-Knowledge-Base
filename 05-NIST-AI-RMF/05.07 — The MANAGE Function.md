# 05.07 — The MANAGE Function

## Part 1 — Introduction to the MANAGE Function

## 295. Introduction to MANAGE

The **MANAGE function** is the fourth and final core function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The four core functions are:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
MANAGE
```

The MANAGE function focuses on **prioritizing, responding to, and managing identified and measured AI risks**.

While:

* **GOVERN** establishes organizational direction and accountability.
* **MAP** establishes context and identifies AI risks.
* **MEASURE** evaluates and monitors those risks.

**MANAGE determines what the organization does about those risks.**

---

# 296. The Purpose of MANAGE

The fundamental purpose of MANAGE is to ensure that identified AI risks are addressed appropriately according to:

* Their likelihood.
* Their potential impact.
* The organization's risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Legal and regulatory requirements.
* Business objectives.
* Available controls.
* Available resources.
* The organization's ability to accept, mitigate, transfer, avoid, or otherwise respond to the risk.

A simplified model is:

```text
AI Risk Identified
       ↓
Risk Measured
       ↓
Risk Prioritized
       ↓
Treatment Decision
       ↓
Risk Response
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Risk Acceptance / Further Treatment
       ↓
Ongoing Monitoring
```

---

# 297. MANAGE Is About Action

One of the most important distinctions in the NIST AI RMF is that risk identification and measurement alone do not reduce risk.

An organization may know that:

> An AI model has a high false-negative rate.

That knowledge is valuable, but it does not itself reduce the risk.

MANAGE asks:

> **What should we do about it?**

Possible responses may include:

* Modify the model.
* Improve the training data.
* Introduce human review.
* Restrict the system's use.
* Add technical controls.
* Increase monitoring.
* Change the business process.
* Replace the AI system.
* Suspend deployment.
* Accept the residual risk.

---

# 298. MANAGE Within the AI RMF

The relationship between the four functions can be represented as:

```text
GOVERN
Establish organizational direction
        ↓
MAP
Understand context and identify risks
        ↓
MEASURE
Analyze and monitor risks
        ↓
MANAGE
Prioritize and respond to risks
        ↓
Feedback
        ↓
GOVERN / MAP / MEASURE
```

The process is therefore **iterative rather than linear**.

A management decision may reveal new information that requires the organization to:

* Re-map the AI system.
* Reassess risks.
* Perform additional measurement.
* Change governance requirements.

---

# 299. MANAGE Is Not the Same as Risk Elimination

AI risk cannot always be completely eliminated.

For example, an organization may deploy a useful AI system that has a small residual probability of generating an incorrect recommendation.

The objective may therefore be to:

> Reduce the risk to an acceptable level.

This is different from attempting to eliminate every possible risk.

A practical model is:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Acceptance Decision
```

---

# 300. Inherent AI Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effectiveness of controls or mitigation measures.

For example:

> An AI system makes automated decisions affecting customers.

Before controls are considered, the potential risk may be high because incorrect decisions could materially affect individuals.

The organization should understand this inherent risk before determining the appropriate treatment.

---

# 301. Residual AI Risk

**Residual risk** is the risk remaining after controls and other treatments are implemented.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk:
High

Controls:
Human review
+
Access restrictions
+
Model monitoring
+
Periodic validation

Residual Risk:
Moderate
```

The residual risk should then be compared against the organization's approved risk tolerance.

---

# 302. Risk Treatment

Risk treatment refers to the actions taken to modify risk.

Common approaches include:

1. **Avoid**
2. **Mitigate**
3. **Transfer or share**
4. **Accept**
5. **Terminate or discontinue the activity**

The exact terminology may vary across enterprise risk frameworks.

---

# 303. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means changing the activity so that the particular risk is no longer incurred.

For an AI system, this could mean:

> The organization decides not to use AI for a particular high-impact decision.

For example, if an AI application introduces unacceptable risks that cannot reasonably be controlled, the organization may decide not to deploy it.

---

# 304. Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation reduces the likelihood or impact of a risk.

Examples include:

* Human oversight.
* Additional validation.
* Better training data.
* Access restrictions.
* Output filtering.
* Security controls.
* Additional testing.
* Model improvement.
* Restricted use cases.

Mitigation is often the most practical treatment for AI risks.

---

# 305. Risk Transfer or Sharing

Some risks may be transferred or shared with another party.

Examples may include:

* Contractual requirements.
* Vendor responsibilities.
* Insurance.
* Third-party service arrangements.
* Shared operational responsibilities.

However, transferring responsibility contractually does not necessarily eliminate the organization's accountability for the AI risk.

The organization should understand what risk remains under its control.

---

# 306. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means that an authorized risk owner deliberately decides that the remaining risk is acceptable.

Acceptance should be:

* Explicit.
* Informed.
* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Consistent with risk appetite and tolerance.

Risk acceptance should not simply mean:

> "Nobody has done anything about the risk."

---

# 307. Risk Acceptance Example

Consider:

```text
AI System:
Internal productivity assistant

Risk:
Occasional inaccurate responses

Controls:
User verification
Restricted use
Approved knowledge sources

Residual Risk:
Low

Decision:
Risk accepted by authorized owner
```

The acceptance should be based on evidence rather than assumption.

---

# 308. Risk Avoidance Versus Risk Acceptance

These decisions should not be confused.

### Avoidance

> We will not perform the activity because the risk is unacceptable.

### Acceptance

> We will continue the activity because the remaining risk is within approved tolerance.

For example:

```text
Risk Too High
     ↓
Avoid

Risk Reduced to Tolerance
     ↓
Accept
```

---

# 309. Risk Prioritization

Organizations usually have limited resources.

Therefore, not every AI risk can receive the same level of attention.

MANAGE requires prioritization based on factors such as:

* Potential impact.
* Likelihood.
* Severity.
* Urgency.
* Regulatory implications.
* Affected stakeholders.
* Business criticality.
* Control weaknesses.
* Risk concentration.
* Dependencies.

---

# 310. Risk Prioritization Example

Suppose an organization identifies three AI risks:

| Risk                        | Likelihood | Impact   | Priority  |
| --------------------------- | ---------- | -------- | --------- |
| Minor chatbot error         | High       | Low      | Medium    |
| Confidential data leakage   | Medium     | High     | High      |
| Incorrect critical decision | Low        | Critical | Very High |

The third risk may receive the highest priority even though its likelihood is lower.

This illustrates why organizations should not prioritize risks using probability alone.

---

# 311. Risk Severity

Severity considers the potential consequences of an AI risk.

Possible consequences include:

* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Security compromise.
* Privacy harm.
* Legal exposure.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Reputational damage.
* Customer harm.
* Safety consequences.

Severity should reflect the organization's context.

---

# 312. AI Risk Treatment Must Consider Context

The same technical failure may require different treatment depending on the use case.

For example:

> A 5% error rate in an internal brainstorming assistant may be tolerable.

But:

> A 5% error rate in a high-impact automated decision system may be unacceptable.

Therefore, MANAGE decisions must consider **context and impact**, not merely technical metrics.

---

# 313. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

A practical treatment hierarchy is:

```text
1. Eliminate / Avoid
        ↓
2. Reduce Likelihood
        ↓
3. Reduce Impact
        ↓
4. Add Compensating Controls
        ↓
5. Transfer / Share
        ↓
6. Accept Residual Risk
```

Organizations should consider whether risk can be reduced before accepting it.

---

# 314. AI Risk Treatment Plan

A significant AI risk should have a documented treatment plan.

A treatment plan may contain:

* Risk description.
* Risk owner.
* Risk rating.
* Treatment decision.
* Treatment actions.
* Control owner.
* Target date.
* Required resources.
* Expected residual risk.
* Success criteria.
* Approval.
* Status.

This converts risk management into an actionable process.

---

# 315. Example AI Risk Treatment Plan

```text
Risk:
AI assistant may disclose confidential information.

Inherent Risk:
High

Treatment:
Mitigate

Actions:
- Restrict sensitive data access.
- Implement DLP controls.
- Improve prompt and access controls.
- Conduct leakage testing.
- Monitor usage.

Target Residual Risk:
Low / Moderate

Owner:
AI Product Owner

Status:
In Progress
```

---

# 316. Risk Treatment Ownership

Every significant treatment action should have an accountable owner.

For example:

```text
Risk Owner
    ↓
Treatment Decision
    ↓
Control Owner
    ↓
Implementation
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Review
```

Ownership prevents risk treatment from becoming an abstract governance exercise.

---

# 317. Control Selection

Controls should be selected based on the actual risk.

For example:

### Risk

Unauthorized AI access.

### Potential Controls

* Identity and access management.
* Role-based access control.
* Multi-factor authentication.
* Privileged access management.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The organization should avoid implementing controls simply because they are available.

The control should address a meaningful risk.

---

# 318. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop undesirable events before they occur.

Examples include:

* Access restrictions.
* Input validation.
* Data classification.
* Approved-use policies.
* Human approval requirements.
* Secure configuration.

For AI systems, preventive controls can reduce the opportunity for harmful behavior.

---

# 319. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify undesirable events after or while they occur.

Examples include:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security alerts.
* Data leakage detection.
* Output monitoring.
* Incident detection.
* Anomaly detection.

Detection is particularly important when prevention cannot eliminate the risk.

---

# 320. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls respond to identified problems.

Examples include:

* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data correction.
* Model retraining.
* Configuration changes.
* Incident response.
* Additional human review.

A mature AI control environment generally uses preventive, detective, and corrective controls together.

---

# 321. Compensating Controls

A compensating control provides an alternative risk-reduction mechanism when the preferred control cannot be implemented fully.

Example:

```text
Automated AI decision
       ↓
Model limitation identified
       ↓
Mandatory human approval
       ↓
Compensating control
```

Compensating controls should be assessed to determine whether they actually reduce the relevant risk.

---

# 322. Human Oversight as a Risk Treatment

Human oversight can be an important AI risk treatment.

However, simply stating:

> "A human is in the loop"

does not automatically mean the risk is controlled.

The organization should determine:

* Who reviews the output?
* What information do they receive?
* What authority do they have?
* Can they override the AI?
* When must they intervene?
* Are decisions documented?
* Are reviewers trained?
* Is review actually occurring?

---

# 323. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop arrangement requires meaningful human intervention before a consequential action.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject / Modify
       ↓
Final Decision
```

This can reduce certain risks where human judgment is appropriate.

---

# 324. Human-on-the-Loop

A human-on-the-loop arrangement involves monitoring an automated process and intervening when necessary.

Example:

```text
AI Automated Process
       ↓
Continuous Monitoring
       ↓
Human Intervention
when threshold exceeded
```

This may be appropriate for certain operational systems.

---

# 325. Human Oversight Limitations

Human oversight can fail if:

* Reviewers lack expertise.
* AI outputs appear overly authoritative.
* Workload is excessive.
* Review becomes routine rubber-stamping.
* Reviewers cannot override the system.
* Important context is unavailable.

Therefore, human oversight itself should be treated as a control that requires evaluation.

---

# 326. Technical Risk Treatment

Technical controls may include:

* Secure architecture.
* Model access controls.
* Encryption.
* Data validation.
* Input filtering.
* Output filtering.
* Sandboxing.
* Tool restrictions.
* Network segmentation.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The appropriate controls depend on the AI architecture.

---

# 327. Data Risk Treatment

Where AI risk originates from data, treatment may include:

* Data quality improvement.
* Data validation.
* Data minimization.
* Data classification.
* Access restrictions.
* Data lineage.
* Data retention controls.
* Approved datasets.
* Privacy controls.

The objective is to address the underlying source of risk.

---

# 328. Model Risk Treatment

Model-related treatments may include:

* Model retraining.
* Model redesign.
* Parameter adjustment.
* Additional validation.
* Model replacement.
* Ensemble methods.
* Human review.
* Restricted deployment.

The organization should evaluate whether the treatment reduces the relevant risk.

---

# 329. Security Risk Treatment

AI security treatments may include:

* Strong authentication.
* Authorization.
* Network controls.
* Secure APIs.
* Prompt injection defenses.
* Data-loss prevention.
* Secrets management.
* Vulnerability management.
* Red-team testing.
* Security monitoring.

These controls should be aligned with the AI threat model.

---

# 330. Privacy Risk Treatment

Privacy-related treatments may include:

* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Access control.
* De-identification where appropriate.
* Retention controls.
* Privacy impact assessments.
* User rights processes.
* Privacy monitoring.

The exact requirements depend on applicable law and organizational policy.

---

# 331. Fairness Risk Treatment

Where unfair outcomes are identified, potential treatments may include:

* Improving training data.
* Rebalancing datasets.
* Adjusting decision thresholds.
* Additional human review.
* Restricting use.
* Removing inappropriate features.
* Additional testing.

Treatment should be based on the specific source and nature of the disparity.

---

# 332. Safety Risk Treatment

Safety risks may require:

* Additional safeguards.
* Human approval.
* Restricted functionality.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Emergency shutdown.
* Additional validation.
* Operational restrictions.

For safety-critical AI, risk treatment should be proportionate to potential harm.

---

# 333. Third-Party AI Risk Treatment

For externally provided AI systems, treatment may include:

* Vendor due diligence.
* Contractual requirements.
* Security assessments.
* Independent assurance.
* Data restrictions.
* Service-level requirements.
* Incident notification requirements.
* Alternative provider planning.

Third-party dependency should not be treated as outside the organization's risk management responsibility.

---

# 334. AI Supply Chain Risk Treatment

AI systems may depend on:

```text
Foundation Model
       ↓
AI Provider
       ↓
API
       ↓
Application
       ↓
Business Process
       ↓
Customer
```

A weakness at any layer may affect the overall system.

MANAGE therefore needs to consider relevant supply-chain dependencies.

---

# 335. Residual Risk Assessment

After treatment, the organization should reassess the risk.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk
High
   ↓
Treatment
Human Review + DLP + Monitoring
   ↓
Residual Risk
Moderate
   ↓
Compare Against Tolerance
   ↓
Accept / Further Treat
```

This closes the risk treatment loop.

---

# 336. Treatment Effectiveness

The organization should verify whether treatment actually worked.

For example:

> The organization implemented a DLP control to reduce AI data leakage.

MEASURE should subsequently determine:

* Whether leakage decreased.
* Whether the control operates correctly.
* Whether users can bypass it.
* Whether new risks were introduced.

This illustrates the relationship between **MEASURE and MANAGE**.

---

# 337. MANAGE and MEASURE Feedback Loop

The two functions operate together:

```text
MEASURE
Identifies / quantifies problem
        ↓
MANAGE
Selects response
        ↓
Treatment Implemented
        ↓
MEASURE
Tests treatment effectiveness
        ↓
MANAGE
Reassesses residual risk
```

Risk management therefore becomes iterative.

---

# 338. Risk Treatment Prioritization

Treatment actions should themselves be prioritized.

Factors may include:

* Risk severity.
* Regulatory deadlines.
* Exposure.
* Control weakness.
* Ease of exploitation.
* Business criticality.
* Cost.
* Implementation time.

A critical AI risk should generally not wait behind low-impact improvements simply because the latter are easier to implement.

---

# 339. Risk Treatment Backlog

Organizations with many AI systems may maintain a treatment backlog.

Example:

| Priority | Risk Treatment           | Owner      | Status      |
| -------- | ------------------------ | ---------- | ----------- |
| Critical | Data leakage control     | Security   | In Progress |
| High     | Model validation         | ML Team    | Planned     |
| High     | Human review enhancement | Business   | In Progress |
| Medium   | Monitoring improvement   | Operations | Planned     |

This helps convert governance findings into operational work.

---

# 340. Treatment Deadlines

High-risk findings should have appropriate target dates.

However, deadlines should reflect:

* Risk severity.
* Exposure.
* Complexity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Available compensating controls.

A deadline should not simply be selected for administrative convenience.

---

# 341. Risk Treatment Exceptions

Sometimes an organization cannot immediately implement a required treatment.

The exception should document:

* The risk.
* Why treatment cannot currently be completed.
* Temporary controls.
* Residual risk.
* Risk owner.
* Approval.
* Expiration date.
* Review date.

Exceptions should be actively managed rather than becoming permanent substitutes for treatment.

---

# 342. Risk Treatment and Business Objectives

MANAGE should balance risk reduction with legitimate business objectives.

For example:

```text
Business Benefit
       +
Risk Exposure
       +
Treatment Cost
       +
Regulatory Requirements
       ↓
Management Decision
```

The goal is not to eliminate useful AI simply because some risk exists.

The goal is to make informed decisions about acceptable risk.

---

# 343. Cost of Risk Treatment

Treatment decisions may require investment.

Potential costs include:

* Engineering.
* Security tooling.
* Data improvement.
* Additional personnel.
* Human review.
* Vendor changes.
* Monitoring infrastructure.

Cost should be considered alongside risk, but cost alone should not justify accepting an unacceptable risk.

---

# 344. Risk-Based Investment

A useful principle is:

> Allocate resources according to risk significance.

For example:

```text
Critical AI Risk
      ↓
High Investment Priority

Low AI Risk
      ↓
Routine Treatment
```

This allows organizations to focus limited resources where they provide the greatest risk reduction.

---

# 345. Risk Reduction Versus Risk Acceptance

Management should understand the trade-off.

For example:

```text
Option A
Improve Model
Cost: High
Risk: Low

Option B
Add Human Review
Cost: Medium
Risk: Moderate

Option C
Accept Current Risk
Cost: Low
Risk: High
```

The decision should be documented and made by the appropriate risk authority.

---

# 346. Risk Treatment and Risk Appetite

Risk appetite establishes the organization's overall willingness to accept risk.

For example:

> The organization has very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of customer information.

Therefore, an AI system creating significant data leakage risk may require stronger treatment than an AI system creating minor productivity errors.

---

# 347. Risk Treatment and Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable variation around the organization's risk objectives.

For example:

```text
AI Error Rate
Target: ≤ 3%
Tolerance: ≤ 5%

5%+:
Treatment Required
```

The actual thresholds must be established by the organization.

---

# 348. Risk Appetite Does Not Equal Risk Acceptance

Risk appetite provides strategic direction.

Risk acceptance is a specific decision concerning a particular risk.

For example:

```text
Enterprise Risk Appetite
Low tolerance for privacy risk

Specific AI System
Residual privacy risk assessed as moderate

Decision
Additional treatment required
```

The system-specific decision should align with the broader appetite.

---

# 349. Risk Treatment and Regulatory Requirements

Some AI risks cannot simply be accepted because:

> "The business accepts the risk."

Legal or regulatory requirements may impose mandatory obligations.

Therefore, MANAGE decisions should consider:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Regulatory guidance.
* Contractual obligations.
* Industry requirements.
* Internal policies.

Where a requirement is mandatory, risk acceptance may not be an appropriate substitute for compliance.

---

# 350. Risk Treatment and Stakeholders

Treatment decisions should consider relevant stakeholders.

Potential stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Management.
* Regulators.
* Security teams.
* Privacy teams.
* Legal.
* Business owners.
* AI developers.
* Affected individuals.

Stakeholder impact can materially affect risk prioritization.

---

# 351. Risk Communication

Risk treatment requires clear communication.

A useful risk communication should explain:

* What the AI system does.
* What the risk is.
* Why the risk matters.
* How severe it is.
* What treatment is proposed.
* What residual risk remains.
* Who owns the decision.

This enables informed governance decisions.

---

# 352. Management Decision Record

Significant AI risk decisions should be documented.

A decision record may contain:

```text

Continue AI deployment

Risk:
Potential confidential data leakage

Moderate
```

This creates an auditable record of the decision.

---

# 353. Risk Treatment Documentation

The organization should be able to demonstrate:

> We identified the risk, assessed it, selected a treatment, implemented controls, evaluated the remaining risk, and obtained appropriate approval.

This is a core principle of defensible AI governance.

---

# 354. Common MANAGE Mistakes

Several mistakes can weaken AI risk management.

### Mistake 1 — Treating Identification as Treatment

Knowing the risk exists does not reduce it.

### Mistake 2 — Accepting Everything

Risk acceptance should be a deliberate decision, not the default outcome.

### Mistake 3 — Implementing Controls Without Validation

A documented control does not prove risk reduction.

### Mistake 4 — Ignoring Residual Risk

Treatment should always be followed by reassessment.

### Mistake 5 — No Ownership

Unassigned risk treatment often remains unresolved.

### Mistake 6 — Treating Vendors as Automatically Responsible

Third-party involvement does not eliminate organizational accountability.

### Mistake 7 — Focusing Only on Technical Controls

Business-process and human controls may also be required.

# 356. Practical Cybersecurity Example

Consider an AI-enabled security monitoring system.

### Risk

The AI fails to detect sophisticated attacks.

### Inherent Risk

High.

### Treatment Options

* Improve model training.
* Add threat intelligence.
* Add deterministic detection rules.
* Require analyst review for high-risk events.
* Increase monitoring.
* Conduct adversarial testing.

### Residual Risk

Moderate.

### Management Decision

Continue deployment with compensating controls and periodic reassessment.

This demonstrates how MANAGE converts measurement results into concrete risk responses.

---

# 357. Key Takeaway

The MANAGE function answers one of the most important questions in AI governance:

> **Now that we understand and have measured the AI risk, what are we going to do about it?**

A mature MANAGE capability ensures that AI risks are:

* Prioritized.
* Assigned.
* Treated.
* Controlled.
* Reassessed.
* Accepted only by authorized decision-makers.
* Continuously monitored.

Ultimately:

```text
GOVERN → Direction
MAP     → Context & Risk
MEASURE → Evidence
MANAGE  → Action
```

The MANAGE function therefore turns AI risk intelligence into **practical risk decisions and treatment actions**.
