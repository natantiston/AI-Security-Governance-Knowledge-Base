# 05.07 — The MANAGE Function

## Part 1 — Introduction to the MANAGE Function

## 1. Introduction to MANAGE

The **MANAGE function** is one of the four core functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The four functions are:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
MAP
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
MANAGE
```

The MANAGE function focuses on **prioritizing, responding to, and managing identified and measured AI risks**.

While:

* **GOVERN** establishes organizational direction, accountability, and policies.
* **MAP** establishes context and identifies AI risks.
* **MEASURE** analyzes, evaluates, and monitors AI risks.

**MANAGE focuses on what the organization does about those risks.**

A simplified representation is:

```text
GOVERN
Establish direction
       ↓
MAP
Understand context and identify risks
       ↓
MEASURE
Evaluate and monitor risks
       ↓
MANAGE
Prioritize and respond to risks
```

MANAGE therefore represents the transition from **risk understanding to risk action**.

---

## 2. Purpose of the MANAGE Function

The fundamental purpose of MANAGE is to ensure that identified AI risks are addressed according to their:

* Likelihood.
* Potential impact.
* Severity.
* Urgency.
* Business significance.
* Regulatory significance.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Available controls.
* Available resources.

The organization should determine whether a risk should be:

* Avoided.
* Reduced.
* Controlled.
* Transferred or shared.
* Accepted.
* Restricted.
* Suspended.
* Terminated.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every AI risk.

The objective is to ensure that **AI risks are intentionally managed to an acceptable level**.

---

## 3. MANAGE as an Action-Oriented Function

The previous AI RMF functions generate information that enables management decisions.

For example:

```text
MAP
AI system creates potential privacy risk
       ↓
MEASURE
Risk evaluated and evidence collected
       ↓
MANAGE
Determine appropriate treatment
```

Suppose an AI system is found to have a significant risk of exposing confidential information.

The organization now needs to determine what to do.

Possible actions include:

* Restricting access.
* Implementing data-loss prevention.
* Limiting the information available to the model.
* Improving authentication.
* Adding monitoring.
* Restricting certain use cases.
* Requiring human approval.
* Replacing the technology.
* Suspending the system.

This demonstrates the central purpose of MANAGE:

> **Convert AI risk information into deliberate management action.**

---

## 4. MANAGE Is Not the Same as Risk Elimination

AI risk cannot always be completely eliminated.

For example, an AI system may occasionally generate an incorrect answer despite extensive testing and controls.

The organization may determine that:

* The remaining risk is understood.
* Appropriate controls are implemented.
* The residual risk is within tolerance.
* The business benefit justifies continued use.

In that situation, the organization may continue operating the AI system while monitoring the residual risk.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Exists
      ↓
Risk Assessed
      ↓
Risk Treated
      ↓
Residual Risk Remains
      ↓
Risk Compared Against Tolerance
      ↓
Management Decision
```

Effective AI risk management is therefore about **informed risk decisions**, not the unrealistic elimination of all uncertainty.

---

## 5. Inherent AI Risk

**Inherent risk** represents the level of risk before considering the effect of controls or other risk treatments.

For example:

> An AI system automatically recommends whether a customer should receive a particular service.

Before controls are considered, the system may present significant risks because an incorrect recommendation could affect customers, create financial consequences, or produce regulatory concerns.

The organization should understand this underlying exposure before evaluating the effectiveness of controls.

A simplified model is:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Inherent Risk
   ↓
Controls
   ↓
Residual Risk
```

Understanding inherent risk helps management determine how much risk reduction may be required.

---

## 6. Residual AI Risk

**Residual risk** is the risk that remains after controls and other treatments have been implemented.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk:
High

Controls:
Human review
Access controls
Monitoring
Model validation

Residual Risk:
Moderate
```

The organization should then determine whether the residual risk falls within its approved risk tolerance.

If it does not, additional treatment may be required.

---

## 7. Risk Treatment

Risk treatment refers to actions taken to modify AI risk.

A practical enterprise risk-treatment model includes:

1. **Avoid the risk**
2. **Reduce or mitigate the risk**
3. **Transfer or share the risk**
4. **Accept the residual risk**

Additional operational decisions may include:

* Restricting the system.
* Suspending the system.
* Replacing the system.
* Decommissioning the system.

The selected treatment should be appropriate to the nature and significance of the AI risk.

---

## 8. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means changing or stopping an activity so that the organization no longer incurs the particular risk.

For an AI system, avoidance could mean:

> The organization decides not to use AI for a particular high-impact decision.

Other examples include:

* Not deploying the AI system.
* Removing a high-risk feature.
* Discontinuing an AI use case.
* Choosing a non-AI alternative.
* Restricting the system from a particular business process.

Risk avoidance may be appropriate when the risk cannot reasonably be reduced to an acceptable level.

---

## 9. Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation means reducing the likelihood or impact of a risk.

AI risk mitigation may include:

* Improving training data.
* Increasing testing.
* Adding human oversight.
* Restricting access.
* Implementing security controls.
* Adding monitoring.
* Improving model design.
* Restricting system functionality.
* Adding additional validation.

For example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
High Risk of Incorrect Decisions
   ↓
Human Review
+
Model Validation
+
Monitoring
   ↓
Reduced Risk
```

Mitigation is often an important component of AI risk management because many AI risks cannot be completely eliminated.

---

## 10. Risk Transfer or Sharing

Some risks may be transferred or shared with another organization or party.

Examples include:

* Contractual obligations.
* Vendor responsibilities.
* Third-party service arrangements.
* Insurance.
* Shared operational responsibilities.

However, contractual transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's overall accountability.

For example, an enterprise may use a third-party AI provider, but it may still remain responsible for determining whether the provider is appropriate for the organization's intended use.

Therefore:

> **Transferring operational responsibility does not automatically eliminate organizational risk.**

---

## 11. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance occurs when an authorized person or body deliberately decides that the remaining risk is acceptable.

A proper risk acceptance decision should be:

* Explicit.
* Informed.
* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Consistent with organizational risk appetite.
* Consistent with applicable risk tolerance.

Risk acceptance should not mean:

> "The organization has not yet addressed the risk."

Instead, it should mean:

> "The organization understands the residual risk and an authorized decision-maker has formally agreed to accept it."

---

## 12. Risk Acceptance Example

Consider an internal AI productivity assistant.

### Risk

The AI may occasionally generate inaccurate information.

### Controls

* Approved use cases.
* User verification.
* Restricted access.
* Approved knowledge sources.
* User training.

### Residual Risk

Low.

### Decision

The authorized risk owner accepts the residual risk because it is within the organization's approved tolerance.

The decision should be documented.

---

## 13. Risk Avoidance Versus Risk Acceptance

Risk avoidance and risk acceptance represent fundamentally different decisions.

### Risk Avoidance

> The organization will not perform the activity because the risk is unacceptable.

### Risk Acceptance

> The organization will continue the activity because the residual risk is within approved tolerance.

The distinction can be represented as:

```text
Risk Too High
     ↓
Avoid / Stop / Restrict

Risk Reduced to Acceptable Level
     ↓
Continue
     ↓
Accept Residual Risk
```

This distinction is important for AI governance because not every risky AI use case should automatically be rejected.

---

## 14. Risk Prioritization

Organizations usually have limited resources.

An enterprise may have:

* Hundreds of AI systems.
* Thousands of AI-related controls.
* Multiple risk findings.
* Numerous treatment actions.

It is therefore necessary to determine which risks require the greatest attention.

Risk prioritization may consider:

* Potential impact.
* Likelihood.
* Severity.
* Urgency.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory implications.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Control effectiveness.
* Risk concentration.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.

---

## 15. AI Risk Severity

Risk severity represents the significance of the consequences associated with a risk.

Potential AI consequences may include:

### Financial

* Financial losses.
* Fraud.
* Compensation.
* Increased operating costs.

### Operational

* Business disruption.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Service failures.

### Security

* Unauthorized access.
* Data disclosure.
* System compromise.

### Privacy

* Exposure of personal information.
* Inappropriate processing.

### Legal and Regulatory

* Regulatory violations.
* Enforcement actions.
* Litigation.

### Reputational

* Loss of customer confidence.
* Public criticism.

### Human

* Discrimination.
* Physical harm.
* Loss of opportunity.
* Other adverse effects.

---

## 16. AI Risk Is Multi-Dimensional

Traditional information-security risk often focuses heavily on:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.

AI risk may require a broader perspective.

Relevant dimensions can include:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Safety.
* Fairness.
* Reliability.
* Robustness.
* Transparency.
* Explainability.
* Human oversight.
* Accountability.

Therefore, an AI risk assessment should consider the characteristics that are relevant to the system's intended purpose and context.

---

## 17. Risk Interdependencies

AI risks may interact with one another.

For example:

```text
Poor Data Quality
       ↓
Model Error
       ↓
Incorrect Decision
       ↓
Customer Harm
       ↓
Regulatory Exposure
       ↓
Reputational Damage
```

A single underlying problem can therefore produce multiple risk consequences.

MANAGE should consider these relationships when determining treatment priorities.

---

## 18. Root-Cause-Based Risk Treatment

Risk treatment should address the underlying cause whenever possible.

For example:

> The AI system produces incorrect outputs.

A superficial response might be:

> Add more human review.

But further investigation may reveal:

> The training data is incomplete and poorly representative.

A stronger treatment may therefore include:

* Improving the dataset.
* Validating the data.
* Retraining the model.
* Testing the model again.
* Adding appropriate human oversight.

The objective is to address the **source of the risk**, not merely its visible symptoms.

---

## 19. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

A practical treatment hierarchy can be represented as:

```text
Eliminate / Avoid
       ↓
Reduce Likelihood
       ↓
Reduce Impact
       ↓
Add Compensating Controls
       ↓
Transfer / Share
       ↓
Accept Residual Risk
```

The organization should determine which treatment provides an appropriate balance between:

* Risk reduction.
* Business requirements.
* Cost.
* Complexity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Operational feasibility.

---

## 20. AI Risk Treatment Plan

A significant AI risk should have a documented treatment plan.

The plan may contain:

1. Risk description.
2. Risk owner.
3. Risk rating.
4. Treatment decision.
5. Treatment actions.
6. Control owner.
7. Target completion date.
8. Required resources.
9. Expected residual risk.
10. Success criteria.
11. Approval.
12. Current status.

A treatment plan converts a risk assessment into an actionable management process.

---

## 21. Example AI Risk Treatment Plan

```text
Risk:
AI assistant may disclose confidential information.

Inherent Risk:
High

Treatment:
Mitigate

Actions:
- Restrict sensitive-data access.
- Implement DLP controls.
- Improve access controls.
- Conduct leakage testing.
- Monitor AI usage.

Expected Residual Risk:
Moderate

Risk Owner:
AI Product Owner

Control Owner:
Cybersecurity

Status:
In Progress
```

This provides a clear connection between:

```text
Risk
 ↓
Treatment
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Owner
 ↓
Expected Outcome
```

---

## 22. Risk Treatment Ownership

Every significant AI risk should have an accountable owner.

A practical structure is:

```text
Risk Owner
    ↓
Treatment Decision
    ↓
Control Owner
    ↓
Implementation
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Residual Risk Review
```

The **risk owner** is accountable for the risk decision.

The **control owner** may be responsible for implementing and operating a specific control.

These responsibilities should not automatically be assumed to be the same person.

---

## 23. Control Selection

Controls should be selected based on the specific risk.

For example:

### Risk

Unauthorized users access an AI system containing sensitive information.

### Potential Controls

* Identity and access management.
* Role-based access control.
* Multi-factor authentication.
* Privileged access management.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

The organization should not implement controls simply because they are available.

The control should provide a reasonable response to the identified risk.

---

## 24. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to prevent an undesirable event before it occurs.

Examples include:

* Access restrictions.
* Input validation.
* Data classification.
* Approved-use policies.
* Human approval requirements.
* Secure configuration.
* Network restrictions.

For AI systems, preventive controls can reduce opportunities for misuse or harmful outcomes.

---

## 25. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify undesirable events after or while they occur.

Examples include:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security alerts.
* Data leakage detection.
* Output monitoring.
* Anomaly detection.
* Incident detection.
* Model behavior monitoring.

Detection is particularly important where prevention cannot completely eliminate the risk.

---

## 26. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls respond to identified problems.

Examples include:

* Model rollback.
* Account suspension.
* Data correction.
* Model retraining.
* Configuration changes.
* Incident response.
* Additional human review.
* Service restriction.

Corrective controls help reduce the duration and impact of AI-related incidents.

---

## 27. Compensating Controls

A compensating control provides an alternative risk-reduction mechanism when the preferred control cannot be fully implemented.

For example:

```text
Automated AI Decision
       ↓
Risk Limitation Identified
       ↓
Mandatory Human Approval
       ↓
Compensating Control
```

Compensating controls should themselves be assessed to determine whether they adequately address the original risk.

---

## 28. Human Oversight as a Risk Treatment

Human oversight can be an important AI risk treatment.

However, simply stating:

> "There is a human in the loop"

does not automatically demonstrate effective risk management.

The organization should determine:

* Who performs the review?
* What information is provided?
* What authority does the reviewer have?
* Can the reviewer override the AI?
* When must intervention occur?
* Is the review documented?
* Are reviewers appropriately trained?
* Is the review actually being performed?

Human oversight should therefore be treated as a control that requires governance and effectiveness evaluation.

---

## 29. Human-in-the-Loop

A human-in-the-loop arrangement generally requires meaningful human intervention before a consequential action.

Example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Approve / Reject / Modify
       ↓
Final Decision
```

This can reduce certain risks where human judgment is necessary.

---

## 30. Human-on-the-Loop

A human-on-the-loop arrangement involves monitoring an automated process and intervening when predefined conditions occur.

Example:

```text
AI Automated Process
       ↓
Continuous Monitoring
       ↓
Threshold Exceeded
       ↓
Human Intervention
```

This may be appropriate where continuous human involvement in every individual transaction is impractical but meaningful intervention remains necessary.

---

## 31. Human Oversight Limitations

Human oversight can itself fail.

Potential problems include:

* Reviewers lack sufficient expertise.
* Reviewers are overloaded.
* AI outputs appear overly authoritative.
* Review becomes routine rubber-stamping.
* Reviewers cannot override the system.
* Important context is unavailable.
* Review requirements are unclear.

Therefore:

> **Human oversight must be designed, implemented, monitored, and evaluated like any other important control.**

---

## 32. MANAGE and Business Decision-Making

AI risk management should support informed business decisions.

The organization should balance:

```text
Business Benefit
       +
AI Risk
       +
Treatment Cost
       +
Regulatory Requirements
       +
Stakeholder Impact
       ↓
Management Decision
```

The objective is not to prevent the organization from using AI.

The objective is to ensure that AI is used in a manner consistent with the organization's:

* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Objectives.
* Obligations.
* Governance requirements.

---

## 33. Core MANAGE Principle

The central principle of the MANAGE function can be summarized as:

> **AI risks should not merely be identified and documented; they should be prioritized, assigned, treated, monitored, and subject to appropriate management decisions.**

A mature MANAGE capability connects:

```text
Risk Identification
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Prioritization
       ↓
Treatment Decision
       ↓
Control Implementation
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Management Decision
```

This establishes MANAGE as the action-oriented component of the NIST AI RMF.
