# 05.03 — Characteristics of Trustworthy AI

## Part 1 — Introduction and Foundation

### 1. Introduction

Trustworthy AI is one of the central concepts in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The purpose of trustworthy AI is not simply to determine whether an AI system is technically functional.

An AI system can produce technically correct outputs and still create significant risks.

For example, an AI system may:

* Produce inaccurate results.
* Expose sensitive information.
* Treat groups unfairly.
* Operate without sufficient human oversight.
* Be vulnerable to manipulation.
* Make decisions that cannot be adequately understood.
* Continue operating even when its behavior becomes unsafe.

Therefore, AI governance needs to consider a broader question:

> **Can the organization reasonably trust the AI system to perform its intended function while managing the risks associated with its use?**

Trustworthiness provides a structured way to approach this question.

---

# 2. Trustworthy AI in the NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI RMF identifies several characteristics associated with trustworthy AI.

These include:

1. **Validity and Reliability**
2. **Safety**
3. **Security and Resilience**
4. **Accountability and Transparency**
5. **Explainability and Interpretability**
6. **Privacy Enhancement**
7. **Fairness with Harmful Bias Managed**

These characteristics are interconnected.

They should not be treated as independent checkboxes.

```text
                    TRUSTWORTHY AI
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       │                  │                  │
       ▼                  ▼                  ▼
   Validity             Safety           Security
   & Reliability       & Resilience
       │                  │                  │
       └──────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       ▼                  ▼                  ▼
 Accountability      Explainability       Privacy
 & Transparency     & Interpretability    Enhancement
       │                  │                  │
       └──────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                          ▼
              Fairness / Bias Management
```

The exact importance of each characteristic depends on the AI system's context and intended use.

---

# 3. Trustworthiness Is Context-Dependent

There is no universal definition of how much trustworthiness is sufficient for every AI system.

Consider two systems.

### System A

An AI tool summarizes internal meeting notes.

Potential concerns may include:

* Accuracy.
* Confidentiality.
* Data protection.

### System B

An AI system supports decisions about employment candidates.

Additional concerns may include:

* Fairness.
* Bias.
* Explainability.
* Privacy.
* Human oversight.
* Accountability.

The systems may use similar AI technologies, but their risk profiles are different.

Therefore:

> **Trustworthiness requirements should be proportionate to the intended use, potential impact and risk of the AI system.**

---

# 4. Trustworthiness Is Not the Same as Accuracy

One of the most important distinctions is:

> **An accurate AI system is not automatically a trustworthy AI system.**

Consider an AI model that produces highly accurate predictions but processes personal information without appropriate privacy safeguards.

It may have strong technical performance while still creating significant privacy risk.

Similarly, a model could be:

* Accurate but insecure.
* Accurate but unfair.
* Accurate but opaque.
* Accurate but unsafe in certain operating conditions.

Therefore:

```text
Technical Accuracy
        ≠
Complete Trustworthiness
```

Trustworthiness requires consideration of multiple dimensions.

* Governance.

# 6. Trustworthy AI and the AI Lifecycle

Trustworthiness should be considered throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text
CONCEPT

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
DEPLOYMENT
   ↓
OPERATION
   ↓
MONITORING
   ↓

   ↓

```

A system that was trustworthy during development may become less trustworthy later.

For example:

* Data may change.
* The model may be updated.
* Users may change.
* Threats may evolve.
* The operating environment may change.
* The AI may be used for purposes that were not originally anticipated.

Therefore, trustworthiness requires continual evaluation.

# 7. Trustworthiness and Risk

Trustworthiness is closely connected to risk management.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Context
    ↓
Potential Harm
    ↓
Risk
    ↓
Trustworthiness Characteristics
    ↓
Controls
    ↓
Measurement
    ↓
Risk Decision
```

The objective is not to eliminate every possible AI risk.

Instead, organizations should understand the risks and establish appropriate safeguards.

---

# 8. Trustworthiness and Risk Appetite

An organization's risk appetite influences what it considers acceptable.

For example, an organization may have:

* Very low tolerance for privacy violations.
* Very low tolerance for critical security weaknesses.
* Low tolerance for harmful discriminatory outcomes.
* Greater tolerance for minor inaccuracies in low-impact applications.

This can be represented as:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Impact Assessment
   ↓
Organizational Risk Appetite
   ↓
Acceptable?
   ├── Yes → Monitor
   └── No  → Treat / Modify / Reject
```

Trustworthiness requirements should therefore align with organizational risk decisions.

# 9. Trustworthiness and Stakeholders

Different stakeholders may evaluate trustworthiness differently.

### Business Owner

May focus on:

* Performance.
* Reliability.
* Business value.

### Security Team

May focus on:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.
* Attack resistance.

### Privacy Team

May focus on:

* Personal data.
* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Privacy risks.

### Legal and Compliance

May focus on:

* Regulatory obligations.
* Accountability.
* Documentation.
* Rights.

### End Users

May focus on:

* Usability.
* Accuracy.
* Transparency.
* Predictability.

### Affected Individuals

May focus on:

* Fairness.
* Harm.
* Privacy.
* Ability to challenge decisions.

Trustworthy AI therefore requires a multi-stakeholder perspective.

---

# 10. Trustworthiness Characteristics Can Conflict

An important concept is that trustworthy characteristics can involve trade-offs.

For example:

```text
More Transparency
       ↓
Potentially More Information Exposed
       ↓
Security / Privacy Consideration
```

Another example:

```text
Maximum Model Complexity
       ↓
Potentially Higher Performance
       ↓
Potentially Lower Explainability
```

Another:

```text
Extensive Data Collection
       ↓
Potentially More Information for Training
       ↓
Greater Privacy Exposure
```

Therefore, AI governance requires informed trade-offs.

---

# 11. No Single Trustworthiness Characteristic Is Sufficient

A system should not be considered trustworthy merely because it performs well in one dimension.

For example:

```text
Reliability = High
Security = Low
Privacy = Low
Fairness = Unknown
```

This should not automatically result in a conclusion that the system is trustworthy.

Instead, the organization should determine:

* Which characteristics are relevant?
* What level is required?
* What risks exist?
* What controls are implemented?
* What residual risks remain?

---

# 12. Proportionality

Trustworthiness requirements should be proportional to the potential consequences of failure.

A low-impact AI system may require:

* Basic testing.
* Standard access controls.
* Basic monitoring.
* Basic documentation.

A high-impact system may require:

* Extensive validation.
* Independent review.
* Enhanced security testing.
* Privacy assessment.
* Bias evaluation.
* Human oversight.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Formal governance approval.

Conceptually:

```text
Higher Potential Impact
          ↓
Higher Risk
          ↓
Stronger Governance
          ↓
Stronger Controls
          ↓
Greater Assurance
```

---

# 13. Intended Use

The intended use of an AI system is fundamental to trustworthiness.

For example, a generative AI system may be suitable for:

> Drafting internal brainstorming notes.

But the same system may require additional safeguards before being used for:

> Automatically making decisions affecting customers.

Therefore, organizations should document:

* Intended purpose.
* Authorized uses.
* Authorized users.
* Prohibited uses.
* Operating constraints.
* Known limitations.

---

# 14. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

Governance should also consider how the system is actually being used.

An organization may approve:

> "AI assists employees in preparing draft customer responses."

But employees may eventually begin using it to:

> "Automatically send responses to customers without human review."

This represents a significant change in risk.

```text
Approved Use
     ↓
Actual Use
     ↓
Difference
     ↓
Potential New Risk
```

Monitoring should therefore consider **use drift**.

---

# 15. Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse

Organizations should consider not only intended use but also reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Examples include:

* Users entering sensitive information into an unapproved AI service.
* Employees relying on AI outputs without verification.
* AI being used outside its approved business process.
* Users attempting to circumvent restrictions.
* AI outputs being reused for higher-impact purposes.

The objective is not to predict every possible misuse.

It is to identify foreseeable scenarios that could materially change risk.

---

# 16. Human Oversight

Human oversight can be an important part of trustworthy AI.

Human involvement may occur at different stages:

```text
Human
  ↓
AI Input
  ↓
AI Processing
  ↓
AI Output
  ↓
Human Review
  ↓
Final Decision
```

However, simply placing a human somewhere in the process does not automatically create effective oversight.

The human must have:

* Appropriate authority.
* Appropriate competence.
* Sufficient information.
* Adequate time.
* Ability to challenge the AI output.
* Ability to override the system where appropriate.

---

# 17. Automation Bias

Humans may place excessive confidence in automated outputs.

This is often referred to as **automation bias**.

For example:

> An employee accepts an AI recommendation because "the system is usually correct" without performing meaningful review.

Therefore, human oversight must be designed carefully.

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Critical Evaluation
       ↓
Accept / Reject / Modify
```

The objective is meaningful human judgment rather than symbolic human involvement.

---

# 18. Human-in-the-Loop

Human-in-the-loop approaches require a person to participate in the decision process.

Example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Decision
```

This can be appropriate where the consequences of an incorrect AI output are significant.

However, organizations should define:

* When human review is required.
* Who performs it.
* What qualifications are required.
* What must be reviewed.
* When escalation is required.

---

# 19. Human-on-the-Loop

In a human-on-the-loop model, the AI may operate automatically while humans monitor the process.

```text
AI
 ↓
Automated Action
 ↓
Human Monitoring
 ↓
Intervention if Required
```

This can be useful for certain operational environments.

However, monitoring must be capable of detecting meaningful failures quickly enough to prevent unacceptable harm.

---

# 20. Human-in-Command

For some high-impact systems, humans may retain ultimate authority over the AI system.

The organization should be able to:

* Stop the system.
* Override decisions.
* Restrict operation.
* Escalate incidents.
* Decommission the system.

This supports organizational accountability.

---

# 21. Trustworthiness and Human Oversight

Human oversight should therefore be evaluated as a control.

A GRC assessment could ask:

1. Is human oversight required?
2. At what stage?
3. Who is responsible?
4. What information is available?
5. Can the person challenge the AI?
6. Can the AI be overridden?
7. Is the override logged?
8. Are reviewers trained?
9. Is oversight monitored for effectiveness?

This transforms a broad principle into an auditable governance requirement.

---

# 22. Trustworthiness and Organizational Accountability

AI cannot be treated as an independent decision-maker from a governance perspective.

The organization should be able to identify:

* System owner.
* Business owner.
* Technical owner.
* Data owner.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Escalation authority.

A simple structure:

```text
AI System
    │
    ├── Business Owner
    ├── Technical Owner
    ├── Data Owner
    ├── Security Owner
    ├── Privacy Function
    └── Risk / Governance
```

Clear accountability is essential for trustworthy AI.

---

# 23. Trustworthiness and Documentation

Documentation supports trustworthiness by creating traceability.

Documentation may include:

* Purpose.
* Scope.
* Intended use.
* Data sources.
* Model information.
* Testing.
* Risks.
* Controls.
* Limitations.
* Approvals.
* Monitoring.
* Changes.
* Incidents.

Documentation should be proportionate to the system's risk.

---

# 24. Trustworthiness and Evidence

Governance claims should be supported by evidence.

For example:

### Claim

> "The model is monitored."

### Evidence

* Monitoring methodology.
* Monitoring dashboard.
* Alert thresholds.
* Monitoring records.
* Escalation procedures.
* Incident records.

This distinction is important in GRC.

```text
Policy Statement
      ↓
Control Requirement
      ↓
Implementation
      ↓

      ↓

```

---

# 25. Trustworthiness and Assurance

Assurance provides confidence that governance and controls are operating effectively.

Possible assurance activities include:

* Internal control testing.
* Model validation.
* Security testing.
* Privacy assessment.
* Independent review.
* Internal audit.
* External assessment.

Higher-risk systems may justify stronger independent assurance.

---

# 26. Trustworthiness and Continuous Monitoring

Trustworthiness is not a one-time certification.

An AI system can degrade or change over time.

Examples include:

* Model drift.
* Data drift.
* Performance degradation.
* New attacks.
* New vulnerabilities.
* New users.
* New use cases.

Therefore:

```text
Initial Assessment
       ↓
Deployment
       ↓
Monitoring
       ↓
Change Detection
       ↓
Reassessment
       ↓
Control Adjustment
```

# 28. Trustworthiness and Third Parties

Many organizations do not develop AI systems entirely internally.

They may depend on:

* Cloud AI providers.
* Foundation model providers.
* SaaS vendors.
* Data providers.
* AI development partners.

Trustworthiness therefore extends into the supply chain.

The organization should understand:

* Who operates the model.
* Where data is processed.
* What controls exist.
* What changes the provider can make.
* What evidence is available.
* What contractual protections exist.

---

# 29. Trustworthiness and the AI Supply Chain


---

# 30. Trustworthiness and Security

Security is one of the core dimensions of trustworthy AI.

Security concerns may include:

* Unauthorized access.
* Data leakage.
* Model manipulation.
* Prompt injection.
* Adversarial attacks.
* Supply-chain compromise.
* Model theft.
* Service disruption.

Security controls should be proportionate to the AI system's exposure and impact.

---

# 31. Trustworthiness and Privacy

Privacy is another important dimension.

Organizations should consider:

* What personal data is processed.
* Why it is processed.
* Whether the processing is necessary.
* Who can access it.
* How long it is retained.
* Whether data can be exposed through AI outputs.

Privacy should be considered throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

# 32. Trustworthiness and Fairness

Fairness concerns whether AI systems create unjust or harmful differences in outcomes.

Potential sources include:

* Training data.
* Historical patterns.
* Model design.
* Feature selection.
* Deployment context.
* Human interpretation.

Fairness should therefore be evaluated in the context of the specific AI use case.

---

# 33. Trustworthiness and Transparency

Transparency helps stakeholders understand relevant aspects of an AI system.

Depending on the context, this may include:

* System purpose.
* AI involvement.
* Data usage.
* Known limitations.
* Decision processes.
* Governance responsibilities.
* Monitoring.

Transparency requirements should be balanced against:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Intellectual property.
* Confidentiality.

---

# 34. Trustworthiness and Explainability

Explainability concerns the ability to provide meaningful explanations about AI outputs or behavior.

The level of explanation required depends on:

* Use case.
* Impact.
* Stakeholders.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Decision consequences.

A low-impact recommendation may require limited explanation.

A high-impact decision may require substantially greater transparency and interpretability.

---

# 35. Trustworthiness and Reliability

Reliability concerns whether the AI system performs consistently under expected conditions.

Questions include:

* Does the system produce consistent results?
* Does it remain within expected performance ranges?
* Does it fail predictably?
* Can degradation be detected?
* Can failures be recovered?

Reliability therefore involves more than average accuracy.

---

# 36. Trustworthiness and Safety

Safety focuses on preventing or reducing harmful consequences.

Safety considerations may include:

* Unsafe outputs.
* Unsafe actions.
* Unexpected behavior.
* Failure conditions.
* Human harm.
* Operational hazards.

Safety requirements should reflect the potential severity of harm.

---

# 37. Trustworthiness and Resilience

Resilience concerns the ability to withstand, respond to and recover from adverse conditions.

For AI systems this may include:

* Cyberattacks.
* Data corruption.
* Model failures.
* Provider outages.
* Infrastructure failures.
* Unexpected inputs.

A resilient system should have appropriate:

* Detection.
* Response.
* Recovery.
* Fallback mechanisms.

---

# 38. Trustworthiness Is a Governance Decision

Ultimately, the organization must decide whether the AI system's residual risks are acceptable.

The decision can be represented as:

```text
Trustworthiness Assessment
          ↓
Risk Assessment
          ↓
Control Effectiveness
          ↓
Residual Risk
          ↓
Risk Appetite
          ↓

```

Possible decisions include:

* Approve.
* Approve with conditions.
* Restrict.
* Require remediation.
* Escalate.
* Reject.
* Retire.

# 41. The Principle of Proportionate Governance

Not every AI system needs the same level of governance.

A useful model is:

```text
                RISK
                 ▲
                 │
          HIGH   │  Enhanced Governance
                 │  Independent Assurance
                 │  Continuous Monitoring
                 │
        MEDIUM   │  Formal Assessment
                 │  Standard Controls
                 │
          LOW    │  Basic Governance
                 │  Standard Monitoring
                 └──────────────────────►
                         IMPACT
```

The organization should avoid both:

* **Under-governance**, where important risks are missed.
* **Over-governance**, where low-risk systems receive unnecessary controls.

# 43. Practical Example — Generative AI Assistant

Suppose an organization deploys an internal generative AI assistant.

### Validity and Reliability

Test whether the system provides useful and sufficiently accurate responses.

### Security

Restrict access and protect organizational information.

### Privacy

Prevent unauthorized personal or confidential information from being processed.

### Transparency

Inform users that they are interacting with an AI system.

### Explainability

Document important limitations and explain how outputs should be interpreted.

### Fairness

Assess whether outputs create harmful differential treatment where relevant.

### Accountability

Assign a business owner and technical owner.

### Monitoring

Track incidents, performance and misuse.

The resulting governance model might be:

```text
AI Assistant
    ↓
Risk Assessment
    ↓
Trustworthiness Assessment
    ↓
Controls
    ↓
Approval
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Periodic Reassessment
```

# 45. Key GRC Insight

A critical distinction is:

> **Trustworthy AI is not simply a technical property of a model. It is a governance outcome created by the interaction of technology, data, people, processes, controls and organizational accountability.**

This is particularly important for GRC professionals.

A model can be technically sophisticated while the overall AI system remains poorly governed.

# 49. Foundational Principle

The most important concept to remember is:

> **Trustworthy AI requires the organization to evaluate whether an AI system is appropriate, reliable, safe, secure, accountable, transparent, explainable where necessary, privacy-enhancing and fair within the context in which it is used.**

Not every characteristic has equal importance for every AI system.

The appropriate emphasis depends on:

* Context.
* Risk.
* Impact.
* Stakeholders.
* Intended use.
* Applicable requirements.
