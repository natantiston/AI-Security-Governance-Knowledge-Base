# 05.03 — Characteristics of Trustworthy AI

## Part 1 — Introduction and Foundation

### 1. Introduction

Trustworthy AI is one of the central concepts in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF).

The purpose of trustworthy AI is not simply to determine whether an AI system is technically functional.

An AI system can produce technically correct outputs and still create significant risks.

For example, an AI system may:

* Produce inaccurate results.
* Expose sensitive information.
* Treat groups unfairly.
* Operate without sufficient human oversight.
* Be vulnerable to manipulation.
* Make decisions that cannot be adequately understood.
* Continue operating even when its behavior becomes unsafe.

Therefore, AI governance needs to consider a broader question:

> **Can the organization reasonably trust the AI system to perform its intended function while managing the risks associated with its use?**

Trustworthiness provides a structured way to approach this question.

---

# 2. Trustworthy AI in the NIST AI RMF

The NIST AI RMF identifies several characteristics associated with trustworthy AI.

These include:

1. **Validity and Reliability**
2. **Safety**
3. **Security and Resilience**
4. **Accountability and Transparency**
5. **Explainability and Interpretability**
6. **Privacy Enhancement**
7. **Fairness with Harmful Bias Managed**

These characteristics are interconnected.

They should not be treated as independent checkboxes.

```text
                    TRUSTWORTHY AI
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       │                  │                  │
       ▼                  ▼                  ▼
   Validity             Safety           Security
   & Reliability       & Resilience
       │                  │                  │
       └──────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
       ▼                  ▼                  ▼
 Accountability      Explainability       Privacy
 & Transparency     & Interpretability    Enhancement
       │                  │                  │
       └──────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                          ▼
              Fairness / Bias Management
```

The exact importance of each characteristic depends on the AI system's context and intended use.

---

# 3. Trustworthiness Is Context-Dependent

There is no universal definition of how much trustworthiness is sufficient for every AI system.

Consider two systems.

### System A

An AI tool summarizes internal meeting notes.

Potential concerns may include:

* Accuracy.
* Confidentiality.
* Data protection.

### System B

An AI system supports decisions about employment candidates.

Additional concerns may include:

* Fairness.
* Bias.
* Explainability.
* Privacy.
* Human oversight.
* Accountability.

The systems may use similar AI technologies, but their risk profiles are different.

Therefore:

> **Trustworthiness requirements should be proportionate to the intended use, potential impact and risk of the AI system.**

---

# 4. Trustworthiness Is Not the Same as Accuracy

One of the most important distinctions is:

> **An accurate AI system is not automatically a trustworthy AI system.**

Consider an AI model that produces highly accurate predictions but processes personal information without appropriate privacy safeguards.

It may have strong technical performance while still creating significant privacy risk.

Similarly, a model could be:

* Accurate but insecure.
* Accurate but unfair.
* Accurate but opaque.
* Accurate but unsafe in certain operating conditions.

Therefore:

```text
Technical Accuracy
        ≠
Complete Trustworthiness
```

Trustworthiness requires consideration of multiple dimensions.

# 8. Trustworthiness and Risk Appetite

An organization's risk appetite influences what it considers acceptable.

For example, an organization may have:

* Very low tolerance for privacy violations.
* Very low tolerance for critical security weaknesses.
* Low tolerance for harmful discriminatory outcomes.
* Greater tolerance for minor inaccuracies in low-impact applications.

This can be represented as:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Impact Assessment
   ↓
Organizational Risk Appetite
   ↓
Acceptable?
   ├── Yes → Monitor
   └── No  → Treat / Modify / Reject
```

Trustworthiness requirements should therefore align with organizational risk decisions.

# 10. Trustworthiness Characteristics Can Conflict

An important concept is that trustworthy characteristics can involve trade-offs.

For example:

```text
More Transparency
       ↓
Potentially More Information Exposed
       ↓
Security / Privacy Consideration
```

Another example:

```text
Maximum Model Complexity
       ↓
Potentially Higher Performance
       ↓
Potentially Lower Explainability
```

Another:

```text
Extensive Data Collection
       ↓
Potentially More Information for Training
       ↓
Greater Privacy Exposure
```

Therefore, AI governance requires informed trade-offs.

---

# 11. No Single Trustworthiness Characteristic Is Sufficient

A system should not be considered trustworthy merely because it performs well in one dimension.

For example:

```text
Reliability = High
Security = Low
Privacy = Low
Fairness = Unknown
```

This should not automatically result in a conclusion that the system is trustworthy.

Instead, the organization should determine:

* Which characteristics are relevant?
* What level is required?
* What risks exist?
* What controls are implemented?
* What residual risks remain?

---

# 12. Proportionality

Trustworthiness requirements should be proportional to the potential consequences of failure.

A low-impact AI system may require:

* Basic testing.
* Standard access controls.
* Basic monitoring.
* Basic documentation.

A high-impact system may require:

* Extensive validation.
* Independent review.
* Enhanced security testing.
* Privacy assessment.
* Bias evaluation.
* Human oversight.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Formal governance approval.

Conceptually:

```text
Higher Potential Impact
          ↓
Higher Risk
          ↓
Stronger Governance
          ↓
Stronger Controls
          ↓
Greater Assurance
```

---

# 13. Intended Use

The intended use of an AI system is fundamental to trustworthiness.

For example, a generative AI system may be suitable for:

> Drafting internal brainstorming notes.

But the same system may require additional safeguards before being used for:

> Automatically making decisions affecting customers.

Therefore, organizations should document:

* Intended purpose.
* Authorized uses.
* Authorized users.
* Prohibited uses.
* Operating constraints.
* Known limitations.

---

# 14. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

Governance should also consider how the system is actually being used.

An organization may approve:

> "AI assists employees in preparing draft customer responses."

But employees may eventually begin using it to:

> "Automatically send responses to customers without human review."

This represents a significant change in risk.

```text
Approved Use
     ↓
Actual Use
     ↓
Difference
     ↓
Potential New Risk
```

Monitoring should therefore consider **use drift**.

---

# 15. Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse

Organizations should consider not only intended use but also reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Examples include:

* Users entering sensitive information into an unapproved AI service.
* Employees relying on AI outputs without verification.
* AI being used outside its approved business process.
* Users attempting to circumvent restrictions.
* AI outputs being reused for higher-impact purposes.

The objective is not to predict every possible misuse.

It is to identify foreseeable scenarios that could materially change risk.

---

# 16. Human Oversight

Human oversight can be an important part of trustworthy AI.

Human involvement may occur at different stages:

```text
Human
  ↓
AI Input
  ↓
AI Processing
  ↓
AI Output
  ↓
Human Review
  ↓
Final Decision
```

However, simply placing a human somewhere in the process does not automatically create effective oversight.

The human must have:

* Appropriate authority.
* Appropriate competence.
* Sufficient information.
* Adequate time.
* Ability to challenge the AI output.
* Ability to override the system where appropriate.

---

# 17. Automation Bias

Humans may place excessive confidence in automated outputs.

This is often referred to as **automation bias**.

For example:

> An employee accepts an AI recommendation because "the system is usually correct" without performing meaningful review.

Therefore, human oversight must be designed carefully.

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Critical Evaluation
       ↓
Accept / Reject / Modify
```

The objective is meaningful human judgment rather than symbolic human involvement.

---

# 18. Human-in-the-Loop

Human-in-the-loop approaches require a person to participate in the decision process.

Example:

```text
AI
 ↓
Recommendation
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Decision
```

This can be appropriate where the consequences of an incorrect AI output are significant.

However, organizations should define:

* When human review is required.
* Who performs it.
* What qualifications are required.
* What must be reviewed.
* When escalation is required.

---

# 19. Human-on-the-Loop

In a human-on-the-loop model, the AI may operate automatically while humans monitor the process.

```text
AI
 ↓
Automated Action
 ↓
Human Monitoring
 ↓
Intervention if Required
```

This can be useful for certain operational environments.

However, monitoring must be capable of detecting meaningful failures quickly enough to prevent unacceptable harm.

---

# 20. Human-in-Command

For some high-impact systems, humans may retain ultimate authority over the AI system.

The organization should be able to:

* Stop the system.
* Override decisions.
* Restrict operation.
* Escalate incidents.
* Decommission the system.

This supports organizational accountability.

---

# 21. Trustworthiness and Human Oversight

Human oversight should therefore be evaluated as a control.

A GRC assessment could ask:

1. Is human oversight required?
2. At what stage?
3. Who is responsible?
4. What information is available?
5. Can the person challenge the AI?
6. Can the AI be overridden?
7. Is the override logged?
8. Are reviewers trained?
9. Is oversight monitored for effectiveness?

This transforms a broad principle into an auditable governance requirement.

# 30. Trustworthiness and Security

Security is one of the core dimensions of trustworthy AI.

Security concerns may include:

* Unauthorized access.
* Data leakage.
* Model manipulation.
* Prompt injection.
* Adversarial attacks.
* Supply-chain compromise.
* Model theft.

# 31. Trustworthiness and Privacy

Privacy is another important dimension.

Organizations should consider:

* What personal data is processed.
* Why it is processed.
* Whether the processing is necessary.
* Who can access it.
* How long it is retained.
* Whether data can be exposed through AI outputs.

Privacy should be considered throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

# 32. Trustworthiness and Fairness

Fairness concerns whether AI systems create unjust or harmful differences in outcomes.

Potential sources include:

* Training data.
* Historical patterns.
* Model design.
* Feature selection.
* Deployment context.
* Human interpretation.

Fairness should therefore be evaluated in the context of the specific AI use case.

---

# 33. Trustworthiness and Transparency

Transparency helps stakeholders understand relevant aspects of an AI system.

Depending on the context, this may include:

* System purpose.
* AI involvement.
* Data usage.
* Known limitations.
* Decision processes.
* Governance responsibilities.
* Monitoring.

Transparency requirements should be balanced against:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Intellectual property.
* Confidentiality.

---

# 34. Trustworthiness and Explainability

Explainability concerns the ability to provide meaningful explanations about AI outputs or behavior.

The level of explanation required depends on:

* Use case.
* Impact.
* Stakeholders.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Decision consequences.

A low-impact recommendation may require limited explanation.

A high-impact decision may require substantially greater transparency and interpretability.

---

# 35. Trustworthiness and Reliability

Reliability concerns whether the AI system performs consistently under expected conditions.

Questions include:

* Does the system produce consistent results?
* Does it remain within expected performance ranges?
* Does it fail predictably?
* Can degradation be detected?
* Can failures be recovered?

Reliability therefore involves more than average accuracy.

---

# 36. Trustworthiness and Safety

Safety focuses on preventing or reducing harmful consequences.

Safety considerations may include:

* Unsafe outputs.
* Unsafe actions.
* Unexpected behavior.
* Failure conditions.
* Human harm.
* Operational hazards.

Safety requirements should reflect the potential severity of harm.

---

# 37. Trustworthiness and Resilience

Resilience concerns the ability to withstand, respond to and recover from adverse conditions.

For AI systems this may include:

* Cyberattacks.
* Data corruption.
* Model failures.
* Provider outages.
* Infrastructure failures.
* Unexpected inputs.

A resilient system should have appropriate:

* Detection.
* Response.
* Recovery.
* Fallback mechanisms.

## Part 2 — Validity, Reliability, Safety, Security and Resilience

## 51. Validity and Reliability

**Validity and reliability** are fundamental characteristics of trustworthy AI.

They address whether an AI system performs its intended function correctly and consistently under defined conditions.

Although the terms are related, they represent different concepts.

### Validity

Validity concerns whether the AI system is appropriate for the intended purpose.

The question is:

> **Does the system actually perform the function it is supposed to perform?**

### Reliability

Reliability concerns whether the system continues to perform that function consistently and dependably.

The question is:

> **Does the system continue to perform as expected under relevant conditions?**

A simplified distinction is:

```text
VALIDITY
"Are we solving the right problem correctly?"
          +
RELIABILITY
"Does the solution continue to work consistently?"
          ↓
TRUSTWORTHY PERFORMANCE
```

---

# 52. Validity in AI Systems

An AI system may produce technically plausible outputs while still being invalid for its intended purpose.

For example, suppose an organization develops an AI system intended to identify fraudulent transactions.

The model may achieve high predictive accuracy in a test environment.

However, if the test dataset does not represent the organization's actual transaction patterns, the model may not be valid for the production environment.

Therefore, validity requires consideration of:

* Intended purpose.
* Operating environment.
* Data characteristics.
* Relevant populations.
* Expected outputs.
* Decision requirements.
* Performance objectives.

---

# 53. Validity Is Context-Specific

An AI model cannot be evaluated only by asking:

> "How accurate is the model?"

A better question is:

> **"How well does the system perform the intended function within the environment where it will actually be used?"**

For example:

```text
Model Accuracy
      ↓
Test Environment
      ↓
Production Environment
      ↓
Actual Business Use
      ↓
Real-World Effectiveness
```

A model can perform well in laboratory testing but poorly in production.

---

# 54. Reliability in AI Systems

Reliability concerns consistency and dependability.

Important questions include:

* Does the system behave consistently?
* Does performance remain within acceptable thresholds?
* Does the system degrade over time?
* Can failures be detected?
* Can the system recover?
* Does it behave predictably under expected conditions?

Reliability should therefore be evaluated over time rather than only at initial deployment.

---

# 55. Reliability Is More Than Accuracy

An AI system may have good average accuracy while still being unreliable.

For example:

```text
Average Accuracy = 95%
```

may look strong.

However, if the system occasionally produces catastrophic errors in high-impact situations, the average figure may not provide sufficient information.

Therefore, organizations should consider:

* Error distribution.
* Severity of errors.
* Frequency of errors.
* Edge cases.
* Failure conditions.
* Performance by relevant population.
* Performance over time.

---

# 56. AI Performance Metrics

Depending on the use case, organizations may monitor:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Mean absolute error.
* Calibration.
* Latency.
* Availability.
* Failure rate.

The appropriate metrics depend on the AI system.

For example:

### Fraud detection

False negatives may be particularly important.

### Spam detection

False positives may be particularly important.

### Medical decision support

Both error types may require careful consideration because of potential consequences.

Therefore:

> **Metrics should be selected according to the actual risk and purpose of the AI system.**

---

# 57. Performance Thresholds

Organizations should define acceptable performance thresholds where appropriate.

For example:

```text
Performance Metric
       ↓
Target
       ↓
Warning Threshold
       ↓
Critical Threshold
       ↓
Required Action
```

An illustrative example:

| Metric              | Target | Warning | Critical |
| ------------------- | -----: | ------: | -------: |
| Accuracy            |   ≥95% |    <93% |     <90% |
| False-negative rate |    ≤3% |     >4% |      >6% |
| Availability        | ≥99.9% |  <99.5% |     <99% |

These values are examples only.

Actual thresholds should be established according to business requirements and risk tolerance.

---

# 58. Baseline Performance

Before production deployment, organizations should establish a baseline.

A baseline provides a reference against which future performance can be compared.

```text
Initial Testing
      ↓
Baseline
      ↓
Production Monitoring
      ↓
Compare
      ↓
Detect Degradation
```

Without a baseline, identifying meaningful performance deterioration becomes more difficult.

---

# 59. Model Drift

AI performance can change when the environment changes.

This can happen through:

* Changes in user behavior.
* Changes in data.
* Changes in business processes.
* Changes in external conditions.
* Changes in threat patterns.

This may result in **model drift**.

A simplified model:

```text
Initial Model
      ↓
Stable Performance
      ↓
Environment Changes
      ↓
Performance Changes
      ↓
Potential Model Drift
      ↓
Reassessment
```

---

# 60. Data Drift

**Data drift** occurs when the characteristics of input data change over time.

For example, a fraud detection model trained using historical transaction patterns may encounter significantly different transaction behavior later.

The model itself may not have changed.

The input environment has changed.

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Production Data
      ↓
Distribution Changes
      ↓
Potential Performance Impact
```

Data drift should therefore be part of AI monitoring where relevant.

---

# 61. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

For example:

> A pattern previously associated with fraudulent behavior may no longer have the same meaning because attackers have changed their techniques.

Concept drift can therefore affect the assumptions underlying a model.

Organizations should determine whether the AI system requires:

* Retraining.
* Revalidation.
* Model replacement.
* Additional controls.
* Temporary restriction.

---

# 62. Reliability Under Adverse Conditions

Reliability should not only be evaluated under normal operating conditions.

Organizations should consider:

* Unexpected inputs.
* Missing data.
* Corrupted data.
* High traffic.
* System outages.
* External service failures.
* Security attacks.
* Integration failures.

This leads to the concept of **robustness**.

---

# 63. Robustness

Robustness concerns the ability of an AI system to continue performing appropriately when conditions vary or when it encounters unexpected situations.

For example:

```text
Normal Input
     ↓
Expected Output
```

versus:

```text
Unexpected Input
     ↓
Controlled / Safe Behavior
```

A robust system should not fail unpredictably when faced with reasonable variations in operating conditions.

---

# 64. Edge Cases

AI testing should consider edge cases.

An edge case is a situation near the boundary of expected operating conditions.

Examples include:

* Unusual input combinations.
* Rare events.
* Unexpected language.
* Missing fields.
* Extreme values.
* Unusual user behavior.

Testing only common scenarios can create false confidence.

A mature testing program includes:

```text
Normal Cases
+
Boundary Cases
+
Rare Cases
+
Adversarial Cases
```

---

# 65. AI Hallucination and Reliability

For generative AI systems, hallucination can represent a reliability concern.

A generative AI system may produce an answer that appears plausible but is factually incorrect.

The governance concern is not simply:

> "Can the model hallucinate?"

The more useful questions are:

* How frequently does this occur?
* In what circumstances?
* What is the consequence?
* Can the error be detected?
* Is human verification required?
* What controls reduce the risk?

This converts a technical behavior into a GRC risk question.

---

# 66. Reliability and Human Oversight

Human oversight can reduce the consequences of AI errors.

For example:

```text
AI Output
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Human Review
    ↓
Approved Output
```

However, human review should not be treated as an automatic solution.

The organization must evaluate whether the reviewer can realistically identify the errors.

---

# 67. Safety

**Safety** concerns whether an AI system avoids causing unacceptable harm.

Safety is especially important where AI outputs can influence:

* Physical safety.
* Health.
* Critical infrastructure.
* Financial outcomes.
* Public services.
* Employment.
* Other consequential decisions.

The safety question is:

> **Could the AI system cause or contribute to unacceptable harm under reasonably foreseeable conditions?**

---

# 68. Safety Is Different from Security

Safety and security are related but different.

### Safety

Concerned primarily with preventing harmful outcomes.

### Security

Concerned primarily with protecting the system against malicious or unauthorized activity.

For example:

```text
AI produces unsafe output
        ↓
SAFETY ISSUE
```

Whereas:

```text
Attacker manipulates AI system
        ↓
SECURITY ISSUE
```

A security attack can create a safety problem, so the two domains often overlap.

---

# 69. Safety Hazards

Organizations should identify potential AI safety hazards.

Examples include:

* Incorrect recommendations.
* Unsafe automated actions.
* Failure to detect dangerous conditions.
* Unexpected system behavior.
* Excessive automation.
* Inadequate human intervention.

A practical safety assessment asks:

```text
Hazard
  ↓
Cause
  ↓
Potential Harm
  ↓
Likelihood
  ↓
Severity
  ↓
Control
  ↓
Residual Risk
```

---

# 70. Safety Controls

Possible safety controls include:

* Human approval.
* Input validation.
* Output validation.
* Safety filters.
* Operational limits.
* Fail-safe behavior.
* Emergency shutdown.
* Manual fallback.
* Escalation procedures.
* Incident response.

The controls should be proportional to the consequences of failure.

---

# 71. Fail-Safe Behavior

A trustworthy AI system should have appropriate behavior when it cannot operate safely.

Instead of:

```text
Failure
 ↓
Continue Automatically
```

a safer design may be:

```text
Failure
 ↓
Detect
 ↓
Restrict / Stop
 ↓
Human Intervention
 ↓
Recovery
```

The correct behavior depends on the use case.

---

# 72. Fail-Open and Fail-Closed Considerations

Organizations may need to determine whether an AI-related control should fail open or fail closed.

### Fail open

The system continues operation when a control fails.

### Fail closed

The system blocks or restricts operation when a critical control fails.

The appropriate choice depends on:

* Safety.
* Business continuity.
* Security.
* Operational consequences.

For high-impact functions, failure behavior should be deliberately designed rather than left undefined.

---

# 73. Safety Boundaries

Organizations should define boundaries within which an AI system is authorized to operate.

For example:

```text
Approved Operating Range
        │
        ▼
   AI Operation
        │
        ▼
Boundary Exceeded
        │
        ▼
Alert / Restrict / Escalate
```

This helps prevent the system from operating beyond its validated conditions.

---

# 74. Security

Security is a fundamental characteristic of trustworthy AI.

AI systems can introduce traditional cybersecurity risks as well as AI-specific attack vectors.

Security objectives include protecting:

* Confidentiality.
* Integrity.
* Availability.
* Authenticity.
* Accountability.

---

# 75. AI Security Threats

AI systems may be exposed to threats such as:

* Prompt injection.
* Data poisoning.
* Model manipulation.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Model extraction.
* Sensitive information leakage.
* Unauthorized access.
* Supply-chain attacks.
* Malicious use of AI capabilities.

The specific threat profile depends on the AI architecture and use case.

---

# 76. Prompt Injection

Generative AI systems can be manipulated through malicious or unexpected instructions.

A simplified example:

```text
User / External Content
        ↓
Malicious Instruction
        ↓
AI System
        ↓
Unexpected Behavior
```

From a GRC perspective, the important questions include:

* Has the threat been identified?
* Has it been tested?
* What controls exist?
* What is the potential impact?
* How is the risk monitored?

---

# 77. Data Poisoning

Data poisoning involves manipulating training or other relevant data in a way that can adversely affect AI behavior.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Data
     +
Malicious Data
     ↓
Model Training
     ↓
Compromised Behavior
```

Potential governance controls include:

* Data provenance.
* Data validation.
* Data integrity controls.
* Access controls.
* Dataset review.
* Change management.

---

# 78. Model Integrity

Organizations should protect the integrity of AI models and associated components.

This can include:

* Model version control.
* Secure repositories.
* Access restrictions.
* Cryptographic integrity mechanisms where appropriate.
* Change approval.
* Deployment controls.
* Model provenance.

The objective is to ensure that the model deployed in production is the model that was authorized.

---

# 79. AI Supply-Chain Security

AI systems may depend on third-party:

* Models.
* Libraries.
* Datasets.
* APIs.
* Cloud services.
* Development tools.

This creates supply-chain risk.

A simplified chain:

```text
Third-Party Model
      ↓
AI Framework
      ↓
Application
      ↓
Cloud Infrastructure
      ↓
Enterprise System
```

A compromise in one component can affect the entire system.

---

# 80. AI Security and Traditional Cybersecurity

AI security should not replace conventional cybersecurity.

It should complement it.

Traditional controls may include:

* Identity and access management.
* Network security.
* Endpoint security.
* Encryption.
* Vulnerability management.
* Logging.
* Security monitoring.
* Incident response.

AI-specific controls may include:

* Prompt security.
* Model integrity.
* AI-specific red teaming.
* Dataset integrity.
* Model monitoring.

Therefore:

```text

     =
Traditional Cybersecurity
+
AI-Specific Security Controls
```

# 81. Resilience

**Resilience** concerns the ability of an AI system and its supporting environment to withstand, respond to and recover from adverse events.

Potential disruptions include:

* Cyberattacks.
* Infrastructure failure.
* Cloud outage.
* Model failure.
* Data corruption.
* Service-provider outage.
* Unexpected demand.

A resilient AI service should have appropriate recovery capabilities.

---


Each stage supports trustworthy operation.

# 83. AI Business Continuity

Organizations should determine what happens if an AI service becomes unavailable.

Possible alternatives include:

* Manual processing.
* Alternative AI provider.
* Previous model version.
* Backup infrastructure.
* Reduced service mode.
* Temporary suspension.

For critical AI services:

> **AI availability and recovery should be connected to business continuity planning.**

# 84. Recovery Time and Recovery Point

Where AI supports important business processes, organizations may define:

### Recovery Time Objective — RTO

How quickly the service must be restored.

### Recovery Point Objective — RPO

How much data or state the organization can afford to lose.

These concepts can apply to AI-supporting infrastructure and data.

---

# 85. AI Resilience and Dependency Management

AI systems may depend on multiple services.

For example:

```text
 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
```

Security and resilience are closely connected.

```text
      ↓

      ↓

      ↓
```

```text
                 TRUSTWORTHY AI
```

* A secure system may still be unsafe.

The principle is:

> **The higher the potential impact, the stronger the justification for deeper testing and assurance.**

# 95. Key Interview Answer — Validity and Reliability

If asked:

> **"What is the difference between validity and reliability in AI?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"Validity concerns whether the AI system is appropriate and performs its intended function in its intended context. Reliability concerns whether it continues to perform consistently and dependably under relevant conditions. In GRC terms, I would establish intended-use requirements, define performance criteria, validate the system before deployment, monitor performance in production, and reassess it when there are material changes or evidence of degradation."**

---

# 96. Key Interview Answer — Safety

If asked:

> **"How would you address AI safety?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"I would first identify the potential harms associated with the intended use, assess their likelihood and impact, and define appropriate safety controls. Depending on the risk, these could include human oversight, input and output validation, operational boundaries, fail-safe behavior, escalation and incident response. I would also establish monitoring and reassessment triggers because safety risk can change during the AI lifecycle."**

---

# 97. Key Interview Answer — AI Security

If asked:

> **"How is AI security different from traditional cybersecurity?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"AI security includes traditional cybersecurity controls such as identity, access control, encryption, vulnerability management and monitoring, but it also considers AI-specific threats such as prompt injection, data poisoning, model manipulation, adversarial inputs and model or data leakage. I would integrate these into the existing cybersecurity and enterprise risk framework rather than creating an isolated security process."**

---

# 98. Key Interview Answer — Resilience

If asked:

> **"How would you make an AI system resilient?"**

A strong answer is:

> **"I would identify critical dependencies and failure scenarios, define recovery requirements, establish monitoring and incident response, and provide appropriate fallback mechanisms such as manual processing or an alternative service. For critical AI services, I would connect the AI recovery strategy to business continuity and disaster recovery requirements."**

---

# 99. GRC Mental Model

For the first group of trustworthy AI characteristics, remember:

```text
VALIDITY
"Does it perform the intended function?"

RELIABILITY
"Does it continue to perform consistently?"

SAFETY
"Can it cause unacceptable harm?"

SECURITY
"Can it be compromised or manipulated?"

RESILIENCE
"Can it withstand and recover from disruption?"
```

These five questions provide a practical foundation for evaluating whether an AI system can be trusted from a technical and governance perspective.

---

# 100. Core Principle

The central governance principle is:

> **Trustworthy AI requires evidence that the system is fit for purpose, performs consistently, operates safely, is protected against relevant threats, and can withstand and recover from adverse conditions.**

These characteristics should be evaluated throughout the AI lifecycle and translated into proportionate requirements, controls, testing, monitoring and management decisions.

# 05.03 — Characteristics of Trustworthy AI

## Part 3 — Accountability, Transparency, Explainability, Interpretability, Privacy and Fairness

### 101. Accountability and Transparency

**Accountability and transparency** are closely related characteristics of trustworthy AI.

They address two fundamental governance questions:

> **Who is responsible for the AI system and its outcomes?**

and:

> **What information about the AI system should be made available to relevant stakeholders?**

Accountability establishes responsibility.

Transparency provides visibility.

Together, they support informed governance and oversight.

---

# 102. Accountability

Accountability means that an organization and its responsible individuals can be held answerable for the design, deployment, operation and outcomes of an AI system.

AI should not become an accountability gap.

A weak governance model might look like:

```text
AI System
    ↓
AI Decision
    ↓
"No one is responsible"
```

A stronger model is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Responsible Owner
    ↓
Governance
    ↓
Oversight
    ↓
Decision / Action
    ↓
Accountability
```

The organization remains responsible for how AI is used.

---

# 103. Accountability Is Different from Ownership

Ownership and accountability are related but not identical.

### Ownership

Identifies who manages or operates the system.

### Accountability

Identifies who is ultimately answerable for decisions and outcomes.

For example:

```text
AI System
   │
   ├── Technical Owner
   ├── Business Owner
   ├── Data Owner
   ├── Security Owner
   └── Risk Owner
             │
             ▼
       Accountability
```

An organization should clearly define these responsibilities.

---

# 104. Accountability Across the AI Lifecycle

Accountability should exist throughout the lifecycle.

```text
Planning
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

At each stage, the organization should know:

* Who is responsible?
* Who approves?
* Who reviews?
* Who monitors?
* Who can stop the system?
* Who accepts residual risk?

---

# 105. Accountability and Risk Ownership

Risk ownership is particularly important.

Suppose an AI system has a significant residual risk.

Someone must have authority to decide whether the organization:

* Accepts the risk.
* Mitigates the risk.
* Restricts the system.
* Suspends the system.
* Rejects the use case.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Assessed
      ↓
Residual Risk
      ↓
Risk Owner
      ↓
Management Decision
```

AI risk should not remain indefinitely with the technical team simply because the risk originated from technology.

---

# 106. Accountability and the Three Lines

AI accountability can be integrated with the Three Lines model.

```text
                 BOARD / EXECUTIVES
                        │
                        ▼
                 AI GOVERNANCE
                        │
       ┌────────────────┼────────────────┐
       ▼                ▼                ▼
   FIRST LINE        SECOND LINE       THIRD LINE
   Operations        Risk / GRC        Internal Audit
       │                │                │
       ▼                ▼                ▼
   Own & Operate     Oversee        Independently
                     & Challenge       Assure
```

This separation helps prevent conflicts of interest.

---

# 107. Accountability and Human Oversight

Human oversight supports accountability when properly designed.

A human reviewer should understand:

* What the AI is doing.
* What information it is using.
* What limitations exist.
* What warning signs to look for.
* When intervention is required.

The reviewer should also have sufficient authority to challenge the AI.

Human involvement without meaningful authority does not create effective accountability.

---

# 108. Accountability and Documentation

Documentation provides evidence of accountability.

Useful records may include:

* AI system owner.
* Business owner.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Decision records.
* Risk assessments.
* Exceptions.
* Changes.
* Incidents.
* Monitoring results.

For example:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Owner
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Approval
   ↓
Operational Monitoring
   ↓
Review Records
```

This creates an auditable accountability trail.

---

# 109. Accountability and Auditability

A mature organization should be able to reconstruct important decisions.

An auditor might ask:

> "Why was this AI system approved?"

The organization should be able to demonstrate:

```text
Business Need
     ↓
Use Case
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Control Assessment
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Residual Risk
     ↓

```

Accountability therefore requires more than assigning names to systems.

It requires evidence of decisions.

---

# 110. Transparency

Transparency concerns providing appropriate information about an AI system to relevant stakeholders.

Transparency does **not** mean that every technical detail must be publicly disclosed.

The appropriate level depends on:

* Stakeholder needs.
* Risk.
* Business context.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Intellectual property.
* Legal requirements.

---

# 111. Different Levels of Transparency

Transparency may exist at multiple levels.

### Organizational Transparency

Information for management and governance functions.

### Technical Transparency

Information about system architecture, data, models and controls.

### User Transparency

Information provided to users interacting with AI.

### External Transparency

Information provided to customers, regulators or other external stakeholders where appropriate.

```text
Internal
   ↓
Technical
   ↓
User
   ↓
External
```

Each audience may require different information.

---

# 112. Transparency Is Not Full Disclosure

An organization may need to provide meaningful information without exposing sensitive implementation details.

For example, it may be appropriate to disclose:

> "AI is used to assist with customer-service response generation."

without disclosing:

* Confidential model configuration.
* Security mechanisms.
* Proprietary algorithms.
* Sensitive infrastructure information.

Therefore:

> **Transparency should be meaningful, proportionate and appropriate to the stakeholder.**

---

# 113. Transparency and User Awareness

Users may need to know when AI is involved.

For example:

```text
User
  ↓
AI Interaction
  ↓
Notice
  ↓
User understands AI involvement
```

The exact disclosure requirements depend on the context and applicable obligations.

The governance objective is to avoid situations where stakeholders make important decisions based on a false assumption about how the output was generated.

---

# 114. Transparency and Documentation

Internal transparency can be supported by documentation such as:

* System purpose.
* Model type.
* Data sources.
* Known limitations.
* Intended use.
* Prohibited use.
* Performance characteristics.
* Risk classification.
* Controls.
* Monitoring requirements.

This creates organizational visibility.

---

# 115. Transparency and Traceability

Transparency is strengthened when organizations can trace AI decisions and outputs.

For example:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Output
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Decision
```

Where appropriate, logs and records can support reconstruction of the process.

This is particularly important for higher-impact AI.

---

# 116. Explainability

**Explainability** concerns the ability to provide meaningful information about how an AI system produces an output or contributes to a decision.

The appropriate level of explanation depends on context.

For example:

> "The AI recommends this transaction for review because it identified characteristics associated with previous fraudulent transactions."

may be more useful to a business user than simply stating:

> "The model gave it a high risk score."

---

# 117. Explainability Is Contextual

Not every AI system requires the same level of explanation.

A simple internal recommendation may require limited explanation.

A high-impact decision may require substantially more.

A useful relationship is:

```text
Higher Impact
     ↓
Greater Need for Explanation
     ↓
Greater Governance Requirement
```

This is a risk-based approach to explainability.

---

# 118. Explainability Versus Interpretability

These concepts are closely related but can be distinguished.

### Explainability

Concerned with providing an understandable account of how or why an AI output was produced.

### Interpretability

Concerned with how readily humans can understand the internal relationship between inputs, processes and outputs.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

```text
Explainability
"Can we explain the result?"

Interpretability
"Can humans understand how the system behaves?"
```

Both can support trustworthy AI.

---

# 119. Why Explainability Matters

Explainability can support:

* User understanding.
* Human oversight.
* Error detection.
* Accountability.
* Risk management.
* Compliance.
* Dispute handling.
* Model validation.

For example, if an AI produces an unexpected recommendation, an understandable explanation may help the reviewer identify whether:

* The data was incorrect.
* The model behaved unexpectedly.
* The output was reasonable.
* A control failed.

---

# 120. Explainability and Human Oversight

Explainability becomes particularly important when humans are expected to review AI outputs.

Consider:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Explanation
       ↓
Human Evaluation
       ↓
Accept / Reject / Override
```

Without sufficient information, the human reviewer may simply accept the AI recommendation.

This can contribute to automation bias.

---

# 121. Explainability Does Not Guarantee Correctness

An explanation can be understandable and still be incorrect or incomplete.

Therefore, organizations should not assume:

> "The AI gave an explanation, therefore the explanation is reliable."

Governance should consider:

* How explanations are generated.
* Whether they are consistent.
* Whether they accurately represent relevant system behavior.
* Whether they are appropriate for the intended audience.

---

# 122. Explainability and Model Complexity

Some AI models are easier to interpret than others.

Conceptually:

```text
Simple Model
    ↓
Often Easier to Interpret

Complex Model
    ↓
Potentially More Difficult to Interpret
```

However, organizations should not automatically choose a simpler model solely for explainability.

The appropriate choice depends on:

* Performance.
* Risk.
* Impact.
* Required explanations.
* Security.
* Operational requirements.

This is another example of a trustworthiness trade-off.

---

# 123. Privacy Enhancement

**Privacy enhancement** is another characteristic of trustworthy AI.

AI systems can process substantial quantities of data, including potentially sensitive information.

Privacy considerations should therefore be integrated throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text

      ↓
Data Preparation
      ↓
Training / Processing
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Inference
      ↓
Storage
      ↓
Deletion / Retention
```

Privacy risk can occur at every stage.

---

# 124. Privacy by Design

Privacy should not be added only after the AI system has already been developed.

A stronger approach is:

> **Consider privacy requirements during system design and throughout the lifecycle.**

Potential measures include:

* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Access controls.
* Retention controls.
* De-identification where appropriate.
* Encryption.
* Privacy impact assessment.
* Data governance.
* Monitoring.

---

# 125. Data Minimization

Data minimization means avoiding unnecessary collection or processing of personal information.

For example:

```text
Business Need
     ↓
Required Data
     ↓
Minimize Collection
     ↓
Reduce Privacy Exposure
```

The principle is not:

> "Collect everything because AI may need it later."

Instead:

> **Collect and process information appropriate to the defined purpose and applicable requirements.**

---

# 126. Privacy and AI Training Data

Organizations should understand the origin and use of training data.

Questions include:

* Where did the data come from?
* Was the data collected appropriately?
* Does it contain personal information?
* What permissions or legal basis apply?
* How is the data protected?
* How long is it retained?
* Can sensitive information appear in outputs?

Data governance is therefore closely connected to AI governance.

---

# 127. Privacy and AI Outputs

* Personal information.
* Confidential business information.
* Sensitive training information.
* Information about individuals.

# 130. Fairness

**Fairness with harmful bias managed** is another important characteristic of trustworthy AI.

Fairness concerns whether AI systems produce unjust or harmful differences in outcomes.

Potential impacts can affect:

* Individuals.
* Groups.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Applicants.
* Communities.

Fairness is highly context-dependent.

---

# 131. Bias

Bias can enter AI systems through multiple sources.

For example:

```text
Historical Data
      ↓
Data Preparation
      ↓
Feature Selection
      ↓
Model Development
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Human Interpretation
```

Bias can therefore arise before, during and after model development.

---

# 132. Sources of Harmful Bias

Potential sources include:

* Historical discrimination.
* Underrepresentation.
* Measurement problems.
* Sampling bias.
* Labeling errors.
* Data quality problems.
* Model design.
* Deployment conditions.
* Human decisions.

Therefore:

> **Bias management requires lifecycle controls rather than a single test performed before deployment.**

---

# 133. Fairness Is Not Simply Equal Accuracy

A model may have high overall accuracy while performing differently for different populations.

For example:

```text
Overall Accuracy = 95%

Group A = 98%
Group B = 91%
```

The overall average may hide meaningful differences.

Therefore, where relevant, organizations should evaluate performance across appropriate populations.

---

# 134. Fairness Metrics

Depending on the use case, organizations may evaluate metrics such as:

* False-positive rates.
* False-negative rates.
* Selection rates.
* Error rates.
* Predictive performance by group.

The correct metric depends on:

* Use case.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Legal requirements.
* Business context.
* Risk.

There is no universal fairness metric that is appropriate for every AI system.

---

# 135. Fairness Trade-Offs

Different fairness objectives can sometimes conflict.

For example, optimizing one statistical fairness criterion may affect another.

Therefore, organizations should avoid treating fairness as a single mathematical value.

Instead:

```text
Use Case
   ↓
Stakeholders
   ↓
Potential Harms
   ↓
Relevant Fairness Criteria
   ↓
Assessment
   ↓
Management Decision
```

---

# 136. Harmful Bias

The focus should be on identifying and managing **harmful bias**.

Not every difference between groups is automatically evidence of harmful bias.

Differences may sometimes reflect:

* Legitimate business factors.
* Relevant risk differences.
* Data characteristics.
* Operational requirements.

The governance question is:

> **Does the AI system create or reinforce unjustified or harmful differences in outcomes within the context of its use?**

---

# 137. Fairness and Human Review

```text
     ↓

```

However, human reviewers can also introduce bias.

Therefore, human oversight itself may need governance.

# 148. Interview Question — What Is Accountability in AI?

A strong answer:

> **"Accountability means the organization maintains clear responsibility for the AI system and its outcomes. I would establish ownership across the business, technical, data, security and risk dimensions, define approval and escalation authority, document significant decisions, and maintain evidence that controls are operating. AI should never become a governance gap where responsibility is attributed to the model itself."**

---

# 149. Interview Question — What Is Transparency?

A strong answer:

> **"Transparency means providing appropriate information about the AI system to the stakeholders who need it. It does not necessarily mean exposing every technical detail. I would consider the system's purpose, limitations, AI involvement, data usage, relevant risks and governance responsibilities while balancing privacy, security, confidentiality and intellectual property."**

---

# 150. Interview Question — What Is Explainability?

A strong answer:

> **"Explainability is the ability to provide meaningful information about how or why an AI system produced an output or contributed to a decision. The required level depends on context and impact. For higher-impact AI, I would establish explicit explanation requirements and ensure that human reviewers have enough information to appropriately challenge AI outputs."**

---

# 151. Interview Question — How Would You Address AI Privacy?

A strong answer:

> **"I would address privacy throughout the AI lifecycle, beginning with understanding what personal data is collected and why. I would apply appropriate data minimization, access control, retention, protection and assessment requirements, and consider privacy risks in both inputs and outputs. I would also ensure privacy requirements are integrated with the organization's broader privacy and data governance program."**

---

# 152. Interview Question — How Would You Address AI Bias?

A strong answer:

> **"I would first understand the use case and the potential harm. Then I would evaluate relevant data and outcomes for harmful bias, considering appropriate populations and fairness criteria. I would implement controls such as data quality measures, testing, human review and monitoring, and reassess the system after significant changes or evidence of disparate outcomes. I would treat fairness as a lifecycle governance issue rather than a one-time model test."**

# 154. Core GRC Principle

The central governance principle is:

> **Trustworthy AI requires more than technically correct outputs. # 198. Key Interview Question — How Do You Operationalize Trustworthy AI?

A strong answer:

> **"I would operationalize trustworthy AI by translating the characteristics into risk-based governance requirements. First I would classify the use case and understand its potential impact. Then I would define applicable requirements for validity, reliability, safety, security, resilience, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy and fairness. Those requirements would become control objectives, assessments, testing, evidence and monitoring. Finally, I would integrate the results into the enterprise risk process, management reporting and continuous improvement."**

---

# 199. Key Interview Question — Is Trustworthy AI Only a Technical Responsibility?

A strong answer:

> **"No. Technical teams implement many of the controls, but trustworthy AI is an enterprise governance responsibility. The business defines the purpose and accepts business risk, GRC establishes governance and oversight, cybersecurity manages security risks, privacy manages data protection requirements, legal and compliance interpret applicable obligations, and independent assurance can evaluate the effectiveness of the framework."**

---

# 200. Key Interview Question — How Would You Assess a High-Risk AI System?

A strong answer:

> **"I would begin with the intended purpose and potential impact, then classify the AI system according to the organization's risk methodology. I would assess the relevant trustworthiness characteristics, including performance, safety, security, privacy, fairness, explainability and resilience. I would identify control requirements, conduct testing, document residual risks and obtain approval from the appropriate risk owner. After deployment, I would establish monitoring and reassessment triggers."**

---

# 201. Key Interview Question — How Do You Demonstrate That AI Is Trustworthy?

A strong answer:

> **"I would not demonstrate trustworthiness through a single certification or test. I would build an evidence chain from requirements to risks, controls, testing, monitoring and management decisions. The evidence should demonstrate that the system is fit for purpose, performs within defined thresholds, has appropriate safety and security controls, protects relevant data, manages applicable fairness risks, has clear accountability and can be monitored throughout its lifecycle."**

# 204. Final Trustworthiness Checklist

Before approving an AI system, a GRC professional should be able to ask:

### Purpose

* Is the intended purpose clearly defined?
* Is the system fit for that purpose?

### Performance

* Is performance measured?
* Are acceptable thresholds defined?
* Is degradation monitored?

### Safety

* What harms could occur?
* Are safety boundaries defined?
* Is human intervention available where necessary?

### Security

* Are relevant AI-specific threats assessed?
* Are models, data and infrastructure protected?
* Are security incidents monitored?

### Resilience

* What happens if the AI fails?
* Is there a fallback?
* Has recovery been tested?

### Accountability

* Who owns the system?
* Who owns the risk?
* Who can approve or stop it?

### Transparency

* What should users and stakeholders know?
* Is the system appropriately documented?

### Explainability

* Can relevant outputs be meaningfully explained?
* Can humans challenge AI recommendations?

### Privacy

* What personal information is processed?
* Is collection minimized?
* Are access and retention controlled?

### Fairness

* Could the system create harmful disparities?
* Has relevant testing been performed?
* Is fairness monitored after deployment?
