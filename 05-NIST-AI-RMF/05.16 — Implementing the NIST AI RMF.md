# 05.16 — AI Risk Monitoring and Continuous Improvement

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Monitoring

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Monitoring

AI risk monitoring is the continuous or periodic process of observing an AI system, its environment, its controls, and its risk indicators to determine whether the system continues to operate within approved risk boundaries.

AI risk management cannot end when an AI system is approved for production.

AI systems can change because of:

* Model updates.
* Training or operational data changes.
* New users.
* New integrations.
* Changes in business processes.
* Changes in threat activity.
* Changes in regulations.
* Changes in AI provider behavior.
* Changes in system configuration.
* Changes in the level of automation or autonomy.

Therefore, AI risk monitoring provides the ongoing visibility necessary to identify when previously acceptable risks become unacceptable.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Risk Monitoring

The primary purpose of AI risk monitoring is to determine whether:

* Identified risks remain within acceptable levels.
* Risk treatment remains effective.
* Controls continue to operate as intended.
* AI performance remains within approved boundaries.
* New risks have emerged.
* Existing assumptions remain valid.
* Risk indicators show deterioration.
* Changes require reassessment.
* Escalation or intervention is necessary.

Monitoring therefore connects operational activity with continuing AI risk governance.

---

### 3. Monitoring as a Continuous Risk Management Activity

A simplified monitoring cycle is:

```text
AI System in Operation
        ↓
Observe
        ↓
Measure
        ↓
Analyze
        ↓
Identify Changes
        ↓
Assess Risk
        ↓
Respond
        ↓
Improve
        ↓
Continue Monitoring
```

The process should operate throughout the AI system lifecycle.

---

### 4. Monitoring Versus One-Time Assessment

An AI risk assessment provides a point-in-time understanding of risk.

Monitoring provides continuing visibility.

For example:

```text
Risk Assessment:
Risk is Moderate
        ↓
AI System Deployed
        ↓
Monitoring
        ↓
Performance Declines
        ↓
Risk Increases
        ↓
Reassessment
```

Without monitoring, the organization may continue relying on an outdated risk assessment.

---

### 5. Monitoring Versus Risk Assessment

These activities are related but different.

**Risk assessment** asks:

> What could go wrong, how likely is it, and what could be the impact?

**Risk monitoring** asks:

> Is the risk changing, and are the controls and assumptions still valid?

Monitoring can therefore trigger a new risk assessment when significant changes are detected.

---

### 6. Monitoring Versus Control Testing

Control testing determines whether a specific control is functioning effectively.

Risk monitoring has a broader scope.

For example:

```text
Risk Monitoring
   ├── AI Performance
   ├── Security
   ├── Privacy
   ├── Model Behavior
   ├── Controls
   ├── Threats
   └── Business Context
```

Control testing is therefore one component of a broader monitoring capability.

---

### 7. Monitoring Scope

AI risk monitoring may cover several dimensions:

| Monitoring Area | Examples                                      |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Security        | Attacks, unauthorized access, vulnerabilities |
| Privacy         | Data exposure, inappropriate processing       |
| Performance     | Accuracy, reliability, latency                |
| Model behavior  | Drift, unexpected outputs                     |
| Governance      | Policy violations, exceptions                 |
| Human oversight | Overrides, approvals, escalation              |
| Operations      | Availability, failures                        |
| Compliance      | Regulatory or contractual obligations         |
| Third parties   | Provider performance and incidents            |

The scope should be proportionate to the AI system's risk.

---

### 8. Risk-Based Monitoring

Not every AI system requires the same level of monitoring.

A practical model is:

```text
Low Risk
→ Routine Monitoring

Moderate Risk
→ Periodic Monitoring

High Risk
→ Enhanced Monitoring

Critical Risk
→ Continuous / Near-Real-Time Monitoring
```

The exact monitoring requirements should be defined by organizational policy and risk methodology.

---

### 9. Monitoring Objectives Should Be Defined Before Deployment

Monitoring requirements should ideally be established before the AI system enters production.

The organization should define:

* What will be monitored?
* Why will it be monitored?
* Who owns monitoring?
* How frequently will it occur?
* What thresholds apply?
* What constitutes an alert?
* Who investigates alerts?
* What actions are triggered?
* How is evidence retained?

This prevents monitoring from becoming an afterthought.

---

### 10. Monitoring Requirements

A monitoring requirement should be specific enough to operate consistently.

Example:

**Objective:**

Detect unauthorized AI agent actions.

**Indicator:**

Unauthorized tool invocation attempts.

**Threshold:**

Any confirmed unauthorized execution attempt.

**Owner:**

AI Security Operations.

**Response:**

Investigate and, where necessary, suspend the agent.

---

### 11. Monitoring Data Sources

AI risk monitoring may use multiple sources.

Examples include:

* Application logs.
* Model logs.
* Security logs.
* API gateways.
* Identity systems.
* SIEM platforms.
* DLP systems.
* Model monitoring platforms.
* User reports.
* Customer complaints.
* Incident-management systems.
* Audit findings.

Effective monitoring often requires correlating information from multiple sources.

---

### 12. AI Telemetry

Telemetry is the collection of operational information generated by an AI system.

Telemetry may include:

* Requests.
* Responses.
* Errors.
* Latency.
* Tool calls.
* Model versions.
* User identity.
* System identity.
* Data-access events.
* Policy decisions.

Telemetry provides the evidence required for monitoring and investigation.

---

### 13. AI Monitoring Architecture

A simplified architecture may look like:

```text
AI Application
      ↓
AI Model
      ↓
Tools / APIs
      ↓
Operational Telemetry
      ↓
Monitoring Platform
      ↓
Analytics
      ↓
Alerts
      ↓
Risk / Security / Operations
```

The architecture should provide sufficient visibility for the risk level of the system.

---

### 14. Monitoring Data Quality

Monitoring itself depends on reliable data.

Poor telemetry can result from:

* Missing logs.
* Incorrect timestamps.
* Incomplete event information.
* Incorrect user identification.
* Unmonitored components.
* Logging failures.

Therefore, organizations should also assess the quality and completeness of monitoring data.

---

### 15. Logging Coverage

An organization should understand which AI activities are actually logged.

For example:

```text
User Request       → Logged
Model Response     → Logged
Tool Invocation    → Logged
Administrative Change → Logged
Security Event     → Logged
```

If important events are not logged, the organization may have monitoring blind spots.

---

### 16. Monitoring Blind Spots

A monitoring blind spot occurs when the organization cannot sufficiently observe an important aspect of AI behavior.

Examples include:

* Unlogged AI actions.
* Unmonitored APIs.
* Unknown AI applications.
* Untracked model changes.
* Unobserved third-party processing.

Blind spots can create significant governance risk.

---

### 17. Shadow AI Monitoring

Organizations should consider monitoring for unauthorized AI usage.

Shadow AI can occur when employees use:

* Unauthorized AI applications.
* Personal AI accounts.
* Unapproved APIs.
* External AI platforms.
* AI browser extensions.

The objective is not simply to identify unauthorized tools but to understand the associated risk.

---

### 18. AI Asset Discovery

Effective monitoring requires knowledge of the AI environment.

Organizations should maintain visibility into:

```text
AI Models
AI Applications
AI Agents
AI APIs
AI Providers
AI Data Sources
AI Integrations
AI Users
```

An unknown AI system cannot be reliably governed.

---

### 19. AI Inventory Monitoring

The AI inventory itself should be monitored for changes.

Examples include:

* New AI applications.
* Retired systems.
* New models.
* New providers.
* New integrations.
* Changes in system owners.
* Changes in classification.

Inventory changes may trigger risk assessment activities.

---

### 20. Monitoring Model Versions

The organization should know which model version is operating in production.

For example:

```text
Production
Model v2.4

Provider Update
        ↓
Model v2.5

Change Detected
        ↓
Risk Review
        ↓
Testing
        ↓
Approval
```

Unexpected model changes should be investigated.

---

### 21. Model Drift Monitoring

Model drift occurs when AI behavior or performance changes over time.

Monitoring may identify:

* Reduced accuracy.
* Increased error rates.
* Changes in prediction distributions.
* Increased false positives.
* Increased false negatives.
* Changes in output characteristics.

Drift does not automatically mean the model has failed, but it should trigger appropriate analysis.

---

### 22. Data Drift Monitoring

Data drift occurs when operational input data changes.

For example:

```text
Training Data
Customer Behavior A
Customer Behavior B
Customer Behavior C

Operational Data
Customer Behavior A
Customer Behavior D
Customer Behavior E
```

The change may affect model performance and therefore risk.

---

### 23. Concept Drift Monitoring

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

For example, a model may have been trained under one market environment and later operate under substantially different conditions.

Monitoring should determine whether the model's underlying assumptions remain valid.

---

### 24. Performance Monitoring

AI performance monitoring may include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* Error rate.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Latency.
* Availability.
* Throughput.

The appropriate measures depend on the AI system's intended purpose.

---

### 25. Risk-Based Performance Monitoring

Performance metrics should be connected to risk.

For example:

```text
Accuracy Declines
      ↓
Customer Impact Increases
      ↓
Risk Increases
```

A small technical performance change may become significant if the AI system is used for a high-impact decision.

---

### 26. Threshold Monitoring

Monitoring can use predefined thresholds.

Example:

```text
Model Error Rate

< 2%       → Normal
2–5%       → Review
> 5%       → Escalation
```

Thresholds should be established using appropriate evidence and risk tolerance.

---

### 27. Thresholds Should Be Contextual

A threshold appropriate for one AI system may be inappropriate for another.

For example:

```text
Marketing Recommendation AI
→ Higher tolerance for errors

Medical Decision Support
→ Much lower tolerance
```

Monitoring thresholds should therefore reflect the consequences of failure.

---

### 28. Monitoring AI Accuracy

Accuracy monitoring should consider the intended use.

A model can achieve high overall accuracy while performing poorly for a specific population or scenario.

Therefore, monitoring may need to examine:

* Overall performance.
* Segment performance.
* Edge cases.
* High-impact cases.
* Known failure scenarios.

---

### 29. Monitoring Fairness

Where fairness is relevant, monitoring may examine whether outcomes change across relevant groups or contexts.

Potential indicators include:

* Error-rate differences.
* Outcome differences.
* Representation changes.
* Distribution changes.

Fairness monitoring should use appropriate definitions and context rather than assuming one universal fairness metric.

---

### 30. Monitoring for Bias

Bias may emerge after deployment because of:

* New data.
* Changing populations.
* Changing behavior.
* Model updates.
* Feedback loops.

Therefore, bias monitoring may need to continue throughout the operational lifecycle.

---

### 31. Monitoring Explainability

Where explainability is a control requirement, organizations can monitor whether required explanations remain available and useful.

Examples include:

* Explanation generation success.
* Explanation completeness.
* Human reviewer usability.
* Documentation availability.

---

### 32. Monitoring Human Oversight

Human oversight should be monitored rather than assumed.

Possible indicators include:

* Number of human approvals.
* Override rates.
* Escalation rates.
* Review duration.
* Rejection rates.
* Missed reviews.
* Unauthorized bypasses.

These indicators can reveal whether human oversight is functioning as designed.

---

### 33. Human Override Monitoring

A high override rate may indicate that:

* The AI is performing poorly.
* Business rules are inappropriate.
* Users do not trust the AI.
* The AI is being used outside its intended purpose.

Therefore, override activity can provide useful risk signals.

---

### 34. Automation Bias Monitoring

Human reviewers may become overly dependent on AI recommendations.

Monitoring should consider whether users:

* Routinely approve AI recommendations.
* Rarely challenge outputs.
* Ignore warning indicators.
* Accept AI recommendations without sufficient verification.

Human oversight is only meaningful when humans can and do exercise independent judgment.

---

### 35. Monitoring AI Autonomy

For autonomous AI systems, monitoring should include:

* Actions initiated by the AI.
* Tools accessed.
* Transactions executed.
* Permissions used.
* Escalations.
* Human interventions.
* Failed actions.

Greater autonomy generally requires stronger operational visibility.

---

### 36. Agentic AI Monitoring

AI agents can perform sequences of actions rather than simply generate content.

Monitoring may therefore track:

```text
Goal
 ↓
Planning
 ↓
Tool Selection
 ↓
Tool Invocation
 ↓
Result
 ↓
Next Action
```

This provides visibility into the agent's operational behavior.

---

### 37. Tool-Use Monitoring

For AI agents, each tool invocation may be important.

Monitoring can capture:

* Which tool was called.
* Who initiated the request.
* Which identity was used.
* What parameters were supplied.
* Whether the action was authorized.
* What result was returned.

This supports accountability and investigation.

---

### 38. Privilege Monitoring

AI systems should be monitored for unnecessary or abnormal privilege use.

Examples include:

* Administrative access.
* Unusual database queries.
* Unexpected API access.
* Access to restricted data.
* Attempts to bypass authorization.

Abnormal privilege activity can indicate misuse or compromise.

---

### 39. Data Access Monitoring

Monitoring should determine whether AI systems access data consistent with their approved purpose.

For example:

```text
Approved:
Customer Ticket Data

Unexpected:
Payroll Records
```

Unexpected access should generate an investigation or alert according to risk.

---

### 40. Data Leakage Monitoring

Potential leakage indicators include:

* Sensitive information in outputs.
* Secrets in prompts.
* Confidential data sent to external providers.
* Restricted data appearing in logs.
* Unauthorized data transfers.

Data-loss prevention mechanisms can support this monitoring.

---

### 41. Prompt Monitoring

Where appropriate and lawful, organizations may monitor prompt metadata or content to identify:

* Sensitive information.
* Malicious instructions.
* Policy violations.
* Unauthorized use cases.
* Prompt injection attempts.

Monitoring should respect applicable privacy and data-protection requirements.

---

### 42. Output Monitoring

Output monitoring can identify:

* Harmful content.
* Sensitive information.
* Policy violations.
* Unsupported claims.
* Abnormal behavior.
* High-risk recommendations.

Output monitoring is especially important where AI results are consumed by downstream systems.

---

### 43. Hallucination Monitoring

Organizations can establish mechanisms to identify unreliable AI-generated information.

Potential approaches include:

* Fact verification.
* Retrieval validation.
* Rule-based checks.
* Human review.
* Source attribution.
* Confidence thresholds.

The appropriate method depends on the AI system's purpose and consequences.

---

### 44. Hallucination as a Risk Signal

A hallucination is not automatically a material risk.

Its significance depends on context.

For example:

```text
Creative Writing
→ Low Consequence

Internal Business Summary
→ Moderate Consequence

Legal / Financial Decision Support
→ Potentially High Consequence
```

Monitoring should therefore connect model behavior to potential impact.

---

### 45. Security Event Monitoring

AI environments should integrate with security monitoring where appropriate.

Security monitoring may detect:

* Unauthorized access.
* Credential abuse.
* Prompt injection.
* Data exfiltration.
* Malicious API calls.
* Suspicious tool usage.
* Infrastructure attacks.

AI monitoring and cybersecurity monitoring should complement each other.

---

### 46. AI Security Operations

Security operations may use AI-specific telemetry alongside traditional security telemetry.

```text
AI Logs
+
Identity Logs
+
Network Logs
+
Endpoint Logs
+
API Logs
        ↓
Security Analytics
        ↓
Detection
        ↓
Investigation
```

This improves visibility across the AI environment.

---

### 47. Monitoring AI Supply Chain Risk

Third-party AI components should also be monitored.

Examples include:

* Provider incidents.
* Model updates.
* Service changes.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Security advisories.
* Availability.
* Contract compliance.

Third-party risk does not end after vendor onboarding.

---

### 48. Provider Change Monitoring

An external AI provider may change:

* Model behavior.
* Model version.
* Terms.
* Data processing.
* Security controls.
* Infrastructure.
* API functionality.

Material provider changes should trigger appropriate review.

---

### 49. Regulatory Change Monitoring

AI-related obligations can change over time.

Organizations should monitor:

* New laws.
* Regulations.
* Regulatory guidance.
* Standards.
* Contractual requirements.
* Industry expectations.

A regulatory change may alter the organization's AI risk treatment requirements.

---

### 50. Monitoring as an Early-Warning System

The ultimate purpose of monitoring is not merely to collect data.

It is to provide enough timely information for the organization to:

* Detect deterioration.
* Investigate emerging risks.
* Escalate material issues.
* Modify controls.
* Reassess risk.
* Intervene before significant harm occurs.

Effective AI risk monitoring therefore turns operational evidence into actionable risk intelligence.
