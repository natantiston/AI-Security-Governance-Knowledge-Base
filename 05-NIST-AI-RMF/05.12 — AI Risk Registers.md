# 05.12 — AI Risk Registers

## Part 1 — Foundations, Purpose, Structure, and Governance of AI Risk Registers

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Registers

An **AI Risk Register** is a structured record used to document, track, assess, assign, treat, and monitor risks associated with artificial intelligence systems.

It provides a centralized view of identified AI risks and enables the organization to answer fundamental governance questions:

* What AI risks have been identified?
* Which AI systems are affected?
* How significant are those risks?
* Who owns them?
* What controls are in place?
* What treatment is required?
* What residual risk remains?
* When was the risk last reviewed?
* What evidence supports the assessment?

An AI risk register therefore acts as an operational bridge between **AI risk identification** and **ongoing AI risk management**.

---

### 2. Purpose of an AI Risk Register

The primary purpose of an AI risk register is to create a consistent and traceable mechanism for managing AI-related risk.

It should support:

* Risk visibility.
* Risk ownership.
* Risk prioritization.
* Risk treatment.
* Management reporting.
* Control tracking.
* Risk acceptance.
* Auditability.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Governance decisions.

A risk register should not merely be a list of problems.

It should function as an **active management instrument**.

---

### 3. AI Risk Register in the NIST AI RMF

The AI risk register supports multiple functions of the NIST AI RMF.

```text
GOVERN
Policies, accountability, roles
        ↓
MAP
Context and risk identification
        ↓
MEASURE
Risk analysis and evidence
        ↓
MANAGE
Prioritization and treatment
        ↓
Risk Register
        ↺
Continuous Review
```

The register provides a persistent record that allows information generated through the different functions to remain connected.

---

### 4. Risk Register Versus Risk Assessment

A **risk assessment** evaluates risk.

A **risk register** records and manages the results.

For example:

```text
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Likelihood = High
Impact = High
       ↓
Risk Rating = High
       ↓
AI Risk Register
       ↓
Owner + Controls + Treatment + Status
```

The assessment is therefore an analytical activity, while the register provides an ongoing governance record.

---

### 5. Risk Register Versus AI Inventory

An **AI inventory** answers:

> What AI systems do we have?

An **AI risk register** answers:

> What risks are associated with those AI systems?

They should be connected.

```text
AI Inventory
     ↓
AI System
     ↓
AI Risk Profile
     ↓
AI Risk Register
```

One AI system may have multiple risks.

One risk may also affect multiple AI systems if the underlying issue is common.

---

### 6. AI Risk Register Versus Enterprise Risk Register

An enterprise risk register normally contains risks across the entire organization.

An AI risk register focuses specifically on risks associated with AI systems and AI-enabled processes.

For example:

| Enterprise Risk    | AI-Specific Risk                   |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Cybersecurity Risk | Prompt Injection                   |
| Data Privacy Risk  | AI Disclosure of Personal Data     |
| Operational Risk   | AI Model Failure                   |
| Third-Party Risk   | External Model Provider Dependency |
| Compliance Risk    | Non-compliant AI Use               |
| Reputational Risk  | Harmful AI Output                  |

AI risks may eventually be consolidated or linked to broader enterprise risks.

---

### 7. Why Organizations Need a Dedicated AI Risk Register

Traditional enterprise risk registers may not contain enough detail to manage AI-specific characteristics.

AI systems introduce considerations such as:

* Model behavior.
* Training data.
* Inference data.
* Model drift.
* Hallucination.
* Prompt injection.
* AI agents.
* Human-AI interaction.
* Explainability.
* Fairness.
* AI supply chains.

A dedicated AI risk register can capture these characteristics while remaining integrated with enterprise GRC.

---

### 8. Centralized Versus Distributed Risk Registers

Organizations can structure AI risk registers in different ways.

### Centralized Model

One enterprise AI risk register is maintained.

```text
Business A ─┐
Business B ─┼→ Enterprise AI Risk Register
Business C ─┤
Business D ─┘
```

Advantages:

* Central visibility.
* Consistent taxonomy.
* Easier reporting.
* Easier executive oversight.

---

### 9. Distributed Model

Each business unit maintains its own AI risk register.

```text
Business A → AI Risk Register
Business B → AI Risk Register
Business C → AI Risk Register
```

Advantages may include:

* Greater business ownership.
* Local context.
* Easier operational management.

However, decentralized registers can create inconsistent terminology, duplicated risks, and fragmented reporting.

---

### 10. Federated Model

A common approach for large organizations is a **federated model**.

```text
Business Unit A ─┐
Business Unit B ─┼→ Central AI Governance
Business Unit C ─┤
Business Unit D ─┘
```

Business units maintain operational responsibility while central governance establishes:

* Common taxonomy.
* Minimum documentation requirements.
* Risk methodology.
* Reporting standards.
* Escalation criteria.

This provides both local ownership and enterprise visibility.

---

### 11. Scope of the AI Risk Register

The organization should define what belongs in the AI risk register.

Potential scope includes:

* AI models.
* AI applications.
* AI-enabled business processes.
* Generative AI.
* Machine-learning systems.
* AI agents.
* Third-party AI services.
* Embedded AI capabilities.
* AI APIs.
* AI development environments.

The scope should be aligned with the organization's AI governance policy.

---

### 12. Risk Inclusion Criteria

Not every observation needs to become a formal risk-register entry.

Organizations should define criteria for inclusion.

Examples include:

* Material business impact.
* Significant security exposure.
* Privacy implications.
* Regulatory relevance.
* Safety implications.
* High stakeholder impact.
* Risk above defined thresholds.
* Management attention required.

Low-level observations may instead be handled through operational issue-management processes.

---

### 13. Risk Register Entry

A basic AI risk-register entry might look like:

```text
Risk ID: AI-RISK-001
AI System: Customer-Service AI
Risk: Unauthorized disclosure of customer information
Category: Privacy / Cybersecurity
Likelihood: Medium
Impact: High
Risk Rating: High
Owner: Customer Operations
Controls: RBAC, DLP, Monitoring
Treatment: Implement data-level authorization
Status: In Progress
Review Date: 30 September 2026
```

This provides a concise management view while allowing detailed supporting evidence to exist elsewhere.

---

### 14. Unique Risk Identifier

Every risk should have a unique identifier.

Example:

```text
AI-RISK-001
AI-RISK-002
AI-RISK-003
```

A structured identifier may also include business or risk categories.

Example:

```text
AI-SEC-001
AI-PRIV-002
AI-OPS-003
```

The numbering convention should remain stable and documented.

---

### 15. Risk Title

The risk title should be short but meaningful.

Good:

> Unauthorized AI Data Disclosure

Weak:

> AI Problem

Better titles allow management to understand the issue quickly.

---

### 16. Risk Description

The detailed description should explain:

* What could happen.
* Why it could happen.
* What the consequence could be.

Example:

> Excessive permissions granted to an AI assistant could allow users to retrieve information outside their authorized business scope, resulting in unauthorized disclosure of confidential information.

---

### 17. AI System Association

Each risk should identify the affected AI system.

For example:

```text
AI System ID:
AI-CRM-004

AI System:
Customer-Service Assistant

Business Process:
Customer Support
```

This enables risk analysis by system, business unit, or process.

---

### 18. Risk Category

A consistent taxonomy helps classify risks.

Possible categories include:

* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Safety.
* Fairness.
* Reliability.
* Transparency.
* Explainability.
* Compliance.
* Operational.
* Strategic.
* Third-Party.
* Reputational.
* Human Factors.

A risk may belong to multiple categories where the GRC platform supports multi-classification.

---

### 19. Risk Cause

The register should identify the underlying cause where practical.

Examples:

* Weak authorization.
* Poor data quality.
* Insufficient testing.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Excessive AI permissions.
* Incomplete governance.
* Vendor dependency.

Understanding the cause helps determine treatment.

---

### 20. Risk Event

The risk event describes what could occur.

Examples:

* Unauthorized data access.
* Incorrect AI recommendation.
* Model compromise.
* AI service outage.
* Harmful AI output.
* Unauthorized automated transaction.

The event should be clearly distinguished from its cause and consequence.

---

### 21. Risk Consequence

The register should identify potential consequences.

Examples:

* Financial loss.
* Customer harm.
* Privacy violation.
* Regulatory action.
* Operational disruption.
* Reputational damage.
* Intellectual-property loss.

Consequences support impact assessment.

---

### 22. Affected Stakeholders

The register may identify stakeholders affected by the risk.

Examples:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Suppliers.
* Business partners.
* Regulators.
* Members of the public.

This is particularly important for AI systems that can affect individuals indirectly.

---

### 23. Risk Owner

Each risk should have an accountable owner.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is appropriately:

* Assessed.
* Treated.
* Monitored.
* Escalated.
* Accepted where authorized.

Risk ownership should not be ambiguous.

---

### 24. Control Owner

The **control owner** is responsible for operating or maintaining a specific control.

For example:

```text
Risk Owner:
Customer Operations Director

Control Owner:
IAM Manager
```

These roles can be different.

The business owner may own the risk while a technical team operates the control.

---

### 25. Inherent Risk

The register should capture inherent risk where the organization's methodology requires it.

Example:

```text
Likelihood: High
Impact: High

Inherent Risk:
Critical
```

This represents exposure before considering existing controls.

---

### 26. Existing Controls

The register should identify relevant controls.

Examples:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Data-loss prevention.
* Encryption.
* Human review.
* Output validation.
* Security testing.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Vendor controls.

Controls should be linked to the specific risk they address.

---

### 27. Control Effectiveness

A control should not automatically be assumed effective.

Possible ratings include:

* Effective.
* Partially Effective.
* Ineffective.
* Not Tested.
* Not Applicable.

The organization's methodology should define these terms.

---

### 28. Residual Risk

Residual risk represents the remaining exposure after considering controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk:
High

Existing Controls:
Effective

Residual Risk:
Medium
```

The residual risk should be compared with the organization's risk appetite and tolerance.

---

### 29. Risk Treatment

The register should record the selected treatment.

Possible values include:

* Avoid.
* Mitigate.
* Transfer.
* Accept.

Treatment should be supported by a documented decision.

---

### 30. Treatment Action

Where mitigation is selected, the register should track specific actions.

Example:

```text
Treatment:
Implement AI data-level authorization

Action Owner:
IAM Team

Target Date:
30 September 2026

Status:
In Progress
```

This transforms risk management from documentation into execution.

---

### 31. Risk Status

The register should clearly indicate the current state of the risk.

Typical statuses include:

```text
Identified
   ↓
Under Assessment
   ↓
Open
   ↓
Treatment in Progress
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Accepted / Closed
```

Status definitions should be standardized.

---

### 32. Risk Due Date

Where treatment is required, a target date should be documented.

This allows management to identify overdue risks and remediation actions.

For example:

```text
Treatment Due:
30 September 2026

Current Date:
26 August 2026

Status:
On Track
```

---

### 33. Risk Review Date

The review date determines when the risk should next be reassessed.

The review schedule may depend on:

* Risk severity.
* AI lifecycle stage.
* Regulatory requirements.
* System change frequency.
* Business criticality.

High-risk systems may require more frequent reviews.

---

### 34. Risk Evidence

The register should reference supporting evidence.

Examples:

* Risk assessment.
* Architecture diagram.
* Threat model.
* Test results.
* Audit finding.
* Incident report.
* Vendor assessment.
* Control evidence.

The register does not necessarily need to contain all evidence directly; it should maintain traceability to the evidence repository.

---

### 35. Risk Source

The register should record how the risk was identified.

Examples:

```text
Security Assessment
Privacy Assessment
Risk Workshop
Audit
Incident
Red Team
Monitoring
Vendor Assessment
Regulatory Review
```

This provides useful context and supports trend analysis.

---

### 36. Risk Identification Date

The date the risk was first identified should be recorded.

This allows organizations to measure:

* Risk age.
* Time to treatment.
* Time to closure.
* Persistent risk exposure.

---

### 37. Risk Age

Risk age is the amount of time a risk has remained open.

Example:

```text
Risk Identified:
1 January

Current Date:
26 August

Risk Age:
237 days
```

Long-open high risks may require management escalation.

---

### 38. Risk Escalation Flag

The register can include a field indicating whether escalation is required.

Example:

```text
Risk Rating:
Critical

Escalation:
YES

Escalation Level:
Executive Risk Committee
```

This improves management visibility.

---

### 39. Risk Acceptance Information

Where risk is accepted, the register should capture:

* Acceptance decision.
* Approver.
* Acceptance date.
* Expiration or review date.
* Business justification.
* Conditions.

Example:

```text
Decision:
Risk Accepted

Approved By:
Business Executive

Review:
Quarterly
```

---

### 40. Risk Exception Information

If a control requirement is temporarily not met, the register may reference an associated exception.

Example:

```text
Risk:
AI Logging Gap

Exception:
EXC-AI-004

Expiration:
30 September 2026
```

This allows risk and exception management to remain connected.

---

### 41. Risk Dependencies

Some AI risks depend on other risks or controls.

Examples:

```text
AI Data Risk
      ↓
IAM Control
      ↓
Identity Platform Availability
```

The register should capture important dependencies where they materially affect risk treatment.

---

### 42. Risk Relationships

AI risks may also be related to:

* Enterprise risks.
* Cybersecurity risks.
* Privacy risks.
* Vendor risks.
* Business continuity risks.
* Compliance risks.

Example:


### 49. Risk Register and Risk Appetite

The register should help determine whether residual risk is within organizational appetite.

Example:

```text
Residual Risk:
High

Risk Appetite:
Low

Decision:
Treatment Required
```

If:

```text
Residual Risk:
Low

Risk Appetite:
Low

Decision:
Risk Within Appetite
```

The register therefore supports risk-based decision-making.

---

### 50. Risk Register and Risk Tolerance

Risk appetite defines the organization's general willingness to accept risk.

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable variation around a specific risk or objective.

The register may capture tolerance thresholds such as:

```text
Maximum Acceptable AI Error Rate:
2%

Current:
4%

Status:
Outside Tolerance
```

This provides a measurable trigger for management action.

---

### 51. Risk Register Governance Rules

An organization should define rules for:

* Who can create risks.
* Who can modify risks.
* Who can approve risks.
* Who can close risks.
* Who can accept risks.
* Who can access the register.
* How often risks are reviewed.
* How records are retained.

This prevents uncontrolled changes to risk information.

---

### 52. Segregation of Duties

Risk-register governance should consider separation between:

* Risk identification.
* Risk ownership.
* Control operation.
* Independent review.
* Risk acceptance.

For example:

```text
Business
   ↓
Owns Risk

Security / GRC
   ↓
Provides Challenge

Internal Audit
   ↓
Provides Independent Assurance
```

This reduces conflicts of interest.

---

### 53. Risk Register Access Control

The register may contain sensitive information.

Access should therefore follow least privilege.

Potential roles include:

* Risk contributor.
* Risk owner.
* GRC administrator.
* Executive viewer.
* Auditor.

Not every user needs permission to modify risk records.

---

### 54. Risk Register Audit Trail

Changes to risk records should ideally be traceable.

The system should be able to show:

* Who changed the risk.
* What changed.
* When it changed.
* Previous value.
* New value.

Example:

```text
Risk Rating:
Medium → High

Changed By:
GRC Manager

Date:
26 August 2026

Reason:
New security assessment identified additional exposure.
```

This supports accountability and auditability.

---

### 55. Data Quality in the AI Risk Register

A risk register is only useful when its information is reliable.

Organizations should monitor:

* Missing owners.
* Missing ratings.
* Overdue reviews.
* Incomplete treatment plans.
* Duplicate risks.
* Invalid statuses.
* Outdated evidence.

Data-quality controls should therefore be part of register governance.

---

### 56. Duplicate Risk Management

Duplicate risks can distort the organization's view of exposure.

For example:

```text
Risk A:
AI Data Leakage

Risk B:
Unauthorized AI Information Disclosure

Risk C:
AI Confidentiality Breach
```

These may represent the same underlying risk.

A governance process should identify and consolidate duplicates where appropriate.

Possible systems include:

* Enterprise GRC platform.

Spreadsheets may be appropriate for smaller or early-stage programs but can become difficult to control at enterprise scale.

Organizations should select the implementation approach according to scale and maturity.

### 65. Common AI Risk Register Mistakes

Organizations should avoid:

1. Creating a register without clear ownership.
2. Recording vague risks.
3. Failing to connect risks to AI systems.
4. Treating the register as a static document.
5. Not documenting residual risk.
6. Not tracking treatment actions.
7. Allowing risks to remain open indefinitely.
8. Failing to update risks after AI changes.
9. Creating duplicate risks.
10. Treating controls as automatically effective.
11. Allowing uncontrolled editing.
12. Ignoring evidence.
13. Reporting only risk counts without risk severity.
14. Separating AI risks completely from enterprise GRC.
15. Using the register only for audit preparation.

---

### 66. Characteristics of a Mature AI Risk Register

A mature AI risk register should be:

**Complete**

Material AI risks are captured.

**Accurate**

Risk information reflects the actual environment.

**Current**

Risk records are regularly reviewed.

**Traceable**

Risks connect to systems, controls, evidence, and decisions.

**Owned**

Every material risk has accountability.

**Actionable**

Treatment actions are clearly defined.

**Auditable**

Changes and approvals can be reconstructed.

**Integrated**

AI risk information connects to enterprise governance.

```text
      ↓
```

---

### 68. Core Principle

The AI risk register should not be viewed as a spreadsheet containing risk descriptions.

It should be viewed as a **controlled governance mechanism that connects risk identification, ownership, assessment, controls, treatment, evidence, decision-making, and continuous monitoring**.

A mature AI risk register therefore transforms:

> **"We identified an AI risk."**

into:

> **"We know what the risk is, where it exists, who owns it, how significant it is, what controls address it, what residual exposure remains, what treatment is required, who approved the decision, and how the risk will be monitored."**

# 05.12 — AI Risk Registers

## Part 2 — AI Risk Register Design, Risk Fields, Scoring, Ownership, and Control Mapping

### 69. Designing an AI Risk Register

An AI risk register should be designed around the decisions the organization needs to make.

The register should allow stakeholders to move from:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Risk
    ↓
Assessment
    ↓
Control
    ↓
Treatment
    ↓
Residual Risk
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Monitoring
```

A well-designed register therefore captures more than a risk description. It provides the information necessary to manage the risk throughout its lifecycle.

---

### 70. Risk Register Data Architecture

A mature AI risk register can be structured into several information groups:

```text
1. Identification
2. AI System Context
3. Risk Description
4. Risk Assessment
5. Controls
6. Treatment
7. Ownership
8. Evidence
9. Approval
10. Monitoring
11. Review History
```

Separating these information groups improves consistency and makes the register easier to integrate with GRC platforms.

---

### 71. Risk Identification Fields

The identification section may contain:

* Risk ID.
* Risk title.
* Risk description.
* Risk category.
* Risk source.
* Date identified.
* AI system ID.
* Business process.
* Lifecycle stage.

Example:

```text
Risk ID: AI-RISK-024
Title: Unauthorized AI Data Disclosure
Category: Privacy / Cybersecurity
Source: Security Assessment
Identified: 26 August 2026
```

---

### 72. AI System Context Fields

The register should establish the AI context.

Possible fields include:

* AI system name.
* AI system owner.
* Business owner.
* AI capability.
* Model/provider.
* Deployment environment.
* Business criticality.
* User population.
* Data classification.

This makes the risk understandable without requiring the reader to investigate the entire AI inventory.

---

### 73. Business Process Association

The register should identify the business process affected by the AI risk.

Examples:

* Customer service.
* Fraud detection.
* Recruitment.
* Finance.
* Marketing.
* Network operations.
* Security operations.

This allows management to understand the business consequence of the risk.

---

### 74. AI Lifecycle Association

The risk should be associated with the relevant lifecycle stage.

Example:

```text

    ↓

    ↓
```

### 81. Risk Scoring

Organizations need a consistent method to determine risk significance.

A basic model is:

```text
Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact
```

For example:

```text
Likelihood = 4
Impact = 5

Risk Score = 20
```

The organization should define what the resulting score means.

---

### 82. Example Five-by-Five Risk Matrix

A common model uses:

* Likelihood: 1–5.
* Impact: 1–5.

| Impact ↓ / Likelihood → |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |
| ----------------------- | -: | -: | -: | -: | -: |
| 5                       |  5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 |
| 4                       |  4 |  8 | 12 | 16 | 20 |
| 3                       |  3 |  6 |  9 | 12 | 15 |
| 2                       |  2 |  4 |  6 |  8 | 10 |
| 1                       |  1 |  2 |  3 |  4 |  5 |

The organization then defines thresholds for categories such as:

```text
1–4     Low
5–9     Medium
10–14   High
15–25   Critical
```

These values are illustrative and should be replaced by the organization's approved methodology.

### 87. Likelihood Assessment

Factors may include:

* Exposure.
* Threat capability.
* Vulnerability.
* Existing controls.
* Historical incidents.
* Frequency of use.
* User population.
* Attack complexity.

Avoid assigning likelihood solely based on intuition.

### 88. Impact Assessment

Impact should consider realistic consequences.

For example:

> If an AI assistant discloses one internal document, the impact may be limited.

> If the AI system can disclose thousands of customer records, the impact may be significantly greater.

The scope and scale of potential consequences matter.

### 89. Inherent Risk Versus Residual Risk

**Residual Risk**

Risk after considering controls.

```text
Controls:
Strong

Residual Risk:

```

This allows management to see both the original exposure and the effectiveness of risk treatment.

A single control may mitigate several AI risks.

For example:

This makes the relationship between risk and control clearer.

```text
What is the AI risk?

2. WHERE?
Which AI system and business process are affected?

3. WHO?
Who owns and manages the risk?

4. HOW MUCH?
What is the inherent and residual exposure?

5. WHAT NOW?
What treatment, decision, and monitoring are required?
```

If the register cannot answer these questions, it is unlikely to provide effective governance.

## Part 3 — AI Risk Register Operations, Integration, Monitoring, Reporting, and Governance

### 128. Operating the AI Risk Register

An AI risk register becomes valuable when it is embedded into the organization's normal governance and operational processes.

The register should not be updated only when an audit occurs.

It should operate continuously through a defined lifecycle:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Record
   ↓
Assess
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
Review
   ↓
Update
   ↓
Close / Accept / Escalate
   ↺
```

This lifecycle ensures that the register reflects the current AI risk environment.

---

### 129. AI Risk Register Lifecycle

A practical lifecycle can be divided into seven stages:

1. Risk identification.
2. Risk documentation.
3. Risk assessment.
4. Risk treatment.
5. Risk monitoring.
6. Risk review.
7. Risk closure or continued acceptance.

Each stage should have defined responsibilities.

---

### 130. Risk Identification Sources

AI risks can originate from multiple sources.

Examples include:

* AI impact assessments.
* Security assessments.
* Privacy assessments.
* Threat modeling.
* AI testing.
* Red-team exercises.
* Internal audits.
* External audits.
* Incidents.
* Near misses.
* Security monitoring.
* Vendor assessments.
* Regulatory changes.
* Model-performance monitoring.
* Business-user feedback.

The register should capture the source of identification.

---

### 131. Risk Intake Process

Organizations should establish a controlled process for submitting new AI risks.

A simplified workflow is:

```text
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Intake
      ↓
Initial Review
      ↓
Duplicate Check
      ↓
Risk Classification
      ↓

```

This prevents multiple teams from creating inconsistent or duplicate records.

---

### 132. Initial Risk Screening

Before a risk becomes a formal register entry, an initial screening may determine:

* Is this actually a risk?
* Is it an issue?
* Is it an incident?
* Is it already recorded?
* Does it relate to an AI system?
* Does it meet the organization's risk threshold?

This distinction is important for maintaining register quality.

---

### 133. Risk Versus Issue

A **risk** represents a potential future event or condition.

An **issue** represents something that has already occurred or currently exists.

Example:

**Risk:**

> The AI model may produce inaccurate recommendations.

**Issue:**

> The AI model has already produced inaccurate recommendations in production.

The issue may create or increase an associated risk.

---

### 134. Risk Versus Incident

An incident is an actual event that causes or potentially causes harm.

For example:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Security Incident
   ↓
Investigation
   ↓
Risk Reassessment
```

An incident should therefore be linked to the relevant risk record rather than replacing it.

---

### 135. Risk Intake Standardization

A risk intake form should collect sufficient information to perform initial assessment.

Possible fields:

* AI system.
* Business process.
* Risk description.
* Cause.
* Potential consequence.
* Source.
* Reporter.
* Date identified.
* Supporting evidence.
* Initial severity.

This reduces incomplete risk submissions.

---

### 136. Risk Validation

A GRC or AI governance function should validate submitted risks.

Validation may check:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Correct classification.
* Appropriate risk owner.
* Duplicate records.
* Correct relationship to the AI system.
* Appropriate risk rating.

Validation improves the consistency of the register.

---

### 137. Risk Ownership Assignment

Every material risk should have an accountable owner.

Ownership may be assigned based on:

* Business responsibility.
* Process responsibility.
* AI system ownership.
* Regulatory accountability.
* Operational authority.

The person responsible for the AI technology is not necessarily the person who owns the business risk.

---

### 138. Risk Owner Responsibilities

The risk owner should normally:

* Understand the risk.
* Ensure assessment is completed.
* Review controls.
* Approve treatment plans.
* Monitor residual exposure.
* Escalate material changes.
* Participate in risk acceptance decisions.
* Ensure periodic review.

Risk ownership is therefore an accountability role, not merely an administrative field.

---

### 139. Treatment Owner Responsibilities

Treatment owners are responsible for executing specific actions.

They should:

* Define remediation activities.
* Establish milestones.
* Provide evidence.
* Report progress.
* Escalate delays.
* Confirm implementation.

A single risk can have multiple treatment owners.

---

### 140. Risk Review Frequency

Risk review frequency should be proportional to risk.

Example:

| Risk Level | Example Review Frequency   |
| ---------- | -------------------------- |
| Critical   | Monthly or more frequently |
| High       | Quarterly                  |
| Medium     | Semi-annually              |
| Low        | Annually                   |

These frequencies are illustrative.

The organization should define its own requirements based on risk appetite, regulatory requirements, and AI system characteristics.

---

### 141. Event-Driven Risk Review

Periodic reviews alone are not sufficient.

A risk should also be reassessed when significant events occur.

Examples:

* Major model change.
* New training data.
* New AI provider.
* New business use case.
* Significant architecture change.
* Security incident.
* Privacy incident.
* Regulatory change.
* Material performance degradation.
* Change in user population.

This creates **event-driven risk management**.

```text
      ↓
```

* Bias characteristics.
* Explainability.
* Data requirements.
* System dependencies.

Therefore, a model change should not automatically be treated as technically equivalent to the previous model.

### 145. Data Change as a Risk Trigger

Changes to training, validation, or operational data may introduce new risks.

Examples:

* New data source.
* New geographic population.
* New data classification.
* New data retention period.
* New data provider.

The register should be reviewed when material data changes occur.

---

### 146. Third-Party Change as a Risk Trigger

Third-party AI providers may change:

* Models.
* APIs.
* Terms.
* Security controls.
* Data processing practices.
* Hosting locations.
* Subprocessors.

These changes can affect existing AI risks.

---

### 147. Risk Treatment Monitoring

Treatment actions should be monitored independently from the risk rating.

For example:

```text

High

Implement AI Gateway Controls

Treatment Status:
70% Complete

Residual Risk:
High
```

The risk should not automatically become Medium merely because remediation is underway.

---

### 148. Treatment Completion

A treatment should only be marked complete when sufficient evidence demonstrates implementation.

Example:

```text
Action:
Implement Human Approval

Status:
Completed

Evidence:
Approved workflow
Configuration
Test results
Operating procedure
```

Completion should be evidence-based.

---

### 149. Post-Treatment Assessment

After treatment is completed, the risk should be reassessed.

```text
Before Treatment
      ↓
Inherent Risk: High
      ↓
Treatment
      ↓
Control Validation
      ↓
Residual Risk Assessment
      ↓
Residual Risk: Medium
```

This confirms whether the treatment actually reduced exposure.

---

### 150. Risk Closure

A risk should not be closed simply because a treatment action was completed.

Closure should consider:

* Whether the underlying risk still exists.
* Whether the AI system remains active.
* Whether controls are effective.
* Whether residual risk is acceptable.
* Whether the risk has been transferred or eliminated.

---

### 151. Risk Closure Criteria

Possible closure conditions include:

* AI system retired.
* Business process discontinued.
* Risk eliminated.
* Architecture changed so the risk no longer applies.
* Risk consolidated into another record.
* Risk transferred under approved governance.

Closure should be documented and approved.

---

### 152. Risk Acceptance Instead of Closure

If the risk remains but management chooses to tolerate it, the correct state is normally **accepted**, not closed.

```text
Risk Exists
```

### 235. Risk Treatment Hierarchy

Treatment options should be considered systematically.

A practical hierarchy is:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
```

The organization should select the strategy appropriate to the risk and business context.

### 236. AI Risk Avoidance

Avoidance may involve:

* Not deploying the AI system.
* Removing a high-risk use case.
* Removing an unnecessary data source.
* Disabling autonomous functionality.

Example:

> An AI agent is not permitted to execute high-impact financial transactions autonomously.

The organization avoids the associated autonomous-action risk.

---

### 237. AI Risk Reduction

Reduction involves implementing controls that lower likelihood or impact.

Examples:

* Access control.
* Human oversight.
* Output validation.
* Data filtering.
* Model testing.
* Monitoring.
* Rate limiting.
* Segmentation.

### 238. AI Risk Transfer or Sharing

* Service-level agreements.
* Vendor obligations.
* Indemnification.

### 239. AI Risk Acceptance

Acceptance means management knowingly agrees to retain the residual risk.

Acceptance should be:

* Explicit.
* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Consistent with risk appetite and tolerance.

It should not simply result from failure to complete treatment.

### 283. Common Mistakes

Common mistakes include:

**Mistake 1: Treating the register as a spreadsheet**

A register is a governance process, not merely a file.

**Mistake 2: Recording risks without owners**

A risk without accountability is unlikely to be effectively managed.

**Mistake 3: Recording controls without testing them**

Control existence does not prove control effectiveness.

**Mistake 4: Closing risks after treatment**

Treatment completion does not automatically eliminate residual risk.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring systemic risks**

Individual AI risks may combine into enterprise-level exposure.

**Mistake 6: Not updating after AI changes**

AI risks change when models, data, providers, and use cases change.

### 284. Practical GRC Operating Principle

A strong principle is:

### 287. Practical Interview Answer

If asked **"How would you implement an AI risk register?"**, a strong answer is:

> "I would start by defining a standardized AI risk methodology, taxonomy, scoring model, ownership model, and treatment process. I would then connect each risk to the relevant AI system, business process, lifecycle stage, risk category, cause, consequence, controls, and evidence. The register would distinguish inherent from residual risk and would include treatment actions, owners, due dates, acceptance and escalation decisions, and review dates. I would integrate it with the enterprise GRC platform and connect it to cybersecurity, privacy, third-party risk, incident management, change management, and AI lifecycle governance. Finally, I would establish KRIs, dashboards, periodic reviews, and event-driven reassessments so that significant changes in the model, data, provider, threat environment, or controls automatically trigger appropriate risk review."

### 288. Practical Senior-Level Perspective

At a senior GRC level, the AI risk register should ultimately answer:

> **"What AI risks does the organization currently carry, who is accountable for them, how effectively are they controlled, whether the remaining exposure is acceptable, and what management decisions are required?"**
