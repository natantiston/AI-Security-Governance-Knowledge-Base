# 05.08 — AI RMF Categories and Subcategories

## Part 1 — Structure, Purpose, and Governance Context

## 1. Introduction to AI RMF Categories and Subcategories

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) organizes AI risk-management activities into a structured set of **Functions, Categories, and Subcategories**.

The hierarchy provides increasing levels of practical detail:

```text
AI RMF
   ↓
Functions
   ↓
Categories
   ↓
Subcategories
```

The four AI RMF Functions are:

1. **GOVERN**
2. **MAP**
3. **MEASURE**
4. **MANAGE**

Categories organize related outcomes and activities within each Function.

Subcategories provide more specific statements of outcomes and practices that organizations can use to guide implementation.

---

## 2. Purpose of Categories and Subcategories

Categories and Subcategories help organizations translate the high-level AI RMF Functions into actionable risk-management activities.

The Functions establish the overall operating structure.

Categories organize the major areas of activity.

Subcategories provide more detailed outcomes or actions.

A simplified model is:

```text
Function
   ↓
What major AI risk-management activity
must the organization perform?
   ↓
Category
   ↓
What specific area of that activity
must be addressed?
   ↓
Subcategory
   ↓
What more specific outcome or practice
should be considered?
```

This hierarchy makes the AI RMF more useful for operational GRC implementation.

---

## 3. The Four AI RMF Functions

The AI RMF is structured around four core Functions.

### GOVERN

Establishes organizational policies, processes, accountability, and governance for AI risk.

### MAP

Establishes context and identifies risks associated with AI systems.

### MEASURE

Analyzes, assesses, benchmarks, and monitors AI risks.

### MANAGE

Prioritizes and treats identified AI risks.

The four Functions are interconnected.

```text
             GOVERN
                ↓
               MAP
                ↓
             MEASURE
                ↓
              MANAGE
                ↓
        Continuous Feedback
                ↓
             GOVERN
```

The framework is intended to operate iteratively rather than as a strictly linear process.

---

## 4. Function, Category, and Subcategory

These three levels should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Function

A broad area of AI risk management.

### Category

A more specific grouping of outcomes or activities within a Function.

### Subcategory

A detailed outcome or practice within a Category.

For example:

```text
GOVERN
   ↓
Category
   ↓
Subcategory
```

This structure allows an organization to move from strategic governance to practical implementation.

## 6. Categories Are Not Controls

An important distinction is that an AI RMF Category or Subcategory should not automatically be interpreted as a technical control.

The AI RMF primarily describes **outcomes and risk-management practices**.

An organization determines how those outcomes will be achieved.

For example:

```text
AI RMF Outcome
      ↓
Organizational Requirement
      ↓
Policy / Process
      ↓
Control
      ↓
Implementation
      ↓
Evidence
```

Different organizations may implement the same AI RMF outcome using different controls.

---

## 7. Categories as Risk-Management Organization

Categories help organizations group related AI risk activities.

This prevents AI risk management from becoming a collection of unrelated tasks.

Instead, activities can be organized according to a common framework:

```text
GOVERN
 ├── Governance Area
 ├── Governance Area
 └── Governance Area

MAP
 ├── Context Area
 ├── Risk Area
 └── Impact Area

MEASURE
 ├── Assessment Area
 ├── Testing Area
 └── Monitoring Area

MANAGE
 ├── Prioritization Area
 ├── Treatment Area
 └── Monitoring / Response Area
```

The actual Categories and Subcategories provide the detailed structure within these Functions.

## 8. Categories Support Consistency

Without a structured framework, different business units may manage AI risk differently.

For example:

```text
Business Unit A
→ Strong AI risk governance

Business Unit B
→ Informal AI assessment

Business Unit C
→ No documented AI risk process
```

A common AI RMF structure can establish a consistent baseline.

The organization can then define:

* Common terminology.
* Common assessment criteria.
* Common responsibilities.
* Common documentation.
* Common evidence expectations.

## 15. Categories and AI System Context

The same AI capability can create different risks depending on context.

For example, an AI classification model could be used for:

* Marketing recommendations.
* Fraud detection.
* Employee screening.
* Customer eligibility decisions.

The technical model may be similar, but the consequences of errors can be very different.

Therefore:

> AI RMF Categories and Subcategories should be interpreted within the context of the specific AI system and its intended use.

## 16. Categories and Intended Purpose

AI risk cannot be assessed effectively without understanding intended purpose.

Important questions include:

* Why is the AI system being used?
* What decision or activity does it support?
* Who uses the output?
* Who is affected?
* What happens if the output is wrong?
* Is the AI output advisory or automatically executed?
* What level of human oversight exists?

These contextual questions influence which AI RMF outcomes are most relevant.

## 39. "Not Applicable" Is a Governance Decision

Declaring something **Not Applicable** should not become a shortcut for avoiding controls.

A mature process asks:

> Why is this outcome not relevant to this AI system?

The rationale should be documented when appropriate.

For example:

```text
Requirement:
Human Oversight

Applicability:
Not Applicable

Rationale:
AI system only performs internal document classification
and cannot independently make decisions affecting individuals.
```

The example is illustrative; actual applicability depends on the organization's context and risk assessment.

## Part 2 — GOVERN Categories and Subcategories

## 64. Overview of the GOVERN Function Structure

The **GOVERN** Function establishes the organizational foundation for managing AI risks.

It addresses the policies, processes, accountability structures, organizational practices, and governance mechanisms required to manage AI risks throughout the AI lifecycle.

The GOVERN Function is organized into seven Categories:

1. **GOVERN 1 — Policies, Processes, Procedures, and Practices**
2. **GOVERN 2 — Accountability Structures and Responsibilities**
3. **GOVERN 3 — Workforce Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Accessibility**
4. **GOVERN 4 — Organizational Teams and Stakeholder Engagement**
5. **GOVERN 5 — Organizational Policies, Processes, and Procedures for AI Risks**
6. **GOVERN 6 — Policies, Processes, Procedures, and Practices for AI Systems**
7. **GOVERN 7 — AI Governance Processes**

The exact interpretation and implementation should be adapted to the organization's context, AI portfolio, risk profile, and applicable requirements.

---

## 65. GOVERN 1 — Policies, Processes, Procedures, and Practices

The first governance area establishes the organization's overall approach to AI risk management.

The organization should establish policies and processes that define how AI risks are governed.

# MEASURE Categories and Subcategories

## 145. MEASURE Function Overview

The MEASURE Function evaluates AI risks using:

* Testing.
* Assessment.
* Analysis.
* Metrics.
* Monitoring.
* Validation.
* Measurement.

Its purpose is to produce evidence about AI system performance and risk.

The MEASURE Function contains four major Categories:

2. **MEASURE 2 — AI Systems are Evaluated for Trustworthiness**
3. **MEASURE 3 — Mechanisms for Tracking Existing and Emerging Risks are Established**
4. **MEASURE 4 — Feedback about Measurement is Collected and Integrated**

* Drift.

The correct metric depends on the AI system.

# MANAGE Categories and Subcategories

## 160. MANAGE Function Overview

It includes:

* Treatment.

* Risk acceptance.
* Risk escalation.

The MANAGE Function contains four major Categories:

3. **MANAGE 3 — AI Risks and Benefits are Managed Across the AI Lifecycle**
4. **MANAGE 4 — Residual Risks are Documented and Monitored**

AI risks should be prioritized according to factors such as:

* Impact.
* Business criticality.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Security exposure.
* Safety considerations.

* Mitigate.
* Transfer.
* Accept.

AI trustworthiness has multiple dimensions.

AI behavior and risk can change after deployment.

This demonstrates that the framework is being understood operationally rather than memorized as terminology.

## 266. Senior GRC Perspective

At a senior level, the important question is not:

> "Did we implement every AI RMF Subcategory?"

The more important questions are:

* Are material AI systems known?
* Are significant AI risks identified?
* Are risks measured with appropriate evidence?
* Are controls effective?
* Are residual risks within tolerance?
* Are management decisions documented?
* Can the organization detect when AI risk changes?

This is the difference between **framework adoption** and **effective AI risk governance**.

## 269. Key Takeaways

The practical implementation of AI RMF Categories and Subcategories should follow these principles:

1. Establish an accurate **AI inventory**.
2. Understand the **context and purpose** of each AI system.
3. Classify AI systems according to **risk and impact**.
4. Determine which AI RMF outcomes are applicable.
5. Map outcomes to **organizational risks and controls**.
6. Reuse existing enterprise controls where they adequately address the risk.
7. Create AI-specific controls only where genuine gaps exist.
8. Define measurable **AI risk and performance indicators**.
9. Maintain evidence demonstrating implementation.
10. Assess control effectiveness rather than merely control existence.
11. Treat AI risks throughout the entire lifecycle.
12. Document residual risk after treatment.
13. Establish clear risk acceptance and escalation authority.
14. Integrate AI risks with enterprise GRC.
15. Connect AI governance with cybersecurity, privacy, data governance, and compliance.
16. Monitor AI systems continuously where risk justifies it.
17. Periodically reassess AI systems when technology, data, purpose, users, or threats change.
18. Use current and target profiles to manage AI governance maturity.
19. Treat the AI RMF as a **risk-management framework**, not simply a compliance checklist.
20. Focus ultimately on whether the organization can make informed, accountable decisions about AI risk.
