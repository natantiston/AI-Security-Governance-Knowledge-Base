# 05.18 — AI Risk Management Metrics, KRIs, KPIs, and Reporting

## Part 1 — Foundations of AI Risk Metrics and Measurement

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk Metrics

AI governance requires more than policies, assessments, and controls.

Organizations need measurable evidence to determine whether AI risks are:

* Identified.
* Controlled.
* Within appetite.
* Increasing or decreasing.
* Properly monitored.
* Being effectively treated.

AI risk metrics provide this evidence.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Risk Metrics

The primary purpose of AI risk metrics is to support decision-making.

A useful metric should help answer questions such as:

> Are our AI risks increasing?

> Are our controls working?

> Are we operating within risk appetite?

> Where should management focus?

> Which AI systems require intervention?

Metrics should therefore be connected to governance decisions.

---

### 3. Measurement Versus Monitoring

Measurement and monitoring are related but different.

**Measurement** determines a value or condition.

**Monitoring** observes that value or condition over time.

For example:

```text
Measurement:
AI error rate = 2.3%

Monitoring:
AI error rate increased from
1.2% → 1.8% → 2.3%
```

Monitoring provides the trend and context necessary for risk management.

---

### 4. Metrics Should Support Risk Management

A metric becomes more valuable when it connects to a risk.

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Risk Indicator
   ↓
Threshold
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Action
```

Without the final decision or action, the metric may simply become information that nobody uses.

---

### 5. AI Risk Metric Categories

AI governance metrics can generally be organized into several categories:

* Governance.
* Risk.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Model performance.
* Reliability.
* Human oversight.
* Compliance.
* Third-party risk.
* Incident management.
* Control effectiveness.
* Remediation.
* Lifecycle management.

Different AI systems require different combinations.

---

### 6. Governance Metrics

Governance metrics measure whether required governance activities are being performed.

Examples include:

* AI inventory coverage.
* AI systems with assigned owners.
* Risk assessment completion.
* Validation completion.
* Review completion.
* Training completion.
* Policy exception rates.

These metrics help determine whether the governance framework is actually operating.

---

### 7. AI Inventory Coverage

A fundamental governance metric is the percentage of known AI systems recorded in the organization's AI inventory.

A simplified formula is:

```text
AI Systems in Inventory
----------------------- × 100
Known AI Systems
```

A low inventory coverage percentage may indicate:

* Shadow AI.
* Poor discovery.
* Incomplete governance.
* Unknown risk exposure.

---

### 8. Ownership Coverage

Organizations can measure how many AI systems have clearly assigned owners.

```text
AI Systems With Assigned Owners
------------------------------- × 100
Total AI Systems
```

A system without an owner represents an accountability gap.

---

### 9. Risk Assessment Coverage

A useful metric is:

```text
AI Systems With Current Risk Assessment
--------------------------------------- × 100
AI Systems Requiring Assessment
```

This can be particularly important for high-risk AI systems.

---

### 10. Risk Assessment Currency

An assessment may exist but still be outdated.

Therefore, organizations should distinguish between:

* Assessment exists.
* Assessment is current.
* Assessment reflects the current system.

For example:

```text
Assessment Date:
2024

Major Model Change:
2026

Current Assessment:
No
```

The existence of the original assessment does not necessarily demonstrate current risk understanding.

---

### 11. Validation Coverage

Organizations may measure:

```text
High-Risk AI Systems With Current Validation
-------------------------------------------- × 100
High-Risk AI Systems Requiring Validation
```

This provides visibility into whether higher-risk systems have received appropriate assurance.

---

### 12. Monitoring Coverage

Monitoring coverage can measure whether required AI systems have active monitoring.

```text
AI Systems With Required Monitoring Enabled
------------------------------------------- × 100
AI Systems Requiring Monitoring
```

A high-risk AI system without appropriate monitoring may represent a significant governance weakness.

---

### 13. Governance Gate Compliance

Organizations can measure whether AI systems passed required lifecycle gates.

Examples include:

* Use-case approval.
* Architecture review.
* Risk assessment.
* Security assessment.
* Validation.
* Production approval.

The metric may be:

```text
AI Systems Passing Required Gates
--------------------------------- × 100
AI Systems Subject to Gates
```

---

### 14. AI Risk Metrics

Risk metrics focus on actual exposure rather than simply governance activity.

Examples include:

* Number of high-risk AI systems.
* Inherent risk.
* Residual risk.
* Risk appetite breaches.
* Open high-risk findings.
* Risk trend.
* Concentration of AI risk.

These metrics help management understand the organization's AI risk profile.

---

### 15. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk before considering controls.

For example:

```text
AI Makes High-Impact Decisions
+
Sensitive Data
+
High Degree of Automation
+
Large User Population
```

These characteristics may result in high inherent risk.

---

### 16. Residual Risk

Residual risk represents the remaining risk after controls and treatments are considered.

Conceptually:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Residual risk is often more relevant to management decisions because it reflects the organization's actual remaining exposure.

---

### 17. Risk Appetite

Risk metrics should be compared with the organization's risk appetite.

For example:

```text
Approved Risk Appetite:
Moderate

Current Residual Risk:
High

Result:
Risk Appetite Breach
```

The metric becomes meaningful because it is evaluated against a defined boundary.

---

### 18. Risk Threshold

A threshold defines when a metric requires attention.

For example:

```text
AI Error Rate

Green:
< 2%

Amber:
2% – 5%

Red:
> 5%
```

Thresholds should be based on the characteristics and risk tolerance of the specific AI system.

---

### 19. Thresholds Should Be Risk-Based

A single threshold should not necessarily apply to every AI system.

For example:

```text
AI Chatbot:
5% Error Rate → Potentially Manageable

Medical Decision AI:
5% Error Rate → Potentially Unacceptable
```

The acceptable level depends on potential impact.

---

### 20. Key Performance Indicators

A **Key Performance Indicator (KPI)** measures performance against an objective.

Examples include:

* AI service availability.
* Processing time.
* Accuracy.
* Assessment completion.
* Remediation completion.
* Training completion.

KPIs primarily answer:

> Are we achieving the desired performance or process objective?

---

### 21. Key Risk Indicators

A **Key Risk Indicator (KRI)** provides information about risk exposure or changes in risk.

Examples include:

* Number of AI incidents.
* Unauthorized AI usage.
* Model drift.
* Risk appetite breaches.
* Critical control failures.
* AI security events.
* High-risk findings.

KRIs primarily answer:

> Is risk increasing, decreasing, or approaching an unacceptable level?

---

### 22. KPI Versus KRI

The distinction can be illustrated as:

| Type | Example                                 | Primary Question               |
| ---- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| KPI  | 98% assessment completion               | Are we executing the process?  |
| KRI  | 7 high-risk AI systems outside appetite | How much risk are we carrying? |

Both are valuable but serve different purposes.

---

### 23. Key Control Indicators

A third category can be useful:

**Key Control Indicators (KCIs)**

KCIs measure whether important controls are functioning.

Examples:

* Access reviews completed.
* Monitoring controls operational.
* Required approvals completed.
* Security tests passed.
* Human-review controls operating.

A KCI answers:

> Are the controls intended to manage the risk actually functioning?

---

### 24. KPI, KRI, and KCI Relationship

These can work together:

```text
KPI
 ↓
Process Performance

KCI
 ↓
Control Effectiveness

KRI
 ↓
Risk Exposure
```

For example:

```text
KPI:
98% of AI reviews completed

KCI:
95% of access reviews completed successfully

KRI:
Unauthorized AI access increased 20%
```

The three metrics provide different perspectives.

---

### 25. Leading Indicators

Leading indicators provide signals that risk may increase in the future.

Examples include:

* Increasing model drift.
* Increasing failed security tests.
* Growing exception volume.
* Increasing overdue remediation.
* Increasing unauthorized AI usage.

These indicators can support preventive action.

---

### 26. Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators reflect events that have already occurred.

Examples include:

* AI incidents.
* Data breaches.
* Regulatory findings.
* Customer complaints.
* Confirmed control failures.

Lagging indicators are useful for understanding historical outcomes.

---

### 27. Leading Versus Lagging Indicators

```text
Leading Indicator
       ↓
Potential Future Risk
       ↓
Preventive Action
       ↓
Reduced Probability

Lagging Indicator
       ↓
Risk Event Already Occurred
       ↓
Response
       ↓
Lessons Learned
```

A mature program uses both.

---

### 28. Example of Leading Indicator

Suppose:

```text
Model Drift:
1%
 ↓
2%
 ↓
3%
 ↓
4%
```

The increasing trend may indicate emerging performance risk before a major incident occurs.

Management can intervene early.

---

### 29. Example of Lagging Indicator

Suppose:

```text
AI Security Incidents:
2024 = 2
2025 = 5
2026 = 11
```

This indicates that security incidents have increased.

The organization should investigate the causes rather than simply reporting the number.

---

### 30. Trend Analysis

A single metric provides a snapshot.

A trend provides more information.

For example:

```text
Current Risk:
Moderate

Trend:
↑ Increasing

Interpretation:
Risk may require additional attention.
```

Metrics should therefore be analyzed over time where practical.

---

### 31. Rate Versus Absolute Number

Absolute numbers can be misleading.

For example:

```text
AI Incidents:
2025 = 100
2026 = 150
```

This appears negative.

But if AI transaction volume increased from:

```text
1 million → 10 million
```

the incident rate may actually have decreased.

---

### 32. Normalized Metrics

Normalized metrics account for scale.

For example:

```text
AI Incidents
------------
AI Transactions
```

or:

```text
AI Security Events
------------------
Number of AI Systems
```

Normalization can make comparisons more meaningful.

---

### 33. Metric Context

Metrics should be accompanied by contextual information.

A dashboard showing:

> 12 AI incidents

does not explain:

* Severity.
* Affected systems.
* Business impact.
* Trend.
* Root cause.
* Response status.

A useful report should provide enough context to support decision-making.

---

### 34. AI Risk Dashboard

A management dashboard may include:

```text
AI SYSTEMS
Total: 120

HIGH-RISK SYSTEMS
18

OPEN HIGH RISKS
7

APPETITE BREACHES
2

ACTIVE INCIDENTS
1

MONITORING COVERAGE
96%

VALIDATION COVERAGE
100%

OVERDUE TREATMENTS
5
```

The exact metrics should be tailored to organizational needs.

---

### 35. Executive Reporting

Executives generally need:

* Current exposure.
* Significant trends.
* Material incidents.
* Appetite breaches.
* Major control weaknesses.
* Remediation status.
* Decisions required.

They generally do not need every technical telemetry metric.

---

### 36. Technical Reporting

Technical teams may require detailed information such as:

* Model error rates.
* Latency.
* Token usage.
* API failures.
* Authentication events.
* Prompt injection attempts.
* Tool execution failures.
* Infrastructure events.

Technical reporting supports operational action.

---

### 37. Risk Committee Reporting

A risk committee may require a combination of:

```text
Risk Profile
+
Control Effectiveness
+
Incidents
+
Emerging Risks
+
Appetite Breaches
+
Treatment Status
+
Management Decisions
```

This provides a governance-level view.

---

### 38. Board Reporting

Board reporting should focus on materiality.

Potential topics include:

* Strategic AI exposure.
* Significant AI risks.
* Regulatory developments.
* Material incidents.
* Risk appetite breaches.
* Major third-party dependencies.
* Significant investments.
* Governance maturity.

Technical details should be translated into business consequences.

---

### 39. Metric Ownership

Each important metric should have an owner.

The metric owner should understand:

* Definition.
* Data source.
* Calculation.
* Frequency.
* Threshold.
* Interpretation.
* Escalation.

Without ownership, metrics may become unreliable.

---

### 40. Metric Definition

A metric should have a clear definition.

A metric definition can include:

```text
Metric Name
Purpose
Definition
Formula
Data Source
Frequency
Owner
Threshold
Target
Escalation
```

This improves consistency.

---

### 41. Data Source

AI metrics should use reliable data sources.

Possible sources include:

* AI platforms.
* SIEM.
* GRC systems.
* IAM systems.
* Model monitoring platforms.
* Application logs.
* Incident management systems.
* Vendor reports.

The source should be documented.

---

### 42. Metric Data Quality

Poor data produces unreliable metrics.

Potential data-quality problems include:

* Missing logs.
* Duplicate events.
* Incorrect timestamps.
* Inconsistent classifications.
* Manual data-entry errors.
* Incomplete AI inventory.

Data quality should therefore be treated as part of metric governance.

---

### 43. Metric Frequency

Different metrics require different reporting frequencies.

Examples:

```text
Critical Security Indicator
→ Near Real Time / Daily

Operational AI Performance
→ Daily / Weekly

Risk Profile
→ Monthly

Governance Maturity
→ Quarterly

Strategic AI Risk
→ Quarterly / As Needed
```

Frequency should reflect how quickly the underlying risk can change.

---

### 44. Metric Thresholds

A threshold should define when action is required.

For example:

```text
Model Drift

Normal:
< 3%

Warning:
3% – 5%

Critical:
> 5%
```

Thresholds should be validated against actual system behavior and risk tolerance.

---

### 45. Threshold Escalation

A threshold can trigger predefined actions.

```text
Warning
 ↓
Increased Monitoring

Critical
 ↓
Investigation

Severe
 ↓
Escalation / Restriction / Suspension
```

This creates a connection between measurement and risk management.

---

### 46. Metric Baselines

Before thresholds can be established, organizations may need a baseline.

A baseline represents normal or expected behavior.

For example:

```text
Normal AI Error Rate:
1.5%

Observed:
1.6%

Interpretation:
Within expected variation
```

A sudden increase to 5% may require investigation.

---

### 47. Dynamic Baselines

AI systems may have changing operating patterns.

A static baseline may therefore become inaccurate.

Dynamic baselines can account for:

* Seasonality.
* User population.
* Transaction volume.
* Model versions.
* Environmental conditions.

The approach should be appropriate to the system's risk.

---

### 48. False Positives

A monitoring system can produce alerts that do not represent meaningful risk.

Too many false positives can cause:

* Alert fatigue.
* Wasted resources.
* Missed important alerts.

Thresholds should therefore be periodically reviewed.

---

### 49. False Negatives

False negatives occur when meaningful risk events are not detected.

These can be particularly dangerous because the organization may believe monitoring is effective when important events are being missed.

Monitoring effectiveness should therefore consider both:

* False positives.
* False negatives.

---

### 50. Metric Governance

AI metrics themselves should be governed.

Organizations should periodically review:

* Whether metrics remain relevant.
* Whether definitions remain accurate.
* Whether data remains reliable.
* Whether thresholds remain appropriate.
* Whether metrics drive useful decisions.

The objective is to ensure that **the measurement system itself remains fit for purpose**.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Indicators, Control Metrics, Thresholds, and Measurement Design

### 51. AI Security Metrics

AI security metrics measure the security posture of AI systems and supporting infrastructure.

Examples include:

* Number of AI-related security incidents.
* Prompt-injection attempts.
* Unauthorized model access.
* Suspicious API activity.
* Data-exfiltration events.
* Authentication failures.
* Privilege violations.
* Vulnerability exposure.
* Security-testing findings.

Security metrics should be connected to actual risks rather than collected simply because the data is available.

---

### 52. AI Privacy Metrics

Privacy metrics can measure exposure involving personal or sensitive information.

Examples include:

* Number of AI systems processing personal data.
* Privacy assessment completion.
* Unauthorized data-access events.
* Data-retention exceptions.
* Privacy incidents.
* Sensitive-data exposure.
* Data-minimization compliance.
* Third-party data-processing exceptions.

Privacy metrics should reflect the organization's applicable privacy obligations.

---

### 53. AI Model Performance Metrics

Model-performance metrics depend heavily on the AI use case.

Potential measures include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Error rate.
* Hallucination rate.
* Robustness.
* Drift.

The appropriate metric should reflect the business purpose and consequences of model failure.

---

### 54. Accuracy

Accuracy measures how often the AI produces a correct result.

A simplified formula is:

```text id="4e2a9x"
Correct Predictions
------------------- × 100
Total Predictions
```

Accuracy can be useful, but it may be insufficient for imbalanced datasets or high-impact decisions.

---

### 55. Precision

Precision measures how many positive predictions were actually correct.

```text id="j8jz3v"
True Positives
--------------------------
True Positives + False Positives
```

Precision can be particularly important when false positives have significant consequences.

---

### 56. Recall

Recall measures how many actual positive cases were successfully identified.

```text id="r7j2qk"
True Positives
--------------------------
True Positives + False Negatives
```

Recall becomes particularly important where missing a true case carries significant risk.

---

### 57. Precision-Recall Tradeoff

Increasing precision can sometimes reduce recall, and vice versa.

Therefore, the organization should determine which error type is more significant.

For example:

```text id="z6a4nh"
False Positive
→ Incorrectly flags legitimate activity

False Negative
→ Fails to detect harmful activity
```

The appropriate balance depends on the use case.

---

### 58. False-Positive Rate

False-positive rate measures how frequently legitimate cases are incorrectly classified as positive.

High false-positive rates can result in:

* Operational inefficiency.
* Customer frustration.
* Excessive manual review.
* Alert fatigue.

For security systems, excessive false positives can also cause analysts to ignore alerts.

---

### 59. False-Negative Rate

False-negative rate measures how frequently actual positive cases are missed.

This can be particularly important for:

* Fraud detection.
* Security monitoring.
* Safety systems.
* Compliance monitoring.

A low false-negative rate may be more important than high overall accuracy in certain high-impact applications.

---

### 60. Hallucination Rate

For generative AI systems, organizations may measure the frequency with which the system produces unsupported or factually incorrect information.

A simplified concept is:

```text id="l7x4s2"
Unsupported Outputs
------------------- × 100
Evaluated Outputs
```

The exact methodology should be defined carefully because hallucination assessment can be context-dependent.

---

### 61. Groundedness

Groundedness measures how well an AI response is supported by approved or authoritative information sources.

For example:

```text id="v8w1pt"
AI Response
     ↓
Retrieved Evidence
     ↓
Supported?
```

Low groundedness may increase misinformation or decision-making risk.

---

### 62. Toxicity and Harmful Content Metrics

For applicable generative AI systems, organizations may monitor:

* Toxic outputs.
* Hate-related outputs.
* Unsafe content.
* Harassment.
* Self-harm-related content.
* Policy violations.

These metrics should be aligned with the organization's responsible-AI requirements and applicable use case.

---

### 63. Bias and Fairness Metrics

AI systems that influence people may require fairness-related measurement.

Potential metrics include:

* Selection-rate differences.
* Error-rate differences.
* False-positive differences.
* False-negative differences.
* Group performance differences.

The selected fairness measure should be appropriate to the context rather than assuming that one universal metric applies to every system.

---

### 64. Fairness Measurement Requires Context

A fairness metric cannot be interpreted without understanding:

* The population.
* The decision.
* The intended outcome.
* Relevant protected or sensitive characteristics.
* Legal requirements.
* Business context.

Therefore:

> A numerical fairness metric without contextual interpretation may produce a misleading conclusion.

---

### 65. Explainability Metrics

Where explainability is relevant, organizations may assess:

* Explanation availability.
* Explanation consistency.
* User comprehension.
* Feature attribution stability.
* Ability to reproduce or justify decisions.

The objective is not necessarily to make every model fully interpretable, but to provide an appropriate level of transparency for the risk.

---

### 66. Robustness Metrics

Robustness measures how well an AI system performs under expected variations or challenging conditions.

Testing may include:

* Input variation.
* Noisy data.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Distribution changes.
* Unexpected prompts.
* Missing information.

A robust system should not fail unpredictably under reasonably foreseeable conditions.

---

### 67. Resilience Metrics

Resilience measures the ability of an AI system to continue operating safely under disruption.

Possible indicators include:

* Availability.
* Recovery time.
* Recovery point.
* Failover success.
* Degraded-mode performance.
* Dependency availability.

Resilience is particularly important for business-critical AI systems.

---

### 68. Availability

Availability measures whether the AI service is accessible when required.

A simplified calculation is:

```text id="i2e0e6"
Available Time
-------------- × 100
Total Required Time
```

High availability may be a critical business requirement, but availability should not override safety or security controls.

---

### 69. Latency

Latency measures how long the AI system takes to respond.

For example:

```text id="o7j2p8"
Average Response Time
P95 Response Time
P99 Response Time
```

Percentile measurements can be more informative than averages because they reveal extreme delays.

---

### 70. Model Drift

Model drift occurs when model behavior or the underlying data changes over time.

Monitoring may include:

* Input distribution changes.
* Output distribution changes.
* Performance degradation.
* Data-quality changes.

A simplified lifecycle is:

```text id="8g9y2v"
Stable Model
    ↓
Environment Changes
    ↓
Performance Changes
    ↓
Drift Detected
    ↓
Reassessment
```

---

### 71. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and expected outputs changes.

For example:

```text id="e8k4yx"
Historical Pattern
       ↓
Business Environment Changes
       ↓
Historical Model Assumptions Become Less Accurate
```

The model may need retraining, recalibration, or replacement.

---

### 72. Data Drift

Data drift occurs when the characteristics of incoming data change compared with the data used during model development or validation.

Examples include changes in:

* Distribution.
* Population.
* Values.
* Frequency.
* Data sources.

Data drift can be an early indicator of model-performance risk.

---

### 73. AI Data Quality Metrics

Potential metrics include:

* Completeness.
* Accuracy.
* Consistency.
* Timeliness.
* Validity.
* Uniqueness.

Poor data quality can directly affect AI reliability.

---

### 74. Data Completeness

Completeness measures whether required data is present.

```text id="j5f3kq"
Required Data Elements Present
------------------------------ × 100
Required Data Elements
```

Incomplete data can create unpredictable model behavior.

---

### 75. Data Accuracy

Data accuracy measures whether data correctly represents the underlying reality.

For example:

```text id="z3q6mc"
Incorrect Customer Information
        ↓
Incorrect AI Input
        ↓
Potentially Incorrect Output
```

Data-quality controls are therefore part of AI risk management.

---

### 76. Data Lineage Metrics

Organizations may measure whether AI inputs can be traced to their sources.

Possible indicators include:

* Percentage of critical data with documented lineage.
* Percentage of datasets with identified owners.
* Percentage of training datasets with documented provenance.

Traceability improves accountability and investigation capability.

---

### 77. Data Provenance

Data provenance answers:

> Where did this data come from, and how was it transformed?

A provenance chain might be:

```text id="l7x7j3"
Source
 ↓
Collection
 ↓
Transformation
 ↓
Storage
 ↓
Training / Inference
 ↓
AI Output
```

This can be important for auditability and incident investigation.

---

### 78. AI Security Testing Metrics

Security testing metrics may include:

* Percentage of AI systems tested.
* Critical findings.
* High findings.
* Average remediation time.
* Repeat findings.
* Unresolved vulnerabilities.
* Prompt-injection test results.
* Adversarial-test results.

The metrics should focus on meaningful security exposure.

---

### 79. Vulnerability Remediation Rate

A simplified metric is:

```text id="x8m2kq"
Vulnerabilities Remediated
-------------------------- × 100
Vulnerabilities Identified
```

However, organizations should also consider severity and remediation deadlines.

Remediating 99% of low-risk issues while leaving one critical vulnerability unresolved may still represent unacceptable risk.

---

### 80. Mean Time to Remediate

MTTR can measure how quickly AI-related findings are resolved.

```text id="q9d8r3"
Total Remediation Time
----------------------
Number of Remediated Findings
```

Organizations may define separate targets for:

* Critical.
* High.
* Medium.
* Low findings.

---

### 81. Mean Time to Detect

MTTD measures the average time required to detect an AI-related security or operational event.

```text id="c7m5x8"
Detection Time
--------------
Number of Events
```

Lower MTTD can improve incident response effectiveness.

---

### 82. Mean Time to Respond

MTTR can also be used in incident-management contexts to mean **Mean Time to Respond**.

Because the acronym can be ambiguous, organizations should explicitly define whether MTTR means:

* Mean Time to Remediate.
* Mean Time to Respond.
* Mean Time to Recover.

---

### 83. AI Incident Metrics

AI incident reporting may measure:

* Number of incidents.
* Severity.
* Business impact.
* Detection time.
* Response time.
* Recovery time.
* Root cause.
* Repeat incidents.
* Affected systems.

Incident counts should not be interpreted without severity and exposure context.

---

### 84. Incident Severity Distribution

Instead of reporting only:

> 50 AI incidents.

Management may receive:

```text id="8m5x1p"
Critical: 1
High:     4
Medium:  15
Low:     30
```

This provides significantly more useful information.

---

### 85. Repeat Incident Rate

A high repeat-incident rate may indicate that root causes are not being addressed.

A simplified calculation is:

```text id="m0z9yr"
Repeat Incidents
---------------- × 100
Total Incidents
```

This can be an important indicator of control effectiveness.

---

### 86. AI Incident Root Cause

Incident metrics become more valuable when categorized by root cause.

Potential categories include:

* Human error.
* Configuration.
* Model failure.
* Data quality.
* Security attack.
* Vendor failure.
* Process failure.
* Control failure.

Root-cause trends can guide risk treatment.

---

### 87. Human Oversight Metrics

For AI systems requiring human oversight, organizations may measure:

* Percentage of decisions reviewed.
* Override rate.
* Escalation rate.
* Human-review response time.
* Review completion.
* Reviewer agreement rate.

These metrics can help determine whether human oversight is functioning as intended.

---

### 88. Human Override Rate

A high override rate may indicate that the AI system frequently produces outputs that humans reject.

For example:

```text id="u4m6g7"
AI Decisions:
10,000

Human Overrides:
1,500

Override Rate:
15%
```

The organization should investigate whether this indicates:

* Model-quality problems.
* Poor thresholds.
* Inappropriate use.
* Overly conservative reviewers.

---

### 89. Human Review Completion

For systems requiring human review:

```text id="j2h6pc"
Completed Human Reviews
----------------------- × 100
Required Human Reviews
```

A low completion rate may create a governance or safety risk.

---

### 90. Human Oversight Effectiveness

Completion alone does not prove effective oversight.

The organization should also consider:

* Reviewer competence.
* Review quality.
* Time available.
* Access to supporting evidence.
* Ability to reject AI decisions.
* Override capability.

The objective is meaningful oversight rather than merely documenting human involvement.

---

### 91. Third-Party AI Metrics

For externally provided AI services, metrics may include:

* Security incidents.
* SLA performance.
* Data-processing exceptions.
* Vendor risk rating.
* Subprocessor changes.
* Model-change notifications.

Third-party dependency can introduce risks outside the organization's direct control.

---

### 92. Vendor Concentration Risk

Organizations should understand whether critical AI capabilities depend heavily on one provider.

For example:

```text id="8q0hcm"

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓
Multiple Business Functions Affected
```

Concentration may increase resilience and continuity risk.

### 93. Third-Party Change Metrics

Organizations may monitor:

Material third-party changes should potentially trigger reassessment.

---

### 94. AI Compliance Metrics

Compliance metrics may include:

* Regulatory requirement coverage.
* Open compliance findings.
* Overdue compliance actions.
* Policy exceptions.

Compliance metrics should focus on material obligations rather than generating unnecessary administrative activity.

### 95. Exception Metrics

Exception management should be measured.

Examples include:

### 97. Remediation Metrics

Remediation metrics can include:

These metrics indicate whether identified weaknesses are actually being addressed.

---

### 98. Control Effectiveness Metrics

A mature AI governance program should measure whether controls actually reduce risk.

Possible indicators include:

* Control pass rate.
* Control failure rate.
* Repeat control failures.
* Testing coverage.
* Exception rate.
* Control remediation time.

The objective is to determine whether controls are functioning as intended.

---

### 99. Risk Reduction Measurement

Where practical, organizations should demonstrate that risk treatment actually reduces exposure.

For example:

```text id="z0r7vp"
Inherent Risk
High
  ↓
Security Control
  ↓
Validation
  ↓
Residual Risk
Moderate
```

This demonstrates the relationship between controls and risk reduction.

---

### 100. The Measurement Chain

A mature AI measurement program connects the complete chain:

```text id="v7u4qa"
OBJECTIVE
   ↓
RISK
   ↓
CONTROL
   ↓
METRIC
   ↓
THRESHOLD
   ↓
MONITORING
   ↓
INTERPRETATION
   ↓

   ↓
```

A metric is valuable not because it is measurable, but because it provides **reliable evidence that enables the organization to make a better risk decision**.

# 05.18 — AI Risk Management Metrics, KRIs, KPIs, and Reporting

## Part 3 — AI Risk Dashboards, Thresholds, Reporting, and Decision-Making

### 101. AI Risk Dashboard

An AI risk dashboard provides management with a consolidated view of the organization's AI risk posture.

A useful dashboard may combine:

* AI inventory.
* Risk exposure.
* Control effectiveness.
* Model performance.
* Security events.
* Privacy events.
* Incidents.
* Exceptions.
* Remediation.
* Emerging risks.

The dashboard should help management identify where attention is required.

---

### 102. Dashboard Design Principle

A dashboard should answer:

> **What has changed, why does it matter, and what decision is required?**

A dashboard that only displays large numbers without interpretation provides limited governance value.

A useful structure is:

```text
Metric
  ↓
Current Status
  ↓
Trend
  ↓
Threshold
  ↓
Risk Interpretation
  ↓
Required Action
```

---

### 103. Executive AI Risk Dashboard

An executive dashboard should prioritize material information.

Example:

```text
AI SYSTEMS                         120
HIGH-RISK SYSTEMS                  18
RISK APPETITE BREACHES              2
CRITICAL FINDINGS                   1
ACTIVE AI INCIDENTS                 1
MONITORING COVERAGE                96%
VALIDATION COVERAGE               100%
OVERDUE REMEDIATIONS                5
```

The executive view should avoid excessive technical detail.

---

### 104. Technical AI Dashboard

Technical teams require more detailed information.

A technical dashboard may include:

* Model error rate.
* Latency.
* Availability.
* Data drift.
* Model drift.
* Prompt-injection attempts.
* Authentication failures.
* API errors.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Tool execution failures.

The same AI system can therefore have different dashboards for different audiences.

---

### 105. Risk Committee Dashboard

A risk committee may require a middle layer between technical and executive reporting.

It may include:

```text

Incidents
Exceptions
Risk Appetite
Emerging Risks
Third-Party Risk
Remediation
```

This allows the committee to challenge management and make risk decisions.

---

### 106. Board-Level Reporting

Board reporting should emphasize:

* Material AI risks.
* Strategic dependencies.
* Significant incidents.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Risk appetite breaches.
* Major third-party dependencies.
* Governance maturity.
* Significant investments or decisions.

The board generally needs the **business consequence**, not raw technical telemetry.

---

### 107. Reporting by Audience

| Audience                | Primary Focus                     |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Board                   | Strategic and material risk       |
| Executive Management    | Enterprise exposure and decisions |
| Risk Committee          | Risk, controls, treatment         |
| CISO                    | Security exposure                 |
| CIO / CTO               | Technology and resilience         |
| Privacy Officer         | Privacy exposure                  |
| AI Governance Committee | Lifecycle governance              |
| Technical Team          | Operational telemetry             |
| Internal Audit          | Control and evidence              |

The same underlying data can therefore support multiple reporting layers.

---

### 108. Risk Appetite Dashboard

An important dashboard capability is comparison against approved risk appetite.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite: Moderate

Current Risk:
High

Status:
BREACH
```

This provides a clear management signal.

---

### 109. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable variation around an objective or risk appetite.

For example:

```text
Risk Appetite:
Moderate

Tolerance:
Temporary moderate-to-high exposure
for up to 30 days with approved treatment.
```

Risk appetite establishes the broader boundary; tolerance can define operational limits.

---

### 110. Threshold Categories

Thresholds can use several states.

A common approach is:

```text
Green
↓
Normal / Within Target

Amber
↓
Warning / Management Attention

Red
↓
Action Required

Critical
↓
Immediate Escalation
```

The terminology should be standardized within the organization.

---

### 111. Thresholds Must Have Actions

A threshold without an associated action may have limited value.

For example:

```text
Red:
Model drift > 5%

Action:
1. Investigate
2. Increase monitoring
3. Assess impact
4. Revalidate model
5. Escalate if material
```

This transforms a metric into a control mechanism.

---

### 112. Dynamic Thresholds

Some AI risks may require thresholds that change based on context.

For example:

```text
Normal Transaction Volume
→ Standard Threshold

High-Volume Period
→ Adjusted Threshold

Critical Business Event
→ More Sensitive Threshold
```

Dynamic thresholds should be controlled and documented.

---

### 113. Threshold Governance

Thresholds should be periodically reviewed.

Questions include:

* Is the threshold still appropriate?
* Has the AI system changed?
* Has the threat landscape changed?
* Has the business impact changed?
* Are there too many false positives?
* Are important events being missed?

Threshold changes should themselves be governed.

---

### 114. Alert Fatigue

Excessive alerts can reduce the effectiveness of monitoring.

For example:

```text
10,000 Alerts
      ↓
Too Many False Positives
      ↓
Analyst Fatigue
      ↓
Important Alert Missed
```

Monitoring systems should therefore prioritize alerts based on risk and severity.

---

### 115. Risk-Based Alerting

A useful model is:

```text
Likelihood
    ×
Impact
    ×
Confidence
    ×
Exposure
```

The resulting priority can help determine which alerts require immediate investigation.

The exact calculation should be defined by the organization's risk methodology.

---

### 116. Confidence as a Measurement Dimension

AI systems may produce confidence scores.

However:

> **Model confidence does not automatically mean model correctness.**

A model may be highly confident and still be wrong.

Therefore, confidence should be evaluated against actual performance.

---

### 117. Calibration

Calibration evaluates whether confidence scores correspond reasonably to actual outcomes.

For example:

```text
Model Confidence = 90%

Actual Correctness ≈ 90%
```

If the model is correct only 60% of the time when reporting 90% confidence, the model may be poorly calibrated.

---

### 118. AI Performance Thresholds

Performance thresholds should be tied to business requirements.

For example:

```text
Customer Support AI
Minimum Acceptable Accuracy = 90%

Fraud Detection AI
Maximum False-Negative Rate = 1%

Critical Safety AI
Specific error threshold established
through safety requirements
```

The appropriate threshold depends on the consequences of failure.

---

### 119. Risk-Adjusted Performance

AI performance should not be evaluated solely by technical metrics.

Consider:

```text
Model Accuracy
+
Business Impact
+
Risk Exposure
+
User Impact
+
Regulatory Requirements
```

A technically accurate model may still create unacceptable risk if used in an inappropriate context.

---

### 120. Business Impact Metrics

AI risk reporting should include business consequences where relevant.

Examples include:

* Revenue impact.
* Customer impact.
* Operational disruption.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Financial loss.
* Productivity impact.
* Reputational impact.

This translates technical risk into business language.

---

### 121. Customer Impact Metrics

For customer-facing AI systems, metrics may include:

* Customer complaints.
* Escalation rate.
* Incorrect responses.
* Abandonment rate.
* Customer-impacting incidents.
* Human escalation rate.

These can reveal risks that technical performance metrics may not capture.

---

### 122. AI Availability Versus Safety

A common governance mistake is treating availability as the highest priority.

For example:

```text
Availability = 99.99%
```

does not necessarily mean the AI system is safe or trustworthy.

A system could be continuously available while producing harmful or incorrect outputs.

Therefore, availability should be assessed together with:

* Accuracy.
* Security.
* Safety.
* Reliability.
* Governance.

---

### 123. AI Reliability Metrics

Reliability can include:

* Failure rate.
* Error rate.
* Availability.
* Mean time between failures.
* Recovery time.
* Dependency failures.

For critical AI systems, reliability should be part of the overall risk assessment.

---

### 124. Model Change Metrics

Organizations should track significant model changes.

Examples:

* Model version changes.
* Prompt changes.
* Fine-tuning.
* Training-data changes.
* Configuration changes.
* Retrieval-source changes.
* Tool changes.

A material change may trigger reassessment.

---

### 125. Change Risk Indicator

A useful KRI can be:

```text
Material AI Changes
Without Completed Reassessment
```

A rising number indicates potential lifecycle-governance weakness.

---

### 126. Model Version Governance

Each production model should have traceable version information.

Example:

```text

Approved: 15 July
Validation: Passed
Production: 20 July
Monitoring: Active
```

This makes it easier to correlate incidents with specific model versions.

---

### 127. Prompt and Configuration Governance

For generative AI systems, the model itself may not change while prompts or configurations do.

Therefore, governance may also track:

* System prompts.
* Guardrails.
* Retrieval configuration.
* Temperature.
* Tool permissions.
* Context configuration.

Material configuration changes should be governed appropriately.

---

### 128. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Metrics

For RAG systems, organizations may monitor:

* Retrieval accuracy.
* Retrieval relevance.
* Groundedness.
* Citation accuracy.
* Retrieval failure rate.
* Unsupported answer rate.

This helps separate model problems from retrieval or knowledge-base problems.

---

### 129. Agentic AI Metrics

For AI agents, traditional model metrics may be insufficient.

Additional metrics may include:

* Number of tool calls.
* Failed tool calls.
* Unauthorized tool attempts.
* High-risk actions.
* Human overrides.
* Autonomous action rate.
* Action reversal rate.
* Escalation rate.

Agent governance should therefore measure **actions**, not just generated text.

---

### 130. Agent Action Risk

A useful conceptual model is:

```text

   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Tool Invocation
   ↓
External Action
```

Risk can increase significantly as the AI moves from generating information toward executing actions.

---

### 131. Autonomy Metrics

Organizations may track the proportion of actions performed without human approval.

For example:

```text
Autonomous Actions
------------------ × 100
Total AI Actions
```

The acceptable level depends on:

* Impact.
* Reversibility.
* Business context.
* Risk classification.

---

### 132. Reversibility Metrics

For autonomous systems, organizations may classify actions as:

* Easily reversible.
* Moderately reversible.
* Difficult to reverse.
* Irreversible.

Higher-risk actions may require stronger approval and monitoring.

---

### 133. AI Control Effectiveness Dashboard

A control dashboard may include:

```text
Access Control                 98%
Human Review                   96%
Security Testing              100%
Monitoring                     97%
Risk Assessment               100%
Change Approval                94%
Incident Response Testing      92%
```

The objective is to identify control weaknesses before they become major incidents.

---

### 134. Control Failure Rate

A simple metric is:

```text
Failed Control Tests
-------------------- × 100
Total Control Tests
```

However, criticality should also be considered.

One critical control failure may deserve more attention than dozens of low-risk failures.

---

### 135. Control Coverage

Control coverage measures whether required risks have corresponding controls.

```text
Risks With Required Controls
---------------------------- × 100
Risks Requiring Controls
```

A low value indicates potential control gaps.

---

### 136. Control Effectiveness Versus Coverage

These concepts should not be confused.

**Coverage:**

> Do we have controls?

**Effectiveness:**

> Do the controls work?

Example:

```text
Control Coverage = 100%
Control Effectiveness = 65%
```

The organization has controls, but many are not operating effectively.

---

AI risk may become concentrated in particular:

* Business units.
* Vendors.
* Models.
* Data sources.
* Cloud providers.
* Critical applications.

Concentration analysis helps identify systemic dependencies.

### 138. AI Dependency Metrics

An organization may track:

```text
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓
```

```text
          Low Medium High
              Likelihood
```

The heatmap should support decisions rather than replace detailed risk analysis.

Risk reporting should show whether exposure is:


Risk B may require faster detection and response.

Organizations may also consider how long a risk remains unresolved.

For example:

```text
↓

↓

↓
```

The longer a material risk remains open, the greater the potential exposure.

### 143. Risk Aging

Risk-aging reports can categorize open risks:

```text
91–180 days
180+ days
```

Long-aged risks should be reviewed by appropriate management.

---

### 144. Risk Treatment Effectiveness

A treatment should not be considered successful merely because it was implemented.

The organization should determine:

> Did the treatment actually reduce the risk?

For example:

```text

High Risk

Moderate Risk

Confirmed
```

This demonstrates measurable risk reduction.

### 145. Management Action Tracking

AI risk reporting should track decisions requiring management action.

Examples:

* Approve risk acceptance.
* Fund remediation.
* Restrict AI capability.
* Suspend deployment.

* Replace vendor.

A useful report should identify:

```text
Owner

```

### 146. Exception Escalation

```text
   ↓
```

Reporting frequency should be proportional to risk.

```text
→ Monthly / Quarterly

```

The exact frequency should reflect the organization's environment.

Not every metric needs real-time reporting.

Real-time or near-real-time monitoring may be appropriate for:

* Security events.
* Critical autonomous actions.
* Fraud.
* Safety events.

Periodic reporting may be sufficient for:

* Governance maturity.
* Policy compliance.
* Risk inventory.
* Strategic AI exposure.

Metrics should sometimes be analyzed together.

For example:

```text

       +

       +

```

Together, these indicators provide stronger evidence that the AI system may require reassessment.

A single metric might not reveal the problem.

---

### 150. From Dashboard to Decision

The ultimate purpose of AI risk reporting is decision support.

The complete process is:

```text
DATA
 ↓
METRIC
 ↓
INDICATOR
 ↓
TREND
 ↓
THRESHOLD
 ↓
RISK INTERPRETATION
 ↓
MANAGEMENT DECISION
 ↓
ACTION
 ↓
VALIDATION
```

A mature AI governance program therefore does not measure everything simply because it can.

It identifies the **smallest useful set of reliable metrics that provide management with actionable visibility into AI performance, control effectiveness, and risk exposure**.

### 154. Single Source of Truth

Where possible, AI reporting should rely on authoritative sources.

For example:

```text

     ↓

     ↓

     ↓

```

Instead of:

```text

Spreadsheet B
Email
Manual Report
Presentation
```

Multiple uncontrolled sources increase the likelihood of inconsistent reporting.

### 155. Metric Data Lineage

Metric lineage explains how a reported number was produced.

For example:

```text

   ↓
Log Collection
   ↓
Data Processing
   ↓
Metric Calculation
   ↓
Dashboard
   ↓
Executive Report
```

This improves auditability and confidence in reporting.

---

### 156. Metric Calculation Integrity

Metric calculations should be:

* Consistent.
* Reproducible.
* Documented.
* Protected from unauthorized modification.

If a critical metric changes because someone manually altered a spreadsheet formula, management may make decisions using unreliable information.

---

### 157. Manual Versus Automated Metrics

Metrics can be collected manually or automatically.

**Manual:**

* Governance assessments.
* Risk acceptance.
* Management reviews.

**Automated:**

* Security events.
* Availability.
* API activity.
* Model telemetry.
* Access events.

Automation can improve consistency, but automated metrics still require validation.

---

### 158. Automated Metrics Are Not Automatically Reliable

Automation can produce inaccurate results if:

* Data sources are incomplete.
* Sensors are incorrectly configured.
* Events are misclassified.
* Integration fails.
* Calculation logic is incorrect.

Therefore:

> Automation reduces manual effort, but it does not eliminate the need for measurement assurance.

---

### 159. Metric Validation

Critical metrics should be periodically validated.

Validation may confirm:

* Correct data source.
* Correct calculation.
* Correct classification.
* Correct threshold.
* Correct reporting.

For example:

```text
Dashboard:
AI Incident Rate = 2.5%

Validation:
Source logs → 2.5%
Calculation → Correct
Classification → Correct
```

---

### 160. Metric Quality Dimensions

Metric quality can be evaluated across:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Timeliness.
* Consistency.
* Relevance.
* Reliability.
* Traceability.

A metric can be technically correct but still be useless if it is irrelevant to the decision being made.

---

### 161. Metric Relevance

Organizations should periodically ask:

> Does this metric still help us manage risk?

If the answer is no, the metric may need to be:

* Removed.
* Replaced.
* Combined.
* Reclassified.

This prevents dashboard overload.

---

### 162. Metric Overload

Too many metrics can reduce decision quality.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Too Much Information
      ↓
Attention Diluted
      ↓
Important Risk Lost
```

A mature program prioritizes meaningful indicators.

---

### 163. Metric Prioritization

Metrics can be categorized into:

**Tier 1 — Critical**

Required for executive or risk decisions.

**Tier 2 — Important**

Required for management.

**Tier 3 — Operational**

Used by technical teams.

**Tier 4 — Diagnostic**

Used for investigations.

This creates different levels of reporting.

---

### 164. Metrics and Materiality

Not every metric deserves executive attention.

Executive reporting should emphasize metrics connected to material:

* Financial impact.
* Customer impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Security exposure.
* Strategic risk.
* Operational disruption.

Materiality helps determine reporting priority.

---

### 165. Risk-Based Reporting

AI reporting should be proportional to risk.

```text
Low Risk
→ Basic Metrics

Moderate Risk
→ Periodic Metrics + Review

High Risk
→ Detailed Metrics + Thresholds + Escalation

Critical Risk
→ Enhanced / Continuous Monitoring
```

This avoids applying excessive governance to low-risk use cases.

---

### 166. Metric Relationships

Metrics should be analyzed as a system.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Model Performance ↓
      ↓
Customer Complaints ↑
      ↓
AI Incident Rate ↑
```

This creates a causal chain that can be more useful than reviewing each metric separately.

---

### 167. Correlated Indicators

Multiple indicators moving in the same direction can provide stronger evidence.

For example:

```text

Error Rate        ↑
Human Overrides   ↑
Complaints        ↑
```

Together, these may indicate deteriorating model performance.

---

### 168. Metric Threshold Calibration

Thresholds should be calibrated against:

* Historical performance.
* Expected operating range.
* Business impact.
* Risk appetite.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Security requirements.

Poorly calibrated thresholds can either:

* Generate too many alerts.
* Fail to detect meaningful risk.

---

### 169. Threshold Review

Threshold review should occur after:

* Major model changes.
* Business changes.
* New threats.
* Significant incidents.
* Regulatory changes.
* Changes in data.
* Significant performance changes.

This ensures thresholds remain relevant.

---

### 170. Benchmarking

Organizations may compare AI metrics against:

* Historical internal performance.
* Business targets.
* Industry benchmarks.
* Vendor commitments.
* Regulatory expectations.

Benchmarking can identify abnormal performance.

However, external benchmarks should not automatically become internal targets because organizational context may differ.

### 171. Internal Benchmarking


* Differences in risk are considered.

### 172. External Benchmarking

External benchmarks may help management understand whether performance is broadly reasonable.

Potential sources include:

* Industry studies.
* Security reports.

* Vendor benchmarks.
* Independent assessments.

External benchmarks should be interpreted carefully.

### 173. AI Risk Scorecards

A scorecard can combine several dimensions.

| ----------------- | ----: |

### 174. Composite Risk Scores

A composite score may combine multiple risk factors.

Conceptually:

```text

```

The exact methodology should be defined by the organization's enterprise risk framework.

The score should be explainable.

---

### 175. Avoiding False Precision

A score such as:

may imply greater precision than the underlying assessment supports.

Risk scores should therefore communicate appropriate confidence and limitations.

For example:

```text

High

Confidence:
Moderate

Trend:
Increasing
```

This may be more meaningful than an overly precise numerical score.

---

### 176. Confidence in Risk Metrics

Organizations should consider confidence in their measurements.

Confidence may be affected by:

* Data quality.
* Sample size.
* Measurement methodology.
* Missing information.
* Model uncertainty.

For example:

```text
Risk:
High

Confidence:
Low

Reason:
Insufficient production data
```

This tells management that additional evidence may be required.

---

### 177. Statistical Significance

Where appropriate, organizations can use statistical methods to determine whether observed changes are meaningful.

For example:

```text
Error Rate:
2.0% → 2.2%
```

may not be meaningful if normal variation is ±0.5%.

But:

```text
2.0% → 7.5%
```

may represent a significant change.

Statistical interpretation should be appropriate to the use case.

---

### 178. Sample Size

AI metrics based on small samples may be unreliable.

For example:

```text
10 Evaluations
→ 90% Accuracy
```

does not provide the same confidence as:

```text
100,000 Evaluations
→ 90% Accuracy
```

The measurement methodology should therefore account for sample size.

---

### 179. Measurement Bias

The measurement process itself can introduce bias.

For example, if AI performance is evaluated only on easy test cases, the reported performance may appear better than real-world performance.

Testing populations should therefore be appropriately representative.

---

### 180. Measurement Drift

The measurement methodology can become outdated.

For example:

```text

      ↓
New Model Capability
      ↓
New User Population
      ↓
Original Metric No Longer Captures Risk
```

Metrics should evolve with the system.

### 200. The Mature AI Measurement Model

The complete measurement model can be summarized as:

```text
AI OBJECTIVE
     ↓
AI RISK
     ↓
RISK APPETITE
     ↓
CONTROL
     ↓
KPI / KCI / KRI
     ↓
DATA
     ↓
MEASUREMENT
     ↓
THRESHOLD
     ↓
TREND
     ↓
RISK INTERPRETATION
     ↓
MANAGEMENT DECISION
     ↓
ACTION
     ↓
VALIDATION
     ↓
CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT
```

The central principle of **05.18** is that AI metrics should not become a reporting exercise where the organization simply collects numbers.

A mature AI risk measurement program creates a **closed feedback loop between evidence and governance**.

The organization measures AI performance and risk, compares results against defined thresholds and risk appetite, investigates meaningful deviations, escalates material issues, implements treatment, validates whether the treatment worked, and continuously improves the measurement framework itself.

In practical GRC terms:

> **KPI tells you how well the process or system is performing. KCI tells you whether the control is functioning. KRI tells you how the risk is changing. Management action connects all three to actual risk governance.**
