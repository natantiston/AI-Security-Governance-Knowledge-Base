# 05.01 — Introduction to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework

## Part 1 — Foundations of the NIST AI RMF

### 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence creates opportunities for organizations to improve productivity, automate decisions, analyze information, develop new products, and transform business processes.

At the same time, AI introduces risks that may not be adequately addressed through traditional technology or cybersecurity risk management alone.

AI systems can produce:

* Incorrect outputs.
* Biased outcomes.
* Unreliable predictions.
* Unsafe behavior.
* Privacy problems.
* Security vulnerabilities.
* Lack of transparency.
* Unexpected impacts on individuals or groups.
* Operational and reputational damage.

The **NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** provides a structured approach for organizations to manage these risks.

The central purpose of the AI RMF is to help organizations:

> **Manage risks associated with AI while promoting trustworthy and responsible development and use of AI systems.**

---

# 2. What Is the NIST AI RMF?

The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** is a framework developed by the **U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)** to help organizations manage AI risks.

The framework was designed to be:

* Voluntary.
* Flexible.
* Technology-neutral.
* Use-case independent.
* Adaptable to different organizations.
* Applicable across the AI lifecycle.

The AI RMF is intended to help organizations incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, deployment, use, and evaluation of AI systems.

---

# 3. Why Was the AI RMF Created?

Traditional risk-management approaches were largely developed around conventional information systems, business processes, financial risks, operational risks, and cybersecurity.

AI introduces additional dimensions.

For example, a traditional application may be evaluated primarily for:

```text
Availability
Confidentiality
Integrity
Security
```

An AI system may additionally require consideration of:

```text
Accuracy
Reliability
Bias
Explainability
Interpretability
Privacy
Safety
Human oversight
Accountability
```

The AI RMF provides a common structure for considering these dimensions.

---

# 4. AI Risk Is Sociotechnical

One of the most important concepts in the NIST AI RMF is that AI risk is not purely technical.

AI operates within a broader **sociotechnical system**.

A useful model is:

```text
                 AI MODEL
                    │
        ┌───────────┼───────────┐
        ▼           ▼           ▼
       Data      Technology    Software
        │           │           │
        └───────────┼───────────┘
                    ▼
                 People
                    │
                    ▼
               Organization
                    │
                    ▼
                  Society
```

The behavior and impact of an AI system can therefore depend on:

* Data.
* Algorithms.
* Infrastructure.
* Developers.
* Users.
* Business processes.
* Organizational decisions.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Social context.

This is why AI risk management cannot be reduced to model testing alone.

---

# 5. AI Risk Management

AI risk management is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, prioritizing, treating, monitoring, and communicating risks associated with AI.

A simplified lifecycle is:

```text
Identify
   ↓
Understand
   ↓
Measure
   ↓
Prioritize
   ↓
Treat
   ↓
Monitor
   ↓
Improve
```

The AI RMF organizes these activities around four core Functions:

```text
        GOVERN
           │
           ▼
          MAP
           │
           ▼
        MEASURE
           │
           ▼
         MANAGE
           │
           └──────► Continuous Feedback
```

These four Functions are the foundation of the AI RMF.

---

# 6. The Four AI RMF Functions

## GOVERN

**GOVERN** establishes the organizational structures and processes needed to manage AI risk.

It addresses questions such as:

* Who is accountable?
* What policies apply?
* What laws and regulations matter?
* What risk culture exists?
* Who owns AI risks?
* How are third parties governed?

---

## MAP

**MAP** establishes the context in which AI risks exist.

It asks:

* What is the AI system?
* What is its purpose?
* Who uses it?
* Who can be affected?
* What data does it use?
* What could go wrong?
* What is the operating environment?

---

## MEASURE

**MEASURE** evaluates and monitors identified AI risks.

It asks:

* How significant is the risk?
* How reliable is the system?
* How accurate is it?
* How secure is it?
* Are harmful biases present?
* Are controls effective?
* What evidence supports our conclusions?

---

## MANAGE

**MANAGE** prioritizes and responds to AI risks.

It asks:

* Which risks require action?
* What treatment is appropriate?
* Who owns the treatment?
* Should the risk be accepted?
* Should the AI system be modified?
* Should deployment be restricted?
* Should the system be discontinued?

---

# 7. The AI RMF Core

The **AI RMF Core** is the central operational structure of the framework.

It contains:

1. **GOVERN**
2. **MAP**
3. **MEASURE**
4. **MANAGE**

Each Function contains more detailed:

* Categories.
* Subcategories.

This creates a hierarchy:

```text
AI RMF
  │
  └── Core
       │
       ├── GOVERN
       │    ├── Categories
       │    └── Subcategories
       │
       ├── MAP
       │    ├── Categories
       │    └── Subcategories
       │
       ├── MEASURE
       │    ├── Categories
       │    └── Subcategories
       │
       └── MANAGE
            ├── Categories
            └── Subcategories
```

This structure allows organizations to move from broad AI governance concepts toward specific implementation activities.

---

# 8. The AI RMF Is Not a Checklist

A common misunderstanding is to treat the AI RMF as a simple compliance checklist.

That is not its intended role.

The framework provides a **risk-management structure** that organizations can adapt to their circumstances.

For example, two organizations may both use the AI RMF but implement it differently.

### Organization A

A financial institution may emphasize:

* Fraud detection.
* Customer impact.
* Model risk.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Explainability.
* Data governance.

### Organization B

A telecommunications organization may emphasize:

* Network optimization.
* Customer analytics.
* Security.
* Operational resilience.
* Supplier AI.
* Automated decision-making.

The framework remains useful because its core risk-management principles can be adapted to different contexts.

---

# 9. Voluntary and Flexible Nature

The NIST AI RMF is designed as a voluntary framework.

This means organizations can use it as a structured approach to AI risk management without treating the framework itself as a universal legal requirement.

However, organizations may still have mandatory obligations arising from:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Contracts.
* Industry requirements.
* Internal policies.
* Customer commitments.

Therefore:

> **The AI RMF can support compliance, but using the AI RMF does not automatically mean that an organization is compliant with every applicable legal or regulatory requirement.**

This distinction is important for GRC professionals.

---

# 10. AI RMF and Enterprise Risk Management

AI risk should not exist in isolation from enterprise risk management.

A mature organization should connect:

```text
Enterprise Risk Management
          │
          ├── Financial Risk
          ├── Operational Risk
          ├── Cybersecurity Risk
          ├── Privacy Risk
          ├── Compliance Risk
          └── AI Risk
```

AI risk may overlap with several existing risk categories.

For example:

**AI-generated customer misinformation**

could involve:

* Operational risk.
* Reputational risk.
* Compliance risk.
* Customer risk.
* AI risk.

The AI RMF helps provide an AI-specific perspective while remaining compatible with broader enterprise risk management.

---

# 11. AI RMF and Cybersecurity

AI security is an important part of AI risk management, but cybersecurity does not cover every AI risk.

For example:

| Risk                   | Primarily Cybersecurity? | AI Governance Concern? |
| ---------------------- | -----------------------: | ---------------------: |
| Model theft            |                      Yes |                    Yes |
| Prompt injection       |                      Yes |                    Yes |
| Data poisoning         |                      Yes |                    Yes |
| Unauthorized access    |                      Yes |                    Yes |
| Algorithmic bias       |                       No |                    Yes |
| Lack of explainability |                       No |                    Yes |
| Unsafe AI output       |                Partially |                    Yes |
| Poor model reliability |                       No |                    Yes |
| Privacy impact         |                Partially |                    Yes |

This demonstrates why AI governance requires a broader perspective than cybersecurity alone.

# 12. AI RMF and AI Lifecycle

The AI RMF can be applied throughout the AI lifecycle.

```text
AI Concept
    ↓
Design
    ↓
Development
    ↓
Testing
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Deployment
    ↓
Operation
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Modification
    ↓
Retirement
```

AI risk management should not begin only when the AI system reaches production.

Risks should be considered as early as possible.

---

# 13. AI Risk Is Dynamic

AI risk can change over time.

A system that was considered acceptable when deployed may become higher risk because:

* Its purpose changes.
* New data is introduced.
* The user population expands.
* The model changes.
* The threat environment changes.
* Regulations change.
* The AI becomes more autonomous.
* New vulnerabilities emerge.
* New impacts are identified.

Therefore:

> **AI risk management must be continuous rather than a one-time assessment.**

---

# 14. Trustworthy AI

The AI RMF places significant emphasis on trustworthy AI characteristics.

Trustworthiness is multidimensional.

An AI system may need to be:

* Valid and reliable.
* Safe.
* Secure and resilient.
* Accountable and transparent.
* Explainable and interpretable.
* Privacy-enhanced.
* Fair, with harmful biases managed.

These characteristics can interact with each other.

For example:

```text
Higher Security
      ↕
Higher Transparency

Higher Privacy
      ↕
Greater Data Availability

Higher Accuracy
      ↕
Potentially Greater Complexity
```

AI governance therefore requires balancing different objectives based on context and risk.

---

# 15. AI RMF Risk Management Philosophy

The AI RMF is fundamentally based on the idea that organizations should make **informed, context-specific decisions about AI risk**.

The objective is not necessarily to eliminate every possible risk.

Instead, organizations should:

```text
Understand Risk
      ↓
Evaluate Risk
      ↓
Prioritize Risk
      ↓
Manage Risk
      ↓
Monitor Risk
```

This is consistent with the broader principles of enterprise risk management.

---

# 16. AI RMF as a Governance Framework

From a GRC perspective, the AI RMF can be viewed as a bridge between:

```text
Business Objectives
       ↓
AI Use Cases
       ↓
AI Risk
       ↓
Governance
       ↓
Controls
       ↓
Assurance
```

This makes the framework particularly useful for cybersecurity and GRC professionals moving into AI governance.

---

# 17. Practical Example

Consider an organization deploying an AI system to screen job applications.

A simplistic approach might ask:

> "Is the AI model technically accurate?"

The AI RMF encourages broader questions:

### GOVERN

Who is accountable for the AI system?

### MAP

Who is affected by its decisions?

### MEASURE

Does the system perform differently across relevant groups?

### MANAGE

What should the organization do if harmful bias is detected?

The resulting governance model is therefore much broader than technical model testing.

---

# 18. AI RMF and Risk-Based Thinking

The framework encourages organizations to prioritize resources according to risk.

For example:

```text
AI System A
Low Impact
Low Risk
      ↓
Standard Governance

AI System B
Moderate Impact
Moderate Risk
      ↓
Enhanced Governance

AI System C
High Impact
High Risk
      ↓
Enhanced Assessment
+ Strong Controls
+ Greater Oversight
+ Continuous Monitoring
```

This allows governance to remain proportional.

---

# 19. The GRC Perspective

For a GRC professional, the AI RMF can be understood through five questions:

### Governance

**Who is accountable?**

### Risk

**What can go wrong?**

### Assessment

**How significant is the risk?**

### Treatment

**What are we going to do about it?**

### Assurance

**How do we know the risk is being managed effectively?**

These questions connect the AI RMF with conventional GRC practices.

---

# 20. The Most Important Mental Model

Do not memorize the AI RMF simply as:

> GOVERN → MAP → MEASURE → MANAGE.

Understand what each word means operationally:

```text
GOVERN
Set the rules, accountability and organizational foundation.

MAP
Understand the AI system, context, stakeholders and risks.

MEASURE
Evaluate, test and monitor AI risks and trustworthiness.

MANAGE
Prioritize, treat and continuously manage those risks.
```

Together:

> **GOVERN establishes the foundation, MAP creates understanding, MEASURE provides evidence, and MANAGE drives risk decisions.**

That is the core mental model we will build throughout Chapter 05.
