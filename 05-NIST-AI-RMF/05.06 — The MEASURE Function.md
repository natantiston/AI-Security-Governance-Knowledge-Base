# 05.06 — The MEASURE Function

## Part 1 — Introduction to MEASURE

## 1. Introduction

The **MEASURE** function of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) focuses on **analyzing, assessing, benchmarking, and monitoring AI risks using appropriate evidence, metrics, and evaluation methods**.

If GOVERN establishes the organization's approach to AI risk and MAP establishes the context in which an AI system operates, MEASURE provides the mechanisms for determining:

* What risks actually exist.
* How significant those risks are.
* How well the AI system performs.
* Whether identified risks are changing.
* Whether controls are effective.
* Whether the AI system continues to operate within acceptable boundaries.

MEASURE therefore transforms contextual information into **evidence-based understanding of AI risk**.

A simplified representation is:

```text
GOVERN
Establish the organizational approach
          ↓
MAP
Understand the AI system and its context
          ↓
MEASURE
Evaluate and monitor AI risks
          ↓
MANAGE
Prioritize and respond to those risks
```

---

# 2. Purpose of MEASURE

The primary purpose of MEASURE is to establish an evidence-based understanding of AI risks and the effectiveness of risk controls.

An organization should be able to answer questions such as:

> Is the AI system performing as expected?

> Is the system producing harmful or unacceptable outcomes?

> Are identified risks within organizational tolerance?

> Are implemented controls working?

> Has the risk profile changed?

> Is additional action required?

MEASURE provides the evidence needed to answer these questions.

---

# 3. MEASURE Is More Than Testing

One of the most important concepts is that **MEASURE is broader than technical testing**.

AI evaluation may include:

* Model testing.
* Security testing.
* Privacy assessment.
* Bias and fairness evaluation.
* Accuracy measurement.
* Robustness testing.
* Reliability assessment.
* Explainability evaluation.
* Human oversight assessment.
* Operational monitoring.
* Incident analysis.
* Control effectiveness testing.

Therefore:

> **MEASURE is an integrated risk-evaluation function, not simply a model-testing activity.**

---

# 4. MEASURE and Evidence

AI risk decisions should be supported by evidence.

Evidence may include:

* Test results.
* Model performance metrics.
* Security assessment results.
* Privacy assessment results.
* Audit findings.
* Monitoring data.
* Incident records.
* User feedback.
* Red-team findings.
* Validation results.
* Control-testing results.

For example:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Evaluation
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Risk Understanding
    ↓
Risk Decision
```

Without evidence, risk assessment can become subjective.

---

# 5. MEASURE and the AI RMF

MEASURE operates as one of the four core functions of the NIST AI RMF.

The four functions can be understood as:

| Function | Primary Question                                   |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| GOVERN   | How will the organization govern AI risk?          |
| MAP      | What is the AI system and its context?             |
| MEASURE  | What risks exist and how significant are they?     |
| MANAGE   | What should the organization do about those risks? |

MEASURE therefore acts as the **evaluation and evidence layer** of the framework.

---

# 6. MEASURE and MAP

MEASURE depends heavily on the context established through MAP.

For example, suppose MAP identifies:

* AI system used for fraud detection.
* High business criticality.
* Customer impact.
* Sensitive financial data.
* Human review.
* External AI provider.

MEASURE can then determine what should be evaluated.

Possible measurements include:

* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Detection accuracy.
* Data leakage.
* Model robustness.
* Human-review effectiveness.
* Provider availability.
* Operational failure rates.

The context determines what measurements are meaningful.

---

# 7. Context Determines Measurement

There is no universal set of AI metrics that applies equally to every system.

For example:

### AI Meeting Summarizer

Important measurements may include:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Hallucination rate.
* User satisfaction.

### Medical Decision Support

Potentially important measurements may include:

* Clinical accuracy.
* False-negative rate.
* False-positive rate.
* Safety.
* Reliability.
* Population-specific performance.

### Autonomous Security System

Potentially important measurements may include:

* Detection accuracy.
* False-positive rate.
* Response time.
* Unauthorized actions.
* System availability.
* Containment effectiveness.

The appropriate MEASURE activities depend on the AI system's purpose and potential impact.

---

# 8. Risk-Based Measurement

MEASURE should be **risk-based**.

An organization should not spend the same amount of evaluation effort on every AI system.

For example:

```text
Low Impact
    ↓
Basic Evaluation
    ↓
Periodic Review

Moderate Impact
    ↓
Expanded Evaluation
    ↓
Regular Monitoring

High Impact
    ↓
Extensive Evaluation
    ↓
Continuous / Frequent Monitoring
```

This supports proportional governance.

---

# 9. MEASURE and Trustworthy AI

MEASURE should evaluate characteristics associated with trustworthy AI.

These may include:

* Validity and reliability.
* Safety.
* Security and resilience.
* Accountability and transparency.
* Explainability and interpretability.
* Privacy enhancement.
* Fairness with harmful bias managed.

The exact evaluation approach depends on the AI system.

---

# 10. MEASURE and AI Performance

AI performance measurement determines whether the system is performing its intended function.

Examples include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* Error rate.
* Latency.
* Availability.
* Throughput.
* Task completion rate.

However, technical performance alone does not establish that an AI system is trustworthy.

A model can perform well technically while still creating unacceptable business, privacy, security, or societal risks.

---

# 11. Technical Performance Versus Risk

Consider an AI customer-support system with 98% response accuracy.

That sounds positive.

However, suppose the remaining 2% of errors involve:

* Incorrect financial advice.
* Disclosure of confidential information.
* Incorrect account actions.

The overall accuracy may look strong while the residual risk remains significant.

Therefore:

> **MEASURE must evaluate both system performance and risk significance.**

---

# 12. MEASURE and Risk Outcomes

The organization should distinguish between:

### Performance Metrics

Measure how well the AI performs its intended task.

### Risk Metrics

Measure undesirable outcomes or exposure.

### Control Metrics

Measure whether safeguards are operating effectively.

For example:

| Metric Type | Example                                     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Performance | 95% classification accuracy                 |
| Risk        | 4% harmful error rate                       |
| Control     | 99% of high-risk outputs reviewed by humans |

This provides a more complete picture.

---

# 13. MEASURE and Risk Indicators

A **risk indicator** provides information about the level or direction of risk.

Examples:

* Increase in hallucination incidents.
* Increase in unauthorized AI use.
* Increase in false negatives.
* Increase in privacy violations.
* Increase in human-review bypasses.
* Increase in security incidents.

Risk indicators can help identify deterioration before it becomes a major incident.

---

# 14. MEASURE and Key Risk Indicators

Organizations can establish **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** for important AI systems.

Example:

| KRI                 | Threshold | Current |
| ------------------- | --------: | ------: |
| Hallucination rate  |      ≤ 3% |    2.1% |
| False-negative rate |      ≤ 5% |    4.2% |
| Unauthorized access |         0 |       0 |
| Human-review bypass |      ≤ 1% |    0.4% |

Thresholds should be established according to organizational risk appetite and the characteristics of the AI system.

---

# 15. MEASURE and Key Performance Indicators

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) can measure whether the AI achieves its intended business objective.

For example:

> AI fraud detection improves analyst investigation efficiency by 30%.

That is a performance indicator.

However:

> False-negative fraud detection rate remains below the organization's accepted threshold.

That is a risk-oriented measure.

Both should be considered.

---

# 16. MEASURE and Control Effectiveness

MEASURE should also determine whether controls actually work.

For example:

### Control

All high-risk AI recommendations require human approval.

### Measurement

Percentage of high-risk recommendations that received documented human approval.

If:

```text
Required approvals = 10,000
Actual approvals = 9,950
```

Then:

```text
Control effectiveness = 99.5%
```

The organization can then determine whether the remaining gap is acceptable.

---

# 17. MEASURE and Validation

**Validation** determines whether the AI system meets its intended requirements in its actual operating context.

For example:

> Does the fraud-detection model perform adequately against representative transaction data?

Validation should consider the system's intended purpose and operating environment.

---

# 18. MEASURE and Verification

Verification asks whether the system was built or configured according to specified requirements.

For example:

> Does the implemented access-control mechanism enforce the documented authorization requirements?

A simplified distinction is:

```text
Verification
"Did we build/configure it correctly?"

Validation
"Does it work appropriately for its intended purpose?"
```

Both can be important in AI governance.

---

# 19. MEASURE and Testing

Testing provides evidence about system behavior.

Testing may occur:

### Before Deployment

* Development testing.
* Model validation.
* Security testing.
* Privacy testing.
* Robustness testing.

### During Deployment

* Production validation.
* Monitoring.
* Sampling.
* Control testing.

### After Deployment

* Periodic reassessment.
* Regression testing.
* Incident-driven testing.
* Change-driven testing.

---

# 20. MEASURE Across the AI Lifecycle

MEASURE should not be limited to the development phase.

A lifecycle view is:

```text
Concept
   ↓
Initial Evaluation
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Reassessment
   ↓

```

AI systems can change after deployment, so measurement must continue throughout their lifecycle.

---

# 21. Pre-Deployment Measurement

Before deployment, the organization may evaluate:

* Model performance.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Bias.
* Robustness.
* Explainability.
* Reliability.
* Data quality.
* Human oversight.

The objective is to determine whether the system is sufficiently understood and controlled before it enters production.

---

# 22. Production Measurement

Once deployed, evaluation should consider actual operational behavior.

Examples include:

* Real-world performance.
* Production error rates.
* User behavior.
* Incident rates.
* Drift.
* Control effectiveness.
* Availability.
* Security events.

This can reveal risks that were not visible during testing.

---

# 23. Continuous Measurement

For higher-risk AI systems, organizations may establish continuous or frequent monitoring.

For example:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Telemetry
    ↓
Metrics
    ↓
Thresholds
    ↓
Alerts
    ↓
Investigation
    ↓
Risk Decision
```

Continuous measurement can provide early warning of emerging problems.

---

# 24. Measurement Frequency

Measurement frequency should be proportional to risk.

Factors may include:

* AI criticality.
* Potential impact.
* Autonomy.
* Data sensitivity.
* Rate of change.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Model volatility.
* Business dependence.

A highly critical autonomous system may require substantially more frequent measurement than an internal productivity assistant.

---

# 25. MEASURE and AI Risk Thresholds

Organizations should define thresholds where practical.

For example:

```text
Green
Within acceptable range

Amber
Approaching risk threshold

Red
Threshold exceeded
```

Example:

```text
False-positive rate
       ↓
< 5%      → Acceptable
5–8%      → Review
> 8%      → Escalation
```

Thresholds should be based on evidence and organizational risk tolerance rather than arbitrary numbers.

---

# 26. MEASURE and Risk Appetite

Risk appetite provides an important reference point.

For example:

> The organization has very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of customer information.

Therefore, the corresponding AI measurement may require:

* Data leakage monitoring.
* Access monitoring.
* Privacy testing.
* Incident measurement.
* Strict escalation thresholds.

Risk appetite helps determine what should be measured and how much deviation is acceptable.

---

# 27. MEASURE and Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines the acceptable variation around an objective.

For example:

> The organization may tolerate an AI response error rate below a defined threshold for a low-impact internal assistant.

However, the tolerance may be significantly lower for an AI system supporting high-impact decisions.

Thus:

```text
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Risk Tolerance
      ↓
Measurement Thresholds
      ↓
Monitoring
```

---

# 28. MEASURE and Materiality

Not every measurement has equal importance.

An organization should identify **material AI risks**.

A material risk is one that could significantly affect:

* People.
* Business operations.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.
* Financial performance.
* Reputation.

MEASURE should prioritize evidence that helps understand these material risks.

---

# 29. MEASURE and Impact

Measurements should connect to potential impact.

For example:

```text
Model Error
    ↓
Incorrect Customer Classification
    ↓
Customer Impact
```

The significance of the model error depends on what happens because of it.

This prevents organizations from focusing only on technical metrics without considering consequences.

---

# 30. MEASURE and Error Analysis

Error analysis examines why an AI system produces incorrect outputs.

Questions include:

* What types of errors occur?
* How frequently do they occur?
* Which populations are affected?
* Which data causes errors?
* Are errors concentrated in specific scenarios?
* What happens after the error?
* Can humans detect and correct the error?

This is often more valuable than simply calculating a single overall accuracy score.

---

# 31. MEASURE and Error Distribution

An overall error rate may hide important patterns.

For example:

```text
Overall Accuracy = 95%

Group A = 98%
Group B = 96%
Group C = 81%
```

The overall figure alone may therefore be misleading.

MEASURE should consider relevant distributions and segments where material differences could affect risk.

---

# 32. MEASURE and Bias Evaluation

Where relevant, organizations should assess whether AI outputs exhibit harmful or inappropriate bias.

Potential evaluation areas include:

* Demographic groups.
* Geographic groups.
* Language groups.
* Customer segments.
* Operational categories.

The appropriate evaluation depends on the use case and applicable requirements.

---

# 33. MEASURE and Fairness

Fairness is contextual.

A single universal fairness metric may not be appropriate for every AI system.

The organization should first determine:

* What decision is being made?
* Who is affected?
* What outcome matters?
* What disparity would be harmful?
* What legal or policy requirements apply?

MEASURE should then use appropriate evaluation methods.

---

# 34. MEASURE and Privacy

Privacy-related measurement may include:

* Unauthorized data exposure.
* Sensitive-data leakage.
* Data minimization compliance.
* Access violations.
* Retention violations.
* Re-identification risk.
* Privacy incidents.

For example:

> Number of production prompts containing unauthorized personal data.

This can become a useful privacy risk indicator.

---

# 35. MEASURE and Security

Security measurement can include:

* Successful attack attempts.
* Prompt injection detection.
* Unauthorized access.
* Data exfiltration.
* Vulnerability findings.
* Model abuse.
* Security incident frequency.

Security measurements should reflect the actual AI architecture and threat environment.

---

# 36. MEASURE and Robustness

**Robustness** concerns how consistently the AI performs under expected variations and challenging conditions.

Testing may include:

* Unusual inputs.
* Noisy data.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Boundary conditions.
* Missing information.
* Unexpected formats.

The objective is to understand whether system performance deteriorates under relevant conditions.

---

# 37. MEASURE and Resilience

Resilience considers whether the AI system can continue operating or recover appropriately when disrupted.

Measurements may include:

* Availability.
* Recovery time.
* Failure frequency.
* Backup effectiveness.
* Recovery success.
* Dependency failure impact.

For critical AI systems, resilience measurement may be especially important.

---

# 38. MEASURE and Reliability

Reliability refers to the consistency of system behavior over time.

Examples of measurements include:

* Failure rate.
* Error rate.
* Availability.
* Output consistency.
* Service interruptions.

Reliability should be evaluated against the system's intended operating requirements.

---

# 39. MEASURE and Explainability

Where explainability is important, measurement may examine:

* Whether explanations are available.
* Whether explanations are understandable.
* Whether explanations are consistent.
* Whether explanations accurately represent system behavior.

The appropriate level of explainability depends on the use case.

---

# 40. MEASURE and Interpretability

Interpretability concerns the extent to which stakeholders can understand how the system reaches or supports an output.

MEASURE can evaluate whether:

* Relevant stakeholders understand AI limitations.
* Decision makers understand the basis of AI recommendations.
* Explanations are sufficiently useful.
* Users can identify when human review is required.

---

# 41. MEASURE and Transparency

Transparency measurement can assess whether required information is available.

Examples:

* AI system documentation.
* Model information.
* Data-source information.
* Known limitations.
* Intended use.
* User guidance.
* Human oversight procedures.

Transparency should be evaluated relative to the system's stakeholders and governance requirements.

---

# 42. MEASURE and Human Oversight

Human oversight itself can be measured.

Examples:

* Percentage of required decisions reviewed.
* Percentage of AI recommendations overridden.
* Average review time.
* Number of review exceptions.
* Percentage of users completing AI training.

These measurements help determine whether human oversight exists **in practice**, rather than merely in policy.

---

# 43. MEASURE and Human Reliance

A system may technically have human oversight while users routinely accept AI outputs without meaningful review.

For example:

```text
Policy:
Human review required

Actual behavior:
AI recommendation accepted automatically
```

MEASURE should therefore examine actual human behavior where material.

---

# 44. MEASURE and Automation Bias

**Automation bias** occurs when humans place excessive trust in automated recommendations.

Potential indicators include:

* Very high acceptance rates.
* Very low override rates.
* Reduced human investigation.
* Failure to challenge AI outputs.

A high acceptance rate is not necessarily evidence of good AI performance.

It may indicate excessive human reliance.

---

# 45. MEASURE and User Feedback

User feedback can provide important evidence.

Sources may include:

* Surveys.
* Helpdesk tickets.
* Complaints.
* Analyst feedback.
* Operational incidents.
* Usability studies.

User feedback can reveal risks that quantitative model metrics may not capture.

---

# 46. MEASURE and Incident Data

AI incidents are important sources of measurement evidence.

Examples:

* Incorrect AI decision.
* Data leakage.
* Security compromise.
* Harmful output.
* System outage.
* Unauthorized AI use.

Incident analysis can identify:

* Root causes.
* Frequency.
* Impact.
* Control failures.
* Emerging risk patterns.

---

# 47. MEASURE and Near Misses

Organizations should also measure **near misses**.

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not.

For example:

> An AI system generated a confidential document summary and attempted to send it externally, but a human stopped the action.

Even though no actual breach occurred, the event provides valuable risk evidence.

---

# 48. MEASURE and Red Teaming

Red teaming can provide evidence about AI weaknesses under adversarial conditions.

Activities may test:

* Prompt injection.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized behavior.
* Model manipulation.
* Safety boundaries.
* Tool abuse.

Red-team results can feed the AI risk assessment and remediation process.

---

# 49. MEASURE and Scenario Testing

Scenario-based testing evaluates AI behavior under realistic situations.

For example:

> "What happens if the AI receives deliberately misleading information?"

or:

> "What happens if an attacker attempts to manipulate the AI into accessing unauthorized data?"

Scenario testing helps evaluate risks that normal functional testing may miss.

---

# 50. MEASURE and Stress Testing

Stress testing evaluates behavior under extreme or unusual conditions.

Examples:

* Very high request volumes.
* Large datasets.
* Extreme inputs.
* Multiple simultaneous failures.
* Provider outage.
* Infrastructure degradation.

This is particularly relevant for critical AI systems.

---

# 51. MEASURE and Benchmarking

Benchmarking compares AI performance against:

* Internal standards.
* Historical performance.
* Alternative models.
* Industry benchmarks.
* Defined acceptance criteria.

Benchmarking can help determine whether observed performance is acceptable.

---

# 52. MEASURE and Baselines

A baseline establishes a reference point.

For example:

```text
Initial hallucination rate = 4%

After control implementation = 2%

Target = < 2.5%
```

Without a baseline, it can be difficult to determine whether the risk environment is improving or deteriorating.

---

# 53. MEASURE and Trend Analysis

Measurement becomes more useful when trends are examined.

For example:

```text
Month 1 → 2.0%
Month 2 → 2.3%
Month 3 → 2.7%
Month 4 → 3.5%
```

The trend indicates deterioration even before a formal threshold is exceeded.

Therefore, MEASURE should consider both:

* Current value.
* Direction of change.

---

# 54. MEASURE and Drift

AI systems can experience changes in the environment that affect performance.

Examples include:

* Data distribution changes.
* User behavior changes.
* Business process changes.
* Model changes.
* Provider changes.

This can lead to **drift**.

Monitoring for relevant forms of drift is therefore an important MEASURE activity.

---

# 55. MEASURE and Data Drift

Data drift occurs when the characteristics of incoming data change.

For example:

```text
Training Data
Customer Age: 18–65

Production Data
Customer Age: 18–85
```

If the model was not designed for the new distribution, performance may degrade.

The organization should determine whether such changes are material.

---

# 56. MEASURE and Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and expected outcomes changes.

For example:

> Fraud patterns change significantly over time.

A fraud model that previously performed well may become less effective.

Measurement should therefore determine whether model assumptions remain valid.

---

# 57. MEASURE and Model Drift

Model behavior may change due to:

* Retraining.
* Fine-tuning.
* Model replacement.
* Provider updates.
* Configuration changes.

Changes should be detected and evaluated according to their significance.

---

# 58. MEASURE and Generative AI

Generative AI introduces additional measurement challenges.

Possible evaluation areas include:

* Hallucination.
* Factuality.
* Relevance.
* Toxicity.
* Prompt adherence.
* Data leakage.
* Instruction following.
* Refusal behavior.
* Output consistency.

Traditional accuracy metrics may not be sufficient for generative systems.

---

# 59. MEASURE and LLM Evaluation

For large language models, organizations may evaluate:

```text
Input
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Output
  ↓
Evaluation Criteria
  ├── Accuracy
  ├── Relevance
  ├── Safety
  ├── Security
  ├── Privacy
  └── Policy Compliance
```

Evaluation should reflect the intended use case.

---

# 60. MEASURE and Evaluation Datasets

Evaluation datasets should ideally be:

* Relevant.
* Representative.
* Sufficiently diverse.
* Appropriate for the intended use.
* Controlled.
* Documented.

Poor evaluation data can create false confidence in system performance.

---

# 61. MEASURE and Data Quality

Data quality may be evaluated across dimensions such as:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Consistency.
* Timeliness.
* Validity.
* Uniqueness.

Poor data quality can produce poor AI outcomes even when the model itself is technically sound.

---

# 62. MEASURE and Measurement Limitations

AI measurement is not perfect.

Limitations can result from:

* Incomplete data.
* Poor benchmarks.
* Limited representative datasets.
* Changing environments.
* Difficult-to-measure outcomes.
* Subjective criteria.
* Unknown risks.

Organizations should document important measurement limitations.

---

# 63. MEASURE and Uncertainty

AI risk assessments often contain uncertainty.

For example:

> The organization may not know exactly how an external model provider's training process affects a particular output.

Rather than hiding uncertainty, the organization should document it.

A useful approach is:

```text
Known
↓
Partially Known
↓
Uncertain
↓
Unknown
```

This helps stakeholders understand the confidence level of the assessment.

---

# 64. MEASURE and Confidence

Measurement results should be interpreted with appropriate confidence.

For example:

> A model achieved 95% accuracy on a small test dataset.

That does not necessarily mean:

> The model will achieve 95% accuracy in production.

The organization should understand the limitations of the evidence.

---

# 65. MEASURE and Evidence Quality

High-quality evidence should be:

* Relevant.
* Reliable.
* Representative.
* Repeatable where appropriate.
* Traceable.
* Current.
* Sufficient for the decision.

Evidence quality directly affects confidence in AI risk decisions.

---

# 66. MEASURE and Reproducibility

Where practical, evaluation should be reproducible.

This may require documenting:

* Dataset.
* Model version.
* Configuration.
* Evaluation criteria.
* Test conditions.
* Tools.
* Results.

This allows future teams to understand how conclusions were reached.

---

# 67. MEASURE and Model Versioning

Measurements should be associated with the relevant model or system version.

For example:

```text
Model v1.2
Accuracy = 94%

Model v1.3
Accuracy = 96%

Model v2.0
Accuracy = 91%
```

Without version information, historical comparisons can become misleading.

---

# 68. MEASURE and Configuration

Evaluation results can depend on system configuration.

For example:

* Prompt configuration.
* Temperature.
* Retrieval settings.
* Guardrails.
* Tool permissions.
* System instructions.

Therefore, important configuration should be recorded alongside measurement results.

---

# 69. MEASURE and Measurement Governance

Organizations should define:

* What must be measured.
* Who measures it.
* How it is measured.
* When it is measured.
* What evidence is retained.
* What thresholds apply.
* Who reviews results.
* What happens when thresholds are exceeded.

This turns measurement into a governed process.

---

# 70. MEASURE and Accountability

Measurement without accountability is ineffective.

Each important metric should have:

* Metric owner.
* Data source.
* Calculation method.
* Threshold.
* Review frequency.
* Escalation path.

This establishes responsibility for AI risk monitoring.

---

# 71. MEASURE and Decision-Making

The ultimate purpose of measurement is not to generate dashboards.

It is to support decisions.

For example:

```text
Measurement
    ↓
Risk Evidence
    ↓
Interpretation
    ↓
Decision
```

Possible decisions include:

* Continue operation.
* Increase monitoring.
* Implement additional controls.
* Restrict use.
* Retrain.
* Reconfigure.
* Revalidate.
* Suspend.
* Retire.

---

# 72. MEASURE and Risk Communication

Measurement results should be communicated according to the audience.

### Technical Team

May need:

* Detailed model metrics.
* Error analysis.
* Security findings.
* Logs.

### GRC

May need:

* Risk ratings.
* Control effectiveness.
* Threshold breaches.
* Exceptions.

### Executives

May need:

* Material risks.
* Trends.
* Business impact.
* Risk treatment status.

### Board

May need:

* Significant AI risks.
* Major incidents.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Overall governance posture.

---

# 74. MEASURE and False Confidence

Too many metrics can create a false sense of control.

For example:

> "We monitor 50 AI metrics."

That does not necessarily mean:

> "We understand our AI risk."

The important question is:

> **Are the measurements relevant to the material risks of the AI system?**

---

# 75. MEASURE and Meaningful Metrics

A good metric should ideally answer a useful governance question.

For example:

> "How often does the AI produce a high-impact incorrect recommendation?"

This is more useful than simply measuring:

> "How many tokens did the model generate?"

The metric should connect to risk or performance objectives.

---

# 76. MEASURE and Measurement Hierarchy

A useful hierarchy is:

```text
Business Objective
       ↓
AI Objective
       ↓
Risk
       ↓
Control
       ↓
Metric
       ↓
Threshold
       ↓
Action
```

This creates traceability from organizational objectives to measurable evidence.

# 77. MEASURE and Example

Suppose the business objective is:

> Reduce fraud losses.

The AI objective is:

> Identify potentially fraudulent transactions.

A relevant risk is:

> False negatives allow fraudulent transactions to pass.

A control is:

> High-risk transactions receive secondary review.

Measurements may include:

* False-negative rate.
* Fraud detection rate.
* Human-review completion.
* Confirmed fraud rate.

This creates a logical relationship between business objectives, risk, controls and measurements.

```text
        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

```

# 79. MEASURE and Practical GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, MEASURE can be viewed as the process that answers:

> **"What evidence do we have that the AI system is operating within its intended and acceptable risk boundaries?"**

That evidence can then support:

* Risk assessments.
* Control testing.
* Compliance.
* Audit.
* Management reporting.
* Risk acceptance.
* Corrective actions.

---

# 80. MEASURE Core Principle

The central principle of MEASURE is:

> **AI risk should be evaluated using appropriate, relevant, and sufficiently reliable evidence rather than assumptions alone.**

Measurement should be:

* Contextual.
* Risk-based.
* Evidence-driven.
* Proportionate.
* Continuous where appropriate.
* Transparent about uncertainty.
* Connected to decisions.

The objective is not simply to determine whether an AI model "works."

The objective is to determine whether the **AI system performs appropriately, risks are understood, controls are effective, and the system remains within acceptable boundaries in its actual operating context**.

## Part 2 — MEASURE Objectives and Risk Evaluation

### 81. The Role of MEASURE in AI Risk Management

The MEASURE function provides the organization with a structured approach for evaluating AI risks using evidence.

Its fundamental role is to convert the understanding developed during AI system mapping into **measurable evidence about performance, risk, impacts, and controls**.

The organization should be able to move from:

> "We believe this AI system presents certain risks."

to:

> "We have evaluated these risks using defined methods and evidence, and we understand their current significance."

This distinction is important because effective AI governance requires more than identifying theoretical risks. The organization must determine whether those risks actually manifest, how significant they are, and whether existing controls are sufficient.

```text

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
Measurement
       ↓
Evidence
       ↓
Risk Understanding
       ↓
Management Decision
```

This creates a relationship between governance decisions and objective or structured evidence.

* What constitutes disclosure.
* Which information is considered confidential.
* How disclosure will be tested.
* What data will be used.
* What scenarios will be evaluated.

* How results will be documented.
* How frequently testing should occur.

# 83. Measurement Objectives

A mature AI measurement program should establish clear objectives.

Typical objectives include:

1. Determine whether the AI system performs its intended function.
2. Identify harmful or undesirable outcomes.
3. Evaluate the likelihood and impact of identified risks.
4. Determine whether controls are operating effectively.
5. Detect changes in AI risk over time.
6. Identify emerging risks.
7. Provide evidence for governance decisions.
8. Support continuous improvement.

Measurement objectives should be aligned with the AI system's purpose and risk profile.

# 84. Measurement Must Be Purpose-Specific

An AI system should be evaluated against its intended purpose.

For example, consider an AI model used to classify cybersecurity alerts.

Its evaluation may include:

* Detection accuracy.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Detection latency.
* Severity classification accuracy.
* Analyst workload.
* Missed critical alerts.

These measurements are relevant because they relate directly to the system's purpose.

Measuring unrelated characteristics would provide limited governance value.

---

# 85. Intended Use and Measurement

The organization should distinguish between:

**Intended use**

How the AI system is officially designed and approved to be used.

**Actual use**

How users actually use the AI system in practice.

**Potential misuse**

How the system could be used in ways that were not intended.

These differences can create additional risks.

For example:

```text
Intended Use
Customer-service assistance
        ↓
Actual Use
Employees use it to draft legal responses
        ↓
Potential Risk
Unauthorized high-impact use
```

MEASURE should therefore consider whether actual system behavior and usage remain consistent with approved assumptions.

---

# 86. Measuring AI Risk Rather Than Only Model Performance

A common governance weakness is focusing entirely on model performance.

For example:

> Accuracy = 97%

This does not answer:

* Is the model secure?
* Is personal data protected?
* Are harmful outputs occurring?
* Are users relying on incorrect outputs?
* Are controls effective?
* Is the system being used as approved?

MEASURE should therefore examine multiple dimensions of AI risk.

# 90. Measurement Thresholds

Thresholds provide boundaries for action.

* Acceptable performance.
* Warning condition.
* Escalation condition.
* Control failure.
* Potential suspension.

For example:

```text

Within tolerance

Approaching tolerance

Red
Outside tolerance
```

Thresholds should be supported by risk analysis rather than selected arbitrarily.

---

# 91. Measurement and Risk Appetite

Risk appetite provides an important basis for establishing thresholds.

Suppose an organization has:

> Very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of confidential customer information.

The corresponding AI measurement program may establish extremely strict thresholds.

By contrast, an internal low-impact productivity tool may tolerate a higher rate of minor errors.

Therefore:

```text
Risk Appetite
      ↓

      ↓
Measurement Criteria
      ↓
Thresholds
      ↓

```

# 92. Measurement and Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines how much variation the organization is willing to accept.

For example:

> An internal AI writing assistant may tolerate occasional minor inaccuracies.

However:

> An AI system supporting a safety-critical decision may have extremely limited tolerance for incorrect outputs.

Measurement must therefore be interpreted in relation to the consequences of failure.

---

# 93. Measurement and Impact

The same technical error can have dramatically different consequences depending on the use case.

Consider:

```text
AI Error
   ↓
Incorrect Output
   ↓
Impact
```

For a low-impact application:

> Incorrect meeting summary.

For a high-impact application:

> Incorrect medical recommendation.

The error rate alone does not adequately describe the risk.

MEASURE should therefore consider **impact alongside performance**.

---

# 94. Measurement and Likelihood

Risk generally depends on both:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.

Measurement can provide evidence about likelihood.

For example:

> 1,000,000 AI transactions were processed and 2,000 resulted in material errors.

The organization can use this evidence to estimate the frequency of the event.

However, historical frequency should not automatically be treated as future probability because AI systems and operating environments can change.

---

# 95. Measurement and Consequence

Measurements should also help determine consequences.

For example:

```text
Event
AI generates incorrect recommendation
        ↓
Human fails to detect it
        ↓
Incorrect business decision
        ↓
Financial/customer impact
```

The organization should measure not only the AI error but also the effectiveness of downstream controls.

---

# 96. Measurement of Control Effectiveness

Controls should be evaluated to determine whether they reduce the intended risk.

For example:

### Risk

AI generates harmful recommendations.

### Control

Human approval required before execution.

### Measurement

Percentage of high-risk AI recommendations receiving documented human approval.

This allows the organization to determine whether the control is functioning as designed.

---

# 97. Design Effectiveness and Operating Effectiveness

A control may be:

### Design Effective

The control is appropriately designed to address the risk.

### Operating Effective

The control actually operates as intended in practice.

For example:

```text
Policy:
Human approval required.

System:
Approval capability exists.

Actual Operation:
Users bypass approval.
```

The control may be appropriately designed but ineffective in operation.

MEASURE should help identify this difference.

---

# 98. Control Coverage

Organizations should also measure whether controls cover the relevant AI risk population.

For example:

```text
High-risk AI transactions = 100,000

Transactions subject to required control = 98,000
```

Control coverage:

```text
98%
```

The remaining 2% may represent a governance gap requiring investigation.

---

# 99. Control Failure Rate

Another useful measurement is the frequency of control failures.

For example:

```text
Required security checks = 10,000
Failed checks = 50

Failure rate = 0.5%
```

Whether 0.5% is acceptable depends on the risk and organizational tolerance.

The number becomes meaningful only when compared with established criteria.

---

# 100. Measurement and Residual Risk

Controls do not necessarily eliminate risk.

Instead:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

MEASURE can provide evidence about whether residual risk remains within acceptable limits.

For example:

> Before controls, the AI system had a high data leakage risk.

After controls:

> Testing shows that leakage has been significantly reduced but not eliminated.

The remaining exposure becomes the residual risk.

---

# 101. Measuring Residual Risk

Residual risk measurement may consider:

* Frequency of incidents.
* Severity of incidents.
* Control effectiveness.
* Exposure.
* Remaining vulnerabilities.
* Monitoring results.

The organization can then determine whether the residual risk should be:

* Accepted.
* Reduced.
* Transferred.
* Avoided.
* Escalated.

---

# 102. Measurement and Risk Treatment

MEASURE supports the MANAGE function by providing evidence about whether risk treatment is effective.

For example:

```text

      ↓
Control Implemented
      ↓
Measure
      ↓
Risk Reduced?
      ↓
Yes → Continue Monitoring

No → Reassess / Improve Control
```

This creates a feedback loop between measurement and risk management.

# 103. Measurement and Continuous Improvement

Measurement should support continuous improvement.

Suppose an AI system shows:

```text

Month 2: 3.4%
Month 3: 2.8%
Month 4: 2.2%
```

This trend may demonstrate that corrective measures are effective.

Conversely:

```text

Month 2: 2.5%
Month 3: 3.2%
Month 4: 4.1%
```

This indicates deterioration and may require intervention.

# 104. Measurement and Trends

A single measurement represents a point in time.

For example:

Historical measurements allow organizations to identify:

* Improvement.
* Deterioration.
* Stability.
* Seasonal variation.
* Sudden changes.
* Emerging risks.

```text
Target:

4.5%
```

The measurement demonstrates improvement but also shows that the target has not yet been achieved.

# 05.06 — The MEASURE Function

## Part 3 — AI Testing, Evaluation, Metrics, and Monitoring

## 134. AI Testing and Evaluation

Testing and evaluation are central activities within the MEASURE function.

Although the terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they can represent different activities.

**Testing** generally examines whether an AI system behaves as expected under defined conditions.

**Evaluation** is broader and may involve determining the significance, quality, reliability, safety, or trustworthiness of the observed results.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Testing
   ↓
Observed Results
   ↓
Evaluation
   ↓
Risk Interpretation
   ↓
Management Decision
```

The objective is not simply to produce a test result but to understand what that result means for AI risk.

---

# 135. Functional Testing

Functional testing determines whether the AI system performs its intended functions.

Examples include:

* Correctly classifying inputs.
* Generating the required output.
* Performing the expected workflow.
* Calling authorized tools.
* Applying required business rules.
* Returning results within defined conditions.

For example, if an AI system is designed to classify customer complaints, functional testing may determine whether it correctly assigns complaints to the intended categories.

---

# 136. Performance Testing

Performance testing evaluates how effectively the AI system performs its intended task.

Common measurements include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* Error rate.
* Response time.
* Throughput.
* Availability.

The appropriate metric depends on the AI system.

For example, accuracy may be useful for some classification systems, while recall may be more important for systems designed to detect rare security events.

---

# 137. Accuracy

**Accuracy** represents the proportion of correct results among the evaluated results.

A simplified calculation is:

```text
Accuracy =
Correct Predictions
-------------------
Total Predictions
```

For example:

```text
Correct predictions = 9,500
Total predictions   = 10,000

Accuracy = 95%
```

However, accuracy can be misleading when the classes are highly imbalanced.

---

# 138. Precision

**Precision** measures how many results identified as positive are actually positive.

```text
Precision =
True Positives
-------------------------
True Positives + False Positives
```

For example, in fraud detection:

> Of all transactions classified as fraudulent, how many were actually fraudulent?

Precision can be particularly useful when false alarms create significant operational costs.

---

# 139. Recall

**Recall** measures how many actual positive cases the system successfully identifies.

```text
Recall =
True Positives
-------------------------
True Positives + False Negatives
```

For cybersecurity:

> Of all actual malicious events, how many did the AI system detect?

A low recall may indicate that important threats are being missed.

---

# 140. F1 Score

The **F1 score** combines precision and recall into a single measure.

It is commonly used when both false positives and false negatives are important.

However, an organization should not automatically select F1 simply because it is a commonly known metric.

The measurement should reflect the actual risk and business objective.

---

# 141. False Positives

A **false positive** occurs when the AI identifies something as positive when it is not.

Example:

> A legitimate transaction is classified as fraudulent.

Potential consequences include:

* Customer inconvenience.
* Additional investigation.
* Operational costs.
* Customer dissatisfaction.

The significance depends on the use case.

---

# 142. False Negatives

A **false negative** occurs when the AI fails to identify an actual positive case.

Example:

> A fraudulent transaction is classified as legitimate.

False negatives may be particularly significant where missing an event creates greater harm than generating a false alarm.

This demonstrates why metric selection must be risk-based.

---

# 143. Confusion Matrix

For classification systems, a confusion matrix can help analyze results.

```text
                    Actual
                Positive   Negative

Predicted
Positive          TP          FP

Negative          FN          TN
```

Where:

* **TP** = True Positive.
* **TN** = True Negative.
* **FP** = False Positive.
* **FN** = False Negative.

The matrix provides more information than a single accuracy percentage.

---

# 144. Error Rate

Error rate represents the proportion of incorrect outputs.

A simplified calculation is:

```text
Error Rate =
Incorrect Outputs
-----------------
Total Outputs
```

However, organizations should consider the **type and consequence of errors**, not only their frequency.

One catastrophic error may be more significant than hundreds of minor errors.

---

# 145. Error Severity

AI errors can be classified according to their consequences.

For example:

| Severity | Example                           |
| -------- | --------------------------------- |
| Low      | Minor wording error               |
| Moderate | Incorrect internal recommendation |
| High     | Incorrect customer decision       |
| Critical | Safety or major financial impact  |

This allows measurement to incorporate impact rather than treating all errors equally.

---

# 146. Weighted Error Analysis

Organizations may assign greater significance to high-impact errors.

For example:

```text
10 minor errors
+
1 critical error
```

A simple error count may suggest that the 10 minor errors are more important.

A risk-based evaluation may reach the opposite conclusion.

Therefore, MEASURE should consider both:

* Frequency.
* Severity.

# 147. Robustness Testing

Robustness testing evaluates how the AI behaves when conditions vary from the expected operating environment.

Testing may involve:

* Unexpected inputs.
* Noisy data.
* Missing information.
* Unusual formats.
* Ambiguous requests.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Boundary conditions.

The objective is to determine whether the AI continues to behave acceptably under relevant variations.

# 148. Resilience Testing

* Network failure.

* Database unavailability.

* Model service interruption.

Testing may determine:

* Whether the system fails safely.
* Whether alternative processes exist.
* Whether users are informed.
* Whether recovery procedures work.

# 149. Security Testing

* Adversarial testing.

The testing scope should be based on the AI system's architecture and threat model.

# 152. Privacy Testing

# 153. Bias Testing

Bias testing evaluates whether AI behavior differs materially across relevant populations or contexts.

Potential evaluation areas include:

* Different demographic groups.
* Different geographic regions.
* Different languages.
* Different customer segments.
* Different operational conditions.

The relevant populations and evaluation criteria should be determined by the AI use case.

# 154. Fairness Evaluation

Fairness evaluation should begin with a clear definition of what fairness means for the specific AI application.

Questions may include:

* Which groups could be affected?
* What outcomes are being compared?
* What differences could create harm?
* What organizational requirements apply?
* What legal requirements apply?

There is no single fairness metric that is appropriate for every AI system.

---

# 155. Explainability Evaluation

Explainability evaluation determines whether the AI system provides explanations that are sufficiently useful for its intended stakeholders.

Evaluation may consider:

* Clarity.
* Consistency.
* Relevance.
* Accuracy.
* User comprehension.

For example, a technical explanation appropriate for a model engineer may be unsuitable for a customer.

---

# 156. Interpretability Testing

Interpretability testing may examine whether relevant stakeholders can understand important aspects of system behavior.

For example:

> Can an analyst understand why the AI classified an alert as high risk?

The answer may affect whether the AI can appropriately support that operational process.

---

# 157. Human Oversight Testing

Human oversight should be tested rather than assumed.

Testing may examine:

* Whether human review is triggered.
* Whether reviewers receive sufficient information.
* Whether reviewers can override AI outputs.
* Whether overrides are recorded.
* Whether escalation procedures work.
* Whether users understand when intervention is required.

This evaluates the actual human-AI control relationship.

# 158. Human Override Measurement

An organization may measure the percentage of AI recommendations overridden by human reviewers.

For example:

```text

Human overrides    = 600

Override rate = 3%
```

The result should not automatically be interpreted as good or bad.

A very low override rate could mean:

* The AI performs well.

Or it could mean:

* Humans are not critically reviewing outputs.

Context is therefore essential.

# 160. Safety Testing

Safety testing evaluates whether the AI system can produce harmful outcomes under relevant conditions.

* Dangerous outputs.
* Incorrect high-impact decisions.
* Unsafe automation.

Safety evaluation should consider both the AI model and the surrounding system.

The evaluation should reflect the intended use and potential impact of the system.

# 163. Factuality Evaluation

This can be particularly important when AI outputs are used for:

* Compliance.
* Legal analysis.
* Financial decisions.
* Technical instructions.
* Customer information.

# 164. Groundedness

# 165. Relevance

An AI output may be factually correct but still irrelevant to the user's request.

Relevance can be particularly important for AI assistants and generative AI applications.

# 166. Instruction Following

Generative AI systems may also be evaluated on whether they follow authorized instructions.

Testing can examine:

* Correct task execution.

* Business rules.
* Policy constraints.
* System instructions.

# 167. Refusal Behavior

For certain AI systems, appropriate refusal is a desired behavior.

Testing may evaluate whether the AI:

* Refuses prohibited requests.
* Does not reveal restricted information.
* Redirects unsafe requests appropriately.
* Avoids unnecessary refusals to legitimate requests.

Both **under-refusal** and **over-refusal** can create problems.

---

# 168. Availability Measurement

Availability measures whether the AI service is accessible when required.

For example:

```text
Available time
---------------
Required service time
```

Availability may be especially important when AI supports critical operational processes.

---

# 169. Latency Measurement

Latency measures how long the system takes to respond.

For example:

```text

95th percentile       = 3.4 seconds
```

For interactive applications, high latency can affect usability and operational effectiveness.

For some high-risk applications, latency may also affect safety.

# 170. Reliability Measurement

Reliability measures how consistently the AI system operates without failure.

Possible indicators include:

* Failure rate.
* Error frequency.
* Service interruptions.
* Successful transaction percentage.
* Recovery frequency.

Reliability should be evaluated against the required service level.

---

# 171. Availability Versus Reliability

These concepts are related but different.

**Availability** asks:

> Is the system available when needed?

**Reliability** asks:

> Does the system consistently perform correctly over time?

An AI service may be available 99.9% of the time but still produce unreliable outputs.

Therefore, both technical availability and functional reliability may need to be measured.

# 177. Monitoring for Model Drift

Model performance can deteriorate over time.

Potential indicators include:

* Increasing error rate.
* Declining precision.
* Declining recall.
* Changing input distribution.
* Increasing user corrections.

When material drift is detected, the organization may need additional evaluation.

# 178. Data Drift Monitoring

Data drift monitoring examines whether production inputs differ materially from the data used during development or validation.

For example:

```text

        ↓

        ↓
Distribution difference
        ↓

```

# 179. Concept Drift Monitoring

Concept drift monitoring evaluates whether the relationship between input variables and expected outcomes changes.

For example:

> Attack techniques change over time.

A cybersecurity model trained on older attack patterns may become less effective.

Monitoring should therefore consider whether the underlying assumptions remain valid.

# 180. Threshold Monitoring

# 181. Monitoring and Alert Fatigue

Too many alerts can reduce the effectiveness of monitoring.

If users receive hundreds of low-value alerts, they may begin ignoring them.

Therefore, monitoring should prioritize:

* Material risks.
* Meaningful deviations.
* Actionable events.

A smaller number of high-quality alerts can be more valuable than excessive monitoring noise.

# 206. Measurement Uncertainty

For example:

> The observed error rate was 3.2% under controlled testing conditions, but production behavior remains uncertain because the evaluation dataset does not fully represent real-world usage.

> Error rate = 3.2%.

The second statement appears more certain than the evidence may justify.

# 218. The Difference Between Measurement and Management

MEASURE determines:

> **What is happening, how significant it is, and what evidence supports that conclusion.**

MANAGE determines:

> **What the organization should do about it.**

For example:

```text
MEASURE:
False-negative rate = 8%

MANAGE:
Increase human review and initiate model remediation.
```

Keeping these concepts distinct helps maintain a clear governance structure.

---

# 219. The Difference Between Measurement and Governance

GOVERN establishes the organization's overall rules, responsibilities, policies, and accountability.

MEASURE provides evidence that allows the organization to determine whether those governance expectations are being achieved.

For example:

```text
Governance Requirement:
High-risk AI requires human oversight.

MEASURE:
98.7% of required decisions received documented review.

Governance Assessment:
1.3% exception rate requires investigation.
```

Measurement therefore provides evidence about governance effectiveness.

Effective AI measurement should be:

Measure what matters to the AI system's risks and objectives.

Allocate measurement effort according to potential impact.

### Evidence-Based

Use reliable and appropriate evidence.

Use consistent methods where comparison over time is important.

### Transparent

Document assumptions and limitations.

### Actionable

Connect measurements to decisions and responses.

### Lifecycle-Oriented

Evaluate AI throughout relevant stages of its lifecycle.

Avoid unnecessary measurement while maintaining sufficient assurance.

# 287. Common MEASURE Implementation Failures

Organizations should avoid several common weaknesses.

### Failure 1 — Measuring Only Accuracy

Technical accuracy does not represent the complete AI risk picture.

### Failure 2 — No Defined Thresholds

Results are collected but there is no clear interpretation.

### Failure 3 — No Monitoring

The model is tested once and then forgotten.

### Failure 4 — No Control Testing

Controls are documented but their effectiveness is never measured.

### Failure 5 — No Evidence Retention

The organization cannot demonstrate how conclusions were reached.

### Failure 6 — Ignoring Uncertainty

Results are presented with unjustified confidence.

### Failure 7 — Excessive Metrics

The organization collects large quantities of low-value data.

### Failure 8 — No Link to Risk Decisions

Measurements exist but do not influence management action.

# 294. Final Key Principles

The most important principles for enterprise implementation are:

1. **MEASURE must be risk-based.**
2. **Measurement must reflect the AI system's intended purpose and context.**
3. **Technical performance is only one dimension of AI risk.**
4. **Security, privacy, fairness, safety, reliability, robustness, and human oversight should be evaluated where relevant.**
5. **Every important risk should have appropriate evidence or a documented measurement limitation.**
6. **Metrics should have defined methodologies and interpretation criteria.**
7. **Thresholds should connect to risk appetite and tolerance.**
8. **Controls should be tested for both coverage and effectiveness.**
9. **Production monitoring should be used where the risk warrants it.**
10. **Significant changes should trigger additional evaluation.**
11. **Measurement results should feed risk registers and management decisions.**
12. **Evidence should be traceable, documented, and retained appropriately.**
13. **Uncertainty should be explicitly communicated.**
14. **Third-party AI dependencies should be included in the measurement strategy.**
15. **Measurement should support continuous improvement rather than simply produce reports.**
16. **The ultimate objective is not more metrics; it is better AI risk decisions.**
