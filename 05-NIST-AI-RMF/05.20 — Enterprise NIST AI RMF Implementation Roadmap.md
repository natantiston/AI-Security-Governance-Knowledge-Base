# 05.20 — Enterprise NIST AI RMF Implementation Roadmap

## Part 1 — Foundations and Implementation Strategy

### 1. Purpose of an Enterprise AI RMF Implementation Roadmap

An enterprise NIST AI RMF implementation roadmap translates the principles and functions of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework into a practical organizational program.

The objective is not simply to "implement NIST AI RMF."

The objective is to establish a repeatable capability for:

* Identifying AI systems.
* Understanding AI risks.
* Governing AI use.
* Managing AI security risks.
* Measuring risk.
* Implementing controls.
* Monitoring changes.
* Providing assurance.
* Continuously improving AI governance.

The roadmap should connect strategy, governance, risk, cybersecurity, compliance, technology, and business operations.

---

### 2. From Framework to Operating Capability

A framework provides guidance.

An implementation roadmap creates execution.

```text
NIST AI RMF
     ↓
Enterprise AI Strategy
     ↓
Governance Model
     ↓
Policies and Standards
     ↓
AI Inventory
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Controls
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Assurance
     ↓
Continuous Improvement
```

This distinction is fundamental.

An organization can understand a framework without actually having an operational AI risk-management capability.

---

### 3. Implementation Should Be Risk-Based

Organizations should not apply identical governance requirements to every AI system.

A better model is:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Risk Classification
    ↓
Governance Requirements
    ↓
Security Requirements
    ↓
Monitoring Requirements
    ↓
Assurance Level
```

Higher-risk systems should generally receive stronger governance and assurance.

---

### 4. Establish the Implementation Scope

The first step is defining what the AI governance program covers.

Scope may include:

* Generative AI.
* Machine-learning systems.
* AI agents.
* Predictive models.
* Recommendation systems.
* AI-enabled applications.
* Third-party AI services.
* Internally developed models.
* Embedded AI functionality.

The organization should explicitly define what qualifies as an AI system for governance purposes.

---

### 5. Define Organizational Objectives

Before creating controls, management should establish what it wants the AI governance program to achieve.

Typical objectives include:

* Enable responsible AI adoption.
* Reduce AI security risk.
* Protect sensitive information.
* Meet regulatory obligations.
* Establish accountability.
* Prevent unauthorized AI usage.
* Improve AI risk visibility.
* Support trustworthy AI deployment.

The objectives should be aligned with enterprise strategy.

---

AI governance requires executive sponsorship because AI risk crosses organizational boundaries.

Potential executive sponsors include:

* CIO.
* CISO.
* Chief Risk Officer.
* Chief Data Officer.
* Chief AI Officer.
* Business executives.

The sponsor provides authority, resources, and organizational alignment.

### 9. Define Decision Rights

Governance should clearly establish who can:

* Approve AI use cases.
* Classify AI risk.
* Approve high-risk systems.
* Accept residual risk.
* Approve exceptions.
* Suspend an AI system.
* Require remediation.

Without defined decision rights, governance can become advisory rather than effective.

---

### 10. Establish AI Risk Appetite

Management should define how much AI risk the organization is willing to accept.

For example:

```text
Low-Risk AI
→ Standard Controls

Moderate-Risk AI
→ Enhanced Controls

High-Risk AI
→ Formal Approval

Critical AI
→ Executive Oversight
```

Risk appetite should be connected to enterprise risk-management practices.

---

### 11. Define AI Risk Classification

A practical classification model might include:

**Level 1 — Low Risk**

Limited business impact and low data sensitivity.

**Level 2 — Moderate Risk**

Meaningful business or operational impact.

**Level 3 — High Risk**

Significant security, privacy, financial, regulatory, or customer impact.

**Level 4 — Critical Risk**

Potential for severe organizational or societal consequences.

The exact classification criteria should be documented.

---

* Output filtering.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.

### 22. Establish AI Governance Gates

Governance gates provide formal decision points.

```text
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
```

---

### 23. Gate 1 — Use-Case Approval

The organization determines whether the proposed AI use case is appropriate.

Questions include:

* Is there a legitimate business purpose?
* Is AI appropriate?
* Are risks understood?
* Is the use case consistent with policy?
* Are sufficient resources available?

The result is an initial approval or rejection.

---

### 24. Gate 2 — Risk Classification

The proposed AI system receives a formal risk classification.

The classification should consider:

```text
Data Sensitivity
+
Business Criticality
+
Autonomy
+
User Population
+
External Exposure
+
Potential Impact
```

This classification determines subsequent governance requirements.

---

### 25. Gate 3 — Security Assessment

Security professionals evaluate whether the proposed architecture and controls adequately address the identified risks.

Potential outputs include:

* Approved.
* Approved with conditions.
* Remediation required.
* Rejected.

Security approval should be documented.

---

### 26. Gate 4 — Testing Approval

Before production, the organization should verify that required testing has been completed.

Testing may include:

* Security testing.
* Vulnerability assessment.
* Penetration testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Access-control testing.
* Data-leakage testing.
* Model validation.

The testing depth should reflect risk.

---

### 27. Gate 5 — Production Approval

Production approval should confirm that:

* Required controls are implemented.
* Risks are understood.
* Open findings are acceptable.
* Monitoring is operational.
* Ownership is assigned.
* Incident procedures exist.
* Required evidence is available.

Only then should the system move into production.

---

### 28. Gate 6 — Periodic Review

AI systems should not receive permanent approval without reassessment.

Reviews may be triggered by:

* Scheduled review periods.
* Material model changes.
* New data sources.
* New vendors.
* Security incidents.
* Regulatory changes.
* Major architecture changes.

This supports continuous governance.

## Part 2 — Detailed Implementation Phases, Workstreams, and Deliverables

### 41. Turning the Roadmap Into a Program

An enterprise implementation should be managed as a structured transformation program rather than as a documentation exercise.

The program can be organized into parallel workstreams:

```text
                    AI RMF PROGRAM
                         │
       ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
       ↓                 ↓                 ↓
   Governance           Risk             Security
       │                 │                 │
       ├─────────────────┼─────────────────┤
       ↓                 ↓                 ↓
     Data             Technology        Compliance
       │                 │                 │
       └─────────────────┼─────────────────┘
                         ↓
                    GRC / Assurance
```

Each workstream should have an owner, objectives, milestones, dependencies, and measurable outcomes.

---

Key activities include:

* Establish AI governance committee.
* Define AI governance charter.
* Define roles and responsibilities.
* Define decision rights.

* Define risk appetite.
* Approve AI policy.

### 47. Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents exposure before considering controls.

Example:

```text
AI Customer Decision System

Impact:
High

Likelihood:
Medium

Inherent Risk:
High
```

This establishes the baseline against which controls can be evaluated.

---

### 48. Control Assessment

After identifying inherent risk, the organization evaluates existing controls.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk
     ↓
Existing Controls
     ↓
Control Effectiveness
     ↓
Residual Risk
```

Control effectiveness should be supported by evidence wherever possible.

---

### 49. Residual Risk Assessment

Residual risk is the exposure remaining after controls.

Example:

```text
Inherent Risk: High
        ↓
Strong IAM
DLP
Monitoring
Security Testing
        ↓
Residual Risk: Medium
```

Management must then determine whether medium residual risk is acceptable.

### 146. Root-Cause Analysis

When a control fails, the organization should determine the underlying cause.

**Immediate cause:**

Review reminder was missed.

**Root cause:**

The AI system was not integrated into the enterprise GRC workflow.

**Corrective action:**

Automate the review lifecycle through the GRC platform.

The corrective action addresses the system weakness rather than simply reminding someone.

```text id="capa01"

   ↓
```

Both should be considered during significant AI findings.

### 164. Cost of Controls

Organizations should consider whether controls are proportionate.

A control costing €1 million to reduce a low-impact risk may not be justified.

Conversely, a relatively inexpensive control may be highly valuable for a critical AI system.

The principle is:

> **Control investment should be proportional to risk.**

### 188. Interview Answer — Implementation Roadmap

If asked:

**"How would you implement NIST AI RMF in an enterprise?"**

A strong answer is:

> "I would approach it as a phased enterprise program rather than a compliance exercise. First, I would establish executive sponsorship, governance, scope, roles, and risk appetite. Second, I would build an enterprise AI inventory and identify shadow AI. Third, I would classify AI systems by risk and perform risk, security, privacy, and third-party assessments. Fourth, I would integrate the resulting requirements into existing GRC, ISMS, and security controls rather than creating duplicate processes. Fifth, I would implement lifecycle gates, monitoring, incident response, and assurance. Finally, I would establish metrics, continuous control monitoring, periodic reassessment, and a maturity roadmap so the capability continuously improves."

---

### 189. Interview Answer — Short Version

A concise version is:

> **"I would implement NIST AI RMF through seven stages: mobilize, discover, assess, design, implement, assure, and optimize. The key is to start with AI visibility and risk classification, then integrate AI-specific requirements into existing enterprise GRC and security processes. I would measure control effectiveness and residual risk, not just policy completion, and continuously reassess the environment as AI technology and threats change."**
