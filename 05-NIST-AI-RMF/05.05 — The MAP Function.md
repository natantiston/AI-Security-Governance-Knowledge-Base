# 05.05 — The MAP Function

## Part 1 — Introduction, Purpose, and Role of MAP in AI Risk Management

## 1. Introduction to the MAP Function

The **MAP function** of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) establishes the context needed to understand AI risks.

While **GOVERN** establishes the organizational structures, policies, responsibilities and accountability for AI risk management, **MAP** focuses on understanding the AI system, its intended purpose, operating environment, affected stakeholders and potential impacts.

MAP essentially asks:

> **"What are we dealing with, where will it operate, who may be affected, and what could happen?"**

This makes MAP a critical foundation for meaningful AI risk identification and assessment.

Without adequate context, an organization may identify technical risks while overlooking:

* Business risks.
* Human impacts.
* Privacy implications.
* Security consequences.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Social impacts.
* Operational dependencies.
* Third-party dependencies.
* Potential misuse.
* Unintended consequences.

MAP therefore transforms an AI system from a purely technical object into a **business, organizational, human and socio-technical system that must be understood within its operating context**.

---

# 2. Purpose of MAP

The primary purpose of MAP is to establish sufficient context to understand and characterize AI risks.

MAP helps an organization determine:

* Why the AI system exists.
* What problem it is intended to solve.
* Who owns it.
* Who uses it.
* Who is affected by it.
* Where it operates.
* What data it depends on.
* What decisions it supports or makes.
* What systems it interacts with.
* What harms could occur.
* What assumptions are being made.
* What uncertainties exist.

A simplified representation is:

```text
AI SYSTEM
    │
    ├── Purpose
    ├── Context
    ├── Users
    ├── Stakeholders
    ├── Data
    ├── Dependencies
    ├── Environment
    ├── Impacts
    └── Risks
```

MAP creates the contextual foundation required for subsequent risk analysis and management.

---

# 3. MAP Within the NIST AI RMF

The four functions of the NIST AI RMF are:

```text
                 NIST AI RMF
                      │
       ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
       │              │              │
       ▼              ▼              ▼
    GOVERN           MAP          MEASURE
       │              │              │
       └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                      ▼
                    MANAGE
```

Although the functions are presented as distinct areas, they are interconnected.

MAP provides context for the other functions.

For example:

```text
GOVERN
Who is accountable?
        ↓
MAP
What is the AI system and what can it affect?
        ↓
MEASURE
How can its risks and characteristics be evaluated?
        ↓
MANAGE
What should be done about the identified risks?
```

MAP therefore acts as a bridge between organizational governance and practical AI risk analysis.

---

# 4. MAP Is a Context Function

MAP should not be interpreted simply as "identify the model."

An AI system exists within a broader environment.

For example, consider an AI system used for customer-service operations.

The technical model may be:

```text
Customer Question
       ↓
AI Model
       ↓
Generated Response
```

However, the actual operational environment may be:

```text
Customer
   ↓
Digital Channel
   ↓
Application
   ↓
AI Service
   ↓
Foundation Model
   ↓
Enterprise Data
   ↓
Knowledge Base
   ↓
Human Agent
   ↓
Customer
```

The risks associated with the system cannot be understood by examining the model alone.

MAP therefore requires consideration of the **full system context**.

# 5. AI Systems Are Socio-Technical Systems

One of the important concepts behind AI risk management is that AI systems are **socio-technical systems**.

This means that AI behavior is influenced not only by technology but also by:

* People.
* Processes.
* Organizations.
* Data.
* Policies.
* Infrastructure.
* Culture.
* Business decisions.
* User behavior.
* External conditions.

A useful model is:

```text
             AI SYSTEM
                 │
      ┌──────────┼──────────┐
      ▼          ▼          ▼
 Technology    People     Process
      │          │          │
      └──────────┼──────────┘
                 ▼
             Environment
                 │
                 ▼
              Impacts
```

This is why MAP must examine the broader environment surrounding the AI system.

# 7. MAP and Intended Purpose

One of the first questions in MAP is:

> **What is the intended purpose of the AI system?**

This sounds simple, but it is fundamental.

An AI system designed to:

* Recommend products.
* Detect fraud.
* Support customer service.
* Analyze network traffic.
* Screen job applications.
* Diagnose medical conditions.
* Generate marketing content.

may have completely different risk profiles.

The intended purpose establishes the baseline against which the AI system should be evaluated.

---

# 8. Intended Use

The organization should document the intended use of the AI system.

For example:

> "The AI system is intended to classify customer support requests and recommend the appropriate service category to a human customer-service agent."

This is different from:

> "The AI system automatically resolves customer complaints without human review."

The second use case introduces substantially different considerations because the AI has greater influence over the final outcome.

---

# 9. Intended Use Versus Actual Use

MAP should recognize that actual use may differ from the original design.

For example:

```text
Intended Use
     ↓
Customer-Service Classification
     ↓
Actual User Behavior
     ↓
AI Used for Customer Decision-Making
```

The AI system may gradually become relied upon for purposes beyond its original scope.

This creates **use-case drift**.

Therefore, governance should not assume that the original documented purpose always represents actual operational use.

---

# 10. Misuse and Unintended Use

MAP should consider foreseeable misuse.

Examples include:

* Using the AI outside its approved domain.
* Treating predictions as definitive decisions.
* Feeding inappropriate data into the system.
* Using the system for a higher-risk purpose.
* Circumventing human oversight.
* Connecting the AI to unauthorized systems.

A practical question is:

> **"How could this system reasonably be used in a way that was not intended?"**

This helps identify risks that may otherwise remain invisible.

---

# 11. System Boundaries

MAP should establish the boundaries of the AI system.

The organization should determine:

* What components are inside the system boundary?
* What components are external?
* Which systems provide data?
* Which systems consume outputs?
* Which third parties are involved?

Example:

```text
              SYSTEM BOUNDARY
        ┌─────────────────────────┐
        │                         │
Data →  │       AI System         │ → Decision Support
        │                         │
        │  Model                  │
        │  Application            │
        │  Processing             │
        │  Monitoring             │
        │                         │
        └─────────────────────────┘
             ↑             ↓
        External Data   External API
```

Clearly defining boundaries prevents important dependencies from being overlooked.

# 14. MAP and Business Context

The AI system should be understood in relation to the business process it supports.

For example:

```text
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
```

# 15. MAP and Criticality

The organization should determine how important the AI system is to business operations.

Potential classifications include:

* Non-critical.
* Important.
* Business-critical.
* Mission-critical.

Criticality can influence:

* Risk assessment.
* Control requirements.
* Monitoring.
* Resilience.
* Incident response.
* Recovery requirements.

---

# 16. MAP and Business Dependency

Organizations should identify how dependent a business process is on the AI system.

For example:

```text
Low Dependency
AI unavailable → Manual process continues

Medium Dependency
AI unavailable → Reduced efficiency

High Dependency
AI unavailable → Significant operational disruption

Critical Dependency
AI unavailable → Major business interruption
```

This creates important context for AI risk assessment.

# 17. MAP and Stakeholder Identification

AI systems can affect many stakeholders.

Potential stakeholders include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Suppliers.
* Business owners.
* Developers.
* Security teams.
* Privacy teams.
* Regulators.
* Management.
* Shareholders.
* Members of the public.

MAP should identify stakeholders who may influence or be affected by the AI system.

---

# 18. Direct and Indirect Stakeholders

Not all affected parties interact directly with the AI system.

### Direct Stakeholder

A customer interacting with an AI chatbot.

### Indirect Stakeholder

A customer affected by a business decision influenced by an AI model.

For example:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Employee Decision
    ↓
Customer Outcome
```

The customer may never interact with the AI directly but may still be affected by it.

# 20. MAP and Affected Individuals

Special attention should be given to people who may be affected by AI outputs.

Questions include:

* Who could experience harm?
* Who could benefit?
* Who may have limited ability to challenge the outcome?
* Who may not know AI is involved?
* Who may be disproportionately affected?

This helps identify human-centered AI risks.

---

# 21. MAP and Vulnerable Populations

Some AI applications may affect vulnerable populations disproportionately.

Depending on the use case, these may include:

* Children.
* Elderly individuals.
* Individuals with disabilities.
* Financially vulnerable individuals.
* People with limited digital literacy.

The organization should consider whether the AI system's context creates heightened risks for particular groups.

---

# 22. MAP and Human Oversight

MAP should identify the role humans play in the AI system.

Possible models include:

### Human-in-the-Loop

A human reviews the AI output before action.

```text
AI
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Decision
```

### Human-on-the-Loop

The AI operates automatically while humans monitor it.

```text
AI
 ↓
Automatic Action
 ↓
Human Monitoring
```

### Human-in-Command

Humans retain overall authority over the system and can intervene or stop it.

The appropriate model depends on the AI system and risk.

---

# 23. MAP and Decision Authority

The organization should identify:

> **Who ultimately makes the decision?**

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Decision
       ↓
Business Outcome
```

If the AI makes or effectively determines the decision, the risk profile may be significantly different.

---

# 24. Automation Level

MAP should identify the degree of automation.

A useful conceptual scale is:

```text
Level 1
AI provides information

Level 2
AI recommends action

Level 3
AI prepares action

Level 4
AI executes action with human oversight

Level 5
AI executes action autonomously
```

Higher autonomy can increase the consequences of AI failure, depending on the context.

---

# 25. MAP and AI Autonomy

AI autonomy should therefore be documented.

Questions include:

* Can the AI make decisions?
* Can it initiate transactions?
* Can it modify data?
* Can it access external systems?
* Can it send communications?
* Can it execute code?
* Can it take actions without human approval?

Autonomy is an important contextual characteristic.

# 28. MAP and Third-Party AI

Third-party AI creates additional questions:

* Who operates it?

# 29. MAP and Data Context

AI risk cannot be properly understood without understanding the data.

MAP should consider:

* Data source.
* Data owner.
* Data type.
* Data sensitivity.
* Data quality.
* Data volume.
* Data location.
* Data lineage.

# 30. Training Data

For systems involving model training or fine-tuning, the organization should understand:

* What data was used?
* Where did it come from?
* Is its use authorized?
* Is it representative?
* What quality issues exist?
* What sensitive information may be included?

Training data can significantly influence model behavior.

---

# 31. Inference Data

Inference data refers to information provided to the AI system during operational use.

Examples include:

* Customer questions.
* Employee documents.
* Transaction information.
* Network telemetry.
* Personal information.

Inference data may create privacy and security risks even when the underlying model is not trained on the information.

# 33. Data Sensitivity

Data classification should be considered in AI context.

For example:

```text
     ↓
Internal Data
     ↓

     ↓
Highly Sensitive Data
```

# 34. MAP and Data Quality

Poor data quality can produce poor AI outcomes.

Potential issues include:

* Missing data.
* Incorrect data.
* Outdated information.
* Duplicates.
* Inconsistent formats.
* Biased datasets.
* Incomplete representation.

The principle is:

> **AI risk can originate from the data environment, not only from the model.**

# 36. MAP and AI Outputs

Outputs may include:

* Text.
* Recommendations.
* Classifications.

* Predictions.

* Code.

# 37. Output Consequence

Consider two AI outputs:

### Low consequence

> "Suggested marketing slogan."

### High consequence

> "Customer eligibility score."

The technical AI may be similar, but the consequences of an incorrect output are very different.

MAP therefore considers the **context and consequence of outputs**.

For example:


An apparently low-risk AI output can become high-impact if another system uses it to make consequential decisions.

# 43. MAP and Organizational Context

The same AI technology can create different risks in different organizations.

For example, an AI system used by:

* A small retailer.
* A telecommunications operator.
* A bank.
* A hospital.
* A government agency.

may have different:

* Regulatory requirements.
* Customer expectations.
* Risk tolerance.
* Business impacts.
* Security requirements.

Therefore, AI risk cannot be assessed purely from technical specifications.

---

# 44. MAP and Organizational Culture

Culture can influence AI risk.

For example, an organization where employees:

* Overtrust AI.
* Avoid reporting errors.
* Bypass controls.
* Prioritize speed over governance.

may face different AI risks from an organization with a strong risk culture.

MAP should therefore consider relevant organizational behaviors.

---

# 45. MAP and Risk Assumptions

AI risk assessments often depend on assumptions.

Examples:

* Users will review AI outputs.
* Data will remain within a specific environment.
* The model provider will maintain service availability.
* The model will be used only for its intended purpose.
* Human intervention will remain available.

These assumptions should be documented.

---

# 46. Assumption Management

A useful approach is:

```text
Assumption
    ↓
Risk if Assumption Fails
    ↓
Validation Method
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Reassessment
```

This prevents assumptions from becoming invisible dependencies.

# 47. MAP and Uncertainty

AI systems often contain uncertainty.

Sources include:

* Unknown model behavior.
* Limited testing.
* Incomplete data.
* Emerging technology.
* Changing user behavior.
* Evolving threats.
* Limited historical evidence.

MAP should document significant uncertainties rather than assuming they do not exist.

---

# 48. MAP and Knowledge Gaps

A knowledge gap occurs when the organization does not know enough about an AI system to confidently assess its risk.

Examples:

* Unknown training data.
* Unknown model behavior.
* Unknown third-party dependencies.
* Unknown downstream use.
* Unknown user population.

A knowledge gap itself can become a risk-management issue.

# 51. MAP and System Description

A strong system description should answer:

> **What is the system, what does it do, how does it work at a high level, and where is it used?**

For example:

> "The system uses a large language model to assist customer-service agents by generating suggested responses from approved enterprise knowledge sources. Agents review and approve responses before they are sent to customers."

This description establishes significant context without requiring excessive technical detail.

---

# 52. MAP and System Purpose Statement

A useful purpose statement should be:

* Specific.
* Clear.
* Measurable where possible.
* Connected to a business objective.
* Limited to approved use.

Weak:

> "Use AI to improve customer service."

Stronger:

> "Assist customer-service agents in generating responses to routine technical-support inquiries using approved enterprise knowledge sources."

The second statement provides a clearer governance boundary.

---

# 53. MAP and Intended Users

The organization should identify who is expected to use the AI system.

Potential users include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Administrators.
* Analysts.
* Developers.
* Managers.
* External partners.

User characteristics can influence risk.

For example, an AI system designed for trained cybersecurity analysts has different assumptions from one intended for the general public.

---

# 54. MAP and User Capability

MAP should consider whether users have sufficient knowledge to interpret AI outputs.

Questions include:

* Do users understand AI limitations?
* Can they identify incorrect outputs?
* Are they trained?
* Can they challenge AI recommendations?
* Do they have sufficient domain knowledge?

Human oversight is only effective if the human is capable of exercising meaningful oversight.

---

# 55. MAP and Human-AI Interaction

The organization should understand how users interact with AI.

For example:

```text
Human Input
     ↓
AI Processing
     ↓
AI Output
     ↓
Human Interpretation
     ↓
Human Decision
```

Potential risks can arise at each interaction point.

---

# 56. MAP and Automation Bias

Users may place excessive trust in AI outputs.

This is often described as **automation bias**.

For example:

> An employee accepts an AI recommendation without independent verification because the system appears authoritative.

MAP should consider whether the operating context encourages this behavior.

---

# 57. MAP and Human Override

Where appropriate, the organization should identify whether humans can override AI outputs.

Questions include:

* Can users reject the recommendation?
* Can they modify it?
* Can they stop an automated action?
* Is the override technically possible?
* Is the override practical under operational conditions?

The existence of a theoretical override is not sufficient if users cannot realistically exercise it.

---

# 58. MAP and AI Failure Modes

MAP should consider how the AI could fail within its operating context.

Potential failures include:

* Incorrect output.
* Incomplete output.
* Unexpected output.
* Unsafe recommendation.
* Hallucination.
* Model degradation.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized action.
* System unavailability.

Failure analysis provides important context for risk assessment.

---

# 59. MAP and Harm Identification

AI risk ultimately concerns potential harm.

Potential categories include:

### Individual Harm

* Financial loss.
* Privacy harm.
* Discrimination.
* Denial of opportunity.

### Organizational Harm

* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Security incident.

### Societal Harm

* Misinformation.

---

# 60. MAP and Impact Context

Impact should be considered in context.

A wrong AI recommendation may be:

* Inconsequential in one application.
* Material in another.
* Severe in a third.

Therefore:

> **AI risk is determined not only by what the model can do, but also by what happens when its output is wrong.**

---

# 61. MAP and Risk Scenario Context

Risk scenarios can combine the contextual elements.

Example:

> An AI customer-support assistant generates an incorrect technical recommendation. A support agent accepts the recommendation without verification, causing the customer to lose service for an extended period.

The scenario identifies:

```text
AI System
   ↓
Output
   ↓
Human Behavior
   ↓
Business Process
   ↓
Customer Impact
```

This provides a much richer risk picture than simply stating:

> "The model may produce inaccurate output."

# 64. MAP and Enterprise GRC

From a GRC perspective, MAP is particularly important because it converts technical AI information into governance-relevant context.

For example:

```text
Technical Fact:
"Uses an LLM."

          ↓

GRC Context:
"What data does the LLM process?"

          ↓

Risk Context:
"What happens if that data is exposed?"

          ↓

Governance Context:
"Who owns the risk and what controls are required?"
```

This demonstrates how MAP supports practical risk governance.

# 65. MAP and Risk-Based Thinking

MAP encourages organizations to avoid asking only:

> "Is this AI system secure?"

Instead, the organization should ask:

> "What is this AI system being used for, who is affected, what could happen if it fails, and what conditions make those consequences more or less significant?"

That is the essence of risk-based thinking.

# 69. MAP Summary

The **MAP function** establishes the contextual foundation for AI risk management.

It helps the organization understand:

1. The AI system and its purpose.
2. The business and operational environment.
3. The people and stakeholders involved.
4. The data used by the system.
5. The technical and third-party dependencies.
6. The degree of automation and human oversight.
7. The intended and foreseeable uses.
8. The potential impacts and harms.
9. The assumptions and uncertainties.
10. The conditions under which AI risks may emerge.

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI risk cannot be properly understood without understanding the context in which the AI system operates.**

A technically sophisticated AI system can still create unacceptable risk if it is deployed in the wrong context, used for the wrong purpose, exposed to inappropriate data, given excessive autonomy, or relied upon by people who do not understand its limitations.

MAP therefore provides the **contextual intelligence required to identify, evaluate and manage AI risk effectively within the enterprise.**

# 71. Business Context

The business context explains why the organization is using AI.

Questions include:

* What business objective does the AI support?
* What process does it improve?
* What business problem is being solved?
* What happens if the AI is unavailable?
* What happens if the AI produces incorrect results?
* Is the AI replacing an existing process or creating a new one?
* How important is the AI system to business operations?

For example:

> An organization may deploy AI to improve customer-service response times.

That objective alone is insufficient.

The organization should also understand:

```text
Business Objective
      ↓
Customer-Service Process
      ↓
AI Assistance
      ↓
Faster Responses
      ↓
Customer Outcome
```

The risk assessment should consider the entire chain.

---

# 72. Business Process Context

An AI system should be mapped to the business process in which it operates.

For example:

```text
Customer Request
      ↓
Customer-Service Process
      ↓
AI Classification
      ↓
Agent Recommendation
      ↓
Human Decision
      ↓
Customer Response
```

Each step can introduce different risks.

The AI itself may be technically reliable, but the overall process could still produce undesirable outcomes because of:

* Incorrect human interpretation.
* Poor workflow design.
* Inadequate escalation.
* Missing data.
* Excessive automation.
* Weak controls.

MAP therefore considers the **AI-enabled process**, not merely the AI component.

---

# 73. Business Dependency on AI

The organization should determine how dependent the business process is on the AI system.

A simple classification could be:

### Low Dependency

AI improves efficiency but the business can easily operate without it.

### Moderate Dependency

AI significantly improves operations but a manual alternative exists.

### High Dependency

AI is integrated into a critical process and manual operation would be difficult.

### Critical Dependency

AI failure could materially disrupt an essential business service.

The dependency level influences the potential impact of AI failure.

For example:

| ------------------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------- |

| Employee productivity assistant | Medium      | Operational            |
| Fraud detection system          | High        | Financial              |
| Network monitoring AI           | High        | Operational / Security |
| Medical decision-support AI     | Very High   | Potential human harm   |

These classifications are illustrative.

The important principle is:

> **The consequences of AI failure depend heavily on the business context in which the AI is deployed.**

# 76. AI Use Case Definition

A clearly defined use case is one of the most important MAP outputs.

A use case should describe:

* What the AI does.
* Why it does it.
* Who uses it.
* What information it processes.
* What output it produces.
* What action follows the output.

For example:

> "The AI system analyzes network-security events and provides prioritized recommendations to security analysts for investigation."

This is more useful than simply documenting:

> "AI-powered security platform."

---

# 77. Use Case Scope

The organization should define what the AI system **does and does not do**.

Example:

### In Scope

* Analyze security events.
* Prioritize alerts.
* Provide investigation recommendations.

### Out of Scope

* Automatically block network traffic.
* Modify firewall policies.
* Disable user accounts.

This distinction is important because expanding the system's authority can materially change its risk.

---

# 78. Use Case Boundaries

A useful way to document boundaries is:

```text

Classify
Recommend
Summarize

AI MAY NOT:
Approve
Execute
Delete
Modify
Escalate automatically
```

The actual permissions should depend on the organization's design.

This creates a clear relationship between the intended use case and system authority.

# 79. Use Case Evolution

For example:

```text

        ↓

AI recommendations become integrated into workflow

        ↓

AI automatically performs selected actions

        ↓

AI becomes a critical operational dependency
```

Each stage can create new risks.

MAP should therefore be revisited when the purpose or operating model changes.

# 80. AI Use Case Drift

**Use case drift** occurs when an AI system gradually becomes used for purposes beyond its approved scope.

Example:

```text
Approved:
AI summarizes customer feedback

Actual:
AI determines which customers should receive
priority service
```

The second activity may involve significantly greater impact.

Organizations should therefore monitor not only whether AI exists, but **how it is actually being used**.

The distinction helps prevent overly broad AI system definitions.

# 136. MAP and Contextual Risk

A risk cannot always be understood without its context.

For example:

> "The AI may produce incorrect output."

This statement is incomplete.

A more useful risk scenario is:

> "The AI may generate an incorrect network-security recommendation that a SOC analyst accepts without verification, resulting in delayed incident response."

The second statement provides:

* AI function.
* Business process.
* Human behavior.
* Failure mode.
* Consequence.

This is the value of MAP.

---

```text
    +

```

Example:

```text

+

```

This produces a more actionable risk scenario.

# 138. MAP and Enterprise Risk Language

MAP also helps translate AI terminology into enterprise risk language.

For example:

### Technical

> Model hallucination.

### GRC

> Risk that incorrect AI-generated information may be relied upon in a business process, resulting in operational or customer impact.

This translation is important for executives and risk committees.

# 139. MAP and Risk Committee Communication

An AI risk committee may not need to know every technical detail of the model.

Instead, MAP enables the risk team to communicate:

* What the system does.
* Why it matters.
* Who is affected.
* What can go wrong.
* How significant the impact could be.
* What controls are required.

This makes AI risk understandable to decision-makers.

# 140. MAP Practical Checklist

Before moving into detailed AI risk measurement, ask:

### System

* Is the AI system clearly defined?
* Are its boundaries documented?
* Are major components known?

### Purpose

* Is the business objective clear?
* Is intended use documented?
* Are prohibited or out-of-scope uses defined?

### People

* Are users known?
* Are affected stakeholders identified?
* Is human oversight understood?

### Data

* Are data sources known?
* Is data sensitivity understood?
* Is data ownership established?

### Technology

* Are models and dependencies known?
* Are third-party services identified?

### Environment

* Are geographic and regulatory contexts understood?
* Is business criticality known?

### Impact

* Are potential consequences understood?
* Are failure and misuse scenarios considered?

### Change

* Are triggers for reassessment defined?

# 142. MAP Core Principle

The central MAP principle is:

> **Understand the AI system within its complete operational, organizational, technical, human and regulatory context before making decisions about its risk.**

A mature MAP assessment should therefore answer five fundamental questions:

```text
1. WHAT is the AI system?

2. WHY is it being used?

3. WHO uses or is affected by it?

4. HOW does it operate within its environment?

5. WHAT could happen if it succeeds, fails, is misused, or changes?
```

The answers provide the contextual foundation for AI risk management and enable the organization to make proportionate, evidence-based decisions about AI risk.

## 143. MAP and Stakeholder Context

The MAP function requires the organization to identify the people, groups, organizations, and other entities that interact with, depend on, influence, or may be affected by an AI system.

Stakeholder identification is important because AI risks are not always experienced by the person operating the system.

A useful model is:

```text
                         AI SYSTEM
                             │
          ┌──────────────────┼──────────────────┐
          ▼                  ▼                  ▼
       USERS              OWNERS             AFFECTED
          │                  │               PARTIES
          │                  │                  │
          └──────────────────┼──────────────────┘
                             ▼
                          IMPACTS
```

The organization should therefore consider both **direct and indirect stakeholders**.

---

# 144. Direct Stakeholders

Direct stakeholders interact with or operate the AI system.

Examples include:

* AI operators.
* Employees.
* Analysts.
* Administrators.
* Developers.
* Customers using an AI service.

For example, in a security operations environment:

```text
SOC Analyst
     ↓
AI Security Assistant
     ↓
AI Recommendation
```

The SOC analyst is a direct stakeholder because they interact with the AI system.

---

# 145. Indirect Stakeholders

Indirect stakeholders may not interact with the AI system but may nevertheless be affected by its outputs.

For example:

```text
AI System
     ↓
Employee Decision
     ↓
Customer
     ↓
Customer Impact
```

The customer may never interact with the AI, but the AI influenced an outcome affecting that customer.

This distinction is important in AI risk management.

---

# 146. Stakeholder Categories

A practical stakeholder classification may include:

| Category    | Examples                              |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Business    | Business owner, executives            |

Not every AI system requires every category.

The stakeholder analysis should be proportional to the system's context and risk.

# 152. MAP and Affected Individuals

Particular attention should be given to individuals who may experience consequences from AI-generated decisions or recommendations.

Questions include:

* Can the individual be affected without knowing AI was involved?
* Can the individual challenge the result?
* Can the decision be corrected?
* Is the impact reversible?
* Does the individual have meaningful recourse?

These questions help establish the human context of AI risk.

# 153. MAP and Vulnerability

Some stakeholders may be more vulnerable to AI errors than others.

Potential factors include:

* Limited ability to challenge decisions.
* Dependence on the service.
* Limited technical understanding.
* Limited access to alternatives.
* Greater consequences from incorrect decisions.

The organization should determine whether such conditions are relevant to the use case.

# 160. MAP and Consequence Analysis

The organization should ask:

> **What happens if the AI behaves incorrectly?**

Possible consequences include:

* No material impact.
* Minor operational delay.
* Financial loss.
* Customer dissatisfaction.
* Regulatory issue.
* Security incident.
* Significant business disruption.
* Serious harm to individuals.

This provides contextual information for later risk prioritization.

# 161. MAP and Reversibility

The ability to reverse an AI-generated outcome is important.

### Easily Reversible

An incorrect draft is deleted before use.

### Moderately Reversible

An incorrect customer record is corrected later.

### Difficult to Reverse

An incorrect decision affects a person's opportunity.

### Potentially Irreversible

An AI action causes permanent physical, financial, or reputational consequences.

Reversibility should therefore be documented when relevant.

---

# 162. MAP and Recourse

**Recourse** refers to the ability to challenge, correct, or appeal an AI-influenced outcome.

A contextual assessment may ask:

* Can the person challenge the decision?
* Is there a human reviewer?
* Can the decision be corrected?
* Is the appeal process accessible?
* Is the reason for the decision understandable enough to support review?

Recourse becomes particularly important where AI affects individuals materially.

```text
 ↓
```

# 164. Meaningful Human Oversight

Simply placing a human somewhere in the process does not automatically create effective oversight.

The human should have:

* Appropriate authority.
* Relevant knowledge.
* Sufficient time.
* Access to necessary information.
* Ability to reject the AI output.
* Ability to escalate concerns.

MAP should therefore consider whether the human role is meaningful in practice.

# 165. MAP and Automation Bias

Human operators may over-rely on AI outputs.

For example:

```text

"High Priority"

        ↓

Analyst assumes:
"AI must be correct."

        ↓

No independent verification
```

This creates a contextual risk.

The organization should understand whether the operating environment encourages automation bias.

# 166. MAP and Human-AI Trust

Trust in AI should be appropriate to its capabilities.

Too little trust can reduce useful adoption.

Too much trust can cause:

* Over-reliance.
* Insufficient verification.
* Inappropriate automation.
* Reduced human judgment.

MAP should therefore consider the relationship between humans and the AI system.

# 202. MAP and Risk Proportionality

A useful principle is:

> **The greater the potential impact, autonomy, scale, sensitivity, and dependency, the greater the need for rigorous contextual understanding.**

This helps avoid both:

* Under-governance of high-risk AI.
* Excessive bureaucracy for low-risk AI.

# 231. MAP Core Principle

The MAP function is ultimately about **contextual understanding**.

A mature AI risk practitioner should be able to move from:

> **"We have an AI model."**

to:

> **"We understand what the AI system does, why it exists, how it operates, what information it processes, who interacts with it, who may be affected, what dependencies exist, what assumptions apply, and what consequences could arise from its use or failure."**

That transition is fundamental to practical AI risk management.

# 233. MAP Operating Procedure

Document relevant relationships, dependencies, stakeholders and impacts.

Update the context when material changes occur.

# 350. Final MAP Principle

The MAP function is fundamentally about **knowing what you are governing before attempting to measure and manage its risk**.

An organization should not begin with:

> "What controls should we apply to this AI?"

It should first establish:

> **"What is this AI system, why is it being used, how does it operate, what does it depend on, who does it affect, and what could happen in its actual operating environment?"**

A mature MAP process transforms an AI system from an abstract technology into a clearly understood **business, technical, human, data, and risk context**.

That contextual understanding provides the foundation for consistent, proportionate, evidence-based AI risk management.
