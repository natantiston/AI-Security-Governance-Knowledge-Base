# 14.19 — Privacy-Enhancing Technologies for AI

## Part 1 — Privacy-Enhancing Technologies for AI Foundations

### 1. Definition of Privacy-Enhancing Technologies for AI

Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs) are technical mechanisms designed to reduce privacy risks while enabling legitimate processing of information.

In AI environments, PETs can help protect information during:

* data collection;
* preprocessing;
* training;
* fine-tuning;
* inference;
* retrieval;
* memory;
* collaboration;
* analytics;
* and output generation.

Common AI-relevant PETs include:

* encryption;
* pseudonymization;
* anonymization;
* tokenization;
* differential privacy;
* federated learning;
* secure multi-party computation;
* homomorphic encryption;
* trusted execution environments;
* synthetic data;
* private information retrieval;
* and privacy-preserving data sharing.

A PET is a **technical privacy mechanism**, not automatically a legal authorization to process data.

---

### 2. Purpose of PET Governance in AI

PET governance exists to determine when and how privacy-enhancing technologies should be used.

Governance should establish:

* applicable privacy risks;
* technology suitability;
* protection objectives;
* residual risks;
* performance implications;
* implementation requirements;
* testing requirements;
* and ongoing assurance.

PET selection should be based on the actual threat and processing model.

**PET availability ≠ PET necessity.**

---

### 3. AI Privacy Processing Model

AI processing can expose personal or sensitive information at multiple stages.

```text id="pet-3a"
Data Collection
      |
      v
Data Preparation
      |
      v
Training / Fine-Tuning
      |
      v
Model
      |
      v
Inference
      |
      +----> RAG
      |
      +----> Memory
      |
      +----> Tools
      |
      v
Output
      |
      v
Storage / Sharing
```

PETs can be applied at different points in this lifecycle.

The appropriate PET depends on **where the privacy risk occurs and what protection is required**.

---

### 4. PETs versus Traditional Security Controls

PETs and security controls overlap but have different primary objectives.

| Technology           | Primary Objective                                            |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Encryption           | Protect confidentiality                                      |
| Access control       | Restrict access                                              |
| DLP                  | Detect/control sensitive information                         |
| Pseudonymization     | Reduce direct identifier exposure                            |
| Differential privacy | Limit information leakage from aggregate/statistical outputs |
| Federated learning   | Keep training data distributed                               |
| Secure computation   | Protect data during collaborative computation                |
| Synthetic data       | Reduce reliance on directly identifying data                 |
| TEE                  | Protect data during protected computation                    |

Therefore:

**Security control ≠ automatically privacy-enhancing technology.**

A technology can contribute to both security and privacy.

---

### 5. PETs versus Privacy Governance

PETs support privacy governance but do not replace it.

Privacy governance determines:

* why data is processed;
* whether processing is permitted;
* what data is necessary;
* who may access it;
* how long it should be retained;
* and what rights apply.

PETs determine how processing can be performed with reduced exposure.

**PET ≠ lawful basis.**

**PET ≠ purpose authorization.**

**PET ≠ accountability.**

---

### 6. PET Selection Principle

PET selection should begin with the privacy threat rather than the technology.

The organization should ask:

1. What data is being processed?
2. Why is it being processed?
3. Who can access it?
4. Where is it processed?
5. What privacy threat exists?
6. What protection objective is required?
7. Which PET addresses that threat?
8. What residual risk remains?

A sophisticated PET should not be deployed merely because it is technically impressive.

---

### 7. Data Classification for PET Selection

PET selection should consider data classification.

Possible classifications include:

* public;
* internal;
* confidential;
* restricted;
* personal;
* sensitive personal;
* regulated;
* highly restricted.

Higher-risk information may require stronger privacy-preserving mechanisms.

For example:

```text id="pet-7b"
Public Data
    |
    +--> Standard Controls

Personal Data
    |
    +--> Minimization
    +--> Pseudonymization
    +--> Access Control

Sensitive Data
    |
    +--> Stronger Isolation
    +--> Pseudonymization
    +--> Encryption
    +--> Additional PETs

Highly Sensitive Processing
    |
    +--> Advanced PET
    +--> Independent Validation
    +--> Continuous Monitoring
```

---

### 8. Data Minimization and PETs

PETs should complement data minimization.

Data minimization asks:

> Do we need this information at all?

PETs ask:

> If processing is necessary, how can we reduce privacy exposure?

Therefore:

**PET ≠ substitute for data minimization.**

Removing unnecessary data is generally preferable to protecting unnecessary data with increasingly complex technologies.

---

### 9. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with artificial identifiers.

Example:

```text id="pet-9c"
Before:

Name: Maria Santos
Employee ID: 78421
Department: Finance

After:

Subject: P-839271
Employee Token: E-55192
Department: Finance
```

A protected mapping mechanism can preserve the ability to reconnect the pseudonymized information to the individual.

Pseudonymization can reduce exposure while preserving analytical utility.

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

---

### 10. Anonymization

Anonymization aims to transform information so that individuals cannot reasonably be identified under the applicable standard.

AI creates particular challenges because:

* models may generate inferences;
* auxiliary datasets may enable re-identification;
* combinations of attributes can identify individuals;
* and generated outputs can expose information.

Anonymization therefore requires analysis of the complete processing environment, not merely removal of names.

---

### 11. Tokenization

Tokenization replaces sensitive values with tokens.

For example:

```text id="pet-11d"
Original:
Customer Account = 548921734

Tokenized:
Customer Account = TOK-91A7C3
```

The original value can remain in a separately protected token vault.

Tokenization can reduce the amount of sensitive information exposed to an AI system.

However:

**Tokenization ≠ authorization.**

A user must still be authorized to process the tokenized information and any associated mapping.

---

### 12. Encryption as a PET

Encryption can reduce unauthorized disclosure by transforming data into protected ciphertext.

Relevant states include:

* data at rest;
* data in transit;
* and, with specialized technologies, data in use.

AI environments may use encryption for:

* datasets;
* model artifacts;
* prompts;
* RAG stores;
* vector databases;
* memory;
* outputs;
* backups;
* and communication channels.

**Encryption protects information; it does not determine who is authorized to process it.**

---

### 13. Encryption in Use

Traditional encryption often protects information at rest and in transit.

Protecting data while computation occurs is more difficult.

Technologies such as:

* trusted execution environments;
* homomorphic encryption;
* and secure multi-party computation

can provide additional protection during processing.

However, each introduces different:

* performance;
* trust;
* complexity;
* compatibility;
* and operational considerations.

---

### 14. Trusted Execution Environments

A Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) provides an isolated execution environment intended to protect code and data during processing.

In AI workloads, TEEs may help protect:

* confidential inputs;
* model processing;
* encryption keys;
* intermediate computations;
* and outputs.

A TEE does not eliminate every privacy risk.

For example, it does not automatically prevent:

* authorized misuse;
* malicious inputs;
* excessive retention;
* inappropriate outputs;
* or incorrect policy configuration.

---

### 15. Confidential Computing

Confidential computing generally seeks to protect data while it is being processed through hardware-supported or otherwise protected execution environments.

AI applications may use confidential computing when sensitive information must be processed by infrastructure that cannot be fully trusted.

The architecture should identify:

* protected execution boundary;
* hardware trust assumptions;
* attestation;
* key release;
* workload identity;
* administrative access;
* and recovery mechanisms.

**Confidential computing ≠ complete privacy protection.**

---

### 16. Differential Privacy

Differential privacy provides mathematical mechanisms for limiting the information that can be learned about an individual from certain statistical computations.

It typically introduces controlled randomness into results.

The central concept is a privacy budget, commonly represented by **ε (epsilon)**.

Lower privacy loss generally means stronger privacy protection but potentially lower analytical utility.

The organization should govern:

* privacy budget;
* composition;
* query mechanisms;
* release conditions;
* and cumulative privacy loss.

---

### 17. Differential Privacy for AI Training

Differential privacy can be incorporated into some machine-learning training approaches.

One common approach is Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD), which can constrain the contribution of individual records and introduce noise during training.

The architecture should consider:

* clipping;
* noise;
* privacy budget;
* dataset size;
* model utility;
* repeated training;
* and privacy accounting.

**Differential privacy ≠ guarantee that a model cannot reveal any sensitive information under every attack.**

Its protection depends on the actual implementation and privacy parameters.

---

### 18. Differential Privacy for AI Outputs

Differential privacy can also be used when AI systems generate aggregate information.

Potential applications include:

* statistical reporting;
* population analysis;
* telemetry;
* aggregate behavioral analytics;
* and privacy-preserving research.

The privacy mechanism should be applied before information is released outside the protected processing boundary where appropriate.

---

### 19. Privacy Budget Governance

Differential privacy requires governance of cumulative privacy loss.

Repeated queries or training operations can consume privacy budget.

```text id="pet-19e"
Privacy Budget
      |
      +--> Query 1
      |
      +--> Query 2
      |
      +--> Query 3
      |
      +--> Model Training
      |
      v
Cumulative Privacy Loss
      |
      v
Policy Threshold
      |
   +--+--+
   |     |
 Within  Exceeded
   |     |
 Allow  Restrict
```

Privacy budgets should therefore be treated as governed resources.

---

### 20. Federated Learning

Federated learning allows model training to occur across distributed data sources without centrally collecting all raw training data.

A simplified architecture is:

```text id="pet-20f"
Data Site A ----+
                |
Data Site B ----+--> Local Training
                |          |
Data Site C ----+          v
                           |
                           v
                    Model Updates
                           |
                           v
                    Aggregation
                           |
                           v
                     Global Model
```

This can reduce central data exposure.

However:

**Federated learning ≠ automatic privacy.**

Model updates can themselves potentially reveal information.

---

### 21. Federated Learning Privacy Risks

Federated architectures can still be exposed to:

* gradient leakage;
* model-update attacks;
* malicious participants;
* inference attacks;
* membership inference;
* poisoning;
* and insecure aggregation.

Privacy controls may therefore be combined with:

* secure aggregation;
* differential privacy;
* encryption;
* authentication;
* and participant validation.

---

### 22. Secure Aggregation

Secure aggregation allows distributed participants to contribute model updates while limiting the ability to inspect each individual contribution.

The conceptual model is:

```text id="pet-22g"
Participant A --->+
Participant B --->+ 
Participant C --->+--> Secure Aggregator
Participant D --->+
                         |
                         v
                  Combined Update
                         |
                         v
                      Model
```

The objective is to reduce exposure of individual participant updates.

Secure aggregation should nevertheless be evaluated against:

* collusion;
* participant compromise;
* small participant groups;
* inference;
* and implementation weaknesses.

---

### 23. Secure Multi-Party Computation

Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) enables multiple parties to jointly compute results without necessarily revealing their private inputs to one another.

Potential AI applications include:

* collaborative analytics;
* joint model computation;
* cross-organization risk analysis;
* and privacy-preserving scoring.

MPC introduces computational and architectural complexity.

The organization should evaluate whether the privacy benefit justifies the operational cost.

---

### 24. Homomorphic Encryption

Homomorphic encryption enables certain computations to be performed on encrypted data without requiring conventional decryption before computation.

Conceptually:

```text id="pet-24h"
Plain Data
    |
    v
Encryption
    |
    v
Encrypted Data
    |
    v
Computation on Ciphertext
    |
    v
Encrypted Result
    |
    v
Decryption
    |
    v
Result
```

Homomorphic encryption can provide strong confidentiality properties but may impose significant computational overhead depending on the workload.

---

### 25. Fully Homomorphic Encryption

Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) supports arbitrary classes of computation on encrypted information within the capabilities of the scheme.

Potential AI applications include:

* privacy-preserving inference;
* sensitive analytics;
* confidential classification;
* and specialized machine-learning workloads.

FHE should be evaluated against:

* model architecture;
* latency;
* computational cost;
* supported operations;
* key management;
* and deployment maturity.

**Technical possibility ≠ operational suitability.**

---

### 26. Private Information Retrieval

Private Information Retrieval (PIR) enables a client to retrieve information from a data repository while reducing the repository's ability to determine which specific item was requested, depending on the scheme and threat model.

Potential AI applications include:

* private retrieval;
* privacy-preserving knowledge access;
* and sensitive RAG queries.

PIR can be particularly relevant where the query itself reveals sensitive information.

---

### 27. Privacy-Preserving RAG

Privacy-preserving RAG should protect both:

* the information being retrieved;
* and the query used to retrieve it.

Architecture may include:

```text id="pet-27i"
User
 |
 v
Privacy Gateway
 |
 +--> Query Minimization
 +--> Identity Validation
 +--> Authorization
 |
 v
Private Retrieval
 |
 v
Authorized Documents
 |
 v
Protected Context
 |
 v
LLM
 |
 v
Output Controls
```

RAG privacy requires more than protecting the vector database.

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 28. Vector-Store Privacy

Vector embeddings can encode information derived from source data.

Therefore, vector stores should be governed as potentially sensitive repositories.

Controls may include:

* encryption;
* tenant isolation;
* access control;
* metadata filtering;
* deletion;
* retention;
* embedding lifecycle management;
* and inference testing.

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

---

### 29. Synthetic Data

Synthetic data is artificially generated information designed to reproduce useful characteristics of a source dataset without directly reproducing the original records.

Potential benefits include:

* development;
* testing;
* model experimentation;
* training;
* and analytics.

However, synthetic data can still present privacy risks if it:

* memorizes source records;
* reproduces rare individuals;
* preserves unique attributes;
* or enables re-identification.

Therefore:

**Synthetic data ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

---

### 30. Synthetic Data Privacy Validation

Synthetic datasets should be evaluated before being classified as low-risk substitutes for real data.

Validation should consider:

* similarity to source records;
* uniqueness;
* re-identification risk;
* membership inference;
* memorization;
* sensitive attribute reproduction;
* and downstream disclosure.

The privacy classification should be based on evidence rather than the generation method alone.

---

### 31. Data Masking

Data masking transforms sensitive values so that they are not directly exposed.

Examples include:

* partial masking;
* substitution;
* shuffling;
* nullification;
* and format-preserving transformations.

AI systems can use masking before prompts, training, testing, or analytics.

Masking must preserve enough utility for the intended task without unnecessarily exposing sensitive information.

---

### 32. Data Generalization

Generalization reduces precision in data.

For example:

```text id="pet-32j"
Exact:
Age = 47
Location = Specific Street

Generalized:
Age Range = 45–50
Location = Madrid Region
```

Generalization can reduce re-identification risk while retaining analytical value.

However, multiple generalized attributes can sometimes still identify an individual.

---

### 33. Data Perturbation

Data perturbation intentionally modifies data to reduce the ability to infer sensitive information.

Techniques may include:

* noise addition;
* randomization;
* controlled distortion;
* and statistical perturbation.

The organization should evaluate whether the resulting data remains sufficiently useful for the AI task.

---

### 34. Data Aggregation

Aggregation combines individual records into broader statistical groups.

Example:

```text id="pet-34k"
Individual Records
       |
       v
Aggregation
       |
       v
Department-Level Statistics
       |
       v
AI Analysis
```

Aggregation can reduce individual-level exposure.

However, small groups or highly unique combinations can still create disclosure risk.

---

### 35. Privacy-Preserving Analytics

Privacy-preserving analytics combines technical controls to allow useful analysis while limiting exposure.

Potential mechanisms include:

* aggregation;
* differential privacy;
* pseudonymization;
* secure computation;
* access control;
* and synthetic data.

AI analytics should apply the least invasive technique capable of satisfying the business objective.

---

### 36. Privacy-Preserving Model Training

Privacy-preserving model training may combine multiple PETs.

For example:

```text id="pet-36l"
Distributed Data
      |
      v
Local Training
      |
      v
Gradient Clipping
      |
      v
Differential Privacy
      |
      v
Secure Aggregation
      |
      v
Global Model
      |
      v
Privacy Testing
```

The architecture should explicitly define which privacy threats each mechanism addresses.

---

### 37. Privacy-Preserving Inference

Privacy-preserving inference seeks to prevent unnecessary exposure of input data during model execution.

Potential techniques include:

* TEEs;
* homomorphic encryption;
* secure computation;
* local inference;
* data minimization;
* and pseudonymization.

The appropriate approach depends on:

* threat model;
* model complexity;
* latency;
* hardware;
* data sensitivity;
* and operational constraints.

---

### 38. On-Premises and Local AI as a Privacy Mechanism

Running AI locally can reduce external data transmission.

Possible architectures include:

* on-premises models;
* private cloud;
* edge inference;
* isolated AI clusters;
* and dedicated confidential-computing environments.

However:

**On-premises ≠ automatically private.**

Internal systems can still have:

* excessive access;
* poor retention;
* inadequate logging;
* insider threats;
* weak segmentation;
* and insecure model artifacts.

---

### 39. Privacy-Preserving Prompt Processing

Prompt privacy can be strengthened before information reaches an AI model.

Controls may include:

* redaction;
* tokenization;
* pseudonymization;
* minimization;
* local preprocessing;
* and policy enforcement.

Example:

```text id="pet-39m"
Raw Prompt
    |
    v
Sensitive Data Detection
    |
    v
Redaction / Tokenization
    |
    v
Minimized Prompt
    |
    v
Authorized AI Model
    |
    v
Controlled Output
```

The objective is to reduce unnecessary exposure while preserving task utility.

---

### 40. Privacy-Preserving Memory

Persistent AI memory can become a long-lived repository of personal information.

Privacy-preserving memory should provide:

* explicit retention rules;
* purpose restrictions;
* user isolation;
* classification;
* encryption;
* access control;
* deletion;
* and auditability.

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain personal information.**

---

### 41. Privacy-Preserving Agent Architecture

AI agents create additional privacy risks because they can retrieve information and invoke tools autonomously.

Privacy architecture should restrict:

* accessible datasets;
* tool scope;
* action scope;
* memory;
* retention;
* external communications;
* and recipient selection.

```text id="pet-41n"
Agent
 |
 +--> Identity
 |
 +--> Purpose
 |
 +--> Data Authorization
 |
 +--> Retrieval Policy
 |
 +--> Tool Policy
 |
 +--> Output Policy
 |
 v
Allowed Action
```

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

---

### 42. PETs and Data Subject Rights

PETs should support, where applicable, the exercise of data-subject rights.

Architecture should consider:

* access;
* rectification;
* erasure;
* restriction;
* objection;
* and portability.

For example, pseudonymization can complicate rights fulfillment if the organization cannot efficiently reconnect protected records to the relevant individual.

PET design should therefore account for operational rights processes.

---

### 43. PETs and Data Retention

PETs do not eliminate retention requirements.

Organizations should establish lifecycle policies for:

* source data;
* transformed data;
* pseudonymized data;
* encrypted data;
* synthetic datasets;
* embeddings;
* model artifacts;
* logs;
* and privacy-control metadata.

**Encryption ≠ deletion.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ deletion.**

**Synthetic transformation ≠ automatic exemption from retention governance.**

---

### 44. PETs and Data Deletion

AI environments can create multiple derived artifacts.

```text id="pet-44o"
Source Data
   |
   +--> Training Dataset
   |
   +--> Embedding
   |
   +--> Memory
   |
   +--> Prompt
   |
   +--> Output
   |
   +--> Log
   |
   +--> Model Artifact
   |
   +--> Backup
```

Deletion governance must determine which artifacts are affected by a deletion requirement.

**Source-data deletion ≠ automatic deletion of every derived AI artifact.**

---

### 45. PETs and Third-Party AI Providers

External AI providers may offer PET capabilities such as:

* encryption;
* isolated processing;
* regional processing;
* confidential computing;
* customer-managed keys;
* and privacy-preserving training options.

However, provider claims should be evaluated against:

* contractual commitments;
* technical configuration;
* independent assurance;
* actual data flows;
* subprocessors;
* and operational evidence.

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

---

### 46. PETs and Privacy Threat Modeling

PET selection should be driven by threat modeling.

Threats may include:

* re-identification;
* membership inference;
* model inversion;
* training-data extraction;
* linkage attacks;
* unauthorized retrieval;
* cross-tenant leakage;
* insider access;
* provider access;
* and inference attacks.

The architecture should map each PET to the threat it mitigates.

---

### 47. PET Effectiveness

A PET should not be considered effective merely because it has been deployed.

Effectiveness should be validated through:

* technical testing;
* configuration verification;
* privacy metrics;
* attack simulation;
* leakage testing;
* independent assessment;
* and continuous monitoring.

The organization should distinguish:

**PET implementation ≠ PET effectiveness.**

---

### 48. PET Limitations and Residual Risk

Every PET has limitations.

Examples:

| PET                  | Potential Limitation                            |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Pseudonymization     | Re-identification remains possible              |
| Encryption           | Authorized endpoints can still access plaintext |
| Differential privacy | Utility/privacy tradeoff                        |
| Federated learning   | Updates can leak information                    |
| Synthetic data       | May reproduce source characteristics            |
| TEE                  | Depends on hardware and trust assumptions       |
| MPC                  | Computational and operational complexity        |
| FHE                  | Significant computational overhead              |
| Tokenization         | Mapping system remains sensitive                |
| Aggregation          | Small groups can remain identifiable            |

PET selection should therefore include residual-risk analysis.

---

### 49. Enterprise PET Governance Principle

Enterprise AI privacy should treat PETs as components of a broader privacy architecture.

The governance chain is:

**Purpose → Data → Classification → Necessity → Privacy Risk → Threat Model → PET Selection → Technical Enforcement → Testing → Monitoring → Rights → Retention → Deletion → Reassessment.**

The organization should never assume that deploying one PET solves the entire privacy problem.

Key distinctions include:

* **PET ≠ lawful basis.**
* **PET ≠ authorization.**
* **PET ≠ data minimization.**
* **Encryption ≠ authorization.**
* **Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**
* **Tokenization ≠ anonymization.**
* **Synthetic data ≠ automatically anonymous data.**
* **Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**
* **Federated learning ≠ automatic privacy.**
* **Confidential computing ≠ complete privacy protection.**
* **Differential privacy ≠ zero information leakage.**
* **Privacy protection ≠ security assurance.**
* **Technical privacy control ≠ legal compliance.**

---

### 50. Core Principle of Privacy-Enhancing Technologies for AI

The fundamental principle is:

> **Use the minimum amount of identifiable or sensitive information necessary for the authorized AI purpose, and apply the strongest practical privacy-enhancing mechanism appropriate to the threat, processing model, and residual risk.**

A mature AI privacy architecture therefore does not begin with:

**“Which PET should we deploy?”**

It begins with:

**“What processing is necessary, what privacy risk does it create, and how can the processing be redesigned to minimize exposure before selecting technical protections?”**

The resulting principle is:

**Necessity → Minimization → Threat Model → PET Selection → Enforcement → Verification → Continuous Assurance.**


## Part 2 — Privacy-Enhancing Technologies for AI Controls

### 51. AI PET Control Framework

The AI PET Control Framework establishes the enterprise controls required to select, implement, operate, test, and govern privacy-enhancing technologies across AI workloads.

The framework should address:

* data minimization;
* pseudonymization;
* anonymization;
* tokenization;
* encryption;
* differential privacy;
* federated learning;
* secure aggregation;
* secure multi-party computation;
* homomorphic encryption;
* trusted execution environments;
* synthetic data;
* privacy-preserving RAG;
* privacy-preserving inference;
* privacy-preserving memory;
* privacy-preserving agents;
* and continuous assurance.

Controls should be mapped to specific privacy threats and processing activities.

---

### 52. PET Planning Control

The organization should establish PET requirements during AI planning rather than after implementation.

Planning should identify:

* business purpose;
* AI use case;
* data categories;
* privacy risks;
* threat model;
* applicable requirements;
* expected utility;
* required PET capabilities;
* implementation constraints;
* and assurance requirements.

PET planning should occur before confidential or personal information enters the AI processing pipeline.

---

### 53. PET Scope Control

The scope of each PET implementation should be explicitly defined.

Scope should identify:

* datasets;
* models;
* applications;
* users;
* environments;
* providers;
* geographic locations;
* processing activities;
* and AI lifecycle stages.

For example, a PET applied only to training does not automatically protect inference data.

**PET coverage ≠ complete AI privacy protection.**

---

### 54. PET Classification Control

Each PET implementation should be classified according to its protection objective.

Classification may include:

* data-at-rest protection;
* data-in-transit protection;
* data-in-use protection;
* identity protection;
* query protection;
* training-data protection;
* inference protection;
* output protection;
* or privacy-loss reduction.

Classification helps prevent inappropriate substitution of one PET for another.

---

### 55. PET Applicability Control

The organization should determine whether a PET is appropriate for the specific AI workload.

Applicability should consider:

* data sensitivity;
* processing purpose;
* threat model;
* regulatory requirements;
* architecture;
* performance;
* scalability;
* interoperability;
* and residual risk.

A PET should not be deployed simply because it is available.

**Technical availability ≠ applicability.**

---

### 56. PET Requirement-Mapping Control

Each PET requirement should be mapped to a specific:

* privacy requirement;
* threat;
* risk;
* control;
* implementation;
* test;
* and evidence source.

Example:

```text id="pet-control-56"
Privacy Requirement
       |
       v

       |
       v
Risk
       |
       v
PET Control
       |
       v
Technical Implementation
       |
       v
Test
       |
       v
Evidence
```

This creates traceability between privacy objectives and technical controls.

---

### 57. PET Risk-Classification Control

PET implementations should be risk-classified according to the potential consequences of privacy failure.

Higher-risk scenarios may involve:

* sensitive personal data;
* large populations;
* high-impact decisions;
* external providers;
* cross-border processing;
* model training;
* autonomous agents;
* or large-scale inference.

Higher-risk PET implementations should receive stronger validation.

---

### 58. PET Data-Inventory Control

The organization should maintain an inventory of data processed through PET-enabled AI systems.

The inventory should identify:

* source;
* data owner;
* classification;
* purpose;
* PET applied;
* AI system;
* provider;
* location;
* retention;
* and downstream recipients.

The inventory should include derived artifacts where relevant.

---

### 59. PET Data-Ownership Control

Data ownership should remain explicit when PETs transform information.

Ownership should cover:

* source data;
* pseudonymized data;
* tokenized data;
* synthetic data;
* embeddings;
* encrypted datasets;
* training datasets;
* and model artifacts.

Transformation does not automatically eliminate governance responsibility.

---

### 60. PET Data-Classification Enforcement Control

Data classification should remain enforceable after transformation.

For example:

```text id="pet-control-60"

Pseudonymized Dataset
      |
      v

      |
      v
```

The organization should not automatically downgrade data classification simply because a PET was applied.

### 61. PET Data-Minimization Control

The organization should minimize information before applying computationally expensive PETs where practical.

Controls should support:

* field filtering;
* record filtering;
* aggregation;
* redaction;
* tokenization;
* pseudonymization;
* and selective disclosure.

The preferred sequence is generally:

**Avoid unnecessary data → minimize necessary data → transform data → protect remaining data.**

---

### 62. PET Data-Necessity Control

The organization should verify that information processed through AI is necessary for the approved purpose.

Questions should include:

* Why is the data required?
* Could less data accomplish the purpose?
* Could aggregated data be used?
* Could synthetic data be used?
* Could local processing avoid external transmission?
* Could pseudonymized information provide sufficient utility?

**PET deployment ≠ justification for unnecessary processing.**

---

### 63. PET Data-Accuracy Control

Privacy-enhanced transformations should not introduce unacceptable data-quality problems.

The organization should evaluate:

* distortion;
* missing values;
* aggregation effects;
* synthetic-data errors;
* token mapping errors;
* and statistical changes.

Data should remain fit for its approved AI purpose.

---

### 64. PET Data-Utility Control

Privacy controls often introduce a tradeoff between privacy and utility.

The organization should measure:

* model accuracy;
* analytical usefulness;
* statistical validity;
* latency;
* completeness;
* and privacy protection.

The objective should be an appropriate balance rather than maximum privacy at the expense of an unusable system.

---

### 65. PET Purpose-Enforcement Control

PET-enabled processing should remain restricted to its approved purpose.

The control should prevent transformed data from being reused for unrelated activities without appropriate authorization.

For example:

**Approved:** privacy-preserving research.

does not automatically authorize:

**Unapproved:** employee profiling.

### 66. PET Identity Control

PET-enabled systems should establish identities for:

* applications;
* workloads;
* services;
* administrators;
* and external providers.

Mechanisms may include:

* workload identity;
* certificates;
* federated identity;
* hardware-backed credentials;
* and service authentication.

**Authentication ≠ authorization.**

Authorization should consider:

* user;
* data;
* purpose;
* operation;
* PET;
* model;
* provider;
* environment;
* and destination.

A user authorized to access a dataset is not automatically authorized to use it for AI training.

PET infrastructure should operate with minimum required privileges.

Least privilege should apply to:

* encryption keys;
* token vaults;
* mapping tables;
* datasets;
* aggregation systems;
* model-training infrastructure;
* secure enclaves;
* and privacy-control administration.

Administrative privileges should be separately governed and monitored.

AI workloads using PETs should use dedicated workload identities.

Workload identity should support:

* authorization;
* access scoping;
* credential rotation;

Controls should include:

* encryption;
* strict access control;
* key management;
* monitoring;
* backup protection;
* and recovery procedures.

* encryption;
* separation from pseudonymized data;

### 117. Core Control Principle

The core control principle is:

> **A privacy-enhancing technology must be selected according to the specific privacy threat, implemented within an authorized processing purpose, technically enforced, independently validated where appropriate, and continuously monitored for effectiveness and residual risk.**

The complete control chain is:

**Purpose → Data → Classification → Necessity → Minimization → Threat Model → PET Selection → Identity → Authorization → Transformation → Protected Processing → Output Control → Retention → Deletion → Testing → Evidence → Monitoring → Reassessment.**

The critical control distinctions are:

* **PET ≠ lawful basis.**
* **PET ≠ authorization.**
* **PET ≠ data minimization.**
* **Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**
* **Tokenization ≠ anonymization.**
* **Synthetic data ≠ automatically anonymous data.**
* **Encryption ≠ authorization.**
* **Encryption ≠ deletion.**
* **Federated learning ≠ automatic privacy.**
* **Secure aggregation ≠ complete privacy protection.**
* **Confidential computing ≠ complete privacy protection.**
* **Differential privacy ≠ zero information leakage.**
* **DLP detection ≠ authorization.**
* **Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**
* **Memory capability ≠ permission to retain personal information.**
* **Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**
* **Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**
* **Control implementation ≠ control effectiveness.**
* **Testing ≠ permanent privacy assurance.**
* **Risk acceptance ≠ authorization to violate mandatory requirements.**

Re-identification should be treated as a privileged operation.

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

---

### 138. AI PET Anonymization Governance

Anonymization governance should define criteria for claiming that information is sufficiently anonymized.

Assessment should consider:

* singling out;
* linkability;
* inference;
* auxiliary information;
* uniqueness;
* population size;
* and technical capabilities.

The organization should not classify data as anonymous solely because names were removed.

---

### 139. AI PET Tokenization Governance

Tokenization governance should control:

* token generation;
* token uniqueness;
* token vaults;
* mapping;
* access;
* rotation;
* recovery;
* and deletion.

Tokens should be classified according to their potential sensitivity and linkability.

---

### 140. AI PET Purpose Governance

Every PET-enabled AI processing activity should have an explicit purpose.

Purpose governance should prevent transformed data from being reused for:

* unrelated analytics;
* unauthorized profiling;
* general-purpose training;
* secondary commercialization;
* or unrelated model development.

A PET should not be used to legitimize an otherwise unauthorized purpose.

---

### 141. AI PET Data-Minimization Governance

Governance should require minimization before PET processing where possible.

The organization should evaluate whether it can:

* eliminate unnecessary fields;
* aggregate records;
* reduce precision;
* process locally;
* pseudonymize;
* tokenize;
* or use synthetic data.

**The best protected data is often data that never needed to be processed.**

---

### 142. AI PET RAG Governance

Privacy-preserving RAG should be governed as a distinct processing capability.

Governance should establish:

* authorized repositories;
* document classification;
* retrieval permissions;
* query protection;
* tenant isolation;
* embedding protection;
* retention;
* and deletion.

RAG should never bypass existing data-access governance.

---

### 143. AI PET Retrieval-Authorization Governance

Retrieval authorization should occur before confidential or personal information enters the model context.

The authorization model should evaluate:

**User → Purpose → Data → Repository → Operation → Model → Provider.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 144. AI PET Vector-Store Governance

Vector stores should be governed as potentially sensitive data repositories.

Governance should cover:

* embedding classification;
* metadata;
* access control;
* encryption;
* tenant isolation;
* retention;
* deletion;
* and leakage testing.

Embedding generation should not automatically change the underlying governance status of the information.

---

### 145. AI PET Memory Governance

Persistent AI memory should be governed as a data repository.

Governance should establish:

* permitted memory categories;
* purpose;
* retention;
* user scope;
* access;
* deletion;
* classification;
* and monitoring.

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain personal information.**

### 146. AI PET Prompt Governance

Governance should establish:

* prohibited data;
* permitted data;
* minimization;
* redaction;
* pseudonymization;

The architecture should enforce these requirements rather than relying solely on user awareness.

### 147. AI PET Context Governance

* retrieval permissions;
* context length;
* sensitive-data filtering;
* and retention.

Unnecessary context increases privacy exposure.

### 149. AI PET Redaction Governance

* data classification;
* purpose;

* provider;
* model;
* and downstream use.

Redaction should be tested against contextual and indirect identifiers.

### 150. AI PET Pseudonymization Governance

Pseudonymization governance should include lifecycle management.

The governance model should control:

* pseudonym creation;
* mapping;
* access;
* re-identification;

* rotation;

The organization should prevent unnecessary re-identification.

### 151. AI PET Encryption Governance

Governance should define:

* logging purpose;

* retention;
* access;
* geographic location;
* and monitoring.

### 154. AI PET Monitoring Governance

Monitoring should assess whether PET controls remain effective.

Monitoring should cover:

* privacy parameters;
* data flows;
* configuration;
* re-identification;
* membership inference;
* model extraction;
* provider changes;
* and anomalous access.

Monitoring itself should follow privacy principles.

### 155. AI PET Provider Governance

* technical capabilities;

* processing locations;
* provider access;
* security;
* privacy;
* subprocessors;
* retention;
* deletion;
* and independent assurance.

Provider documentation should be treated as evidence, not automatic proof of control effectiveness.

* product improvement;
* model evaluation;

### 177. Strategic Governance Principle

The strategic principle is:

> **Privacy-enhancing technologies should be governed as risk-based technical mechanisms within the broader AI privacy governance framework, with their selection driven by necessity and threat modeling, their implementation subject to authorization and validation, and their effectiveness continuously reassessed.**

The strategic governance chain is:

**Business Purpose → AI Use Case → Data → Classification → Necessity → Minimization → Privacy Risk → Threat Model → PET Selection → Architecture → Authorization → Implementation → Validation → Approval → Monitoring → Incident Response → Change → Reassessment → Reauthorization.**

The central governance distinctions are:

**PET ≠ lawful basis.**

**PET ≠ authorization.**

**PET ≠ data minimization.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

**Tokenization ≠ anonymization.**

**Synthetic data ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

**Encryption ≠ authorization.**

**Encryption ≠ deletion.**

**Federated learning ≠ automatic privacy.**

**Secure aggregation ≠ complete privacy protection.**

**Confidential computing ≠ complete privacy protection.**

**Differential privacy ≠ zero information leakage.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain personal information.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**Control implementation ≠ control effectiveness.**

**Testing ≠ permanent privacy assurance.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ authorization to violate mandatory requirements.**
