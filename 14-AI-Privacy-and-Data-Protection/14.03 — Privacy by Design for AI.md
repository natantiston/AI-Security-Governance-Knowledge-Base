# 14.03 — Privacy by Design for AI

## Part 1 — Privacy by Design for AI Foundations

### 1. Definition of Privacy by Design for AI

Privacy by Design for AI is the practice of embedding privacy protections into the architecture, engineering, data lifecycle, governance, and operation of AI systems from the earliest design stage.

Privacy is therefore treated as an **architectural and engineering requirement**, not merely a compliance review performed after an AI system has been built.

---

### 2. Purpose of Privacy by Design for AI

The purpose is to ensure that privacy risks are identified and addressed before AI processing creates unnecessary exposure to individuals.

The design objective is:

```text id="pbd14p1-01"
Privacy Requirement
       |
       v
Architecture
       |
       v
Implementation
       |
       v
Testing
       |
       v
Deployment
       |
       v
Continuous Assurance
```

Privacy should be embedded throughout the lifecycle.

---

### 3. Privacy by Design and AI

Traditional applications may have relatively predictable data flows.

AI systems can introduce dynamic processing through:

* Prompts
* Context
* Models
* RAG
* Memory
* Tools
* Agents
* Generated outputs
* External providers

Privacy by Design must therefore address both **known data flows and dynamically generated processing paths**.

---

### 4. Privacy as an Architectural Property

Privacy should be designed into the system rather than relying exclusively on policies or user behavior.

Examples include:

* Data minimization
* Tenant isolation
* Purpose-based access
* Encryption
* DLP
* Privacy gateways
* Retention enforcement
* Secure deletion
* Provider restrictions

A privacy policy without technical enforcement provides limited protection.

### 10. Purpose Limitation by Design

AI architecture should constrain data use to approved purposes.

A customer dataset should not automatically become available for:

* Employee profiling
* Model training
* Marketing
* Unrelated analytics
* Automated decision-making

Purpose should be an enforceable policy attribute where technically feasible.

### 11. Privacy-Protective Defaults

AI systems should begin from privacy-protective configurations.

Examples include:

* Minimal logging
* Limited retention
* Disabled unnecessary memory
* Restricted external sharing
* Restricted provider training
* Least-privilege retrieval
* Tenant isolation

Privacy should not depend on users discovering and changing complex settings.

---

Personal and sensitive information should be classified before being processed by AI where practical.

Classification can use:

* Metadata
* Data catalogs
* DLP
* Content inspection

* Application context

Classification should drive downstream controls.

### 50. Core Principle of Privacy by Design for AI

**Privacy by Design for AI means that privacy is engineered into the AI system before personal-data processing occurs and remains embedded throughout design, development, training, inference, retrieval, memory, tool use, agent execution, deployment, monitoring, change, and retirement.**

The architecture should proactively prevent unnecessary privacy exposure rather than relying primarily on detection after disclosure.

The fundamental principles are:

**Privacy requirement ≠ documentation requirement.**

**Privacy policy ≠ technical enforcement.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to process.**

**Model capability ≠ privacy authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorization.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

**Encryption ≠ Privacy by Design.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ automatic anonymization.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human oversight.**

**Privacy testing ≠ privacy assurance.**

**Privacy by Design ≠ one-time architecture review.**

**A privacy-preserving design must remain continuously testable, enforceable, observable, and reassessable as AI models, data, prompts, RAG, memory, tools, agents, providers, processing purposes, and regulatory requirements change.**

* Automated decision-making
* Persistent monitoring
* Persistent memory
* Cross-border processing
* External providers
* Vulnerable populations
