# 14.14 — AI Data Minimization

## Part 1 — AI Data Minimization Foundations

### 1. Definition of AI Data Minimization

AI data minimization is the principle and practice of limiting AI-related data collection, processing, retrieval, retention, disclosure, and generation to information that is adequate, relevant, and necessary for an authorized purpose.

In AI systems, minimization applies not only to information explicitly collected from users but also to information introduced through:

* prompts;
* context;
* conversation history;
* RAG;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* tools;
* agents;
* training datasets;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* model outputs;
* profiles;
* logs;
* analytics;
* backups;
* third-party providers.

The objective is not simply to collect less data.

It is to **reduce unnecessary data exposure throughout the entire AI lifecycle**.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Data Minimization Governance

The purpose of AI data minimization governance is to prevent AI systems from processing information merely because it is technically available.

Governance should establish:

* what information is necessary;
* why it is necessary;
* when it is necessary;
* who needs access;
* how much information is required;
* how long it should be retained;
* whether derived information is necessary;
* whether the same objective can be achieved with less data.

The central question is:

> **What is the minimum information required to achieve the authorized AI purpose effectively and safely?**

---

### 3. AI Data Minimization Lifecycle

Data minimization should operate across the entire AI lifecycle.

```text id="m7q2vx"
Purpose Definition
       ↓
Data Identification
       ↓
Necessity Assessment
       ↓
Data Minimization
       ↓
Collection
       ↓
Processing
       ↓
Retrieval / Context
       ↓
Inference
       ↓
Output
       ↓
Retention
       ↓
Deletion
       ↓
Reassessment
```

Minimization should therefore not be treated as a one-time decision made during data collection.

---

### 4. Data Minimization versus Data Collection

Data collection asks:

> What information are we obtaining?

Data minimization asks:

> Which of that information is actually necessary?

An application may collect ten fields while only three are required for the AI use case.

The presence of a field in an existing database does not establish that the AI system should process it.

**Data availability ≠ permission to process.**

---

### 5. Data Minimization versus Data Retention

Minimization and retention address different lifecycle questions.

**Minimization:**
Should we process this information at all?

**Retention:**
Should we continue keeping this information?

```text id="k4r9wp"
Collect Less
    ↓
Process Less
    ↓
Retrieve Less
    ↓
Expose Less
    ↓
Retain Less
    ↓
Delete Earlier
```

A system that collects minimal information but retains it indefinitely still has an excessive data lifecycle.

---

### 6. Purpose Limitation and Data Minimization

Data minimization is closely connected to purpose limitation.

The organization should first establish:

> **Why is the AI system processing the data?**

Then determine:

> **What minimum data is necessary for that purpose?**

For example, an AI customer-support assistant may require:

* customer account identifier;
* relevant support history;
* current issue.

It may not require unrelated:

* medical information;
* family information;
* political information;
* unrelated financial history.

---

### 7. Necessity Assessment

Necessity assessment determines whether a specific data element is genuinely required.

For each data element, the organization should consider:

* Is it required?
* Is it directly relevant?
* Is there a less intrusive alternative?
* Can the AI perform adequately without it?
* Can a reduced representation be used?
* Can the data be transformed?
* Can the data be retrieved only when needed?

Technical usefulness alone does not establish necessity.

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

---

### 8. Relevance Assessment

A data element may be relevant to an AI system without being necessary.

For example, an employee's entire HR record may be relevant to an HR assistant in a broad sense, but a particular question may require only:

* employee ID;
* department;
* employment status.

Minimization requires selecting only the information needed for the specific task.

Therefore:

**Data relevance ≠ authorization to process.**

---

### 9. Adequacy in AI Processing

Minimized data must still be sufficient for the authorized purpose.

Excessive minimization can cause:

* inaccurate results;
* missing context;
* unsafe decisions;
* poor recommendations;
* incorrect retrieval;
* incomplete analysis.

Therefore, minimization is not simply "remove as much as possible."

The objective is:

**minimum necessary data that remains adequate for the authorized purpose.**

---

### 10. Personal Data Minimization

AI systems should minimize the processing of personal data.

Examples include minimizing:

* names;
* addresses;
* phone numbers;
* email addresses;
* identifiers;
* behavioral information;
* location information;
* employment data;
* customer history.

Where an AI task can be completed without directly identifying an individual, the architecture should consider alternatives.

---

### 11. Sensitive Data Minimization

Sensitive information should receive enhanced minimization controls.

Examples include:

* financial information;
* health information;
* authentication information;
* confidential employment data;
* proprietary information;
* security information.

A system should not expose sensitive information to an AI model simply because the information is present in the source repository.

---

### 12. Special-Category Data Minimization

Special-category personal data requires particularly careful assessment.

AI architectures should prevent unnecessary inclusion of information relating to areas such as:

* health;
* biometric information where applicable;
* genetic information;
* political opinions;
* religious or philosophical beliefs;
* trade-union membership;
* sexual orientation.

Where such information is not necessary for the authorized purpose, it should not be introduced into the AI processing chain.

---

### 13. Prompt Data Minimization

Prompts should contain only information necessary to obtain the intended AI result.

Poor practice:

```text id="v6n2ra"
Entire Customer Record
        ↓
       LLM
        ↓
Answer One Simple Question
```

Better practice:

```text id="c8p5mz"
Customer Question
      +
Minimum Required Attributes
      ↓
     LLM
      ↓
Relevant Answer
```

Users should be trained not to paste entire documents or records when only a small portion is required.

---

### 14. Context Minimization

AI context windows can contain large quantities of information.

More context does not automatically produce better results.

Excessive context can create:

* privacy exposure;
* irrelevant information;
* prompt injection exposure;
* sensitive-data disclosure;
* higher processing cost;
* accidental cross-user exposure;
* unnecessary provider transmission.

Context should therefore be limited to information required for the task.

---

### 15. Conversation Minimization

Conversation systems should avoid retaining or reusing unnecessary historical content.

Conversation minimization may include:

* limited history;
* selective history;
* summarized history;
* expiration;
* user-controlled history;
* sensitive-data filtering.

The AI system should not automatically treat every previous conversation as relevant to every future request.

---

### 16. Memory Minimization

AI memory should retain only information that provides a justified continuing purpose.

Potential memory candidates may include:

* stable preferences;
* workflow settings;
* recurring instructions.

Information that is unnecessary for future personalization should not automatically become persistent memory.

Therefore:

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 17. RAG Data Minimization

RAG systems should index only information necessary for the intended retrieval purpose.

Instead of indexing an entire enterprise repository:

```text id="q3w7kt"
Enterprise Repository
        ↓
     Everything
        ↓
   Vector Store
        ↓
      LLM
```

a minimized architecture uses:

```text id="b5m9px"
Approved Data Sources
        ↓
Purpose Filter
        ↓
Data Classification
        ↓
Relevant Content
        ↓
Vector Store
        ↓
Authorized Retrieval
        ↓
LLM
```

This reduces both retrieval exposure and downstream model exposure.

---

### 18. Vector Store Minimization

Vector stores should minimize:

* indexed documents;
* chunks;
* metadata;
* identifiers;
* embeddings;
* duplicate records.

Metadata should be minimized as well as document content.

For example, an embedding does not necessarily need to include unnecessary identifying metadata if the retrieval workflow can function without it.

---

### 19. Retrieval Minimization

Retrieval should return the minimum information necessary for the task.

A retrieval system should avoid returning:

* entire databases;
* unrelated documents;
* excessive document history;
* unnecessary metadata;
* unrelated personal information.

The retrieval process should therefore apply:

* purpose;
* identity;
* authorization;
* relevance;
* classification;
* minimum-result rules.

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 20. Training Data Minimization

Training datasets should contain only information justified for the model-development purpose.

Training data should be assessed for:

* unnecessary personal information;
* duplicated records;
* irrelevant fields;
* excessive historical data;
* sensitive content;
* obsolete information;
* unnecessary metadata.

The existence of a large dataset does not establish that all of it should be used for training.

---

### 21. Fine-Tuning Data Minimization

Fine-tuning datasets should be narrowly scoped to the capability being developed.

For example, a model being fine-tuned for customer-support classification may not require complete customer profiles.

Minimization may involve:

* removing unrelated fields;
* removing identifiers;
* reducing historical records;
* excluding sensitive attributes;
* using synthetic examples;
* selecting representative samples.

---

### 22. Synthetic Data and Minimization

Synthetic data can reduce the need to process real-world personal information in some AI development scenarios.

Potential uses include:

* development;
* testing;
* demonstrations;
* model evaluation;
* pipeline validation.

However, synthetic data should still be assessed for:

* realism;
* privacy leakage;
* representativeness;
* security;
* unintended reconstruction of source information.

Synthetic data is a minimization technique, not an automatic guarantee of privacy.

---

### 23. Pseudonymization and Minimization

Pseudonymization can reduce unnecessary exposure of direct identifiers while preserving certain analytical relationships.

For example:

```text id="r8m4cy"
Jonathan Morales
       ↓
Pseudonymization
       ↓
Subject-7F31
       ↓
AI Processing
```

However, if the AI does not require any persistent identifier, even the pseudonym may be unnecessary.

Therefore:

**Pseudonymization ≠ automatically minimized processing.**

---

### 24. Anonymization and Minimization

Anonymization may reduce the need to process identifiable information where genuine anonymization is achievable.

However, removing names or identifiers alone does not necessarily make information anonymous.

Minimization should be assessed before and after anonymization.

---

### 25. Redaction and Minimization

Redaction removes unnecessary information before it reaches the AI processing environment.

Potential redaction targets include:

* account numbers;
* personal identifiers;
* credentials;
* medical details;
* financial information;
* internal secrets.

A privacy gateway may implement:

```text id="n2x6vz"
User Input
    ↓
Detection
    ↓
Classification
    ↓
Redaction
    ↓
Minimum Necessary Prompt
    ↓
AI Model
```

---

### 26. Tokenization and Minimization

Tokenization can replace sensitive values with controlled tokens.

For example:

```text id="s7k3mw"
Credit Card: 4111-XXXX-XXXX-1111
          ↓
Token: CC_TOKEN_82A7
```

The AI system may then operate on the token rather than the original value.

Tokenization reduces exposure but does not automatically eliminate governance requirements.

---

### 27. Data Masking and Minimization

Masking can reduce the amount of sensitive information visible to AI systems and users.

Examples include:

* partial account numbers;
* masked email addresses;
* masked telephone numbers;
* truncated identifiers.

Masking should be based on the actual task requirement.

If the complete value is unnecessary, exposing it provides no governance benefit.

---

### 28. DLP and Data Minimization

Data-loss prevention systems can detect information that should not enter an AI workflow.

DLP may identify:

* personal identifiers;
* payment information;
* credentials;
* confidential documents;
* regulated information.

DLP can support minimization but does not determine whether processing is legally or operationally authorized.

**DLP detection ≠ authorization.**

---

### 29. AI Identity and Data Minimization

Identity determines whose data may be accessed.

Minimization determines how much of that data should be exposed.

For example:

```text id="p4f8qx"
Authenticated User
       ↓
Authorized Customer Record
       ↓
Minimum Required Fields
       ↓
AI Context
```

Authentication alone should not result in unrestricted data exposure.

---

### 30. Authorization and Data Minimization

Authorization should be combined with minimization.

A user may be authorized to access an entire customer database for administrative purposes, but the AI application may need only one customer record.

Therefore:

**Authorization to access data ≠ authorization to expose all data to AI.**

---

### 31. Least Privilege and Data Minimization

Least privilege limits what an identity can access.

Data minimization limits what information is actually used.

Together:

```text id="d5k9rw"
Identity
   ↓
Authorization
   ↓
Least-Privilege Access
   ↓
Minimum Necessary Data
   ↓
AI Processing
```

This creates multiple layers of protection.

---

### 32. Cross-User Minimization

AI applications should minimize exposure between users.

A user request should retrieve only the information necessary for that user's authorized task.

Conversation histories, memory, profiles, and RAG data should not be broadly loaded into shared AI context.

---

### 33. Cross-Tenant Minimization

Multi-tenant AI environments should minimize data exposure at the tenant boundary.

A tenant-specific request should retrieve only the minimum information necessary from that tenant.

Tenant isolation should operate before data reaches:

* the model;
* context;
* memory;
* RAG;
* tools;
* agents.

---

### 34. Tool Data Minimization

AI tools can expose large datasets to a model.

For example, an AI agent querying a CRM should not necessarily retrieve the entire customer database when one customer record is required.

Tool APIs should support:

* field-level selection;
* record-level filtering;
* purpose-bound queries;
* minimum-result limits;
* authorization checks.

---

### 35. Agent Data Minimization

Agentic systems can accumulate information across multiple steps.

An agent may:

* retrieve data;
* call tools;
* create files;
* store memory;
* query APIs;
* invoke other agents.

Minimization should therefore apply to every step.

```text id="w3p7mz"
Agent Goal
   ↓
Minimum Required Data
   ↓
Minimum Required Tool Access
   ↓
Minimum Required Context
   ↓
Minimum Required Output
```

---

### 36. Provider Data Minimization

AI providers should receive only the information necessary for the contracted service.

The organization should consider:

* what data is transmitted;
* whether identifiers are necessary;
* whether sensitive fields can be removed;
* whether context can be reduced;
* whether local processing can eliminate transmission.

Provider capability does not justify unnecessary disclosure.

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

---

### 37. Cross-Border Data Minimization

Minimizing data before cross-border transmission reduces exposure.

Before sending information to another jurisdiction, the system should determine whether it can:

* remove unnecessary fields;
* redact identifiers;
* pseudonymize;
* tokenize;
* summarize;
* process locally.

Data that never leaves the original environment creates less transfer exposure than data unnecessarily transmitted across borders.

---

### 38. Logging Minimization

AI logging should capture only information necessary for:

* security;
* troubleshooting;
* accountability;
* compliance;
* operations.

Logging entire prompts and outputs by default may create unnecessary personal-data exposure.

A minimized logging architecture may record:

```text id="h6v2qs"
Event ID
Timestamp
User / Service ID
Model Version
Policy Decision
Security Event
Outcome
```

instead of storing complete sensitive content.

---

### 39. Monitoring Minimization

Monitoring should avoid creating a secondary repository of unnecessary personal data.

Monitoring can use:

* metadata;
* aggregated metrics;
* anonymized statistics;
* event identifiers;
* pseudonymous identifiers.

The objective is to obtain sufficient operational visibility without duplicating unnecessary sensitive information.

---

### 40. Output Minimization

AI output should contain only the information necessary for the intended recipient and purpose.

An AI system should not generate:

* unnecessary personal information;
* confidential details;
* unrelated customer information;
* excessive internal context.

Output minimization reduces the possibility of downstream disclosure.

---

### 41. Profile Minimization

AI profiling should use only the attributes necessary for the authorized profiling purpose.

Organizations should assess whether each feature contributes materially to the intended result.

Features that are:

* unnecessary;
* duplicative;
* weakly relevant;
* highly intrusive;
* proxy variables;
* difficult to justify

should be considered for exclusion.

---

### 42. Inference Minimization

AI systems can infer information that was never explicitly provided.

Inference minimization asks:

> **Does the system need to generate or maintain this inference?**

Potentially unnecessary inferences may include:

* behavioral characteristics;
* sensitive attributes;
* personal preferences;
* risk categories;
* predictions about individuals.

The fact that an AI model can infer something does not mean the organization should permit the inference.

**Inference capability ≠ inference authorization.**

---

### 43. Derived-Data Minimization

AI systems may generate derived information such as:

* embeddings;
* summaries;
* classifications;
* profiles;
* risk scores;
* extracted entities;
* feature vectors.

Derived information should be created only where it serves an authorized purpose.

Organizations should avoid generating large quantities of derived data simply because computation is inexpensive.

---

### 44. AI Cache Minimization

Caches should contain only the information necessary to support performance or functionality.

Controls should minimize:

* cached content;
* cache duration;
* sensitive fields;
* cache scope;
* cross-user access.

The cache should not become an uncontrolled secondary data repository.

---

### 45. AI Backup Minimization

Backup systems inevitably preserve copies of operational data, but backup architecture should avoid unnecessary duplication.

Controls should consider:

* backup scope;
* frequency;
* retention;
* sensitive-data inclusion;
* geographic replication;
* immutable copies.

Where appropriate, backups should inherit appropriate classification and lifecycle controls.

---

### 46. AI Security Data Minimization

Security controls should themselves follow minimization principles.

For example, a security system may need to record that an AI agent accessed a sensitive repository without necessarily storing the complete contents of every retrieved document.

Security evidence should be:

**sufficient for accountability, but not unnecessarily expansive.**

---

### 47. AI Development Data Minimization

Development environments should minimize production-data exposure.

Preferred approaches include:

* synthetic data;
* masked data;
* pseudonymized data;
* representative samples;
* reduced datasets;
* controlled test fixtures.

Production personal data should not be copied into development merely because it is convenient.

---

### 48. AI Testing Data Minimization

AI testing should use the minimum dataset necessary to validate the control or model behavior.

Test data should be:

* representative;
* controlled;
* appropriately classified;
* minimized;
* isolated;
* deleted when no longer required.

Where synthetic or de-identified data can achieve the testing objective, those alternatives should be evaluated.

---

### 49. Enterprise AI Data Minimization Principle

Enterprise AI data minimization should operate as a layered control:

```text id="x9c5kt"
Business Purpose
      ↓
Necessity
      ↓
Minimum Data
      ↓
Minimum Context
      ↓
Minimum Retrieval
      ↓
Minimum Tool Access
      ↓
Minimum Provider Disclosure
      ↓
Minimum Retention
      ↓
Minimum Derived Data
      ↓
Deletion
```

Minimization should therefore occur before, during, and after AI processing.

---

### 50. Core Principle of AI Data Minimization

The fundamental principle is:

> **An AI system should process, retrieve, expose, generate, retain, and transfer only the minimum information that is adequate and necessary for an authorized purpose.**

The essential distinctions are:

**Data minimization ≠ data deletion alone.**

**Data minimization ≠ data collection reduction alone.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to process.**

**Data relevance ≠ necessity.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Authorization to access data ≠ authorization to expose all data to AI.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Agent capability ≠ permission to access unlimited data.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**More context ≠ better AI.**

**More data ≠ better AI.**

**More training data ≠ automatically better governance.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

**DLP detection ≠ authorization.**

**Security logging ≠ unrestricted data collection.**

**Derived data ≠ automatically outside minimization requirements.**

**AI inference capability ≠ inference authorization.**

**Model capability ≠ data-processing authority.**

**Data minimization ≠ permanent assurance.**

## Part 2 — AI Data Minimization Controls

### 51. AI Data Minimization Control Framework

An AI data minimization control framework establishes preventive, detective, corrective, and assurance controls that limit AI processing to the minimum information necessary for an authorized purpose.

The framework should cover:

* prompts;
* context;
* conversations;
* RAG;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* tools;
* agents;
* training datasets;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* model inputs;
* model outputs;
* profiles;
* logs;
* caches;
* backups;
* provider transmissions;
* derived data.

The primary control objective is:

> **Prevent unnecessary data from entering, moving through, remaining within, or being generated by the AI ecosystem.**

---

### 52. AI Data Minimization Planning Control

Before implementation, the organization should define:

* business purpose;
* AI use case;
* required data;
* unnecessary data;
* processing boundaries;
* retention requirements;
* authorized users;
* provider requirements;
* minimization controls;
* evidence requirements.

Minimization should be addressed during architecture and design rather than after excessive data has already entered the system.

---

### 53. AI Data Minimization Scope Control

The minimization assessment should cover the entire AI data flow.

```text id="f6k2rp"
Source Data
    ↓
Collection
    ↓
Prompt
    ↓
Context
    ↓
RAG
    ↓
Model
    ↓
Tool / Agent
    ↓
Output
    ↓
Logs / Memory
    ↓
Retention / Backup
```

Every stage should be evaluated for unnecessary data exposure.

---

### 54. AI Data Minimization Classification Control

Data classification should determine the level of minimization required.

Classification may include:

* public;
* internal;
* confidential;
* restricted;
* personal;
* sensitive;
* special-category;
* regulated;
* security-sensitive.

Higher-risk data should generally require stronger minimization controls before AI processing.

---

### 55. AI Data Minimization Applicability Control

The organization should determine which minimization requirements apply to each AI processing activity.

Factors include:

* data type;
* jurisdiction;
* processing purpose;
* affected individuals;
* AI capability;
* profiling;
* automated decisions;
* provider use;
* cross-border processing.

The control should prevent generic assumptions that the same data-minimization rule applies equally to every AI workload.

---

### 56. AI Data Minimization Requirement-Mapping Control

Each minimization requirement should be mapped to a technical or organizational control.

Example:

| Requirement               | Control           | Evidence              |
| ------------------------- | ----------------- | --------------------- |
| Limit prompt data         | Prompt gateway    | Gateway configuration |
| Limit RAG retrieval       | Retrieval filter  | Query logs            |
| Limit provider disclosure | DLP/redaction     | Policy records        |
| Limit memory              | Memory policy     | Configuration         |
| Limit training data       | Dataset filtering | Dataset manifest      |
| Limit logging             | Selective logging | Log configuration     |

This creates traceability from requirement to implementation.

---

### 57. AI Data Minimization Risk-Classification Control

Minimization risk should consider:

* sensitivity;
* volume;
* number of individuals;
* processing scale;
* model capability;
* downstream use;
* provider access;
* geographic distribution;
* potential individual impact.

High-risk processing should require stronger minimization controls and validation.

---

### 58. AI Data Minimization Purpose Control

Every data element processed by AI should be associated with an authorized purpose.

The control should ask:

> **What purpose requires this specific data element?**

If the purpose cannot be established, the data should be considered for exclusion.

This prevents broad data access from becoming the default AI operating model.

---

### 59. AI Data Inventory Control

The organization should maintain an inventory of data used by AI systems.

The inventory should identify:

* source;
* data category;
* classification;
* purpose;
* owner;
* processing system;
* AI model;
* provider;
* retention;
* minimization rule.

The inventory should include derived information where appropriate.

---

### 60. AI Personal-Data Minimization Control

Personal data should be limited to what is necessary for the authorized AI purpose.

Controls may include:

* field-level filtering;
* record-level filtering;
* redaction;
* tokenization;
* pseudonymization;
* selective retrieval.

For example, an AI assistant requiring employment status should not automatically receive an employee's entire HR record.

---

### 61. AI Special-Category Data Minimization Control

Special-category data should be specifically detected and controlled.

Controls should prevent unnecessary exposure of information relating to categories such as:

* health;
* biometric information where applicable;
* genetic information;
* political opinions;
* religious or philosophical beliefs;
* trade-union membership;
* sexual orientation.

Where such data is not necessary, the system should block, redact, or exclude it.

---

### 62. AI Data-Minimization Control

The system should identify unnecessary fields, records, documents, and attributes before AI processing.

Possible mechanisms include:

* schema filtering;
* allowlists;
* field-level policies;
* data transformation;
* purpose-based filtering.

The goal is to establish a **minimum necessary dataset**.

---

### 63. AI Data-Necessity Control

Each significant data category should undergo a necessity assessment.

Questions should include:

1. Is this data required?
2. What function requires it?
3. Can the function operate without it?
4. Can a less intrusive representation be used?
5. Can the information be retrieved only when required?

Technical convenience should not determine necessity.

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

---

### 64. AI Data-Relevance Control

Relevance should be evaluated separately from necessity.

A document may be relevant to a broad business domain while being unnecessary for a specific AI request.

Retrieval and prompt pipelines should therefore distinguish:

**potentially relevant information**

from

**minimum necessary information**.

---

### 65. AI Prompt-Minimization Control

Prompt pipelines should prevent unnecessary information from being sent to the model.

Controls may include:

* prompt templates;
* field allowlists;
* sensitive-data detection;
* redaction;
* tokenization;
* DLP;
* prompt gateways.

```text id="y4m8qc"
User / Application
       ↓
Prompt Inspection
       ↓
Purpose Validation
       ↓
Data Minimization
       ↓
Redaction / Tokenization
       ↓
Approved Prompt
       ↓
AI Model
```

---

### 66. AI Context-Minimization Control

The AI system should provide only the minimum necessary context.

Controls can limit:

* number of conversation turns;
* retrieved documents;
* document length;
* metadata;
* historical records;
* user attributes.

Large context windows should not become an excuse for unnecessary data exposure.

---

### 67. AI Conversation-Minimization Control

Conversation history should be selectively reused.

Controls may include:

* relevant-turn selection;
* summarization;
* sensitive-data filtering;
* history expiration;
* purpose-bound conversation access.

A model should not automatically receive a user's entire historical conversation when only the current interaction is necessary.

---

### 68. AI Memory-Minimization Control

Persistent memory should contain only information that has a justified continuing purpose.

Controls should evaluate memory candidates based on:

* necessity;
* sensitivity;
* future usefulness;
* purpose;
* retention.

The system should prevent automatic conversion of every conversational detail into persistent memory.

---

### 69. AI RAG Minimization Control

RAG systems should retrieve the smallest appropriate dataset.

Controls should include:

* source allowlists;
* document-level authorization;
* metadata filtering;
* relevance thresholds;
* result limits;
* purpose binding;
* classification filters.

The system should avoid retrieving an entire repository when a small set of records is sufficient.

---

### 70. AI Retrieval-Authorization Control

Retrieval authorization should occur before information enters AI context.

The control should evaluate:

* user identity;
* tenant;
* purpose;
* resource;
* data classification;
* authorization;
* sensitivity.

Therefore:

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 71. AI Vector-Store Minimization Control

Vector stores should minimize both content and metadata.

Controls should consider:

* unnecessary documents;
* duplicate chunks;
* unnecessary identifiers;
* excessive metadata;
* obsolete embeddings.

Only approved information should be indexed.

---

### 72. AI Embedding-Minimization Control

Embedding pipelines should avoid generating embeddings for information that is not required for retrieval.

Controls should support:

* source filtering;
* field exclusion;
* chunk minimization;
* metadata minimization;
* sensitivity filtering.

An embedding pipeline should not simply convert an entire repository into vectors.

---

### 73. AI Training-Data Minimization Control

Training datasets should contain only data necessary to achieve the intended model-development objective.

Controls should include:

* field selection;
* dataset sampling;
* duplicate removal;
* sensitive-data filtering;
* irrelevant-record removal;
* provenance validation.

The goal is not necessarily the largest possible training dataset.

### 74. AI Fine-Tuning Data Minimization Control

Fine-tuning datasets should be purpose-specific.

Controls should remove:

* unnecessary identifiers;
* unrelated fields;
* excessive historical information;
* irrelevant examples;
* unnecessary sensitive attributes.

Where synthetic examples can meet the objective, they should be considered.

* retrieved content;

* tool results;
* user metadata.

### 77. AI Output-Minimization Control

AI systems should avoid producing unnecessary sensitive or personal information.

Controls can include:

* output filtering;
* DLP;
* recipient-aware policies;
* field-level restrictions;
* response templates;
* sensitive-data suppression.

Output minimization protects both the immediate recipient and downstream systems.

### 78. AI Inference-Minimization Control

AI systems should not generate unnecessary inferences about individuals.

Controls should identify:

* sensitive inferences;
* high-impact attributes;
* unnecessary profiling;
* proxy variables;
* speculative characteristics.

The ability to infer information does not automatically establish a legitimate purpose for doing so.

**Inference capability ≠ inference authorization.**

Profiling systems should minimize the number of features used.

Feature selection should consider:

* necessity;
* predictive value;
* sensitivity;
* proxy risk;
* fairness;
* individual impact.

Features that provide little value but significantly increase privacy risk should be excluded.

Controls should limit:

* working memory;
* intermediate artifacts;
* retrieved records;
* cross-step context;
* persistent state.

Agent workflows should not gradually accumulate unnecessary data simply because each individual action appears reasonable.

### 82. AI Provider-Minimization Control

### 83. AI Provider Data-Use Control

Provider processing should be limited to authorized purposes.

The control should distinguish:

* service processing;
* abuse monitoring;
* telemetry;
* analytics;
* model improvement;
* model training.

The provider's technical ability to use submitted information does not establish enterprise authorization.

### 84. AI Cross-Border Minimization Control

Possible measures include:

* removing identifiers;
* excluding unnecessary fields;
* pseudonymizing;
* tokenizing;
* summarizing;
* processing locally.

The objective is to minimize the quantity and sensitivity of information transferred.

### 85. AI Data-Residency Minimization Control

### 117. Core Control Principle

The core control principle is:

> **AI systems should technically enforce the minimum necessary data at every processing boundary—from collection and prompts through context, retrieval, memory, tools, agents, model inputs, outputs, logging, provider transmission, and retention.**

A mature minimization control chain is:

**Purpose → Necessity → Minimum Data → Authorization → Filtering → AI Processing → Minimum Output → Minimum Retention → Monitoring → Testing → Reassessment.**

The control must reduce unnecessary information **before exposure occurs**, rather than relying solely on security controls after excessive data has already entered the AI environment.


## Part 3 — AI Data Minimization Governance

* sensitive-data processing;
* profiling;
* automated decision-making;
* agentic workflows;
* external providers;
* cross-border processing;
* high-impact use cases.

### 129. AI Data Minimization Processing Governance

Minimization should be integrated into processing governance.

* what data is processed;
* why it is processed;
* what is necessary;

* where it goes;
* how long it remains available.

This prevents minimization from becoming an isolated privacy exercise.

### 130. AI Personal-Data Minimization Governance

Governance should identify the categories of personal data used by each AI system.

Examples include:

* identity data;
* contact information;
* employment information;
* financial information;
* behavioral data;
* communication data;
* inferred information.

For each category, governance should establish whether processing is necessary for the stated purpose.

### 131. AI Legal-Basis Minimization Governance

Where applicable, governance should distinguish:

**a lawful basis for processing**

from

**the amount of data necessary for that processing.**

Having a lawful basis does not automatically justify processing every available field.

The organization should still determine what is necessary and proportionate.

> "Assist authorized customer-support personnel in resolving active billing inquiries."

A more precise purpose makes it easier to identify unnecessary data.

### 134. AI Data-Minimization Governance

### 135. AI Data-Accuracy Governance

Minimization should not produce insufficient or materially inaccurate AI processing.

Governance should balance:

* minimum data;
* adequacy;
* accuracy;
* reliability.

The objective is not to remove information blindly but to eliminate information that is unnecessary for the intended result.

### 136. AI Confidentiality Governance

Minimization should reduce the quantity of confidential information exposed to AI components.

Governance should establish:

* confidentiality classifications;
* authorized AI consumers;
* provider restrictions;
* field-level access;
* redaction requirements.

Less exposed information generally means less information available for accidental or malicious disclosure.

### 137. AI Integrity Governance

Minimization controls should preserve the integrity of information that remains necessary.

Filtering must not:

* alter critical values;
* remove essential relationships;
* corrupt context;
* introduce inaccurate substitutions.

Data transformation should therefore be validated.

---

### 138. AI Retention-Minimization Governance

Retention should be treated as part of minimization governance.

The organization should ask:

> **Do we still need this information?**

after asking:

> **Did we need to collect it?**

Governance should establish lifecycle rules for:

* conversations;
* memory;
* RAG;
* vectors;
* logs;
* outputs;
* profiles;
* training data;
* backups.

* primary data;

* memory;
* caches;
* provider copies.

The organization should distinguish deletion from merely hiding information from users.

### 140. AI Logging-Minimization Governance

### 141. AI Monitoring-Minimization Governance

* metadata;
* aggregated metrics;
* pseudonymous identifiers;
* event-based telemetry;
* selective content capture.

### 142. AI DLP Minimization Governance

* prohibited data categories;
* detection requirements;

* escalation;
* exception handling.

### 143. AI Redaction Minimization Governance

Redaction should occur as early as practical in the data flow.

### 144. AI Pseudonymization Minimization Governance

Pseudonymization should be used where it meaningfully reduces unnecessary exposure.

Governance should control:

* pseudonym creation;
* mapping;
* access;
* re-identification;

### 145. AI Anonymization Minimization Governance

Anonymization should be evaluated where it can eliminate unnecessary identifiable processing.

* re-identification risk;
* linkage risk;
* remaining metadata;
* intended use;
* technical robustness.

Simply removing obvious identifiers should not automatically be classified as anonymization.

Change governance should therefore consider data-volume expansion, not just software functionality.

* operational dependencies.

### 152. AI Transparency Minimization Governance

* privacy notices;
* user documentation;
* provider documentation;
* system behavior.

### 153. AI Automated-Decision Minimization Governance

AI-supported decisions should use only the data necessary for the authorized decision.

Governance should challenge:

* unnecessary features;
* excessive historical data;
* proxy variables;
* unrelated personal attributes;
* unnecessary sensitive information.

A larger feature set does not automatically produce a better or fairer decision.
