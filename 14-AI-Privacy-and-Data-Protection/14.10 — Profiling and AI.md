# 14.10 — Profiling and AI

## Part 1 — Profiling and AI Foundations

### 1. Definition of Profiling in AI

Profiling is the use of personal data to evaluate, analyze, or predict aspects relating to an individual.

AI can perform profiling at significant scale by identifying patterns across:

* Behavior
* Preferences
* Characteristics
* History
* Transactions
* Interactions
* Location
* Online activity
* Professional activity
* Risk indicators

Profiling can be explicit, such as calculating a risk score, or implicit, where an AI system derives characteristics from seemingly unrelated information.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Profiling Governance

AI profiling governance establishes controls to ensure that profiling is:

* Purpose-specific
* Lawful
* Necessary
* Proportionate
* Accurate
* Secure
* Transparent
* Fair
* Contestable where applicable
* Appropriately governed

The objective is not merely to determine whether an AI system can create a profile, but whether it is **authorized to create, use, retain, and act upon that profile**.

---

### 3. Profiling versus Automated Decision-Making

Profiling and automated decision-making are related but distinct.

```text id="profile14-01"
Personal Data
     |
     v
AI Analysis
     |
     v
Profile / Prediction
     |
     +---------> Human Decision
     |
     +---------> Recommendation
     |
     +---------> Automated Decision
```

A system may profile an individual without making an automated decision.

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

---

### 4. AI Profiling Lifecycle

AI profiling should be governed throughout its lifecycle.

```text id="profile14-02"
Collection
    |
    v
Classification
    |
    v
Feature Extraction
    |
    v
Profile Creation
    |
    v
Prediction / Scoring
    |
    v
Profile Use
    |
    v
Decision / Recommendation
    |
    v
Monitoring
    |
    v
Retention / Deletion
```

---

### 5. Profiling Purpose

Every profiling activity should have a clearly defined purpose.

Examples include:

* Fraud detection
* Customer segmentation
* Product recommendations
* Risk assessment
* Marketing personalization
* Security monitoring
* Service optimization

A profile created for one purpose should not automatically be reused for another.

**Purpose compatibility ≠ automatic authorization.**

---

### 6. Profiling Data

Profiling may use:

* Direct identifiers
* Transactional information
* Behavioral data
* Demographic information
* Device information
* Location information
* Interaction history
* Derived attributes
* External data

The organization should distinguish between data directly supplied by an individual and characteristics inferred by AI.

---

### 7. Personal Data in AI Profiling

Personal data can remain personal data even when transformed into:

* Scores
* Rankings
* Categories
* Predictions
* Risk levels
* Behavioral segments
* Embeddings
* Derived attributes

Transformation does not automatically eliminate privacy obligations.

---

### 8. Direct Profiling

Direct profiling occurs when data explicitly associated with an identifiable individual is analyzed.

Example:

```text id="profile14-03"
Customer ID
     |
     +--> Purchase History
     +--> Support History
     +--> Product Usage
     |
     v
AI Profile
     |
     v
"High Churn Risk"
```

---

### 9. Indirect Profiling

AI can also create profiles from indirect or seemingly unrelated information.

For example:

* Device characteristics
* Browsing patterns
* Interaction timing
* Purchase behavior
* Communication patterns

Indirect data can still contribute to an identifiable profile.

---

### 10. Inferred Personal Data

AI-generated predictions can constitute personal information when they relate to an identifiable individual.

Examples include inferred:

* Income range
* Risk level
* Preferences
* Likelihood of churn
* Behavioral characteristics
* Eligibility
* Interests

**Observed data ≠ only relevant personal data.**

---

### 11. Derived Attributes

AI systems may generate new attributes from existing information.

```text id="profile14-04"
Raw Data
   |
   v
Feature Engineering
   |
   v
AI Analysis
   |
   v
Derived Attribute
   |
   v
Individual Profile
```

Derived information should be governed according to its privacy and decision impact.

---

### 12. Profiling and Special-Category Data

AI profiling may reveal or infer sensitive characteristics.

Potentially sensitive inferences may relate to:

* Health
* Political opinions
* Religious beliefs
* Trade-union membership
* Biometric characteristics
* Sexual orientation
* Other specially protected characteristics

The fact that the individual did not explicitly provide such information does not necessarily eliminate the privacy implications of the inference.

---

### 13. Profiling and Sensitive Inferences

An AI system may infer sensitive characteristics from non-sensitive inputs.

For example:

```text id="profile14-05"
Non-Sensitive Inputs
        |
        v
Behavioral Patterns
        |
        v
AI Inference
        |
        v
Potentially Sensitive Characteristic
```

This creates a distinct governance risk because the **inference itself** may have privacy and individual-impact implications.

---

### 14. Profiling and Purpose Limitation

Profiling should remain bounded by the approved purpose.

A system authorized to detect fraud should not automatically be authorized to:

* Assess employee performance
* Determine marketing eligibility
* Evaluate unrelated personal characteristics
* Share profiles with unrelated business units

**Authorized purpose ≠ unrestricted secondary use.**

---

### 15. Profiling and Data Minimization

Profiling should use only the data necessary for the approved purpose.

The organization should challenge:

* Whether each feature is necessary
* Whether historical data is required
* Whether sensitive attributes are required
* Whether external datasets are required
* Whether high-granularity information is necessary

---

### 16. Profiling Necessity

Technical ability to create a profile does not establish necessity.

A profiling system may be technically capable of using hundreds of attributes while the approved purpose may require only a small subset.

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

---

### 17. Profiling Proportionality

Profiling should be proportionate to the purpose and potential impact.

Assessment should consider:

* Expected benefit
* Intrusiveness
* Data sensitivity
* Individual expectations
* Scale
* Accuracy
* Potential harm
* Available alternatives

---

### 18. Profiling Accuracy

Profiles should be sufficiently accurate for their intended use.

Potential problems include:

* Outdated information
* Incorrect data
* Correlated attributes
* Statistical assumptions
* Inaccurate inference
* Data-quality defects

**Model accuracy ≠ profile accuracy.**

---

### 19. Profiling and Data Quality

High-quality model performance does not guarantee that the profile of a specific person is correct.

For example, a model may achieve strong aggregate performance while incorrectly classifying a particular individual.

Therefore, governance should distinguish:

**Aggregate model performance ≠ individual-level accuracy.**

---

### 20. Profiling and Bias

Profiling can reproduce or amplify historical bias.

Bias may enter through:

* Training data
* Feature selection
* Proxy variables
* Labels
* Model design
* Thresholds
* Human interpretation

---

### 21. Proxy Variables in AI Profiling

AI may use variables that indirectly represent protected or sensitive characteristics.

Examples may include:

* Geographic area
* Purchasing patterns
* Language
* Device characteristics
* Behavioral patterns

A variable does not need to explicitly contain a protected characteristic to create discriminatory effects.

---

### 22. Profiling and Fairness

Fairness assessment should examine whether profiling produces unjustified disparities.

Possible metrics include:

* Error rates
* False positives
* False negatives
* Selection rates
* Outcome disparities
* Population-level differences

Fairness requirements should be appropriate to the use case rather than reduced to a single numerical metric.

---

### 23. Profiling and Individual Impact

Profiling becomes particularly significant when the resulting profile affects an individual's opportunities, treatment, or access to services.

Potential impacts include:

* Financial decisions
* Employment
* Insurance
* Access to services
* Security investigations
* Marketing exclusion
* Eligibility assessments

---

### 24. Profiling and Automated Decisions

Profiles can become inputs to automated decisions.

```text id="profile14-06"
Personal Data
     |
     v
Profiling
     |
     v
Risk / Preference / Eligibility Score
     |
     v
Decision Engine
     |
     v
Outcome
```

When profiling contributes to significant automated decisions, additional governance may be required.

---

### 25. Profiling and Human Decision-Making

A human decision-maker may rely heavily on an AI-generated profile.

Therefore, the presence of a human does not automatically eliminate profiling risk.

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

A reviewer who merely accepts an AI-generated profile without meaningful ability to challenge it may provide little substantive oversight.

---

### 26. Profiling and Human Review

Meaningful review should allow an authorized reviewer to:

* Examine relevant evidence
* Question the profile
* Identify inaccurate information
* Reject the profile
* Request additional information
* Modify the resulting decision
* Escalate the case

---

### 27. Profiling and Transparency

Individuals may need appropriate information about profiling depending on the processing context and applicable requirements.

Transparency should address relevant aspects such as:

* That profiling occurs
* Purpose
* Categories of data
* Consequences
* Relevant rights
* Available review mechanisms

Transparency should describe the actual processing rather than simply stating that "AI is used."

---

### 28. Profiling Explainability

Where explanation is required or appropriate, the organization should be able to explain meaningful factors influencing a profile.

Potential explanation elements include:

* Relevant inputs
* Derived characteristics
* Important factors
* Scoring methodology
* Model role
* Decision impact

---

### 29. Profiling and Contestability

Individuals should have appropriate mechanisms to challenge inaccurate or inappropriate profiling where applicable.

Contestability may include:

* Requesting review
* Challenging inaccurate information
* Requesting correction
* Objecting to applicable processing
* Requesting human intervention where required

---

### 30. Profiling and Identity

The organization should establish how an AI-generated profile is associated with an individual.

Identity controls should prevent:

* Profile misassociation
* Identity collision
* Account takeover
* Cross-user contamination

---

### 31. Profiling and Authorization

Access to profiles should be explicitly authorized.

A user who can access the underlying customer record should not automatically have unrestricted access to every AI-derived profile.

**Data access ≠ profile access.**

---

### 32. Profiling and Least Privilege

Profile access should follow least privilege.

For example:

```text id="profile14-07"
Employee
   |
   v
Role
   |
   v
Purpose
   |
   v
Authorized Profile Attributes
```

Different roles may require access to different profile attributes.

---

### 33. Profiling and RAG

RAG systems can retrieve profile information into AI context.

The architecture should ensure that retrieval is authorized for the specific purpose.

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 34. Profiling and Vector Stores

Profile-related embeddings may contain information associated with individuals.

Vector databases should therefore be governed for:

* Access
* Tenant isolation
* Purpose
* Retention
* Deletion
* Retrieval authorization

An embedding should not be treated as automatically anonymous simply because it is numerical.

---

### 35. Profiling and AI Memory

AI memory may retain information about an individual's previous interactions.

Memory can create persistent profiles containing:

* Preferences
* Behavioral history
* Previous requests
* Derived characteristics
* Sensitive contextual information

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 36. Profiling and AI Agents

Agents may combine information from multiple systems to construct or enrich profiles.

Potential sources include:

* CRM
* HR
* Financial systems
* Security platforms
* Customer support
* External APIs

Agent capability should not automatically authorize aggregation.

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

---

### 37. Profiling and Tool Access

Tools used by an AI system can expose additional information for profiling.

Examples include:

* Customer databases
* Analytics systems
* Identity systems
* Location services
* Transaction systems

Each tool should have purpose-bound authorization.

---

### 38. Profiling and Provider Processing

External AI providers may process profiling-related information.

Governance should evaluate:

* Processing purpose
* Data use
* Retention
* Training use
* Subprocessors
* Geographic processing
* Security

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

---

### 39. Profiling and Training Data

Profiles may become training inputs if interaction or business data is reused for model development.

The organization should separately assess whether profiling data is authorized for training.

**Profiling authorization ≠ training authorization.**

---

### 40. Profiling and Model Memorization

Sensitive profile information may potentially become memorized by models.

Risks include:

* Information extraction
* Membership inference
* Reconstruction
* Memorization of rare records

Training-data protection alone does not eliminate model-level privacy risk.

---

### 41. Profiling and Inference

AI systems can infer information that was never explicitly stored.

For example:

```text id="profile14-08"
Observed Data
     |
     v
Pattern Recognition
     |
     v
Inference
     |
     v
Profile Attribute
```

Inference therefore creates an additional privacy governance layer.

---

### 42. Profiling and Output Privacy

AI outputs may expose profiles to unauthorized recipients.

Examples include:

* Customer risk classifications
* Employee behavioral assessments
* Predicted preferences
* Sensitive inferred characteristics

Output authorization should be independent from model access.

**Model output ≠ authorized disclosure.**

---

### 43. Profiling and Cross-User Isolation

AI systems should prevent one individual's profile from influencing another individual's interaction without authorization.

Controls should protect:

* Prompt context
* Conversation history
* Memory
* RAG results
* Vector searches
* Outputs

---

### 44. Profiling and Cross-Tenant Isolation

Multi-tenant AI systems require strong profile isolation.

Tenant boundaries should apply to:

* Source data
* Profile records
* Embeddings
* Memory
* Retrieval
* Model context
* Outputs
* Logs

---

### 45. Profiling Retention

Profiles should not be retained indefinitely merely because they may be useful in the future.

Retention should consider:

* Purpose
* Accuracy
* Individual expectations
* Decision impact
* Legal requirements
* Operational necessity

---

### 46. Profiling Deletion

Deletion requirements may need to propagate across the profiling ecosystem.

Potential locations include:

* Source records
* Feature stores
* Profile databases
* Vector stores
* Memory
* Analytics systems
* Logs
* Derived datasets

Deleting the source record does not necessarily mean every derived profile has disappeared.

---

### 47. Profiling Security

Profiling systems should be protected against:

* Unauthorized access
* Data exfiltration
* Model manipulation
* Data poisoning
* Prompt injection
* Retrieval abuse
* Privilege escalation

Security controls should protect both source data and derived profiles.

---

### 48. Profiling Risk Assessment

AI profiling should be evaluated through multiple risk domains.

```text id="profile14-09"
                  Profiling Risk
                       |
       +---------------+---------------+
       |       |       |       |       |
    Privacy Security Fairness Rights  Model
       |       |       |       |       |
       +-------+-------+-------+-------+
                       |
                       v
                 Residual Risk
```

The assessment should consider both the probability and severity of potential individual impact.

---

### 49. Enterprise AI Profiling Principle

Enterprise profiling governance should treat an AI-generated profile as a potentially significant processing artifact rather than merely a technical model output.

The organization should know:

* Why the profile exists
* What data created it
* Who may access it
* What it predicts
* How accurate it is
* What decisions it influences
* How long it is retained
* How it can be challenged
* How it is deleted
* Which providers process it

---

### 50. Core Principle of Profiling and AI

AI profiling must remain **purpose-bound, authorized, proportionate, accurate, secure, transparent, contestable where applicable, and subject to appropriate human and governance controls**.

The complete profiling chain should remain visible:

```text id="profile14-10"
Personal Data
      |
      v
Purpose
      |
      v
Necessity
      |
      v
Authorization
      |
      v
Data / Features
      |
      v
AI Profiling
      |
      v
Derived Profile
      |
      v
Prediction / Score
      |
      v
Recommendation / Decision
      |
      v
Individual Impact
      |
      v
Monitoring
      |
      v
Review / Challenge
      |
      v
Retention / Deletion
```

The following distinctions must remain explicit:

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

**AI capability ≠ profiling authorization.**

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized decision.**

**Profile ≠ automatically accurate representation of an individual.**

**Model accuracy ≠ individual-level accuracy.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for profiling.**

**Data relevance ≠ authorization to process.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Inference ≠ factual truth.**

**Derived data ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Human review ≠ automatic independence.**

**Security assurance ≠ privacy compliance.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ lawful authorization.**

**Profiling testing ≠ permanent privacy assurance.**

An enterprise AI profiling system is appropriately governed only when the organization can demonstrate **why profiling is performed, which data and inferences are used, who is authorized to access the resulting profile, how individual impact is controlled, how accuracy and fairness are evaluated, how individuals can exercise applicable rights, and how the profile remains subject to retention, deletion, monitoring, reassessment, and governance throughout its lifecycle**.
