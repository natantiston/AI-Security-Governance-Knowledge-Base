# 14.18 — Confidential AI Workloads

## Part 1 — Confidential AI Workloads Foundations

### 1. Definition of Confidential AI Workloads

A confidential AI workload is an AI system, application, process, dataset, model, interaction, or agent workflow that handles information requiring protection against unauthorized disclosure, access, use, modification, inference, retention, or transfer.

Confidential AI workloads may process:

* business-confidential information;
* proprietary intellectual property;
* source code;
* customer information;
* employee information;
* financial information;
* legal documents;
* strategic plans;
* security information;
* credentials and secrets;
* regulated information;
* personal data;
* special-category data;
* or highly sensitive operational information.

Confidentiality must apply across the entire AI lifecycle, not merely to the storage location.

---

### 2. Purpose of Confidential AI Workload Governance

The purpose of confidential AI workload governance is to ensure that protected information remains accessible only to authorized identities, systems, providers, models, tools, agents, and processes.

Governance should prevent:

* unauthorized prompt disclosure;
* cross-user leakage;
* cross-tenant leakage;
* unauthorized RAG retrieval;
* memory leakage;
* provider access;
* unauthorized training;
* excessive logging;
* output disclosure;
* agent-mediated exfiltration;
* insecure backups;
* unauthorized geographic transfer;
* and accidental disclosure through AI-generated content.

The objective is to maintain confidentiality from **input through deletion**.

### 4. Confidential AI Processing Model

Confidential AI processing occurs when protected information is provided to or generated by an AI system.

The processing chain may include:

```text id="capm7x"
Confidential Data
       |
       v
Enterprise Application
       |
       v
Identity / Authorization
       |
       v
Privacy / Security Gateway
       |
       v
AI Model
       |
       +--> RAG
       +--> Memory
       +--> Tools
       +--> Agents
       |
       v
AI Output
       |
       v
Authorized Recipient
```

Every component that can observe, store, transform, retrieve, infer, or transmit confidential information becomes part of the confidentiality control boundary.

---

### 5. Confidentiality versus Privacy

Confidentiality and privacy overlap but are not identical.

**Confidentiality** focuses primarily on preventing unauthorized disclosure or access.

**Privacy** addresses the lawful and appropriate processing of information relating to individuals.

For example, an AI system could have:

* excellent encryption;
* strong authentication;
* strong network segmentation;

while still processing personal data for an unauthorized purpose.

Therefore:

**Security confidentiality ≠ privacy compliance.**

Both must be governed independently and together.

---

### 6. Confidentiality versus Integrity

Confidentiality prevents unauthorized disclosure.

Integrity protects information against unauthorized modification or corruption.

AI workloads require both.

For example:

```text id="cain8v"
Confidentiality Failure
        |
        +--> Unauthorized person sees customer data

Integrity Failure
        |
        +--> Unauthorized person changes customer data
```

A confidential AI workload must therefore protect both the secrecy and trustworthiness of its information.

---

### 7. Confidential AI Data Classification

Confidentiality controls should be based on data classification.

A typical model may include:

| Classification      | Example                         | AI Treatment                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Public              | Published content               | Generally unrestricted within policy |
| Internal            | Internal procedures             | Controlled                           |
| Confidential        | Business plans                  | Restricted                           |
| Highly Confidential | Trade secrets                   | Strongly restricted                  |
| Restricted Personal | Customer records                | Strong privacy/security controls     |
| Regulated           | Protected regulated information | Enhanced controls                    |
| Secret              | Credentials, private keys       | Normally prohibited from LLM prompts |

Classification should determine:

* permitted AI services;
* provider eligibility;
* storage location;
* transmission restrictions;
* encryption;
* logging;
* retention;
* and approval requirements.

---

### 8. Confidential AI Data Ownership

Every confidential AI workload should have an identifiable data owner.

The data owner should determine:

* who may access the data;
* why it may be processed;
* which AI systems may use it;
* whether external processing is permitted;
* retention requirements;
* geographic restrictions;
* and deletion requirements.

The fact that an AI application can technically access confidential information does not establish authorization.

Therefore:

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data.**

---

### 9. Confidential AI Data Stewardship

Data stewardship provides operational control over confidential information.

A steward may be responsible for:

* classification;
* metadata;
* quality;
* access review;
* retention;
* data lineage;
* RAG eligibility;
* and deletion coordination.

AI systems should consume confidential information according to stewardship rules rather than treating all accessible data as equally usable.

---

Example:

```text id="catb6m"
                   |
```

Each boundary should have explicit controls.

---

### 11. Confidential AI Zero-Trust Principle

Confidential AI workloads should follow zero-trust principles.

No component should automatically be trusted because it is:

* inside the enterprise;
* part of an approved application;
* an AI model;
* a cloud service;
* an internal employee;
* or a previously authorized integration.

Every access request should be evaluated based on:

**Identity → Resource → Purpose → Data → Context → Authorization.**

---

### 12. Confidential AI Identity

Every user, application, service, model-integrated workload, tool, and agent that accesses confidential information should have an identifiable security identity.

Identity enables:

* authorization;
* accountability;
* auditing;
* incident investigation;
* access reviews;
* and revocation.

Shared identities create ambiguity and should be avoided for sensitive workloads.

---

### 13. Confidential AI Authentication

Authentication verifies the identity of a user or workload requesting access to confidential AI resources.

Controls may include:

* SSO;
* MFA;
* workload identity;
* certificates;
* hardware-backed credentials;
* short-lived tokens;
* and conditional access.

However:

**Authentication ≠ authorization.**

A correctly authenticated user may still be prohibited from accessing a particular confidential dataset.

---

### 14. Confidential AI Authorization

Authorization determines whether an identity is permitted to access or process confidential information.

Authorization should consider:

```text id="cai_auth9"
Identity
   +
Role
   +
Data Classification
   +
Purpose
   +
Tenant
   +
Environment
   +
Operation
   =
Authorization Decision
```

Authorization should be enforced before data is retrieved, not after confidential information has already been exposed.

---

### 15. Confidential AI Least Privilege

Confidential AI workloads should receive only the permissions necessary to perform their approved tasks.

Least privilege should apply to:

* users;
* applications;
* service accounts;
* models;
* RAG systems;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* tools;
* agents;
* administrators;
* and providers.

A model that only needs read access to a specific knowledge base should not receive access to an organization's entire document repository.

---

### 16. Confidential AI Data Isolation

Confidential information should be isolated according to its required security boundary.

Isolation may be based on:

* user;
* tenant;
* business unit;
* application;
* classification;
* geographic region;
* environment;
* or legal entity.

Isolation should cover:

* source data;
* prompts;
* context;
* RAG;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* logs;
* outputs;
* and backups.

---

### 17. Confidential AI Tenant Isolation

Multi-tenant AI systems must prevent confidential information from one tenant becoming available to another tenant.

Tenant isolation should apply to:

* prompts;
* conversation history;
* RAG repositories;
* embeddings;
* memory;
* tool results;
* outputs;
* logs;
* and administrative functions.

Therefore:

**Shared infrastructure ≠ shared authorization.**

---

### 18. Confidential AI Session Isolation

Each session should maintain an appropriate confidentiality boundary.

The architecture should prevent information from one session from unintentionally entering another through:

* conversation history;
* caches;
* memory;
* temporary files;
* context assembly;
* tool results;
* or provider-side session mechanisms.

Session identifiers alone should not be treated as a substitute for authorization.

---

### 19. Confidential AI Prompt Protection

Prompts can contain highly confidential information.

Prompt protection should address:

* source;
* classification;
* authorization;
* minimization;
* DLP;
* redaction;
* encryption;
* provider transmission;
* logging;
* retention;
* and output handling.

The system should inspect the complete prompt payload, including automatically inserted context.

---

### 20. Confidential AI Context Protection

Context may expose more information than the user explicitly enters.

Context can include:

* system instructions;
* previous conversations;
* customer records;
* retrieved documents;
* memory;
* application metadata;
* tool results;
* and hidden application state.

Therefore:

**Visible prompt ≠ complete confidential-data exposure.**

Context assembly must be governed as a controlled data-processing operation.

---

### 21. Confidential AI RAG Protection

RAG systems can expose confidential enterprise information to AI models.

A secure architecture is:

```text id="crag8x"
User
 |
 v
Identity
 |
 v
Authorization
 |
 v
Repository
 |
 v
Authorized Retrieval
 |
 v
Classification
 |
 v
Confidentiality Filtering
 |
 v
Context Assembly
 |
 v
AI Model
```

The retrieval system must verify that the user and purpose are authorized before confidential information enters the AI context.

Therefore:

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 22. Confidential AI Vector-Store Protection

Vector stores can contain embeddings derived from confidential documents.

Confidentiality controls should cover:

* source documents;
* embeddings;
* metadata;
* indexes;
* retrieval APIs;
* tenant boundaries;
* access permissions;
* and deletion.

An embedding should not automatically be classified as non-sensitive merely because its original document cannot be read directly.

Therefore:

**Embedding ≠ automatically non-confidential data.**

---

### 23. Confidential AI Memory Protection

AI memory may preserve confidential information across multiple interactions.

Memory can include:

* preferences;
* business information;
* customer information;
* previous conversations;
* inferred attributes;
* and operational details.

Memory must therefore have:

* explicit authorization;
* retention controls;
* access controls;
* tenant isolation;
* monitoring;
* and deletion mechanisms.

Therefore:

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain confidential information.**

---

### 24. Confidential AI Training-Data Protection

Confidential information used for model training requires strong governance.

Training-data controls should address:

* source authorization;
* classification;
* provenance;
* minimization;
* access;
* provider use;
* retention;
* geographic processing;
* and deletion.

Confidential business information should not become training data merely because an AI platform accepts the dataset.

---

### 25. Confidential AI Fine-Tuning Protection

Fine-tuning can expose confidential enterprise information to external model infrastructure.

Governance should address:

* dataset authorization;
* provider access;
* model isolation;
* training-job security;
* model artifact access;
* retention;
* deletion;
* and memorization risk.

A customized model may itself become a confidential enterprise asset.

---

### 26. Confidential AI Model Memorization

AI models may retain information through learned parameters or behavior.

Confidentiality risks include:

* extraction;
* prompt-based disclosure;
* model inversion;
* membership inference;
* memorization;
* and unintended reproduction.

The organization should assess whether confidential information can be recovered from model behavior.

Therefore:

**Training-data protection ≠ guaranteed protection against model memorization.**

---

### 27. Confidential AI Inference Protection

AI systems may infer confidential information from combinations of otherwise permitted data.

Examples include:

* strategic business plans;
* customer relationships;
* employee characteristics;
* security weaknesses;
* financial conditions;
* or proprietary processes.

Inference should therefore be considered part of the confidentiality threat model.

**Inference ≠ factual truth**, but an inference can still reveal sensitive organizational information.

---

### 28. Confidential AI Output Protection

AI outputs can themselves become confidential information.

An output may contain:

* source information;
* retrieved documents;
* proprietary knowledge;
* personal information;
* inferred information;
* credentials;
* or confidential business conclusions.

Output controls should evaluate:

* recipient;
* classification;
* authorization;
* external sharing;
* storage;
* logging;
* and downstream use.

The output should inherit appropriate confidentiality controls from the information it contains.

---

### 29. Confidential AI Cross-User Protection

AI systems must prevent confidential information belonging to one user from being exposed to another.

Potential leakage mechanisms include:

* shared conversations;
* memory;
* cached context;
* RAG;
* vector stores;
* tools;
* application state;
* provider logging;
* and generated outputs.

Cross-user isolation must be tested rather than assumed.

---

### 30. Confidential AI Cross-Tenant Protection

Tenant isolation must protect confidential information between:

* customers;
* subsidiaries;
* departments;
* legal entities;
* applications;
* and other organizational boundaries.

Controls should apply consistently across:

**Data → Retrieval → Context → Model → Memory → Tools → Output → Logs.**

A failure at any layer may compromise tenant confidentiality.

---

### 31. Confidential AI Provider Protection

External providers introduce additional confidentiality boundaries.

Provider assessment should determine:

* who can access customer data;
* whether provider employees can access content;
* whether subprocessors are involved;
* whether data is logged;
* whether data is retained;
* whether data is used for training;
* where processing occurs;
* and how deletion is performed.

Therefore:

**Provider capability ≠ permission to access confidential information.**

---

### 32. Confidential AI Cloud Protection

Cloud infrastructure may host confidential AI components.

Controls should cover:

* cloud identity;
* storage;
* networking;
* encryption;
* key management;
* segmentation;
* workload isolation;
* logging;
* backups;
* and administrative access.

Cloud adoption does not eliminate the organization's responsibility for confidentiality.

---

### 33. Confidential AI Third-Party Integration Protection

AI applications frequently connect to external services.

Examples include:

* SaaS platforms;
* document repositories;
* CRM systems;
* ticketing systems;
* collaboration platforms;
* databases;
* cloud storage;
* and external APIs.

Each integration creates a potential confidentiality boundary.

The organization must determine:

* what information is transferred;
* why;
* to whom;
* where;
* and under what authorization.

---

### 34. Confidential AI Tool Protection

AI tools may provide access to confidential enterprise systems.

Tool authorization should be independent from model authorization.

For example:

```text id="ctool7m"
LLM
 |
 v
Tool Request
 |
 v
Tool Authorization
 |
 +--> Identity
 +--> Purpose
 +--> Data Scope
 +--> Operation
 |
 v
Authorized Tool Execution
```

An LLM should not inherit unrestricted permissions simply because a tool is available.

---

### 35. Confidential AI Agent Protection

Agents create increased confidentiality risk because they can autonomously combine information from multiple sources.

An agent may:

* retrieve confidential documents;
* call enterprise APIs;
* maintain memory;
* execute tools;
* communicate externally;
* and generate outputs.

Agent access must therefore be bounded by:

* identity;
* purpose;
* data scope;
* tool authorization;
* tenant;
* geography;
* and human approval where appropriate.

Therefore:

**Agent capability ≠ confidentiality authority.**

---

### 36. Confidential AI Secrets Protection

Secrets should normally be excluded from ordinary AI prompts and context.

Secrets include:

* passwords;
* API keys;
* private keys;
* session tokens;
* certificates;
* encryption keys;
* database credentials;
* and privileged authentication information.

AI applications should use secure secret-management mechanisms rather than exposing secrets to models.

A model should receive the minimum authorization necessary to perform an operation without receiving the underlying secret whenever possible.

---

### 37. Confidential AI DLP

DLP provides a control layer for detecting confidential information before it enters an AI processing path.

DLP may identify:

* personal information;
* financial information;
* credentials;
* source code;
* intellectual property;
* strategic documents;
* regulated data;
* and other sensitive content.

Possible actions include:

* allow;
* warn;
* redact;
* tokenize;
* require approval;
* route to an approved environment;
* or block.

However:

**DLP detection ≠ authorization.**

DLP identifies potentially sensitive information; policy determines whether its processing is permitted.

---

### 38. Confidential AI Redaction

Redaction removes unnecessary confidential information before AI processing.

Example:

```text id="crdct9"
Original:
"Project Orion will launch on 15 November.
The acquisition target is Company X and the
negotiated value is €85 million."

        |
        v

Minimized:
"Project Orion has a planned launch date and
involves a confidential acquisition."

        |
        v

AI Processing
```

Redaction should preserve the information necessary for the task while reducing disclosure.

---

### 39. Confidential AI Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization may replace confidential identifiers with controlled tokens.

Example:

```text id="cps7mv"
Customer: Maria Lopez
        |
        v
Customer: CUST-47281
        |
        v
AI Processing
```

The mapping must remain securely protected.

Pseudonymization reduces exposure but does not automatically make the information non-confidential.

Therefore:

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

---

### 40. Confidential AI Encryption

Confidential AI workloads should use appropriate encryption for:

* data in transit;
* data at rest;
* backups;
* RAG repositories;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* logs;
* and exports.

Encryption should protect information against unauthorized access but does not replace authorization.

Therefore:

**Encryption ≠ authorization.**

---

### 41. Confidential AI Key Management

Key-management architecture should protect cryptographic keys separately from the AI workload.

Controls should include:

* key ownership;
* key access;
* rotation;
* revocation;
* separation of duties;
* secure storage;
* and monitoring.

Highly confidential workloads may require stronger key isolation or customer-controlled keys where supported.

---

### 42. Confidential AI Logging

Logs can become a major confidentiality risk.

Potentially sensitive log information includes:

* prompts;
* responses;
* retrieved documents;
* user identities;
* API payloads;
* tool calls;
* filenames;
* database queries;
* and error messages.

Logging should therefore apply:

* minimization;
* masking;
* redaction;
* access control;
* encryption;
* retention;
* and monitoring.

The organization should not create a permanent confidential-data copy merely for debugging.

---

### 43. Confidential AI Monitoring

Monitoring systems must themselves be treated as protected environments.

Monitoring data may reveal:

* user behavior;
* confidential prompts;
* business operations;
* model interactions;
* security events;
* and sensitive outputs.

Monitoring should therefore use appropriate:

* access controls;
* data minimization;
* retention;
* segregation;
* and encryption.

---

### 44. Confidential AI Retention

Confidentiality requires control over how long information remains accessible.

Retention should apply to:

* source data;
* prompts;
* conversations;
* RAG content;
* embeddings;
* memory;
* outputs;
* logs;
* training data;
* fine-tuning data;
* models;
* and backups.

Longer retention increases the period during which unauthorized disclosure can occur.

Therefore:

**Retention capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 45. Confidential AI Deletion

Deletion must consider all relevant AI artifacts.

```text id="cdel8p"
Source Data
   |
   +--> Prompt
   +--> Conversation
   +--> RAG Copy
   +--> Embedding
   +--> Memory
   +--> Output
   +--> Log
   +--> Training Dataset
   +--> Model Artifact
   +--> Backup
```

Deleting the original record does not necessarily prove that every derived or replicated artifact has been removed.

Therefore:

**Source-data deletion ≠ automatic deletion of every AI artifact.**

---

### 46. Confidential AI Backup Protection

Backups can preserve confidential information after primary systems are changed or deleted.

Backup controls should address:

* encryption;
* access;
* retention;
* geographic replication;
* restoration;
* deletion;
* and administrative access.

Backups should have the same or appropriately governed confidentiality classification as the information they contain.

---

### 47. Confidential AI Incident Management

Confidentiality incidents must be detected and managed throughout the AI lifecycle.

Examples include:

* unauthorized prompt disclosure;
* cross-user leakage;
* cross-tenant leakage;
* provider breach;
* RAG authorization bypass;
* memory leakage;
* model extraction;
* secret exposure;
* unauthorized tool access;
* and confidential output disclosure.

Incident management should integrate AI-specific events into the enterprise incident-response framework.

---

Confidential AI risk assessment should evaluate:

* data sensitivity;
* number of affected individuals;
* business criticality;
* provider exposure;
* model behavior;
* RAG;
* memory;
* tools;
* agents;
* training;
* fine-tuning;
* geographic processing;
* retention;
* and potential impact of disclosure.

A simplified risk model is:

```text id="crisk4n"

       |
       v

       +

       |
```

---

### 49. Enterprise Confidential AI Workload Principle

Enterprise confidential AI workloads should operate under a dedicated confidentiality boundary extending from source data through:

**Identity → Authorization → Prompt → Context → RAG → Vector Store → Model → Memory → Tools → Agents → Provider → Output → Logging → Retention → Deletion.**

Every component that can access or influence confidential information must be governed.

The architecture should assume that confidentiality can fail through:

* direct access;
* indirect retrieval;
* inference;
* model memorization;
* tool execution;
* agent behavior;
* logging;
* caching;
* backups;
* or provider processing.

---

### 50. Core Principle of Confidential AI Workloads

The core principle is:

> **Confidential AI workloads must ensure that protected information is accessible, processed, retrieved, retained, transferred, inferred, and disclosed only within explicitly authorized confidentiality boundaries.**

The fundamental distinctions are:

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data.**

**Authentication ≠ authorization.**

**Model capability ≠ data-access authority.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain confidential information.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ confidentiality authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to access confidential information.**

**Embedding ≠ automatically non-confidential data.**

**Inference ≠ factual truth.**

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized disclosure.**

**DLP detection ≠ authorization.**

**Encryption ≠ authorization.**

**Pseudonymization ≠ anonymization.**

**Cloud hosting ≠ transfer of confidentiality accountability.**

**Shared infrastructure ≠ shared authorization.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Documentation ≠ technical enforcement.**

**Control implementation ≠ control effectiveness.**

**Testing ≠ permanent assurance.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ permission to violate mandatory requirements.**

Confidential AI governance therefore requires a continuously enforced confidentiality chain:

**Data → Classification → Identity → Authorization → Minimization → Protection → AI Processing → Retrieval → Memory → Tools → Agents → Output → Monitoring → Retention → Deletion → Verification.**
