# 14.09 — Automated Decision-Making

## Part 1 — Automated Decision-Making Foundations

### 1. Definition of Automated Decision-Making in AI

Automated decision-making refers to decisions made through automated processing of information about individuals, where the system determines or materially contributes to an outcome affecting the individual.

AI may participate through:

* Classification
* Prediction
* Scoring
* Recommendation
* Ranking
* Eligibility determination
* Risk assessment
* Automated approval or rejection

**AI assistance ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

The actual decision architecture must be evaluated.

---

### 2. Purpose of Automated Decision-Making Governance

Automated decision-making governance exists to ensure that AI systems do not exercise uncontrolled authority over decisions affecting individuals.

Governance should address:

* Decision authority
* Personal data
* Purpose
* Legal requirements
* Transparency
* Human involvement
* Accuracy
* Fairness
* Security
* Accountability
* Individual rights
* Monitoring

---

### 3. AI Recommendation versus Automated Decision

An AI system may provide a recommendation without making the final decision.

```text id="adm14p9-01"
      |

```

The distinction depends on how the recommendation is actually used.

**Model recommendation ≠ final decision.**

---

### 4. Fully Automated Decision

A fully automated decision occurs when the relevant decision is determined without meaningful human intervention.

Examples may include:

* Automatic application rejection
* Automatic eligibility determination
* Automatic fraud blocking
* Automatic account restriction
* Automatic pricing decisions

The actual workflow should be examined rather than relying on system labels.

---

### 5. Partially Automated Decision

A partially automated process may involve AI in one or more stages while a human performs another stage.

Examples include:

* AI screening followed by human review
* AI ranking followed by manual selection
* AI risk scoring followed by approval
* AI-generated recommendation followed by human decision

The level and quality of human involvement determine the governance implications.

---

### 6. Significant Effect on Individuals

Automated processing becomes particularly important when it produces legal or similarly significant effects on individuals.

Potential areas include:

* Employment
* Credit
* Insurance
* Financial services
* Education
* Healthcare
* Housing
* Access to services
* Eligibility decisions

The significance of the outcome should be assessed based on the real-world effect.

---

### 7. Legal Effect

A legal effect may involve an outcome that affects an individual's legal rights or obligations.

Examples could include:

* Contractual status
* Eligibility
* Legal entitlement
* Regulatory status
* Access to legally protected rights

The existence of an AI model does not itself establish a legal effect.

---

### 8. Similarly Significant Effect

An AI decision may be significant even when it does not formally change a legal right.

Potential examples include:

* Employment opportunities
* Access to financial services
* Service eligibility
* Educational opportunities
* Insurance access
* Material pricing

The assessment should focus on the practical impact on the individual.

* Data category
* Purpose
* Accuracy
* Retention
* Access
* Processing location

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for automated decision-making.**

The organization should determine:

* Whether the data is necessary
* Whether its use is authorized
* Whether additional safeguards are required
* Whether less intrusive alternatives exist

### 14. Inferred Personal Data

AI may derive information about an individual rather than simply using information explicitly provided.

Examples include:

* Predicted risk
* Predicted preferences
* Behavioral characteristics
* Likelihood scores
* Personality-related attributes

**Inferred information ≠ automatically accurate information.**

---

### 15. Profiling

Profiling involves automated processing of personal data to evaluate or predict aspects of an individual.

Potential characteristics include:

* Behavior
* Preferences
* Economic circumstances
* Reliability
* Performance
* Interests

Profiling and automated decision-making may overlap but are not identical concepts.

---

### 18. Model Capability versus Decision Authority

An AI model may be technically capable of making a decision but not be authorized to do so.

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

Technical capability should therefore be separated from organizational and legal authorization.

---

### 20. Human Involvement

Human involvement should be assessed based on what the person actually does.

A human reviewer should have the ability to:

* Understand the relevant information
* Question the AI result
* Request additional evidence
* Reject the recommendation
* Modify the outcome
* Escalate the case

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

---

### 21. Meaningful Human Involvement

Meaningful human involvement requires more than placing a person at the end of an automated workflow.

A meaningful reviewer should have:

* Sufficient authority
* Relevant competence
* Adequate information
* Time to evaluate
* Ability to disagree
* Ability to override

### 50. Core Principle of Automated Decision-Making

**An AI system must not acquire decision authority merely because it has the technical capability to analyze personal data, generate recommendations, rank individuals, calculate scores, or automatically execute actions.**

The organization must explicitly distinguish:

**AI capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized decision.**

**AI recommendation ≠ final decision.**

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Human review ≠ automatic independence.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for a decision.**

**Data relevance ≠ authorization to process.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**Model accuracy ≠ individual-level accuracy.**

**Model explanation ≠ legal authorization.**

**Security control ≠ privacy compliance.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ lawful authorization.**

**Decision testing ≠ permanent assurance.**

A trustworthy automated decision architecture must ensure that every consequential decision has a clearly defined **purpose, authorized data, decision authority, appropriate human involvement where required, traceable model behavior, adequate transparency, contestability, security controls, privacy safeguards, evidence, monitoring, and reassessment mechanisms**.

The decision system must remain capable of being **challenged, corrected, restricted, suspended, overridden, rolled back, or withdrawn** when its data, model, decision logic, provider, processing purpose, automation level, or impact on individuals changes.

### 117. Core Control Principle

Automated decision-making controls must ensure that AI systems remain within explicitly defined **decision authority, data, purpose, human oversight, privacy, security, transparency, accuracy, fairness, and accountability boundaries**.

The control framework must preserve these distinctions:

**AI capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized decision.**

**AI recommendation ≠ final decision.**

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Human review ≠ automatic independence.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for a decision.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**Model accuracy ≠ individual-level accuracy.**

**Aggregate performance ≠ acceptable individual impact.**

**Security control ≠ privacy compliance.**

**Documentation ≠ technical enforcement.**

**Control implementation ≠ control effectiveness.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ lawful authorization.**

**Decision testing ≠ permanent assurance.**

A consequential AI decision system is adequately controlled only when its **data, purpose, model, decision logic, authority, human oversight, outputs, and downstream actions are explicitly governed and technically enforceable**, with continuous testing, monitoring, evidence, incident response, and reassessment capable of triggering **restriction, human review, correction, suspension, rollback, withdrawal, or reauthorization** when decision risk changes.
