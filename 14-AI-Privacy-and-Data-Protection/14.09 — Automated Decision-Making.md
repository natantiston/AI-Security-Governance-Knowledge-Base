# 14.09 — Automated Decision-Making

## Part 1 — Automated Decision-Making Foundations

### 1. Definition of Automated Decision-Making in AI

Automated decision-making refers to decisions made through automated processing of information about individuals, where the system determines or materially contributes to an outcome affecting the individual.

AI may participate through:

* Classification
* Prediction
* Scoring
* Recommendation
* Ranking
* Eligibility determination
* Risk assessment
* Automated approval or rejection

**AI assistance ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

The actual decision architecture must be evaluated.

---

### 2. Purpose of Automated Decision-Making Governance

Automated decision-making governance exists to ensure that AI systems do not exercise uncontrolled authority over decisions affecting individuals.

Governance should address:

* Decision authority
* Personal data
* Purpose
* Legal requirements
* Transparency
* Human involvement
* Accuracy
* Fairness
* Security
* Accountability
* Individual rights
* Monitoring

---

### 3. AI Recommendation versus Automated Decision

An AI system may provide a recommendation without making the final decision.

```text id="adm14p9-01"
Personal Data
      |
      v
AI Model
      |
      v
Recommendation
      |
      v
Authorized Human
      |
      v
Final Decision
```

The distinction depends on how the recommendation is actually used.

**Model recommendation ≠ final decision.**

---

### 4. Fully Automated Decision

A fully automated decision occurs when the relevant decision is determined without meaningful human intervention.

Examples may include:

* Automatic application rejection
* Automatic eligibility determination
* Automatic fraud blocking
* Automatic account restriction
* Automatic pricing decisions

The actual workflow should be examined rather than relying on system labels.

---

### 5. Partially Automated Decision

A partially automated process may involve AI in one or more stages while a human performs another stage.

Examples include:

* AI screening followed by human review
* AI ranking followed by manual selection
* AI risk scoring followed by approval
* AI-generated recommendation followed by human decision

The level and quality of human involvement determine the governance implications.

---

### 6. Significant Effect on Individuals

Automated processing becomes particularly important when it produces legal or similarly significant effects on individuals.

Potential areas include:

* Employment
* Credit
* Insurance
* Financial services
* Education
* Healthcare
* Housing
* Access to services
* Eligibility decisions

The significance of the outcome should be assessed based on the real-world effect.

---

### 7. Legal Effect

A legal effect may involve an outcome that affects an individual's legal rights or obligations.

Examples could include:

* Contractual status
* Eligibility
* Legal entitlement
* Regulatory status
* Access to legally protected rights

The existence of an AI model does not itself establish a legal effect.

---

### 8. Similarly Significant Effect

An AI decision may be significant even when it does not formally change a legal right.

Potential examples include:

* Employment opportunities
* Access to financial services
* Service eligibility
* Educational opportunities
* Insurance access
* Material pricing

The assessment should focus on the practical impact on the individual.

---

### 10. Decision Purpose

Every automated decision system should have a clearly defined purpose.

Examples:

* Detect fraud
* Assess eligibility
* Prioritize applications
* Identify security threats
* Recommend candidates

Purpose should determine what data and model behavior are permitted.

---

### 11. Decision Data

Automated decisions may rely on:

* Direct personal data
* Historical records
* Behavioral information
* Transaction data
* Derived attributes
* Inferred information
* External data
* AI-generated features

All relevant data sources should be identified.

---

### 12. Personal Data in Automated Decisions

Personal data used in automated decisions should be appropriately governed.

The organization should identify:

* Data source
* Data category
* Purpose
* Accuracy
* Retention
* Access
* Processing location

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for automated decision-making.**

---

### 13. Special-Category Data

Special-category personal data may create additional privacy considerations when used in automated decision-making.

The organization should determine:

* Whether the data is necessary
* Whether its use is authorized
* Whether additional safeguards are required
* Whether less intrusive alternatives exist

---

### 14. Inferred Personal Data

AI may derive information about an individual rather than simply using information explicitly provided.

Examples include:

* Predicted risk
* Predicted preferences
* Behavioral characteristics
* Likelihood scores
* Personality-related attributes

**Inferred information ≠ automatically accurate information.**

---

### 15. Profiling

Profiling involves automated processing of personal data to evaluate or predict aspects of an individual.

Potential characteristics include:

* Behavior
* Preferences
* Economic circumstances
* Reliability
* Performance
* Interests

Profiling and automated decision-making may overlap but are not identical concepts.

---

### 16. Profiling versus Automated Decision-Making

A system can perform profiling without making a final automated decision.

```text id="adm14p9-03"
Personal Data
      |
      v
Profiling
      |
      v
Individual Score
      |
      +----> Analysis Only
      |
      +----> Automated Decision
```

Therefore:

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

---

### 17. Decision Authority

Decision authority should be explicitly assigned.

```text id="adm14p9-04"
Data
 |
 v
AI Model
 |
 v
Recommendation
 |
 v
Decision Authority
 |
 v
Final Outcome
```

The model should not silently acquire authority merely because the application automatically accepts its output.

---

### 18. Model Capability versus Decision Authority

An AI model may be technically capable of making a decision but not be authorized to do so.

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

Technical capability should therefore be separated from organizational and legal authorization.

---

### 19. Model Output versus Authorized Decision

A model's output is an analytical artifact unless an authorized decision process converts it into an official outcome.

```text id="adm14p9-05"
Model Output
     |
     X
Automatic Authority
     
Model Output
     |
     v
Authorized Decision Process
     |
     v
Decision
```

---

### 20. Human Involvement

Human involvement should be assessed based on what the person actually does.

A human reviewer should have the ability to:

* Understand the relevant information
* Question the AI result
* Request additional evidence
* Reject the recommendation
* Modify the outcome
* Escalate the case

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

---

### 21. Meaningful Human Involvement

Meaningful human involvement requires more than placing a person at the end of an automated workflow.

A meaningful reviewer should have:

* Sufficient authority
* Relevant competence
* Adequate information
* Time to evaluate
* Ability to disagree
* Ability to override

---

### 22. Human Rubber-Stamping Risk

A human who routinely accepts AI decisions without independent evaluation may provide only nominal oversight.

```text id="adm14p9-06"
AI Recommendation
       |
       v
Human Reviewer
       |
       v
Automatic Acceptance
       |
       v
Outcome
```

This may not represent meaningful human decision-making.

---

### 23. Human Override

Where human review is required, the architecture should support genuine override.

Override capability should be:

* Accessible
* Authorized
* Logged
* Traceable
* Operationally realistic

---

### 24. Automated Decision Accuracy

Accuracy is critical where automated decisions affect individuals.

The organization should evaluate:

* Input accuracy
* Model accuracy
* False positives
* False negatives
* Data quality
* Model drift

High aggregate accuracy does not necessarily mean that every individual receives an accurate outcome.

---

### 25. Automated Decision Fairness

Automated decisions may produce unequal outcomes across populations.

Assessment may consider:

* Representation
* Error rates
* Outcome differences
* Data quality
* Model behavior
* Protected characteristics where appropriate

Fairness analysis should complement, not replace, privacy analysis.

---

### 26. Automated Decision Transparency

Individuals may need appropriate information about automated processing.

Transparency may address:

* Existence of automated processing
* Purpose
* Relevant data
* General decision logic
* Consequences
* Applicable rights

Transparency should be meaningful without unnecessarily exposing security-sensitive or proprietary information.

---

### 27. Automated Decision Explainability

The organization should be able to explain relevant aspects of an automated decision process.

Potential explanation elements include:

* Input categories
* Decision factors
* Model role
* Thresholds
* Human review
* Outcome

**Explainability ≠ exposing the entire model.**

---

### 28. Automated Decision Contestability

Affected individuals may need mechanisms to challenge relevant outcomes where applicable.

A contestability mechanism may allow:

* Review
* Correction
* Human intervention
* Appeal
* Reconsideration

---

### 29. Automated Decision Human Review

Where human review is required, the process should be designed rather than assumed.

---

### 30. Automated Decision Data Minimization

Only necessary personal data should be used for automated decision-making.

The system should not use every available attribute merely because the model can process it.

**Data availability ≠ decision relevance.**

---

### 31. Automated Decision Purpose Limitation

Data used for one purpose should not automatically be reused for unrelated automated decisions.

```text id="adm14p9-08"
Data
 |
 v
Approved Purpose
 |
 X
Unrelated Decision
```

A new decision purpose should trigger appropriate assessment.

---

### 32. Automated Decision Data Accuracy

Input data should be sufficiently accurate for the decision being made.

Controls may include:

* Validation
* Source verification
* Freshness checks
* Error correction
* Human escalation

---

### 33. Automated Decision Data Retention

Decision-related personal data should not be retained indefinitely merely because it may be useful for future model development.

Retention should be purpose-driven.

---

### 34. Automated Decision Auditability

The organization should be able to reconstruct relevant decision events.

Audit records may include:

* Individual or transaction identifier
* Model version
* Input-data version
* Decision policy
* Output
* Human review
* Final decision
* Timestamp

---

### 35. Automated Decision Traceability

### 36. Automated Decision Security

Security controls should protect automated decision systems from manipulation.

Threats may include:

* Data poisoning
* Model manipulation
* Prompt injection
* Unauthorized access
* Adversarial inputs
* Model extraction

**Security assurance ≠ privacy compliance.**

Both must be addressed.

---

### 37. Automated Decision Prompt Risk

If an AI decision system accepts natural-language prompts, prompt manipulation may influence decisions.

Controls should prevent unauthorized users from changing decision logic through prompts.

---

### 38. Automated Decision RAG Risk

RAG may introduce information into a decision workflow.

The system should verify:

* Source authorization
* Source accuracy
* Relevance
* Freshness
* Tenant isolation

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

---

### 39. Automated Decision Memory Risk

AI memory may influence future decision-making.

Stored information should therefore be:

* Authorized
* Accurate
* Relevant
* Retained appropriately

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 40. Automated Decision Tool and Agent Risk

Tools and agents may retrieve or modify information that affects automated decisions.

```text id="adm14p9-10"
Decision Agent
     |
 +---+---+---+
 |   |   |   |
CRM HR  DB  API
 |   |   |   |
 +---+---+---+
       |
       v
Decision
```

Tool and agent authority should be explicitly constrained.

**Agent capability ≠ decision authority.**

---

### 41. Automated Decision Provider Risk

External AI providers may process information used in automated decisions.

The organization should evaluate:

* Provider role
* Data use
* Retention
* Geography
* Security
* Model changes
* Subprocessors

---

### 42. Automated Decision Model Change

Changing the model may change decision outcomes.

Triggers include:

* New model
* New model version
* New training data
* New fine-tuning
* New threshold
* New prompt
* New provider

Material changes should trigger reassessment.

---

### 43. Automated Decision Drift

Model performance can change after deployment.

Drift may result from:

* Population changes
* Data changes
* Economic conditions
* New behaviors
* Data-source changes

Continuous monitoring is therefore required for material decision systems.

---

### 44. Automated Decision Incident Management

Decision-system incidents may include:

* Incorrect decisions
* Unauthorized decisions
* System manipulation
* Personal-data exposure
* Model drift
* Cross-tenant leakage
* Unauthorized model changes

Incident response should address both technical and individual impact.

---

### 45. Automated Decision Risk Assessment

Risk assessment should consider:

* Nature of decision
* Number of affected individuals
* Severity of impact
* Data sensitivity
* Automation level
* Human involvement
* Model uncertainty
* Security threats

---

### 46. Automated Decision Privacy Assessment

Where personal data is involved, automated decision-making should be considered within applicable privacy assessments.

The assessment should distinguish:

**AI risk ≠ privacy risk.**

**Cybersecurity risk ≠ privacy risk.**

**Privacy risk ≠ automated-decision risk.**

These risks can interact but require separate analysis.

---

### 47. Automated Decision Testing

Decision systems should be tested before and after deployment.

Testing may include:

* Accuracy
* Fairness
* Robustness
* Privacy
* Security
* Human override
* Explanation
* Contestability

---

### 48. Automated Decision Governance

Automated decision systems should have defined:

* Business owner
* Model owner
* Privacy owner
* Security owner
* Decision authority
* Human reviewer
* Escalation process

---

### 50. Core Principle of Automated Decision-Making

**An AI system must not acquire decision authority merely because it has the technical capability to analyze personal data, generate recommendations, rank individuals, calculate scores, or automatically execute actions.**

The organization must explicitly distinguish:

**AI capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized decision.**

**AI recommendation ≠ final decision.**

**Profiling ≠ automatically automated decision-making.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Human review ≠ automatic independence.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to use data for a decision.**

**Data relevance ≠ authorization to process.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process personal data.**

**Model accuracy ≠ individual-level accuracy.**

**Model explanation ≠ legal authorization.**

**Security control ≠ privacy compliance.**

**Risk acceptance ≠ lawful authorization.**

**Decision testing ≠ permanent assurance.**

A trustworthy automated decision architecture must ensure that every consequential decision has a clearly defined **purpose, authorized data, decision authority, appropriate human involvement where required, traceable model behavior, adequate transparency, contestability, security controls, privacy safeguards, evidence, monitoring, and reassessment mechanisms**.

The decision system must remain capable of being **challenged, corrected, restricted, suspended, overridden, rolled back, or withdrawn** when its data, model, decision logic, provider, processing purpose, automation level, or impact on individuals changes.
