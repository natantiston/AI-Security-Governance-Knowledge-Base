# 14.17 — Third-Party LLM Privacy

## Part 1 — Third-Party LLM Privacy Foundations

### 1. Definition of Third-Party LLM Privacy

Third-party LLM privacy is the governance and protection of personal, confidential, sensitive, proprietary, regulated, and otherwise protected information when an organization uses a Large Language Model (LLM) or generative AI service operated, hosted, trained, or otherwise controlled by an external provider.

A third-party LLM may receive information through:

* prompts;
* conversation history;
* uploaded files;
* system instructions;
* retrieved documents;
* RAG pipelines;
* vector databases;
* memory mechanisms;
* API requests;
* tool calls;
* agent workflows;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* evaluation datasets;
* telemetry;
* error logs;
* abuse-monitoring systems; and
* other provider-operated processing components.

Third-party LLM privacy therefore extends beyond the question of whether the provider says it "protects customer data."

The organization must understand **what data leaves the enterprise, why it leaves, where it is processed, who can access it, how long it is retained, whether it is reused, and how privacy obligations remain enforceable throughout the provider relationship.**

---

### 2. Purpose of Third-Party LLM Privacy Governance

The purpose of third-party LLM privacy governance is to ensure that external AI services process organizational and personal data only within explicitly authorized privacy, security, contractual, regulatory, and business boundaries.

The governance objective is to prevent situations where:

* employees submit personal information to an unapproved LLM;
* confidential information is transmitted without authorization;
* provider systems retain prompts longer than permitted;
* customer information is reused for model training without authorization;
* provider subprocessors receive data without appropriate governance;
* data is transferred to unauthorized countries;
* provider logging captures sensitive information;
* deleted information remains in backups or derived systems;
* provider-side memory retains information unexpectedly;
* AI outputs expose information from other customers;
* provider personnel obtain inappropriate access;
* privacy obligations become unclear between the organization and provider; or
* the organization assumes that contractual commitments alone guarantee technical privacy enforcement.

Third-party LLM governance therefore establishes control over the complete external-AI processing chain.

### 4. Third-Party LLM Processing Model

A third-party LLM processing model describes how organizational data moves from enterprise-controlled systems into provider-controlled infrastructure.

A typical architecture may look like:

```text
Enterprise User
      |
      v
Enterprise Application
      |
      v
Privacy / Security Gateway
      |
      +---- Identity
      +---- Authorization
      +---- DLP
      +---- Redaction
      +---- Data Classification
      +---- Purpose Enforcement
      |
      v
LLM API
      |
      v
Provider Processing Layer
      |
      +---- Model
      +---- Context
      +---- RAG
      +---- Memory
      +---- Logging
      +---- Safety Systems
      +---- Abuse Monitoring
      |
      v
Provider Infrastructure
      |
      +---- Subprocessors
      +---- Storage
      +---- Backup
      +---- Support Systems
      |
      v
LLM Response
      |
      v

```

Privacy risk exists at every boundary.

The organization must not treat the API endpoint as the only privacy boundary.

### 5. Third-Party LLM Controller and Processor Roles

The organization must determine the privacy roles of the parties involved in third-party LLM processing.

Depending on the processing arrangement and applicable law, the organization may act as a controller while the provider acts as a processor, or different arrangements may apply.

The assessment should determine:

* who determines the purposes of processing;
* who determines essential means of processing;
* whether the provider processes data only on documented instructions;
* whether the provider independently determines processing purposes;
* whether subprocessors are involved;
* whether provider telemetry creates additional processing;
* whether provider safety or abuse monitoring involves customer data;
* whether provider analytics create separate processing;
* whether data is used for service improvement;
* whether data is used for model training;
* and whether any processing occurs outside the originally authorized purpose.

Role determination must be based on the actual processing arrangement rather than simply the terminology used in a commercial contract.

---

### 6. Third-Party LLM Shared Responsibility

Third-party LLM privacy commonly follows a shared-responsibility model.

The provider may be responsible for controls such as:

* infrastructure security;
* platform availability;
* physical security;
* underlying network security;
* service-level isolation;
* provider-managed encryption;
* infrastructure access management; and
* provider-side operational security.

The customer may remain responsible for:

* determining whether data may be processed;
* selecting the appropriate service;
* configuring privacy settings;
* controlling user access;
* minimizing prompts;
* controlling RAG sources;
* controlling memory;
* configuring retention;
* controlling data residency requirements;
* validating contractual commitments;
* handling data-subject requests;
* monitoring usage;
* and ensuring lawful processing.

Therefore:

**Shared responsibility ≠ shared accountability for every privacy obligation.**

An organization cannot transfer its privacy accountability merely by using a third-party LLM.

---

### 7. Third-Party LLM Data Ownership

Organizations must establish ownership and control expectations for data submitted to third-party LLMs.

Relevant data may include:

* customer data;
* employee data;
* supplier information;
* intellectual property;
* confidential documents;
* source code;
* credentials;
* business records;
* personal communications;
* support tickets;
* legal documents;
* financial information;
* health-related information;
* authentication information; and
* AI-generated or derived information.

Data ownership questions should include:

* Who owns the source data?
* Who controls processing?
* Can the provider use the data for service improvement?
* Can the provider use it for model training?
* Can the provider retain it?
* Can provider personnel access it?
* Can subprocessors process it?
* Can the provider create derived information?
* Can the provider combine it with other data?
* Can the provider export it?
* Can the organization retrieve or delete it?

The organization's ownership rights do not automatically prevent provider-side processing.

---

### 8. Third-Party LLM Data Classification

Before data is transmitted to an external LLM, it should be classified according to enterprise privacy and information-security requirements.

A classification model may include:

| Classification               | Example                              | Typical LLM Treatment           |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Public                       | Published marketing material         | Generally permitted if approved |
| Internal                     | Internal procedures                  | Controlled                      |
| Confidential                 | Business plans                       | Restricted                      |
| Personal                     | Customer records                     | Strong controls                 |
| Sensitive                    | Employee or financial information    | Highly restricted               |
| Special-category / regulated | Protected personal information       | Exceptional controls            |
| Secret / critical            | Credentials, keys, strategic secrets | Normally prohibited             |

The classification should determine:

* whether the data may be transmitted;
* which providers are approved;
* whether redaction is required;
* whether encryption is required;
* whether human approval is required;
* whether the workload must remain inside enterprise infrastructure;
* and whether external processing is prohibited.

### 9. Third-Party LLM Personal Data

Personal data can enter a third-party LLM through seemingly ordinary interactions.

Examples include:

```text
"Summarize this customer complaint."

"Analyze this employee's performance."

"Review this patient's document."

"Draft a response to this applicant."

"Find the customer's account history."

"Classify these support tickets."

"Extract names, addresses, and phone numbers."

"Analyze this CV."
```

Even when the user does not explicitly identify the information as personal data, the content may contain:

* names;
* email addresses;
* telephone numbers;
* addresses;
* identification numbers;
* employment information;
* financial information;
* behavioral information;
* location information;
* account information;
* or inferred characteristics.

Therefore:

**Users should not be expected to identify every privacy risk manually.**

Technical controls should help detect and prevent unauthorized transmission.

---

### 10. Third-Party LLM Sensitive Data

Sensitive information requires stronger governance because disclosure to an external LLM may create significant consequences for individuals and organizations.

Sensitive information may include:

* financial records;
* health information;
* authentication information;
* employment records;
* legal information;
* confidential communications;
* precise location information;
* biometric information;
* special-category personal data;
* regulated information;
* proprietary algorithms;
* intellectual property;
* security configurations;
* credentials and secrets.

The third-party LLM policy should explicitly define which categories:

1. are permitted;
2. require additional safeguards;
3. require explicit approval;
4. require a dedicated provider arrangement; or
5. are prohibited.

---

### 11. Third-Party LLM Data Location

The organization must understand where data is processed.

Data location may involve multiple environments:

```text
Customer Request
      |
      v
API Gateway
      |
      +--> Processing Region
      |
      +--> Model Infrastructure
      |
      +--> Logging Infrastructure
      |
      +--> Monitoring Systems
      |
      +--> Abuse / Safety Systems
      |
      +--> Support Systems
      |
      +--> Backup Systems
      |
      +--> Subprocessors
```

A provider's stated "region" may therefore represent only one component of the overall processing architecture.

Consequently:

**Cloud region ≠ complete data-location visibility.**

The organization should understand relevant processing locations across the complete provider ecosystem.

### 12. Third-Party LLM Data Residency

Data residency defines where organizational or personal data is required or permitted to remain.

Residency requirements may arise from:

* law;
* regulation;
* contractual obligations;
* customer commitments;
* internal policy;
* sector requirements;
* government requirements;
* risk classification; or
* organizational strategy.

A provider offering regional hosting does not automatically guarantee that every associated operation occurs within that region.

Residency assessment should consider:

* prompts;
* responses;
* uploaded documents;
* RAG data;
* embeddings;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* logs;
* telemetry;
* backups;
* support access;
* administrative access;
* subprocessors;
* disaster recovery;
* and provider-side monitoring.

---

### 13. Third-Party LLM Cross-Border Processing

Third-party LLM use may cause personal data to cross national borders even when the organization is located in one country.

Potential transfer paths include:

```text
Enterprise
   |
   v
LLM Provider
   |
   +---- Processing Country A
   |
   +---- Logging Country B
   |
   +---- Support Country C
   |
   +---- Subprocessor Country D
   |
   +---- Backup Country E
```

Cross-border privacy governance must therefore consider the complete processing chain.

The organization should identify:

* countries involved;
* transfer mechanisms;
* applicable restrictions;
* contractual safeguards;
* provider commitments;
* subprocessor locations;
* remote administrative access;
* and changes to geographic processing.

---

### 14. Third-Party LLM Purpose Limitation

Data submitted to a third-party LLM must be processed for an explicitly defined and authorized purpose.

For example:

> "Summarize this approved customer-support interaction."

is materially different from:

> "Use customer-support data to improve the provider's general AI services."

The first may represent an explicitly defined enterprise processing purpose.

The second may represent an additional processing purpose requiring separate assessment and authorization.

Therefore:

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process data for every provider purpose.**

The organization must distinguish between:

* customer-requested processing;
* service operation;
* security monitoring;
* abuse prevention;
* analytics;
* product improvement;
* model training;
* human review;
* and other secondary uses.

---

### 15. Third-Party LLM Data Minimization

Only the minimum information necessary for the approved task should be transmitted to the provider.

For example, if the objective is:

> "Classify a customer complaint."

the system may not need to transmit:

* full customer address;
* account number;
* date of birth;
* payment details;
* unrelated communications;
* internal employee information; or
* other unnecessary identifiers.

A privacy gateway could transform:

```text
Original:
"Jonathan Morales, account 8493921, born 24 December,
address ..., phone ..., reports that his internet service
was unavailable for three days."

        |
        v

Minimized:
"Customer reports that internet service was unavailable
for three days."

        |
        v

Third-Party LLM
```

This reduces the amount of personal data exposed to the external provider.

Privacy controls should be incorporated into the architecture before the third-party LLM is deployed.

A privacy-by-design architecture may include:

Privacy should not depend exclusively on provider promises.

### 17. Third-Party LLM Privacy by Default

Approved third-party LLM services should be configured so that privacy-protective settings are the default.

Examples include:

* minimum retention;
* no unnecessary training use;
* restricted logging;
* restricted memory;
* restricted data sharing;
* approved geographic processing;
* strong authentication;
* least privilege;
* disabled unnecessary tools;
* restricted external integrations;
* restricted administrator access;
* and controlled user enrollment.

A user should not have to discover privacy settings manually before submitting protected information.

Therefore:

**Privacy-protective configuration should be the default state, not an optional enhancement.**

---

### 18. Third-Party LLM Prompt Privacy

Prompts are data-processing events when they contain personal or protected information.

Prompt privacy therefore requires controls over:

* what users enter;
* what applications generate;
* what data is inserted automatically;
* what context is attached;
* what conversation history is included;
* what memory is retrieved;
* what RAG content is added;
* and what information is transmitted to the provider.

A seemingly harmless prompt can become sensitive when application-generated context is appended automatically.

For example:

```text
User:
"Write a response to this customer."

Application automatically adds:
- customer profile
- account history
- payment status
- support history
- internal notes

Actual provider request:
Customer + financial + behavioral + support information
```

The privacy boundary must therefore evaluate the **complete request**, not merely the text typed by the user.

---

### 19. Third-Party LLM Context Privacy

LLM context may contain substantially more information than the user's visible prompt.

Context can include:

* system instructions;
* conversation history;
* retrieved documents;
* user profiles;
* memory;
* application state;
* metadata;
* tool results;
* database records;
* access tokens;
* hidden application fields;
* and prior interactions.

Therefore:

**Visible prompt ≠ complete data transmission.**

Privacy controls should inspect the complete payload and processing context before transmission.

---

### 20. Third-Party LLM RAG Privacy

Retrieval-Augmented Generation introduces additional privacy risks because enterprise documents may be retrieved and transmitted to the external provider.

A typical flow is:

```text
User
 |
 v
Enterprise Application
 |
 v
Authorization
 |
 v
RAG Retrieval
 |
 v
Retrieved Documents
 |
 v
Privacy / DLP Gateway
 |
 v
Third-Party LLM
```

The organization must ensure that retrieval is authorized for the specific user, tenant, purpose, and request.

Therefore:

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

The fact that a document can technically be retrieved does not mean it may be transmitted to the third-party LLM.

---

### 21. Third-Party LLM Vector-Store Privacy

Vector stores can contain embeddings derived from personal or confidential information.

Although embeddings may not look like conventional readable records, they can remain associated with identifiable individuals or protected information.

The organization should therefore consider:

* source-data classification;
* embedding generation;
* metadata;
* access controls;
* tenant isolation;
* retrieval authorization;
* provider transmission;
* retention;
* deletion;
* reconstruction risk;
* and derived-data handling.

Therefore:

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

Vector stores must remain within the applicable privacy and security control boundary when they contain information derived from protected data.

---

### 22. Third-Party LLM Memory Privacy

Provider-managed or application-managed memory can retain information beyond the immediate interaction.

Memory may contain:

* user preferences;
* personal information;
* conversation history;
* inferred characteristics;
* business information;
* previous requests;
* sensitive context;
* or derived information.

Memory governance must define:

* what can be retained;
* why it can be retained;
* how long it can be retained;
* who can access it;
* where it is stored;
* whether it is transmitted to the provider;
* how users can review it;
* and how it is deleted.

Therefore:

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 23. Third-Party LLM Training Data Privacy

Organizations must determine whether information submitted to the provider can become training or model-improvement data.

This distinction is critical:

```text
Customer Prompt
      |
      v
Service Processing
      |
      +----> Temporary processing
      |
      +----> Logging
      |
      +----> Analytics
      |
      +----> Human review
      |
      +----> Product improvement
      |
      +----> Model training
```

Each processing purpose should be explicitly understood and governed.

An organization should never assume:

> "The provider processes our prompt only to answer the request."

The actual provider terms, configuration, product architecture, and contractual commitments must establish what happens to the information.

---

### 24. Third-Party LLM Fine-Tuning Privacy

Fine-tuning introduces a different privacy exposure because organizational datasets may be intentionally provided to the provider for model customization.

Potential data may include:

* customer records;
* employee information;
* support interactions;
* internal documents;
* proprietary terminology;
* conversation histories;
* and other business datasets.

The organization must determine:

* whether the provider retains the fine-tuning dataset;
* whether the dataset is reused;
* whether the resulting model is isolated;
* who can access the customized model;
* whether model artifacts contain learned information;
* how the model is deleted;
* and what happens to backups and derived artifacts.

Fine-tuning therefore requires independent privacy assessment rather than being treated as ordinary API usage.

---

### 25. Third-Party LLM Model Memorization

An LLM may memorize information from training or fine-tuning data.

Privacy risks can include:

* extraction of sensitive training examples;
* unintended reproduction;
* membership inference;
* model inversion;
* leakage through generated outputs;
* and exposure of information to unauthorized users.

The organization should therefore evaluate whether protected information could become embedded in model behavior.

A provider's statement that data is "not used for training" may reduce one category of risk, but it does not automatically eliminate all privacy risks associated with processing, logging, memory, retrieval, support, or other provider systems.

---

### 26. Third-Party LLM Inference Privacy

LLMs can generate information that was not explicitly provided as a factual field.

They may infer:

* preferences;
* characteristics;
* relationships;
* risk categories;
* behavioral patterns;
* likely intentions;
* or other attributes.

These inferences can become privacy-relevant even when they are probabilistic.

Therefore:

**Inference ≠ factual truth.**

An inferred attribute should not automatically be treated as verified personal information, but it may still create privacy, fairness, or decision-making consequences.

---

### 27. Third-Party LLM Output Privacy

Privacy risk does not end when the provider returns a response.

Outputs may contain:

* personal information;
* confidential information;
* hallucinated personal information;
* information retrieved from protected sources;
* information inferred from private data;
* or information belonging to another user or tenant.

Output governance should therefore evaluate:

* recipient;
* authorization;
* sensitivity;
* accuracy;
* external sharing;
* storage;
* logging;
* downstream use;
* and whether the output may trigger an automated decision.

The response must be treated as another controlled data-processing event.

---

### 28. Third-Party LLM Cross-User Privacy

A third-party LLM environment must prevent information belonging to one user from being exposed to another unauthorized user.

Potential leakage paths include:

* conversation history;
* memory;
* RAG;
* cached context;
* application state;
* provider-side session handling;
* shared vector stores;
* logging;
* tool results;
* and incorrectly configured APIs.

A secure design must enforce user-level authorization at every relevant layer.

---

### 29. Third-Party LLM Cross-Tenant Privacy

Multi-tenant LLM deployments introduce additional privacy requirements.

A tenant boundary may exist between:

* customers;
* subsidiaries;
* business units;
* geographic regions;
* legal entities;
* applications;
* or internal organizational groups.

Tenant isolation should apply to:

* prompts;
* context;
* conversations;
* memory;
* RAG;
* vector stores;
* tools;
* logs;
* outputs;
* administrative access;
* and provider-side processing.

Therefore:

**Shared infrastructure ≠ shared authorization.**

A provider may operate a shared platform while still requiring strong logical isolation between customers.

---

### 30. Third-Party LLM Identity and Authentication

Identity controls determine **who** is requesting access to the third-party LLM service.

Controls may include:

* enterprise identity federation;
* SSO;
* MFA;
* service identities;
* workload identities;
* API credentials;
* short-lived tokens;
* device controls;
* and conditional access.

However:

**Authentication ≠ authorization.**

Successfully authenticating a user does not mean the user is authorized to transmit every category of organizational or personal data to the provider.

---

### 31. Third-Party LLM Authorization

Authorization determines whether a specific identity may perform a specific AI operation using specific data.

Authorization should consider:

```text
Identity
   +
Role
   +
Tenant
   +
Data Classification
   +
Purpose
   +
LLM Service
   +
Geography
   +
Operation
   +
Provider Policy
   =
Authorized Processing Decision
```

For example, an employee may be authorized to use an enterprise LLM for public information but not authorized to transmit customer records to an external model.

---

### 32. Third-Party LLM Least Privilege

Third-party LLM integrations should operate with the minimum privileges necessary.

Least privilege should apply to:

* users;
* applications;
* API keys;
* service accounts;
* workload identities;
* tools;
* RAG repositories;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* provider administration;
* support access;
* and downstream systems.

The objective is to prevent an LLM integration from becoming a broad data-access channel.

---

### 33. Third-Party LLM Encryption

Protected information transmitted to a third-party LLM should be appropriately protected in transit and, where applicable, at rest.

Encryption governance should address:

* user-to-application communication;
* application-to-provider communication;
* provider storage;
* RAG repositories;
* vector databases;
* memory;
* logs;
* backups;
* exports;
* and administrative interfaces.

Encryption reduces unauthorized disclosure risk but does not replace:

* purpose limitation;
* minimization;
* authorization;
* retention control;
* or lawful processing.

Therefore:

**Encryption ≠ privacy authorization.**

---

### 34. Third-Party LLM Key Management

Encryption keys may be managed by:

* the organization;
* the provider;
* a cloud platform;
* a managed key service;
* or a customer-controlled key-management architecture.

The organization should understand:

* who controls the keys;
* who can access them;
* where keys are stored;
* how keys are rotated;
* whether customer-managed keys are supported;
* whether provider personnel can access protected data;
* and what happens when the provider relationship ends.

Key management is particularly important for highly confidential or regulated workloads.

---

### 35. Third-Party LLM Logging Privacy

Provider or enterprise logging may capture sensitive information unintentionally.

Potentially sensitive log data includes:

* prompts;
* responses;
* user identities;
* IP addresses;
* document names;
* retrieved content;
* error messages;
* API payloads;
* metadata;
* and tool execution details.

Logging must therefore follow privacy principles such as:

* minimization;
* purpose limitation;
* access control;
* retention limits;
* masking;
* redaction;
* and secure storage.

A log created for security monitoring must not become an uncontrolled secondary personal-data repository.

---

### 36. Third-Party LLM Monitoring Privacy

Monitoring systems may themselves process personal or sensitive information.

Examples include:

* prompt monitoring;
* abuse detection;
* behavioral analytics;
* user activity monitoring;
* content moderation;
* security analytics;
* anomaly detection;
* and quality monitoring.

The organization should identify:

* what monitoring data is collected;
* why it is collected;
* who can access it;
* how long it is retained;
* where it is processed;
* and whether monitoring creates additional privacy obligations.

Monitoring must remain proportional to its authorized purpose.

---

### 37. Third-Party LLM Retention

Retention determines how long provider-controlled data remains available.

Potential retention targets include:

* prompts;
* responses;
* uploaded files;
* conversations;
* RAG content;
* embeddings;
* memory;
* logs;
* telemetry;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* model artifacts;
* backups;
* and support records.

The organization should establish retention requirements based on:

* purpose;
* legal requirements;
* business need;
* sensitivity;
* contractual commitments;
* and risk.

**Retention capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 38. Third-Party LLM Deletion

Deletion must address the complete processing chain.

A deletion request may involve:

```text
Source Data
   |
   +--> Prompt History
   |
   +--> Conversation
   |
   +--> RAG Copy
   |
   +--> Vector Embedding
   |
   +--> Memory
   |
   +--> Logs
   |
   +--> Fine-Tuning Dataset
   |
   +--> Model Artifact
   |
   +--> Backup
   |
   +--> Provider Systems
```

Deleting an application record does not necessarily prove that every provider-controlled artifact has been deleted.

Therefore:

**Application deletion ≠ complete provider deletion.**

Deletion processes should identify which artifacts are covered, which are retained for legitimate reasons, and how deletion is verified.

---

### 39. Third-Party LLM Backup Privacy

Provider backups can create additional privacy challenges.

Information may remain in:

* backup snapshots;
* disaster-recovery environments;
* replicated storage;
* archival systems;
* immutable logs;
* or geographically redundant infrastructure.

The organization should understand:

* backup retention;
* deletion mechanisms;
* restoration behavior;
* geographic replication;
* access controls;
* and provider commitments concerning deletion.

Backup retention should not silently defeat the organization's approved retention policy.

---

### 40. Third-Party LLM Provider Privacy

Provider privacy assessment should evaluate more than the provider's public privacy statement.

The organization should assess:

* service architecture;
* data-use practices;
* training practices;
* retention;
* logging;
* subprocessors;
* geographic processing;
* security controls;
* administrative access;
* support access;
* incident notification;
* deletion;
* rights support;
* auditability;
* contractual commitments;
* and configuration capabilities.

The assessment should distinguish between:

**What the provider can technically do** and **what the provider is contractually and operationally authorized to do.**

---

### 41. Third-Party LLM Subprocessor Privacy

A provider may rely on subprocessors for:

* cloud infrastructure;
* storage;
* content moderation;
* analytics;
* support;
* security;
* monitoring;
* model hosting;
* or other operational functions.

The organization must understand:

* who the subprocessors are;
* what data they receive;
* why they receive it;
* where they operate;
* what controls apply;
* how changes are communicated;
* and what rights the organization has to object or respond to material changes.

Therefore:

**Provider ≠ only the company named in the contract.**

The effective processing chain may include multiple organizations.

---

### 42. Third-Party LLM Provider Data Use

Provider data use must be explicitly understood.

Possible uses include:

| Provider Use        | Privacy Question                               |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Service delivery    | Is it necessary for the contracted service?    |
| Security monitoring | What data is collected and retained?           |
| Abuse prevention    | What information is inspected?                 |
| Analytics           | Is additional processing performed?            |
| Product improvement | Is customer data reused?                       |
| Model training      | Is customer data incorporated into training?   |
| Human review        | Who can access the data?                       |
| Support             | Can support personnel access customer content? |
| Benchmarking        | Is data aggregated or retained?                |

A broad statement such as "we may process information to provide and improve services" should trigger detailed review rather than automatic approval.

---

### 43. Third-Party LLM Confidentiality

Confidentiality means that information submitted to the provider remains accessible only to authorized parties.

Confidentiality controls should address:

* provider employees;
* provider administrators;
* subprocessors;
* other customers;
* support personnel;
* compromised accounts;
* compromised APIs;
* unauthorized applications;
* and accidental disclosure.

Confidentiality should be enforced technically and contractually.

---

### 44. Third-Party LLM Data-Subject Rights

When personal data is processed through a third-party LLM, the organization may need provider cooperation to fulfill applicable data-subject rights.

Relevant operations may include:

* access;
* rectification;
* erasure;
* restriction;
* objection;
* portability;
* and rights related to automated decision-making where applicable.

The organization should know:

* how requests are identified;
* where AI-related data is stored;
* how provider data can be searched;
* how derived data is handled;
* how deletion is performed;
* and what evidence proves completion.

A provider relationship should therefore support, rather than obstruct, the organization's privacy obligations.

---

### 45. Third-Party LLM Automated Decision-Making

Third-party LLMs may participate in decisions affecting individuals.

Examples include:

* candidate screening;
* fraud detection;
* customer eligibility;
* credit-related analysis;
* employee assessment;
* insurance-related evaluation;
* customer prioritization;
* or access decisions.

The provider's model capability does not determine the organization's decision authority.

Therefore:

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

The organization must determine:

* what decision is being made;
* who is legally responsible;
* what human oversight exists;
* whether the decision has significant effects;
* what data is used;
* how the decision can be challenged;
* and whether the third-party LLM's output is merely advisory or actually controls the decision.

---

### 46. Third-Party LLM Profiling

Third-party LLMs can contribute to profiling by:

* classifying individuals;
* generating inferred attributes;
* ranking users;
* identifying behavioral patterns;
* summarizing histories;
* predicting preferences;
* or producing risk scores.

Profiling governance must address:

* purpose;
* data;
* inference;
* accuracy;
* fairness;
* transparency;
* authorization;
* retention;
* and downstream decision use.

The organization should not assume that an AI-generated profile is harmless merely because it is probabilistic.

### 47. Third-Party LLM Privacy Risk Assessment

A third-party LLM should undergo privacy risk assessment before being approved for protected data.

Assessment areas should include:

```text
Data
  |
  +--> Classification
  +--> Purpose
  +--> Necessity
  +--> Minimization
  |
Provider
  |
  +--> Processing
  +--> Training
  +--> Retention
  +--> Subprocessors
  +--> Geography
  |
Architecture
  |
  +--> Prompt
  +--> Context
  +--> RAG
  +--> Memory
  +--> Tools
  +--> Agents
  |
Governance
  |
  +--> Legal Basis
  +--> Contract

  +--> Incident Response
  |
Assurance
  |

  +--> Monitoring
  +--> Evidence
```

The assessment should be risk-based rather than based solely on provider reputation.

---

### 48. Third-Party LLM Privacy Testing

Privacy controls must be tested against realistic provider behavior and system configurations.

Testing may include:

* unauthorized prompt transmission;
* sensitive-data detection;
* redaction bypass;
* cross-user leakage;
* cross-tenant leakage;
* RAG authorization failure;
* memory leakage;
* provider retention verification;
* deletion verification;
* output leakage;
* logging exposure;
* geographic-processing validation;
* configuration drift;
* and provider API behavior.

Testing should verify that documented controls actually operate as intended.

Therefore:

**Documentation ≠ technical enforcement.**

---

### 49. Enterprise Third-Party LLM Privacy Principle

Enterprise third-party LLM privacy should establish the provider as an **external processing boundary subject to enterprise privacy governance**, not as an uncontrolled extension of ordinary user activity.

The organization should maintain explicit control over:

**Purpose → Data → Necessity → Classification → Authorization → Provider → Transmission → Processing → RAG/Memory/Training/Tools/Agents → Subprocessors → Geography → Retention → Output → Rights → Deletion → Monitoring → Reassessment.**

Every material provider capability should be evaluated against the organization's actual authorization requirements.

---

### 50. Core Principle of Third-Party LLM Privacy

The core principle is:

> **A third-party LLM must never receive, retain, reuse, transfer, expose, or derive personal or protected information merely because the provider technically has the capability to process it.**

Third-party LLM privacy requires continuous control over the complete external AI processing chain.

The fundamental distinctions are:

**Provider capability ≠ permission to process.**

**Provider capability ≠ permission to train.**

**Cloud hosting ≠ transfer of privacy accountability.**

**Shared responsibility ≠ shared accountability for every obligation.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to transmit data.**

**Data relevance ≠ authorization to process data.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Authentication ≠ authorization.**

**Retrieval capability ≠ retrieval authorization.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Tool capability ≠ data-access authorization.**

**Agent capability ≠ privacy authority.**

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

**Inference ≠ factual truth.**

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Model output ≠ authorized decision.**

**Application deletion ≠ complete provider deletion.**

**Source-data deletion ≠ automatic deletion of every derived AI artifact.**

**Security assurance ≠ privacy compliance.**

**Documentation ≠ technical enforcement.**

**Control implementation ≠ control effectiveness.**

**Testing ≠ permanent privacy assurance.**

Third-party LLM privacy is therefore not a procurement checkbox or a provider-contract exercise. It is an **ongoing enterprise control discipline that governs data before transmission, during external AI processing, after output generation, and throughout the provider relationship.**
