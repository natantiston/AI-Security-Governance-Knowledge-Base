# 14.13 — AI Data Retention

## Part 1 — AI Data Retention Foundations

### 1. Definition of AI Data Retention

AI data retention is the controlled preservation of information created, collected, processed, generated, retrieved, inferred, or stored during the lifecycle of an AI system.

AI retention can apply to:

* prompts;
* conversation history;
* context;
* retrieved documents;
* RAG indexes;
* vector embeddings;
* memory;
* training datasets;
* fine-tuning datasets;
* model artifacts;
* AI-generated outputs;
* profiles;
* automated-decision records;
* audit logs;
* security logs;
* caches;
* backups;
* provider-held data.

Retention governance determines **what must be retained, why it must be retained, for how long, where it may be retained, who may access it, and how it must eventually be deleted or otherwise disposed of**.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Retention Governance

The purpose of AI retention governance is to ensure that AI data remains available only for a justified and authorized period.

Retention governance should balance:

* legal obligations;
* regulatory requirements;
* business requirements;
* security requirements;
* auditability;
* operational requirements;
* privacy principles;
* contractual obligations;
* litigation or legal-hold requirements;
* data-subject rights;
* deletion requirements.

The objective is not to maximize the amount of data retained.

The objective is to establish **controlled, purpose-based, risk-appropriate retention**.

---

### 3. AI Data Retention Lifecycle

AI data retention should be treated as a lifecycle rather than a storage configuration.

A typical lifecycle is:

```text
Collection
    ↓
Classification
    ↓
Purpose Definition
    ↓
Retention Requirement
    ↓
Authorized Storage
    ↓
Access and Use
    ↓
Retention Monitoring
    ↓
Retention Expiration
    ↓
Deletion / Disposal / Anonymization
    ↓
Verification
    ↓
Evidence
```

The lifecycle should apply across the AI application's primary data store and relevant downstream systems.

A retention policy is incomplete if it defines a retention period but does not define what happens when that period expires.

---

### 4. Retention versus Storage

**Storage** describes where and how data is technically kept.

**Retention** describes the authorized period and purpose for keeping that data.

For example:

* a database provides storage;
* a retention policy determines how long records may remain in that database;
* a deletion workflow removes records after expiration.

An AI system may technically be capable of storing information indefinitely while governance permits retention for only 30 days.

Therefore:

**Storage capability ≠ retention authorization.**

---

### 5. Retention versus Availability

Retained data does not necessarily need to remain immediately available to operational users.

Organizations may use:

* active storage;
* archival storage;
* restricted repositories;
* encrypted archives;
* cold storage;
* immutable compliance repositories.

Availability should be proportionate to the purpose.

For example, an audit record may need to be retained for compliance purposes without remaining available to ordinary AI users.

Therefore:

**Retention ≠ unrestricted availability.**

---

### 6. Retention Purpose

Every retention period should have an identifiable purpose.

Examples include:

* fulfilling a contractual obligation;
* maintaining an audit trail;
* investigating security incidents;
* satisfying regulatory requirements;
* supporting financial records;
* preserving legally required records;
* enabling limited operational continuity;
* supporting approved model evaluation;
* documenting an automated decision;
* responding to a legitimate dispute.

A vague purpose such as "future AI improvement" should not automatically justify indefinite retention.

---

### 7. Retention Legal and Policy Requirements

AI retention requirements can arise from multiple sources:

* privacy law;
* sector regulations;
* employment requirements;
* financial regulations;
* contractual obligations;
* litigation requirements;
* information-security policies;
* records-management policies;
* incident-response requirements;
* internal AI governance standards.

Different data types may therefore have different retention requirements.

An organization should maintain a traceable relationship between:

**Data Type → Purpose → Requirement → Retention Period → Disposal Rule.**

---

### 8. Personal Data Retention

Personal data processed by an AI system should not be retained indefinitely merely because the system can store it.

Examples include:

* names;
* email addresses;
* employee identifiers;
* customer records;
* account information;
* device identifiers;
* conversation content;
* behavioral information;
* inferred attributes.

Retention should be evaluated according to the applicable purpose and requirements.

Where the purpose ends, continued retention should have a separately justified basis.

---

### 9. Sensitive Data Retention

Sensitive information requires stronger retention governance because prolonged storage can increase the consequences of:

* unauthorized access;
* accidental disclosure;
* insider misuse;
* provider compromise;
* ransomware;
* data leakage;
* inappropriate AI reuse.

Retention controls should therefore consider both:

**how long the information is retained**

and

**how much exposure exists during that period.**

Longer retention increases the duration over which security controls must remain effective.

---

### 10. Special-Category Data Retention

Special-category personal data may require particularly careful retention analysis.

Examples can include information relating to:

* health;
* biometric information in applicable contexts;
* genetic information;
* political opinions;
* religious or philosophical beliefs;
* trade-union membership;
* sexual orientation.

Retention should not be justified solely because the data was legitimately collected.

The organization must separately consider whether continued retention remains necessary and permitted for the relevant purpose.

---

### 11. Prompt Retention

Prompts may contain personal, confidential, proprietary, regulated, or security-sensitive information.

AI platforms may retain prompts in:

* conversation databases;
* application logs;
* provider systems;
* observability platforms;
* debugging systems;
* caches;
* analytics platforms;
* security-monitoring systems.

Therefore, prompt retention must be explicitly governed.

A prompt containing sensitive information should not automatically inherit an unlimited retention period simply because the application stores conversation history.

---

### 12. Conversation Retention

Conversation histories may contain substantial amounts of accumulated information.

A single conversation can contain:

```text
User Identity
     ↓
Prompt
     ↓
Personal Information
     ↓
Retrieved Context
     ↓
AI Output
     ↓
Follow-up Information
     ↓
Additional Personal Information
```

Over time, conversation retention can create a detailed record of an individual's activities, interests, decisions, and interactions.

Retention controls should therefore distinguish between:

* active conversation;
* historical conversation;
* archived conversation;
* security records;
* legally preserved records.

---

### 13. Context Retention

AI systems may retain context beyond the immediate request.

Context can exist in:

* session state;
* application state;
* temporary databases;
* caches;
* agent state;
* orchestration systems;
* workflow engines.

Context should have an explicit lifecycle.

Temporary context should not silently become permanent context.

Therefore:

**Context availability ≠ permission to retain context.**

---

### 14. RAG Data Retention

Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems can retain source information in multiple layers:

* original documents;
* document-processing repositories;
* extracted text;
* chunks;
* metadata;
* embeddings;
* vector indexes;
* retrieval caches;
* generated citations;
* query history.

Deleting the original document may therefore not automatically remove every representation derived from it.

A RAG retention architecture should account for the complete representation chain.

```text
Source Document
      ↓
Extraction
      ↓
Chunking
      ↓
Metadata
      ↓
Embedding
      ↓
Vector Index
      ↓
Retrieval Cache
      ↓
AI Context
      ↓
Generated Output
```

---

### 15. Vector Store Retention

Vector embeddings are derived representations of information.

An embedding should not automatically be considered outside retention requirements merely because it is represented numerically.

Retention analysis should consider:

* whether the embedding remains linked to an identifiable person;
* whether the source information remains recoverable;
* whether metadata identifies the source;
* whether the embedding can support retrieval;
* whether deletion of the source requires removal of the embedding;
* whether the vector database has independent backups.

Therefore:

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous data.**

---

### 16. Memory Retention

AI memory mechanisms can retain information across conversations or sessions.

Memory may include:

* user preferences;
* facts;
* previous interactions;
* behavioral patterns;
* inferred information;
* application state;
* agent state.

Memory should have:

* a defined purpose;
* an authorized scope;
* an appropriate retention period;
* access controls;
* deletion mechanisms;
* monitoring;
* auditability.

The fact that an AI system has a technical memory capability does not establish a right to retain information.

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

---

### 17. Training Data Retention

Training datasets may be retained for:

* reproducibility;
* regulatory evidence;
* model development;
* evaluation;
* retraining;
* audit;
* provenance;
* quality assurance.

However, retaining a dataset indefinitely creates continuing exposure.

Training-data retention should therefore consider:

* source;
* purpose;
* personal-data content;
* licensing;
* provenance;
* legal requirements;
* sensitivity;
* model dependencies;
* deletion requirements;
* downstream copies.

---

### 18. Fine-Tuning Data Retention

Fine-tuning datasets may contain specialized organizational or personal information.

Retention may occur in:

* raw datasets;
* preprocessing pipelines;
* cleaned datasets;
* training manifests;
* experiment repositories;
* temporary files;
* checkpoints;
* evaluation datasets;
* backup systems.

Retention governance should cover the complete fine-tuning pipeline rather than only the final dataset.

---

### 19. Model Artifact Retention

AI model artifacts can include:

* model weights;
* checkpoints;
* adapters;
* LoRA artifacts;
* tokenizer configurations;
* model metadata;
* evaluation artifacts;
* deployment packages.

Model artifacts may remain relevant to retention governance when they incorporate or depend on retained training information.

Model lifecycle governance should therefore define:

* artifact retention;
* version retention;
* archival;
* retirement;
* deletion;
* dependency management.

Model artifacts should not be treated as automatically outside data-retention requirements.

---

### 20. AI Output Retention

AI-generated outputs may themselves become records.

Examples include:

* generated reports;
* recommendations;
* customer communications;
* employment-related assessments;
* risk scores;
* automated-decision explanations;
* generated code;
* investigation results.

Retention requirements may differ depending on whether an output is:

* temporary;
* operational;
* advisory;
* legally significant;
* part of an automated decision;
* part of an audit trail;
* part of a contractual record.

Therefore, output retention should be determined by its role, not merely by the fact that it was generated by AI.

---

### 21. Profile Retention

AI profiling can create derived information about individuals.

Profiles may contain:

* risk classifications;
* behavioral categories;
* preferences;
* inferred interests;
* segmentation attributes;
* eligibility assessments;
* predicted characteristics.

Profile retention should be separately evaluated from retention of the underlying source data.

Deleting a source record does not necessarily mean that every derived profile has automatically been deleted.

Conversely, derived data should not automatically be retained simply because the source data remains available.

---

### 22. Decision Record Retention

AI-supported or automated decisions may require records showing:

* decision inputs;
* model version;
* decision logic;
* applicable thresholds;
* relevant context;
* human review;
* final decision;
* decision timestamp;
* responsible authority.

Retention may be necessary for:

* accountability;
* appeals;
* disputes;
* audits;
* regulatory review;
* incident investigation.

Decision records should be protected against unauthorized modification while retained.

---

### 23. Log Retention

AI environments may generate multiple types of logs:

* authentication logs;
* authorization logs;
* prompt logs;
* inference logs;
* tool-use logs;
* agent-action logs;
* retrieval logs;
* security logs;
* administrative logs;
* configuration-change logs.

Logging does not create a blanket exemption from retention requirements.

Each log category should have an appropriate:

* purpose;
* retention period;
* access model;
* protection level;
* deletion process.

Therefore:

**Security logging ≠ blanket retention exemption.**

---

### 24. Cache Retention

AI applications can temporarily cache:

* prompts;
* context;
* retrieved documents;
* embeddings;
* model responses;
* authentication information;
* session state.

Caches are frequently overlooked because they are considered temporary.

However, temporary storage still constitutes storage and can create privacy and security exposure.

A retention design should therefore specify:

* cache lifetime;
* invalidation triggers;
* maximum retention;
* sensitive-data restrictions;
* deletion behavior;
* backup behavior.

---

### 25. Backup Retention

Backups can contain copies of AI data that has already expired in primary systems.

Examples include:

* database backups;
* object-storage snapshots;
* disaster-recovery replicas;
* immutable backups;
* provider-managed backups.

Backup retention should therefore be explicitly governed.

The existence of a backup does not automatically authorize indefinite retention of the underlying information.

**Backup existence ≠ unlimited retention permission.**

---

### 26. Provider Retention

Third-party AI providers may independently retain:

* prompts;
* outputs;
* metadata;
* telemetry;
* abuse-monitoring records;
* support information;
* account information.

The organization's retention policy must account for provider behavior.

Contractual and technical controls should establish:

* provider retention periods;
* permitted purposes;
* deletion mechanisms;
* backup treatment;
* training-use restrictions;
* geographic storage;
* subprocessors;
* deletion evidence where required.

**Provider retention ≠ enterprise authorization.**

---

### 27. Third-Party AI Retention

AI services can introduce retention outside the organization's direct infrastructure.

Examples include:

* SaaS AI assistants;
* hosted LLM APIs;
* managed RAG services;
* external vector databases;
* AI observability platforms;
* AI security services.

The organization should maintain visibility over the retention characteristics of each relevant provider.

Shadow AI creates particular risk because users may unknowingly submit organizational information to systems with unknown retention periods.

---

### 28. Cross-Border Retention

AI data may be stored in jurisdictions different from the location where it was collected or used.

Cross-border retention may occur through:

* cloud regions;
* provider backups;
* disaster-recovery sites;
* support systems;
* subprocessors;
* centralized logging platforms.

Retention governance must therefore consider both:

**how long the data is retained**

and

**where it is retained during that period.**

A valid retention period does not automatically authorize storage in every jurisdiction.

---

### 29. Data Residency and Retention

Data residency defines where data is stored or processed.

Retention governance should map retention requirements to residency requirements.

For example:

```text
Data Classification
       ↓
Purpose
       ↓
Retention Period
       ↓
Permitted Geography
       ↓
Storage Location
       ↓
Backup Location
       ↓
Deletion Verification
```

A system should not satisfy a retention requirement while violating geographic restrictions applicable to the data.

---

### 30. Purpose-Based Retention

Retention should be connected to the purpose for which information is being retained.

For example:

| Data             | Purpose            | Retention Consideration                    |
| ---------------- | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Prompt           | Service delivery   | Short operational period                   |
| Security log     | Incident detection | Defined security-retention period          |
| Decision record  | Accountability     | Period required for applicable obligations |
| Training dataset | Model development  | Defined development lifecycle              |
| Memory           | Personalization    | Limited to justified purpose               |
| Backup           | Recovery           | Defined disaster-recovery lifecycle        |

The exact period must be established through the organization's applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and business requirements.

---

### 31. Data Minimization and Retention

Data minimization and retention are closely connected.

Minimization asks:

> Do we need to collect and process this data?

Retention asks:

> Do we still need to keep this data?

The two controls operate at different lifecycle stages.

```text
Minimization
    ↓
Collect Less
    ↓
Process Less
    ↓
Retain Only What Is Justified
    ↓
Delete When No Longer Required
```

An organization that minimizes collection but retains information indefinitely has not fully minimized its data lifecycle.

---

### 32. Retention Period Determination

A retention period should be determined through a documented analysis rather than an arbitrary technical setting.

Factors can include:

* processing purpose;
* legal requirements;
* contractual requirements;
* regulatory requirements;
* operational necessity;
* security requirements;
* audit requirements;
* litigation requirements;
* data sensitivity;
* individual impact;
* risk of misuse.

The retention period should be **defensible and traceable**.

---

### 33. Retention Trigger

A retention period needs a clearly defined starting point.

Possible triggers include:

* date of collection;
* end of customer relationship;
* completion of transaction;
* end of AI session;
* creation of a record;
* final decision;
* termination of employment;
* closure of an incident;
* completion of a project.

Without a defined trigger, a nominal retention period may be impossible to enforce consistently.

---

### 34. Retention Expiration

Retention expiration occurs when the authorized retention period ends.

At expiration, the system should determine whether the data must be:

* deleted;
* securely destroyed;
* anonymized where appropriate;
* archived under a separate justified requirement;
* placed under a valid legal hold;
* retained under another explicitly documented purpose.

Expiration should trigger an actionable lifecycle event.

---

### 35. Retention Extension

Retention should not be extended automatically merely because data might be useful later.

An extension should have:

* a documented reason;
* an authorized owner;
* a defined new expiration date;
* appropriate legal or policy justification;
* traceable evidence.

Repeated extensions without meaningful review can effectively create indefinite retention.

Therefore:

**Retention extension ≠ automatic permanent retention.**

---

### 36. Legal Hold and AI Data

A legal hold may suspend ordinary deletion for information relevant to litigation or another legally recognized preservation requirement.

AI environments can complicate legal holds because relevant information may exist in:

* prompts;
* conversations;
* logs;
* RAG repositories;
* vector databases;
* memory stores;
* training datasets;
* model-development repositories;
* provider systems;
* backups.

A legal hold should be narrowly scoped and managed under appropriate legal authority.

**Legal hold ≠ unrestricted indefinite retention.**

---

### 37. Deletion after Retention

When the retention period expires, deletion should be technically executed rather than merely documented.

Deletion may require action across:

* primary databases;
* object storage;
* caches;
* vector stores;
* memory systems;
* RAG indexes;
* logs;
* replicas;
* backups;
* provider systems;
* downstream repositories.

The organization should be able to determine whether deletion actually occurred.

Therefore:

**Retention expiry ≠ deletion completion.**

---

### 38. Derived Data Retention

AI systems continuously create derived information.

Examples include:

* embeddings;
* profiles;
* classifications;
* risk scores;
* summaries;
* extracted entities;
* inferred attributes;
* feature vectors.

Derived data requires explicit retention analysis.

The fact that information was transformed does not automatically eliminate its governance obligations.

**Derived data ≠ automatically outside retention requirements.**

---

### 39. Model Memorization and Retention

Model memorization creates a special retention problem.

Traditional deletion removes a stored record.

Model memorization may cause information to exist implicitly within model parameters.

Therefore:

```text
Source Data
     ↓
Training
     ↓
Model Parameters
     ↓
Potential Memorization
     ↓
Inference
     ↓
Potential Reconstruction / Disclosure
```

Model memorization should be assessed separately from ordinary database retention.

Deleting a source dataset does not necessarily prove that information has disappeared from a previously trained model.

**Model memorization ≠ ordinary record retention.**

---

### 40. Retention Risk

AI retention creates risks including:

* unauthorized disclosure;
* excessive accumulation;
* insider misuse;
* ransomware exposure;
* data leakage;
* regulatory noncompliance;
* inappropriate secondary use;
* stale information;
* inaccurate information;
* excessive profiling;
* cross-user exposure;
* cross-tenant exposure;
* provider misuse;
* unauthorized model training.

Risk generally increases when information is retained:

* longer than necessary;
* across more systems;
* across more providers;
* in more jurisdictions;
* with broader access;
* in more derived forms.

---

### 41. Retention Security

Retained AI data must remain protected throughout its authorized lifecycle.

Controls may include:

* encryption;
* key management;
* access control;
* least privilege;
* tenant isolation;
* privileged-access management;
* data-loss prevention;
* monitoring;
* immutable audit records;
* integrity protection;
* secure archival;
* secure deletion.

Security requirements should continue for the entire retention period.

---

### 42. Retention and Data Subject Rights

Data retention can affect the ability to fulfill applicable data-subject rights.

For example, organizations may need to locate information across:

* conversations;
* prompts;
* profiles;
* RAG indexes;
* memory;
* training datasets;
* outputs;
* logs;
* provider systems.

Retention architecture should therefore support appropriate:

* discovery;
* access;
* rectification;
* restriction;
* objection;
* erasure;
* other applicable rights.

Retention should not become a mechanism for making personal information impossible to discover or govern.

---

### 43. Retention and Automated Decisions

Records associated with automated or AI-assisted decisions may require retention to support:

* accountability;
* review;
* appeals;
* dispute resolution;
* audit;
* regulatory investigation.

Relevant evidence may include:

* model version;
* input data;
* decision output;
* threshold;
* policy version;
* human review;
* authorization;
* timestamp.

The retention period should be justified by the applicable requirements and risk.

---

### 44. Retention and Profiling

Profiles and inferred attributes may become sensitive over time because they can accumulate information about an individual.

Retention governance should consider:

* profile age;
* continuing necessity;
* accuracy;
* purpose;
* individual impact;
* access;
* downstream use;
* deletion;
* objection or restriction requirements where applicable.

An old profile may become inaccurate while remaining operationally influential.

Therefore:

**Profile retention ≠ profile validity.**

---

### 45. Retention and Incident Management

Incident investigations may require temporary preservation of relevant AI records.

Potential evidence includes:

* prompts;
* outputs;
* access logs;
* retrieval logs;
* tool calls;
* agent actions;
* configuration changes;
* model versions;
* security events.

Incident preservation should be controlled so that investigative retention does not silently become indefinite operational retention.

---

### 46. Retention Monitoring

Retention should be continuously monitored.

Monitoring can identify:

* expired records;
* overdue deletion;
* unexpected retention extensions;
* unauthorized copies;
* provider-retention changes;
* stale backups;
* orphaned vector records;
* expired memory;
* excessive conversation history;
* retention-policy violations.

A mature architecture measures not merely whether a policy exists, but whether actual data lifecycles conform to it.

---

### 47. Retention Testing

Retention controls should be tested periodically.

Testing can verify:

* retention timers;
* expiration triggers;
* deletion workflows;
* cache expiration;
* vector deletion;
* memory deletion;
* backup lifecycle;
* provider deletion;
* legal-hold behavior;
* evidence generation;
* cross-system propagation.

A useful test is to introduce a controlled record and verify that it progresses through the expected lifecycle.

---

### 48. Retention Evidence

Retention governance requires evidence demonstrating that retention requirements are actually implemented.

Evidence can include:

* approved retention policies;
* data inventories;
* retention schedules;
* system configurations;
* deletion logs;
* workflow records;
* provider commitments;
* backup configurations;
* legal-hold records;
* test results;
* exception approvals;
* monitoring reports.

Evidence should demonstrate both:

**policy intent**

and

**technical execution**.

### 49. Enterprise AI Retention Principle

An enterprise AI retention framework should establish a consistent governance chain:

```text
AI Data
  ↓
Classification
  ↓
Purpose
  ↓
Requirement
  ↓
Necessity
  ↓
Retention Period
  ↓
Authorized Storage
  ↓
Access Control
  ↓
Monitoring
  ↓
Expiration
  ↓
Deletion / Authorized Preservation
  ↓
Verification
  ↓
Evidence
```

This chain should operate across AI applications, models, RAG systems, memory, vector stores, providers, logs, backups, and derived artifacts.

The organization should be able to answer:

> **What are we retaining, why are we retaining it, where is it retained, who can access it, when does retention expire, and how do we prove that the required action occurred?**

### 50. Core Principle of AI Data Retention

The fundamental principle of AI data retention is:

> **AI systems must retain information only for a defined, authorized, and justified purpose and period, with appropriate protection throughout retention and a technically enforceable lifecycle for expiration, deletion, or other authorized disposition.**

The critical governance distinctions are:

**Retention ≠ storage.**

**Retention ≠ unrestricted availability.**

**Retention ≠ business convenience.**

**Data availability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Processing purpose ≠ retention authorization.**

**Longer retention ≠ better governance.**

**Backup existence ≠ unlimited retention permission.**

**Derived data ≠ automatically outside retention requirements.**

**Embedding ≠ automatically anonymous.**

**Model memorization ≠ ordinary record retention.**

**Deletion trigger ≠ deletion completion.**

**Retention expiry ≠ automatic deletion unless deletion is technically executed.**

**Legal hold ≠ unrestricted indefinite retention.**

**Provider retention ≠ enterprise authorization.**

**Model capability ≠ retention authority.**

**Memory capability ≠ permission to retain.**

**Security logging ≠ blanket retention exemption.**

**Rights fulfillment ≠ permanent retention authorization.**

## Part 2 — AI Data Retention Controls

### 51. AI Data Retention Control Framework

An AI data retention control framework establishes the preventive, detective, corrective, and assurance controls required to manage information throughout its authorized lifecycle.

The framework should cover:

* prompts;
* conversations;
* context;
* RAG repositories;
* vector stores;
* memory;
* training data;
* fine-tuning data;
* model artifacts;
* outputs;
* profiles;
* decision records;
* logs;
* caches;
* backups;
* provider-held data;
* derived information.

The control objective is to ensure that retention is **purpose-bound, time-bound, protected, monitored, and enforceable**.

---

### 52. AI Data Retention Planning Control

Before implementation, the organization should define:

* retention objectives;
* applicable requirements;
* data categories;
* business purposes;
* retention owners;
* retention periods;
* deletion triggers;
* legal-hold processes;
* provider obligations;
* evidence requirements.

Retention should be designed before the AI system accumulates significant amounts of information.

### 58. AI Data Retention Purpose Control

Every retained dataset or record should have a documented purpose.

The control should prevent generic reasons such as:

* "keep everything";
* "might be useful later";
* "AI improvement";
* "future analytics"

from automatically establishing indefinite retention.

Purpose should be linked to an approved processing activity.

### 117. Core Control Principle

The core control principle is:

> **AI data retention must be technically enforceable, purpose-bound, time-bound, continuously monitored, and verifiable across primary systems, derived representations, AI memory, RAG and vector stores, models, logs, backups, and third-party providers.**

The strongest retention control is not a document stating that data should be deleted.

It is an enforceable lifecycle in which:

**Authorized Retention → Expiration → Deletion → Verification → Evidence**

can be demonstrated across the complete AI data ecosystem.
