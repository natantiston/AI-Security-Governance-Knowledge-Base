# 14.02 — AI and GDPR

## Part 1 — AI and GDPR Foundations

### 1. Definition of AI and GDPR

AI and GDPR governance is the discipline of applying the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation to AI systems that process personal data.

The GDPR does not become irrelevant because processing is performed by an AI model. AI processing must be evaluated within the applicable data-protection framework, including the roles of the parties, purposes of processing, categories of data, processing activities, risks to individuals, and applicable rights and obligations.

---

### 2. Purpose of GDPR Governance for AI

The purpose is to ensure that AI systems processing personal data are designed, deployed, operated, and changed in a manner consistent with applicable GDPR requirements.

The governance objective is broader than preventing data breaches.

It includes:

* Lawfulness
* Fairness
* Transparency
* Purpose limitation
* Data minimization
* Accuracy
* Storage limitation
* Integrity and confidentiality
* Accountability
* Individual rights
* Appropriate safeguards for higher-risk processing

---

### 4. Personal Data under GDPR

Personal data is information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.

In AI environments, this may include:

* Names
* Email addresses
* Telephone numbers
* Identification numbers
* Location information
* Online identifiers
* Employment information
* Customer records
* Voice information
* Images
* Behavioral information
* IP-related information where applicable
* Information contained in free-text prompts

AI architecture should assume that seemingly ordinary business data can contain personal information.

---

### 5. AI Processing of Personal Data

AI can process personal data through multiple mechanisms.

```text id="gdpr14p1-02"
Personal Data
     |
     +--> Prompt
     +--> Context
     +--> Training
     +--> Fine-Tuning
     +--> RAG
     +--> Memory
     +--> Tool
     +--> Output
     +--> Logging
     +--> Monitoring
```

Each processing activity should be identified and evaluated rather than treating the AI system as a single processing operation.

---

### 6. GDPR Processing Concept

Processing under GDPR is broad and includes activities such as:

* Collection
* Recording
* Organization
* Structuring
* Storage
* Retrieval
* Consultation
* Use
* Disclosure
* Combination
* Restriction
* Erasure

AI systems can perform many of these operations simultaneously.

---

### 7. Controller Role

An organization may act as a controller where it determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

For AI systems, controller responsibilities may include determining:

* Why AI processing occurs
* Which personal data is processed
* The purpose of processing
* Which AI service is used
* Relevant processing parameters
* Applicable safeguards

The precise legal role must be assessed for the actual processing arrangement.

---

### 8. Processor Role

An AI provider may operate as a processor where it processes personal data on behalf of a controller and under the applicable contractual and legal framework.

However, the role should not be assumed merely because a provider describes itself as an AI platform.

The actual processing arrangement must be assessed.

---

### 9. Joint Controller Considerations

Some AI arrangements may create circumstances in which multiple organizations determine purposes and means of processing.

Where relevant, governance should assess whether joint-controller responsibilities arise.

AI procurement should therefore examine actual decision-making and processing behavior rather than relying only on product labels.

---

### 10. Subprocessor Considerations

AI providers may use other organizations to support their services.

Potential subprocessors may provide:

* Cloud infrastructure
* Model hosting
* Storage
* Monitoring
* Support
* Security
* Analytics

Organizations should understand relevant subprocessor relationships and applicable contractual and governance requirements.

---

### 11. Lawfulness of AI Processing

AI processing of personal data requires an applicable legal basis under GDPR where GDPR applies.

Potential legal bases include circumstances involving:

* Consent
* Contract
* Legal obligation
* Vital interests
* Public task
* Legitimate interests

The appropriate basis depends on the specific processing circumstances.

AI capability alone does not establish a legal basis.

---

### 12. Consent and AI

Consent, where relied upon, must satisfy applicable GDPR requirements.

AI interfaces should not assume that a general acceptance of an application automatically constitutes valid consent for every possible future AI processing activity.

Consent-related governance should consider:

* Specificity
* Informed nature
* Freely given nature
* Withdrawal
* Evidence
* Scope

---

### 13. Contractual Necessity and AI

Where processing is justified as necessary for a contract, the organization should determine whether the AI processing is genuinely necessary for performing that contract.

The fact that AI makes a process:

* Faster
* Cheaper
* More convenient
* More scalable

does not automatically establish contractual necessity.

---

### 14. Legitimate Interests and AI

Organizations relying on legitimate interests should assess the relevant circumstances and safeguards.

This may involve considering:

### 15. Special Categories of Personal Data

GDPR establishes heightened protection for special categories of personal data.

These include information such as:

* Racial or ethnic origin
* Political opinions
* Religious or philosophical beliefs
* Trade-union membership
* Genetic data
* Biometric data used for unique identification
* Health data
* Sex life or sexual orientation

AI systems should have mechanisms to identify and restrict such information where required.

---

### 16. Criminal-Conviction Data

Information relating to criminal convictions and offences has additional GDPR requirements.

AI systems processing such information should therefore receive appropriate legal, privacy, security, and governance scrutiny.

---

### 18. Lawfulness, Fairness and Transparency

AI processing should be lawful, fair, and transparent.

This creates challenges where individuals may not understand:

* That AI is being used
* What data is processed
* Why it is processed
* How outputs influence decisions
* Who receives information
* How long information is retained

AI complexity should not eliminate meaningful transparency.

---

### 19. Purpose Limitation

Personal data should be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not processed in ways incompatible with those purposes, subject to the applicable legal framework.

This is particularly important for AI because existing datasets can easily be reused for:

* Training
* Fine-tuning
* Prompt enrichment
* RAG
* Profiling
* Analytics
* Model evaluation

Existing access to data does not automatically establish unrestricted permission for AI reuse.

---

### 20. Data Minimization

Personal data processed by AI should be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose.

For example, an AI customer-service application may require:

```text id="gdpr14p1-05"
Customer ID
Order Number
Issue Description
       |
       v
AI Support Model
```

It may not require unrestricted access to the customer's complete historical profile.

---

### 21. Accuracy

Personal data processed by AI should be accurate and, where necessary, kept up to date.

AI creates additional challenges because models may:

* Generate incorrect information
* Combine records incorrectly
* Infer inaccurate attributes
* Preserve outdated information in memory
* Retrieve stale records

Organizations should distinguish between **model-generated content** and authoritative personal-data records.

---

### 22. AI Hallucination and Personal Data

AI-generated false information can create privacy and fairness risks when it is presented as information about an identifiable person.

Examples include falsely generating:

* Criminal allegations
* Employment history
* Medical information
* Financial information
* Professional qualifications
* Personal relationships

Accuracy controls should therefore consider both stored personal data and AI-generated statements about individuals.

---

### 23. Storage Limitation

Personal data should not be retained longer than necessary for the applicable purpose.

AI systems may unintentionally create multiple persistent copies:

```text id="gdpr14p1-06"

 |
 +--> Logs
 |
 +--> RAG Store
 |
 +--> Vector Index
 |
 +--> Provider
 |
 +--> Backup
```

Storage limitation must therefore be considered across the entire architecture.

---

### 24. Integrity and Confidentiality

Personal data should be protected against unauthorized or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction, or damage using appropriate technical and organizational measures.

AI-specific security controls can include:

* Encryption
* Access control
* Tenant isolation
* DLP
* Secure APIs
* Memory isolation
* Retrieval authorization
* Monitoring
* Incident response

---

### 25. Accountability

The organization should be able to demonstrate compliance with applicable GDPR requirements.

AI accountability therefore requires evidence such as:

* Processing records
* Assessments
* Policies
* Data-flow maps
* Contracts
* Risk assessments
* Testing
* Approvals
* Incident records
* Rights-request records
* Deletion evidence

---

### 26. Records of Processing Activities

Where applicable, AI processing should be represented in the organization's records of processing activities.

Records should accurately reflect relevant:

* Purposes
* Categories of individuals
* Categories of personal data
* Recipients
* Transfers
* Retention
* Security measures

AI systems should not operate outside the organization's privacy inventory merely because they were introduced through an engineering or procurement process.

---

### 27. Data Protection Officer

Where an organization has a Data Protection Officer under applicable GDPR requirements, the DPO should have appropriate involvement in relevant AI privacy governance.

The DPO role should remain appropriately independent.

AI development teams should not replace the DPO's governance responsibilities.

---

### 28. Data Protection by Design

GDPR requires data protection to be incorporated into appropriate processing design.

For AI, this can include:

* Data minimization
* Privacy-preserving architecture
* Access control
* Isolation
* Encryption
* Retention controls
* Deletion mechanisms
* Privacy testing
* Transparency mechanisms

Privacy should be an architectural property rather than a documentation exercise.

---

### 29. Data Protection by Default

AI systems should use privacy-protective defaults where appropriate.

Examples include:

* Minimum data collection
* Restricted sharing
* Limited retention
* Disabled unnecessary memory
* Restricted provider training use
* Restricted logging
* Least-privilege retrieval
* Tenant isolation

Users should not have to manually configure every privacy protection.

---

### 30. Data Protection Impact Assessment

A Data Protection Impact Assessment, or DPIA, is required under GDPR where processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, subject to the applicable GDPR requirements.

AI systems may trigger DPIA consideration because of characteristics such as:

* Large-scale processing
* Systematic monitoring
* Profiling
* Automated decision-making
* Sensitive data
* Novel technologies
* Significant effects on individuals

---

### 32. Necessity and Proportionality

AI processing should be evaluated for necessity and proportionality where required.

The organization should ask:

* Is personal data necessary?
* Is AI necessary?
* Is this amount of data necessary?
* Is this level of automation necessary?
* Is this retention period necessary?
* Is this provider necessary?

The availability of a technically capable AI solution does not itself establish necessity.

---

Potential harms include:

* Reputational harm

* Incorrect decisions
* Surveillance
* Security compromise

### 36. AI Breach and Personal-Data Breach

An AI security incident may constitute a personal-data breach when personal data is affected in a manner falling within the GDPR definition.

Potential AI-related examples include:

* Cross-tenant leakage
* Unauthorized model output
* Prompt exposure
* RAG disclosure
* Memory leakage
* Provider compromise
* Lost datasets
* Unauthorized access to AI logs

Incident classification should therefore connect AI security operations with privacy processes.

---

### 37. Data Protection Impact Assessment and Change

A previously completed privacy assessment may need review when AI processing materially changes.

Examples include:

* New model
* New provider
* New dataset
* New purpose
* New RAG source
* New memory capability
* New agent
* Increased autonomy
* Expanded geographic processing
* Expanded data access

### 38. Automated Decision-Making

GDPR contains specific provisions concerning automated individual decision-making, including profiling.

AI systems should identify whether they:

* Make decisions
* Materially influence decisions
* Recommend decisions
* Rank individuals
* Score individuals
* Profile individuals

The legal treatment depends on the actual processing and applicable circumstances.

---

### 39. Meaningful Human Involvement

### 40. Profiling and AI

Profiling involves automated processing of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to an individual.

AI can perform profiling using:

* Behavioral data
* Transaction data
* Communications
* Location
* Preferences
* Employment information
* Online activity

Profiling should therefore be explicitly identified in privacy governance.

### 41. Data Subject Rights

AI systems must be architected to support applicable GDPR rights.

Depending on the circumstances, these may include:

* Access
* Rectification
* Erasure
* Restriction
* Data portability
* Objection
* Rights relating to automated decision-making

The technical implementation depends on the specific processing activity and applicable legal requirements.

---

### 42. Right of Access

Where applicable, an individual may have rights to obtain information about processing and access their personal data.

AI systems create challenges because information may exist across:

* Applications
* Conversations
* Memory
* RAG
* Databases
* Logs
* Providers
* Backups

---

### 43. Right to Rectification

Where applicable, individuals may have the right to obtain rectification of inaccurate personal data.

AI architecture should distinguish authoritative records from generated content and derived representations.

---

### 44. Right to Erasure

Where applicable, individuals may have the right to obtain erasure of personal data.

AI systems create additional technical considerations because information can exist in:

* Databases
* Memory
* RAG repositories
* Vector stores
* Logs
* Backups
* Provider systems
* Other derived artifacts

---

### 45. Right to Restriction

Where applicable, individuals may have the right to obtain restriction of processing.

AI architectures should therefore be capable of preventing affected data from continuing to enter prohibited processing workflows.

---

### 46. Right to Object

Where applicable, individuals may have the right to object to certain processing activities, including certain forms of direct marketing and processing based on legitimate interests or tasks carried out in the public interest, subject to GDPR conditions.

AI governance should provide mechanisms to capture and enforce applicable objections.

---

### 47. Data Portability

Where applicable, the right to data portability can require personal data to be provided in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format and transmitted under the applicable conditions.

AI architectures should consider how relevant personal data can be identified and exported when the right applies.

---

### 48. Transparency in AI Processing

Transparency should provide individuals with meaningful information about relevant AI processing.

Depending on the circumstances, this may include information about:

* Identity of the controller
* Purpose
* Categories of data
* Recipients
* Retention
* Rights
* Automated decision-making
* Relevant safeguards

Transparency should be understandable rather than merely technically accurate.

---

### 49. AI GDPR Governance Principle

GDPR governance for AI must connect legal obligations to technical architecture.

       |

       |

---

### 50. Core Principle of AI and GDPR

**AI does not create an exemption from GDPR. Where GDPR applies, AI processing of personal data must be governed according to the applicable data-protection requirements, with privacy obligations integrated into the AI system's architecture, lifecycle, security controls, risk management, and operational processes.**

The enterprise must evaluate the complete processing chain:

**Data → Purpose → Legal Basis → AI Processing → Access → Retrieval → Memory → Model → Output → Recipients → Retention → Rights → Deletion.**

The fundamental distinctions remain:

**AI capability ≠ lawful basis.**

**Data access ≠ authorization to process.**

**Technical feasibility ≠ necessity.**

**Security control ≠ GDPR compliance.**

**AI output ≠ authoritative personal-data record.**

**Model capability ≠ decision authority.**

**Human presence ≠ meaningful human involvement.**

**Transparency ≠ meaningful transparency.**

**DPIA ≠ generic AI risk assessment.**

**Risk assessment ≠ legal authorization.**

**Privacy documentation ≠ privacy control effectiveness.**

**GDPR compliance ≠ one-time assessment.**

**Passing a privacy review ≠ permanent privacy assurance.**
