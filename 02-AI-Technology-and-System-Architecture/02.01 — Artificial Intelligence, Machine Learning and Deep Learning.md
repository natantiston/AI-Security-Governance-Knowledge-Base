# 02.01 — Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning and Deep Learning

## Part 1 — Foundations, Definitions, Relationships and Core Concepts

### 1. Introduction to Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the broad field of computing concerned with building systems capable of performing tasks that traditionally require aspects of human intelligence.

These tasks may include:

* Perception
* Pattern recognition
* Prediction
* Classification
* Language understanding
* Decision support
* Planning
* Reasoning
* Content generation
* Problem solving
* Autonomous action

AI is not a single technology. It is an umbrella term covering multiple technical approaches, architectures, algorithms, models, and systems.

For a GRC professional, this distinction is important because governance requirements can differ substantially depending on the type of AI technology being used.

---

### 2. AI as a Technology Domain

AI should be understood as a technology domain rather than a single software product.

An enterprise AI environment can contain:

* Machine-learning models
* Deep-learning models
* Foundation models
* Large language models
* Computer-vision systems
* Speech systems
* Recommendation engines
* Predictive analytics
* Generative AI applications
* AI agents
* AI-enabled business applications

Each can introduce different:

* Security risks
* Data requirements
* Operational dependencies
* Governance requirements
* Regulatory considerations

Therefore, an AI governance program should not treat every AI system as technically identical.

---

### 3. Artificial Intelligence as a System Capability

AI can be viewed from two perspectives.

The first is the **technology perspective**:

> AI is a collection of computational methods used to perform tasks associated with intelligent behavior.

The second is the **system perspective**:

> An AI system is a combination of models, data, software, infrastructure, interfaces, processes, and users that collectively produce AI-enabled outcomes.

The second perspective is particularly important for GRC.

A model by itself is rarely the complete enterprise AI system.

---

### 4. AI Model Versus AI System

An **AI model** is a computational artifact capable of processing inputs and producing outputs according to what it learned or was configured to do.

An **AI system** is broader.

It may contain:

```text
Users
   ↓
Application
   ↓
AI Interface
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Data
   ↓
Infrastructure
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Enterprise Systems
```

For example, an LLM is a model.

A customer-service chatbot using that LLM, organizational data, authentication, APIs, monitoring, and business workflows is an AI system.

This distinction is fundamental to AI Security Governance.

---

### 5. Artificial Intelligence and Automation

AI and traditional automation are related but not identical.

Traditional automation generally follows explicitly defined rules.

For example:

```text
IF invoice amount > €10,000
THEN send for approval
```

The behavior is directly specified by the developer.

AI systems may instead learn patterns from data or use trained models to produce predictions, classifications, or generated outputs.

For example:

```text
Invoice
   ↓
AI Model
   ↓
Risk Classification
   ↓
Recommended Action
```

The distinction matters because AI behavior can depend on training data, model parameters, prompts, context, and inference conditions.

---

### 6. Rule-Based Systems

Rule-based systems represent one of the earlier approaches associated with AI.

They use explicit rules such as:

```text
IF condition A
AND condition B
THEN perform action C
```

Examples include:

* Expert systems
* Decision engines
* Rule-based classification
* Fraud rules
* Eligibility engines

Although rule-based systems may be considered part of the broader history of AI, they operate differently from modern machine-learning systems.

---

### 7. Machine Learning

Machine Learning (ML) is a major subfield of AI.

Instead of programming every decision explicitly, machine-learning systems use data and algorithms to learn patterns that can be used to make predictions or decisions.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Artificial Intelligence
        ↓
Machine Learning
        ↓
Deep Learning
```

This hierarchy is useful conceptually, although AI also includes approaches that are not machine learning.

---

### 8. Machine Learning as Pattern Learning

Machine learning allows systems to learn relationships from examples.

For example, suppose an organization wants to detect fraudulent transactions.

A traditional rule might be:

```text
IF transaction > €10,000
THEN flag as suspicious
```

A machine-learning system could instead analyze historical transaction data and learn patterns associated with fraud.

It might consider:

* Transaction amount
* Location
* Time
* Device
* Account history
* Transaction frequency
* Merchant
* Behavioral patterns

The model then produces a prediction or risk score.

---

### 9. Machine Learning Inputs and Outputs

A simplified machine-learning system can be represented as:

```text
Input Data
    ↓
Machine-Learning Model
    ↓
Prediction / Classification
    ↓
Decision or Action
```

The output could be:

* A class
* A probability
* A numerical prediction
* A recommendation
* An anomaly score
* A ranking

The output may subsequently be consumed by another application or human decision-maker.

---

### 10. Machine Learning Model

A machine-learning model represents patterns learned from data.

During training, an algorithm adjusts model parameters to reduce some form of error or optimize an objective.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Learning Algorithm
      ↓
Model Parameters
      ↓
Trained Model
```

The trained model can then be used during inference.

---

### 11. Training and Inference

Two fundamental concepts in machine learning are **training** and **inference**.

**Training** is the process through which a model learns from data.

**Inference** is the process through which the trained model processes new input and generates an output.

Conceptually:

```text
TRAINING

Data
 ↓
Learning Process
 ↓
Model
```

and later:

```text
INFERENCE

New Input
 ↓
Trained Model
 ↓
Output
```

These two phases have different security and governance requirements.

---

### 12. Deep Learning

Deep Learning is a specialized area of machine learning based primarily on multi-layer neural networks.

A simplified hierarchy is:

```text
Artificial Intelligence
        ↓
Machine Learning
        ↓
Deep Learning
        ↓
Deep Neural Networks
```

Deep learning has become particularly important because it enables highly capable systems for:

* Image recognition
* Speech recognition
* Natural-language processing
* Generative AI
* Computer vision
* Multimodal AI

---

### 13. Neural Networks

A neural network is a computational architecture composed of interconnected processing units commonly referred to as neurons.

A simplified structure is:

```text
Input Layer
     ↓
Hidden Layer
     ↓
Hidden Layer
     ↓
Output Layer
```

Modern neural networks may contain many layers and very large numbers of parameters.

The term **deep learning** generally refers to neural networks with multiple layers capable of learning increasingly complex representations.

---

### 14. Why Deep Learning Became Important

Deep learning became increasingly powerful because of improvements in:

* Computing power
* Graphics processing units
* Specialized AI accelerators
* Training algorithms
* Data availability
* Distributed computing
* Model architectures

These developments enabled models to learn highly complex patterns from large datasets.

This technological progression ultimately contributed to modern foundation models and generative AI.

---

### 15. AI, ML and Deep Learning Relationship

The relationship can be summarized as:

```text
AI
│
├── Rule-Based AI
│
├── Search and Planning
│
├── Expert Systems
│
└── Machine Learning
      │
      ├── Supervised Learning
      ├── Unsupervised Learning
      ├── Reinforcement Learning
      │
      └── Deep Learning
            │
            ├── Neural Networks
            ├── Transformers
            ├── Vision Models
            └── Large Language Models
```

This hierarchy provides a useful conceptual model for GRC professionals.

---

### 16. Artificial Intelligence Is Broader Than Machine Learning

It is incorrect to treat AI and machine learning as exact synonyms.

Machine learning is one approach used to create AI capabilities.

AI can also include approaches such as:

* Rule-based reasoning
* Search
* Planning
* Knowledge representation
* Optimization
* Expert systems
* Machine learning

Therefore:

> **All machine learning can be considered part of AI, but not all AI is machine learning.**

---

### 17. Machine Learning Is Broader Than Deep Learning

Similarly, deep learning is a subset of machine learning.

Machine learning can use techniques such as:

* Decision trees
* Random forests
* Support-vector machines
* Linear regression
* Logistic regression
* Gradient boosting
* Clustering

Deep learning primarily uses neural-network architectures.

Therefore:

> **Deep learning is machine learning, but machine learning is not limited to deep learning.**

---

### 18. AI Application

An AI application is software that uses one or more AI capabilities to perform a business or technical function.

Examples include:

* Fraud detection
* Recommendation systems
* Customer-service chatbots
* Document classification
* Predictive maintenance
* Medical image analysis
* Code assistants
* Security analytics

The application may use an internally developed model or an externally provided AI service.

---

### 19. AI Service

An AI service provides AI capabilities to other applications.

For example:

```text
Enterprise Application
        ↓
AI API
        ↓
External AI Service
        ↓
AI Model
        ↓
Response
```

The organization may not own or operate the underlying model.

This creates additional governance considerations involving:

* Third-party risk
* Data handling
* Service availability
* Contractual requirements
* Provider security
* Model changes
* Data residency

---

### 20. AI Ecosystem

Modern enterprise AI usually exists within an ecosystem rather than as an isolated model.

A simplified ecosystem may include:

```text
Users
  ↓
Applications
  ↓
AI Services
  ↓
Models
  ↓
Data
  ↓
Cloud / Infrastructure
  ↓
External Providers
  ↓
Enterprise Systems
```

Each component may introduce different risks and control requirements.

---

### 21. Data as a Core AI Component

Data is fundamental to many AI systems.

Depending on the architecture, data may be used for:

* Training
* Validation
* Testing
* Fine-tuning
* Prompting
* Retrieval
* Inference
* Monitoring

Data may include:

* Structured data
* Text
* Images
* Audio
* Video
* Logs
* Documents
* Sensor data

The security of the data can directly affect the security of the AI system.

---

### 22. Model Parameters

Model parameters are values learned during training that determine how the model transforms inputs into outputs.

Modern AI models can contain:

* Thousands of parameters
* Millions of parameters
* Billions of parameters
* Hundreds of billions or more parameters

The number of parameters alone does not determine model quality or security.

Other factors include:

* Training data
* Architecture
* Training methods
* Fine-tuning
* Evaluation
* Deployment configuration

---

### 23. Model Weights

The term **model weights** is commonly used to refer to learned numerical values within a model.

These weights encode patterns acquired during training.

From a security perspective, model weights may be valuable intellectual property.

They may therefore require protection against:

* Unauthorized access
* Theft
* Tampering
* Unauthorized modification
* Unauthorized distribution

---

### 24. AI Model Lifecycle

An AI model can pass through multiple stages:

```text
Design
  ↓
Data Preparation
  ↓
Training
  ↓
Evaluation
  ↓
Validation
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Inference
  ↓

Update / Retraining
  ↓

```

Governance and security requirements should exist throughout this lifecycle.

---

### 25. AI System Lifecycle

The broader AI system lifecycle is larger than the model lifecycle.

It may include:

```text
Business Need
    ↓
AI Use Case
    ↓
Architecture
    ↓
Data
    ↓
Model
    ↓

    ↓
Deployment
    ↓
Operation
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Change
    ↓
Retirement
```

This distinction is important because security risks can exist outside the model itself.

---

### 26. Model-Centric Versus System-Centric Thinking

A model-centric perspective focuses on:

* Model architecture
* Parameters
* Training
* Accuracy
* Model behavior

A system-centric perspective additionally considers:

* Users
* Applications
* Data
* APIs
* Infrastructure
* Identity
* Integrations
* Tools
* Monitoring
* Business processes

AI Security Governance should primarily adopt the **system-centric perspective**.

---

### 27. Why the Distinction Matters to GRC

A GRC professional may encounter a statement such as:

> "The model is secure."

That statement alone does not establish that the AI system is secure.

The model could be secure while:

* The API is improperly configured.
* Access controls are weak.
* Sensitive data is exposed.
* Logs contain confidential information.
* An AI agent has excessive permissions.
* A third-party provider has inadequate controls.
* The application lacks monitoring.

Therefore, AI governance must evaluate the **entire AI system and its surrounding ecosystem**.

---

### 28. AI Capability Versus AI Risk

AI capabilities can create both business value and risk.

For example:

```text
Greater AI Capability
        ↓
Greater Automation
        ↓
Greater Business Value
        ↓
Potentially Greater Impact
        ↓
Greater Governance Requirements
```

The objective of AI Security Governance is not necessarily to eliminate AI risk.

It is to ensure that AI risk is:

* Identified
* Understood
* Assessed
* Controlled
* Monitored
* Accepted where appropriate
* Continuously managed

---

### 29. Technical Understanding Required by GRC Professionals

A GRC professional does not necessarily need to build neural networks or train large language models.

However, they should understand concepts such as:

* Models

* Embeddings
* RAG

* AI infrastructure

This level of knowledge allows governance professionals to ask meaningful questions and evaluate technical evidence.

---

# 02.01 — Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning and Deep Learning

## Part 2 — AI Models, Data, Algorithms, Training, Inference and System Components

### 31. AI Algorithms

An algorithm is a defined computational procedure used to solve a problem or perform a task.

In AI and machine learning, algorithms determine how a system:

* Learns from data
* Finds patterns
* Optimizes parameters
* Makes predictions
* Classifies information
* Generates outputs
* Updates its internal representation

The algorithm and the model should not be treated as the same thing.

An algorithm describes the method used to learn or process information, while a model represents the resulting learned computational structure.

---

### 32. Algorithm Versus Model

A useful conceptual distinction is:

```text
Algorithm
    ↓
Learning Process
    ↓
Model Parameters
    ↓
Trained Model
```

For example, a machine-learning algorithm can be used to learn parameters from a dataset.

Once training is complete, the resulting trained model can be used for inference.

For GRC purposes, both may have governance implications, particularly when the algorithm, training process, or model affects business decisions.

---

### 33. Features

In traditional machine learning, a **feature** is an input variable used by a model.

For example, a fraud-detection system could use:

* Transaction amount
* Transaction location
* Transaction frequency
* Account age
* Device type

These variables can become features used by the model.

A simplified architecture is:

```text
Raw Data
   ↓
Feature Engineering
   ↓
Features
   ↓
ML Model
   ↓
Prediction
```

---

### 34. Feature Engineering

Feature engineering involves transforming raw data into useful representations for machine-learning algorithms.

Examples include:

* Normalizing numerical values
* Encoding categorical information
* Extracting dates
* Calculating transaction frequency
* Creating behavioral indicators

Poor feature engineering can affect:

* Model accuracy
* Bias
* Security
* Reliability
* Interpretability

Therefore, feature engineering can become part of the governance scope.

---

### 35. Modern Representation Learning

Modern deep-learning systems often reduce the need for manually engineered features.

Instead, neural networks can learn useful representations directly from data.

Conceptually:

```text
Raw Input
   ↓
Neural Network
   ↓
Learned Representation
   ↓
Prediction / Generation
```

This approach is called **representation learning**.

It is particularly important in modern AI systems.

---

### 36. Training Data

Training data is the data used to develop or train a machine-learning model.

It may include:

* Text
* Images
* Audio
* Video
* Structured records
* Sensor information
* Historical transactions
* Human-generated examples

Training data can influence the model's:

* Capabilities
* Limitations
* Biases
* Security properties
* Behavior

Therefore, training-data governance is a critical AI governance concern.

---

### 37. Validation Data

Validation data is used during model development to help evaluate and tune the model.

A simplified structure is:

```text
Dataset
 ├── Training Data
 ├── Validation Data
 └── Test Data
```

Validation helps determine whether changes to the model or training process improve performance.

---

### 38. Test Data

Test data is generally used to evaluate model performance after development decisions have been made.

The test dataset should provide an independent basis for evaluating how the model performs on data it was not trained on.

Governance should consider whether test data is:

* Representative
* Appropriate
* Secure
* Protected
* Independent enough for the intended evaluation

---

### 39. Data Splitting

A common machine-learning development process separates data into different purposes:

```text
Raw Dataset
     ↓
Data Preparation
     ↓
 ┌───────────────┐
 │               │
Training      Validation
 │               │
 └───────┬───────┘
         ↓
      Testing
```

The exact methodology can vary depending on the use case.

The governance principle is that evaluation should provide credible evidence about model performance.

---

### 40. Data Quality

AI systems depend heavily on data quality.

Important dimensions include:

* Accuracy
* Completeness
* Consistency
* Timeliness
* Relevance
* Validity
* Integrity

Poor-quality data can result in poor model behavior.

This creates a direct relationship between **data governance** and **AI governance**.

---

### 41. Data Provenance

Data provenance describes where data originated and how it was transformed.

A provenance chain may look like:

```text
Source
  ↓
Collection
  ↓
Transformation
  ↓
Storage
  ↓
Training / Retrieval
  ↓
AI Output
```

Provenance can help organizations determine:

* Where data came from
* Who supplied it
* How it was changed
* Where it was used
* Whether its use was authorized

---

### 42. Data Lineage

Data lineage tracks the movement and transformation of data across systems.

For AI systems, lineage may include:

```text
Source Data
   ↓
Data Platform
   ↓
Processing
   ↓
Training Dataset
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Inference
   ↓
Output
```

Data lineage supports:

* Auditability
* Privacy
* Security
* Regulatory compliance
* Incident investigation

---

### 43. Labels

In supervised machine learning, data examples may have associated labels.

For example:

```text
Email → Spam
Email → Legitimate
```

The model learns relationships between inputs and labels.

Labels can be created by:

* Humans
* Existing systems
* Rules
* Automated processes
* External datasets

Label quality can significantly affect model performance.

---

### 44. Human-Generated Training Data

Human annotators may label or evaluate training examples.

This creates additional governance considerations involving:

* Workforce access
* Data confidentiality
* Quality assurance
* Annotation standards
* Third-party providers
* Privacy
* Security

Organizations should understand who can access sensitive training data.

---

### 45. Synthetic Data

Synthetic data is artificially generated data designed to resemble characteristics of real data.

It may be used for:

* Model development
* Testing
* Simulation
* Privacy-sensitive scenarios
* Data augmentation

Synthetic data can reduce some data-exposure risks but does not automatically eliminate governance concerns.

---

### 46. Data Bias

Bias can occur when data does not adequately represent the population, environment, or conditions relevant to the AI system.

Potential causes include:

* Historical bias
* Sampling bias
* Measurement bias
* Labeling bias
* Representation gaps

Bias can affect model outcomes and may create legal, ethical, business, and reputational risks.

---

### 47. Model Training

Training is the process of adjusting model parameters using data.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Training Data
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Prediction
     ↓
Error Measurement
     ↓
Parameter Adjustment
     ↓
Improved Model
```

This process may repeat many times.

---

### 48. Training Objective

A model is trained toward a defined objective.

Examples include:

* Minimize prediction error
* Classify correctly
* Predict the next token
* Maximize reward
* Detect anomalies

The training objective influences what the model learns and how it behaves.

---

### 49. Loss Function

A loss function provides a mathematical measure of how far a model's prediction is from the desired result.

Conceptually:

```text
Prediction
    ↓
Compare with Expected Result
    ↓
Loss
    ↓
Parameter Adjustment
```

The loss function is one of the mechanisms used to improve model performance during training.

A GRC professional does not necessarily need to calculate loss functions, but understanding their role helps explain how models learn.

---

### 50. Optimization

Optimization is the process of adjusting model parameters to improve the training objective.

A simplified sequence is:

```text
Model Parameters
       ↓
Prediction
       ↓
Loss
       ↓
Optimization
       ↓
Updated Parameters
```

This process is repeated during training.

---

### 51. Epoch

An epoch generally represents one complete pass through a training dataset.

For example, if a model is trained for:

```text
1 Epoch
```

the training process has processed the training dataset once.

Multiple epochs may be used to improve learning, although more training does not automatically produce a better model.

---

### 52. Overfitting

Overfitting occurs when a model learns the training data too closely and performs poorly on new data.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Performance
       ↑
       │
       │      Excellent
       │
       │
New Data Performance
       ↓
       Poor
```

Overfitting can reduce model reliability.

It is therefore relevant to:

* Model validation
* Testing
* Risk assessment
* Assurance

---

### 53. Underfitting

Underfitting occurs when a model is too limited to capture the important patterns in the data.

The model may perform poorly on both:

* Training data
* New data

The objective is generally to develop a model that generalizes appropriately to relevant new inputs.

---

### 54. Generalization

Generalization refers to the ability of a trained model to perform effectively on previously unseen data.

A model that performs well only on its training data may not be reliable in production.

Therefore:

> **Model performance must be evaluated in the context of the environment in which the model will actually operate.**

---

### 55. Model Evaluation

Model evaluation measures how well a model performs against defined objectives.

Depending on the use case, metrics may include:

* Accuracy
* Precision
* Recall
* F1 score
* Mean absolute error
* Mean squared error
* Area under the ROC curve

Generative AI may require additional evaluation methods.

---

### 56. Model Performance Versus Model Security

High model performance does not necessarily mean high security.

A model can be highly accurate while still being vulnerable to:

* Adversarial inputs
* Data poisoning
* Model extraction
* Prompt manipulation
* Sensitive-data leakage
* Unauthorized use

Therefore:

```text
Model Quality ≠ Model Security
```

Both must be evaluated.

---

### 57. Model Robustness

Robustness describes how well a model maintains appropriate behavior when conditions change or inputs are unusual.

Robustness may be evaluated against:

* Noisy inputs
* Unexpected inputs
* Distribution changes
* Adversarial inputs
* Edge cases

Robustness is particularly important for systems used in high-impact environments.

---

### 58. Model Drift

Model-related drift can occur when the environment changes after deployment.

For example:

```text
Training Environment
       ↓
Deployment
       ↓
Real-World Environment Changes
       ↓
Model Performance Changes
```

Changes may occur in:

* User behavior
* Data distribution
* Business processes
* Threat patterns
* Market conditions

Monitoring should detect significant changes.

---

### 59. Data Drift

Data drift occurs when the statistical characteristics of incoming data change over time.

For example, a fraud model trained using historical transaction behavior may encounter significantly different behavior later.

Data drift can affect model performance even if the model itself has not changed.

---

### 60. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and the desired output changes.

For example, the characteristics associated with fraudulent behavior may change as attackers adapt.

This can cause previously learned patterns to become less useful.

---

### 61. Model Inference

Inference is the process through which a trained model processes an input.

For example:

```text
User Input
    ↓
Preprocessing
    ↓
Model
    ↓
Inference
    ↓
Prediction / Generation
    ↓
Application
```

Inference may occur:

* On-premises
* In a cloud environment
* At the edge
* Through an API
* Within a SaaS platform

---

### 62. Inference Environment

The inference environment is where the model executes.

It may include:

* Servers
* GPUs
* AI accelerators
* Containers
* Kubernetes
* Cloud services
* Edge devices
* Application runtimes

The inference environment is part of the AI security boundary.

---

### 63. Model Serving

Model serving refers to the mechanisms used to make a trained model available for inference.

A simplified architecture is:

```text
Application
     ↓
API / Endpoint
     ↓
Model Serving Layer
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Inference
     ↓
Response
```

Model-serving infrastructure requires appropriate security controls.

---

### 64. AI Inference Endpoint

An inference endpoint provides access to a model.

For example:

```text
Client
  ↓
Authenticated Request
  ↓
AI Endpoint
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Response
```

Security controls may include:

* Authentication
* Authorization
* Rate limiting
* Network controls
* Logging
* Monitoring
* Input validation

---

### 65. Model Deployment

Model deployment moves a trained and validated model into an environment where it can be used.

Deployment may involve:

* Model packaging
* Infrastructure provisioning
* Configuration
* Security controls
* API exposure
* Monitoring
* Version management

Deployment should be governed rather than treated as an ordinary software release.

---

### 66. Model Versioning

AI models may exist in multiple versions.

For example:

```text
Model v1.0
    ↓
Model v1.1
    ↓
Model v2.0
```

Each version may differ in:

* Training data
* Parameters
* Architecture
* Fine-tuning
* Performance
* Security characteristics

Organizations should maintain appropriate model version control.

---

### 67. Model Registry

A model registry can provide centralized visibility into models.

It may contain:

* Model name
* Version
* Owner
* Purpose
* Training information
* Evaluation results
* Deployment status
* Risk classification
* Approval status

A model registry can therefore support both technical operations and governance.

---

### 68. Model Metadata

Model metadata can describe:

* Model owner
* Model version
* Training date
* Training dataset
* Intended use
* Limitations
* Evaluation results
* Deployment location
* Dependencies

Good metadata improves accountability and auditability.

---

### 69. Model Documentation

Model documentation should explain important characteristics of the model.

Depending on the system, documentation may cover:

* Purpose
* Intended users
* Intended use
* Limitations
* Training approach
* Data
* Evaluation
* Known risks
* Security considerations
* Deployment information

Documentation supports governance throughout the model lifecycle.

---

### 70. AI System Component Model

A simplified AI system can therefore be represented as:

```text
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │       Users       │
                    └─────────┬─────────┘
                              ↓
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │    Application    │
                    └─────────┬─────────┘
                              ↓
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │  AI Interface/API │
                    └─────────┬─────────┘
                              ↓
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │  Model Inference  │
                    └─────────┬─────────┘
                              ↓
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │   AI Model        │
                    └─────────┬─────────┘
                              ↓
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │ Data / Knowledge  │
                    └───────────────────┘
```

## Part 3 — Deep Learning, Neural Networks, Modern AI Models and Enterprise AI Components

### 71. Deep Learning Architecture

Deep learning uses neural networks containing multiple computational layers.

A simplified architecture is:

```text
Input
  ↓
Input Representation
  ↓
Hidden Layer
  ↓
Hidden Layer
  ↓
Hidden Layer
  ↓
Output
```

Each layer transforms the information received from the previous layer.

As information moves through the network, the system can learn increasingly complex representations.

---

### 72. Neural Network Layers

Neural networks commonly contain:

* Input layers
* Hidden layers
* Output layers

The input layer receives information.

Hidden layers transform that information.

The output layer produces the result required by the particular model.

Different architectures can contain very different numbers and configurations of layers.

---

### 73. Artificial Neurons

An artificial neuron is a computational unit that receives inputs, applies learned weights, combines the information, and produces an output.

Conceptually:

```text
Input 1 ──┐
Input 2 ──┼──> Neuron ──> Output
Input 3 ──┘
```

A neural network contains many interconnected computational units.

The exact mathematical implementation varies by architecture.

---

### 74. Weights and Biases

Neural networks use learned numerical parameters commonly referred to as weights and biases.

These parameters influence how information is transformed through the network.

During training, optimization methods adjust these parameters.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Prediction
      ↓
Loss
      ↓
Parameter Adjustment
      ↓
Updated Weights
```

The resulting parameters become part of the trained model.

---

### 75. Activation Functions

Activation functions determine how neurons transform their inputs.

Examples include:

* ReLU
* Sigmoid
* Tanh
* Softmax
* GELU

Activation functions allow neural networks to represent complex relationships rather than behaving as simple linear transformations.

A GRC professional does not normally need to calculate activation functions, but understanding their existence helps explain neural-network architecture.

---

### 76. Forward Propagation

During inference, information generally moves forward through the network.

For example:

```text
Input
  ↓
Layer 1
  ↓
Layer 2
  ↓
Layer 3
  ↓
Output
```

This process is often described as forward propagation.

The model transforms the input into an output using its learned parameters.

---

### 77. Backpropagation

During training, the model's output is compared against an expected result.

The resulting error information is propagated backward through the network to help adjust model parameters.

Conceptually:

```text
Input
  ↓
Neural Network
  ↓
Prediction
  ↓
Loss
  ↓
Backpropagation
  ↓
Parameter Updates
```

Backpropagation is a fundamental mechanism behind the training of many neural networks.

---

### 78. Gradient-Based Optimization

Many neural networks are trained using gradient-based optimization methods.

A gradient indicates how changes to model parameters affect the objective being optimized.

An optimizer then uses this information to update parameters.

Examples of optimization algorithms include:

* Gradient descent
* Stochastic gradient descent
* Adam
* AdamW

These mechanisms are important to the technical understanding of model training.

---

### 79. Batch Processing

Training data is often processed in groups called batches.

A simplified process is:

```text
Dataset
   ↓
Batch 1
   ↓
Model Update
   ↓
Batch 2
   ↓
Model Update
   ↓
Batch 3
   ↓
Model Update
```

Batch size can affect:

* Training performance
* Memory requirements
* Training stability
* Computational cost

---

### 80. Parameters Versus Hyperparameters

Parameters are values learned by the model during training.

Hyperparameters are configuration values selected by developers or researchers.

Examples of hyperparameters include:

* Learning rate
* Batch size
* Number of training epochs
* Model architecture choices
* Regularization settings

The distinction is important because governance should understand which aspects of the system are learned and which are explicitly configured.

---

### 81. Machine Learning Hyperparameters

Hyperparameters can significantly influence model behavior.

For example, a learning rate that is too large may make training unstable, while one that is too small may make training inefficient.

Other hyperparameters can affect:

* Model complexity
* Training duration
* Generalization
* Resource consumption

Therefore, significant training configurations should be documented.

---

### 82. Convolutional Neural Networks

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are neural-network architectures historically associated with computer vision.

They are particularly effective at processing spatial patterns in images.

A simplified structure may be:

```text
Image
 ↓
Convolution
 ↓
Feature Extraction
 ↓
Pooling
 ↓
Feature Representation
 ↓
Classification
```

CNNs remain important for understanding the evolution of computer-vision architectures.

---

### 83. Recurrent Neural Networks

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) were designed to process sequential information.

They were historically important for:

* Language processing
* Speech
* Time-series analysis

RNN-based architectures include:

* Basic RNNs
* LSTMs
* GRUs

Modern language systems increasingly rely on transformer-based architectures, but understanding RNNs provides useful architectural context.

---

### 84. Transformers

Transformers are a major neural-network architecture that transformed modern AI.

They are particularly important for:

* Natural-language processing
* Large language models
* Generative AI
* Multimodal AI

Transformers use attention mechanisms to model relationships between elements of an input sequence.

---

### 85. Attention Mechanism

Attention allows a model to determine which parts of an input are more relevant when processing another part.

For example, in a sentence, the meaning of a word may depend on other words appearing elsewhere in the sentence.

A simplified conceptual representation is:

```text
Token A ─┐
Token B ─┼──> Attention ──> Contextual Representation
Token C ─┘
```

Attention is a central concept behind transformer-based models.

---

### 86. Self-Attention

Self-attention allows elements within the same input sequence to interact with one another.

For a sequence:

```text
Token 1
Token 2
Token 3
Token 4
```

the model can calculate relationships among these tokens.

This enables transformer architectures to capture contextual relationships across the input.

---

### 87. Transformer Architecture

A simplified transformer architecture can be represented as:

```text

  ↓
Tokenization
  ↓
Embeddings
  ↓
Positional Information
  ↓
Attention Layers
  ↓
Feed-Forward Layers
  ↓
Repeated Transformer Blocks
  ↓
Output
```

Modern transformer architectures can contain many repeated blocks.

---

### 88. Encoder and Decoder Architectures

Transformer systems can use different architectural configurations.

**Encoder-oriented models**

are commonly designed to create contextual representations.

**Decoder-oriented models**

are commonly designed for sequential generation.

**Encoder-decoder models**

combine both approaches.

Different architectures are suited to different AI tasks.

### 89. Transformer-Based Language Models

```text
   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

```

The process can be repeated to generate a sequence of tokens.

A token is a unit of text processed by many language models.

A token may represent:

* A complete word
* Part of a word
* Punctuation
* A symbol
* Other text fragments

For example, a sentence is converted into tokens before being processed by the model.

Tokenization affects:

* Context length
* Cost
* Processing
* Model input limits

An embedding represents information as a numerical vector.

For example:

```text
 ↓
```

Embeddings allow systems to compare information based on mathematical similarity.

They are widely used in:

* RAG

* Clustering

Representation learning allows models to automatically learn useful representations of data.

* Images

* Language

### 97. Probabilistic Model Behavior

Many AI models produce outputs based on probability distributions.

For example, a language model can estimate probabilities for possible next tokens.

Conceptually:

```text

     ↓

     ↓
Possible Outputs
 ┌────┼────┐
 ↓    ↓    ↓
A    B    C
```

The system selects or samples from possible outputs according to the model and inference configuration.

This probabilistic behavior is important when considering:

* Reliability
* Testing
* Predictability
* Security
* Human oversight

### 98. Temperature and Sampling

Generative models may expose inference parameters that influence output generation.

One commonly discussed parameter is **temperature**.

Conceptually:

* Lower temperature → more deterministic output
* Higher temperature → more variability

Other sampling approaches may include:

* Top-k sampling
* Top-p sampling

These settings can influence model behavior but do not fundamentally change the trained model.

### 99. Deterministic Versus Probabilistic Systems

Traditional software generally follows explicitly programmed logic.

AI systems may produce probabilistic outputs.

This distinction can be represented as:

```text

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Output
```

versus:

```text

Input
 ↓
Model
 ↓

 ↓
Output
```

This difference creates additional governance requirements around validation, monitoring, and human oversight.

* Automation

* High-impact decisions

* Missing knowledge

Hallucination risk is particularly relevant to applications where inaccurate information can cause material consequences.

* Business impact

### 149. GRC Review of an AI Architecture

When reviewing an AI architecture, a GRC professional should be able to ask:

1. What is the AI system?
2. What business process does it support?
3. What model is being used?
4. Who owns the model?
5. Where is the model hosted?
6. What data does it process?
7. Where does the data originate?
8. Where does the data go?
9. What external services are involved?
10. What users and systems can access it?
11. What actions can the AI perform?
12. What controls protect it?
13. How is activity monitored?
14. How are incidents handled?
15. How is the system reassessed after changes?

These questions connect technical architecture to governance.

### 160. AI Technology and System Architecture — Foundational Perspective

AI can ultimately be understood through the following progression:

```text
Artificial Intelligence
        ↓
Machine Learning
        ↓
Deep Learning
        ↓
Neural Networks
        ↓
Modern AI Models
        ↓
Foundation Models
        ↓
AI Applications
        ↓
Enterprise AI Systems
        ↓
Enterprise AI Architecture
```

The key governance principle is that **the AI model is only one component of the system being governed**.

An enterprise AI capability may involve models, data, applications, APIs, infrastructure, users, external providers, enterprise systems, and autonomous actions. Each component introduces its own risks, dependencies, ownership requirements, and control considerations.

A GRC professional therefore needs enough technical understanding to see the complete system, identify where risk can arise, understand where controls belong, and translate the architecture into governance, risk, compliance, security, and assurance requirements.
