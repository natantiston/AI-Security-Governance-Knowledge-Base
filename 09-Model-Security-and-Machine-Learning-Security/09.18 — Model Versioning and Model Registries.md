# 09.18 — Model Validation & Revalidation

## Part 1

### 1. Model Validation

**Model validation** is the independent and systematic assessment of whether an AI/ML model is fit for its intended purpose and operates within defined technical, business, security, and risk requirements.

Validation provides evidence that a model:

* Performs adequately.
* Uses appropriate data.
* Produces reliable outputs.
* Meets defined requirements.
* Has acceptable limitations.
* Can be deployed within approved risk boundaries.

```text
id="mv101"
MODEL DEVELOPMENT
       ↓
    TESTING
       ↓
   VALIDATION
       ↓
 RISK ASSESSMENT
       ↓
    APPROVAL
       ↓
   DEPLOYMENT
```

---

### 2. Validation vs. Testing

Testing and validation are related but not identical.

**Testing** asks:

> Does the model behave correctly under defined test conditions?

**Validation** asks:

> Is there sufficient evidence that the model is appropriate and acceptable for its intended use?

```text
id="mv102"
Testing
   ↓
Technical Evidence
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Independent Assessment
   ↓
Risk Decision
```

---

### 3. Why Model Validation Matters

A model can achieve excellent technical metrics and still be inappropriate for production.

For example:

```text
id="mv103"
High Accuracy
     ↓
But:
Wrong Data
Wrong Use Case
Poor Security
Unacceptable Risk
     ↓
NOT APPROPRIATE
```

Validation therefore considers more than performance.

---

### 4. Validation Objectives

A comprehensive validation process should establish whether:

1. The model solves the intended problem.
2. The data is appropriate.
3. Performance meets requirements.
4. Results are reliable.
5. Security controls are adequate.
6. Known limitations are understood.
7. Operational requirements can be met.
8. Governance requirements are satisfied.

---

### 5. Intended Use

Validation begins with understanding the **intended use** of the model.

The validation team should establish:

* What the model does.
* Who uses it.
* What decisions it supports.
* What data it consumes.
* What outputs it produces.
* What consequences arise from errors.

---

### 6. Intended Use vs. Actual Use

A model may be validated for one purpose but later used for another.

```text
id="mv106"
Approved Use
     ↓
Validation
     ↓
Production
     ↓
New Use
     ↓
Revalidation Required?
```

Changing the purpose can change the model's risk profile.

---

### 7. Model Scope

Validation should establish the boundaries within which the model is expected to operate.

These may include:

* Supported populations.
* Geographic scope.
* Data ranges.
* Supported languages.
* Transaction volumes.
* Operating environments.

---

### 8. Out-of-Scope Conditions

The model should have explicitly documented limitations.

For example:

```text
id="mv108"
Model Validated For:
     ↓
Environment A
Data Range A
Population A

NOT Validated For:
     ↓
Environment B
Data Range B
Population B
```

Using the model outside validated conditions creates additional risk.

---

### 9. Validation Independence

Validation should have an appropriate degree of independence from model development.

The objective is to avoid a situation where the same team:

```text
id="mv109"
Builds Model
     ↓
Tests Model
     ↓
Approves Model
```

without meaningful independent challenge.

---

### 10. Three Lines of Defense

A governance structure may use:

```text
id="mv110"
1st Line
Model Development / Business
       ↓
Owns Model Risk

2nd Line
Risk / GRC / Independent Validation
       ↓
Challenges & Assesses

3rd Line
Internal Audit
       ↓
Independent Assurance
```

The exact organizational structure varies by enterprise.

---

### 11. Validation Evidence

Validation should be evidence-based.

Potential evidence includes:

* Model documentation.
* Training methodology.
* Dataset descriptions.
* Test results.
* Performance metrics.
* Security assessments.
* Robustness testing.
* Bias/fairness analysis where applicable.
* Monitoring plans.

---

### 12. Validation Documentation

A model validation report should normally explain:

* Model purpose.
* Scope.
* Methodology.
* Data.
* Testing.
* Results.
* Limitations.
* Risks.
* Findings.
* Recommendations.
* Validation conclusion.

---

### 13. Model Documentation

A validator should be able to understand the model sufficiently to challenge it.

Documentation may include:

* Architecture.
* Algorithms.
* Features.
* Hyperparameters.
* Training process.
* Dependencies.
* Model version.
* Deployment environment.

---

### 14. Data Validation

The quality of the model depends heavily on the quality of its data.

Validation should consider:

* Data completeness.
* Accuracy.
* Relevance.
* Representativeness.
* Timeliness.
* Consistency.
* Provenance.

```text
id="mv114"
Data Quality
     ↓
Model Quality
     ↓
Decision Quality
```

---

### 15. Training Data Assessment

Validators should understand:

* Where training data came from.
* How it was collected.
* How it was transformed.
* How it was labeled.
* Whether it contains known limitations.

---

### 16. Data Representativeness

Training data should reasonably represent the environment in which the model will operate.

```text
id="mv116"
Training Population
        ↕
Production Population
```

Large differences may create generalization risk.

---

### 17. Sampling Bias

Validation should consider whether the training dataset systematically over- or under-represents certain conditions.

Potential consequences include:

* Poor performance.
* Unreliable predictions.
* Uneven outcomes.

---

### 18. Data Leakage

**Data leakage** occurs when information unavailable at prediction time is inadvertently used during training.

Example:

```text
id="mv118"
Future Information
      ↓
Training Dataset
      ↓
Artificially High Performance
```

The resulting validation score may be misleading.

---

### 19. Train/Test Separation

Validation should confirm that evaluation data is appropriately separated from training data.

```text
id="mv119"
Training Data
     ↓
Model Development

Validation/Test Data
     ↓
Independent Evaluation
```

---

### 20. Temporal Validation

For time-dependent systems, random splitting may not accurately represent production conditions.

A temporal approach may be more appropriate:

```text
id="mv120"
Past Data
   ↓
Training

Later Data
   ↓
Validation

Future Data
   ↓
Production
```

---

### 21. Cross-Validation

Cross-validation can help assess model stability across different subsets of data.

It is particularly useful during development but should not replace appropriate independent validation.

---

### 22. Performance Validation

The model should be evaluated against predefined performance requirements.

Possible metrics include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1.
* AUC.
* MAE.
* RMSE.

The metric must correspond to the model's purpose.

---

### 23. Performance Thresholds

The organization should establish acceptance criteria before evaluating the model where practical.

For example:

```text
id="mv123"
Recall ≥ 95%
Precision ≥ 90%
Latency ≤ 200 ms
```

These values are illustrative.

---

### 24. Baseline Comparison

The model should be compared against an appropriate baseline.

Possible baselines include:

* Existing production model.
* Rule-based process.
* Previous model version.
* Simple statistical model.
* Human decision process.

---

### 25. Model Benchmarking

A new model should demonstrate meaningful value relative to the existing approach.

```text
id="mv125"
Current Model
     ↕
Candidate Model
     ↓
Performance Comparison
     ↓
Risk/Benefit Assessment
```

---

### 26. Error Analysis

Aggregate metrics can hide important failures.

Validation should examine:

* False positives.
* False negatives.
* Edge cases.
* Misclassification patterns.
* High-impact errors.

---

### 27. Confusion Matrix

For classification models, a confusion matrix can provide useful insight.

```text
id="mv127"
                 Actual
              Positive Negative
Predicted
Positive         TP       FP
Negative         FN       TN
```

This helps identify the types of errors being produced.

---

### 28. False-Negative Risk

In cybersecurity and safety-related applications, false negatives may be especially important.

```text
id="mv128"
Real Threat
    ↓
Model
    ↓
"No Threat"
    ↓
Missed Detection
```

The acceptable false-negative rate should therefore be defined according to the use case.

---

### 29. False-Positive Risk

False positives can also create operational and business consequences.

Examples:

* Analyst overload.
* Customer friction.
* Unnecessary investigations.
* Service disruption.

---

### 30. Threshold Validation

Classification thresholds should be validated rather than selected arbitrarily.

```text
id="mv130"
Model Score
     ↓
Decision Threshold
     ↓
Business/Security Outcome
```

Different thresholds can produce substantially different risk profiles.

---

### 31. Calibration Validation

Where model probabilities are used for decisions, validation should assess whether predicted probabilities correspond reasonably to observed outcomes.

A model stating:

```text
90% probability
```

should, over an appropriate population, behave approximately like a 90% outcome frequency.

---

### 32. Robustness Validation

Robustness testing examines how the model behaves when conditions change.

Potential tests include:

* Noisy inputs.
* Missing values.
* Outliers.
* Distribution changes.
* Boundary conditions.

---

### 33. Adversarial Robustness

Security-sensitive models should also be assessed against potential adversarial manipulation.

Examples include:

* Manipulated inputs.
* Evasion attempts.
* Adversarial examples.
* Query manipulation.

---

### 34. Stress Testing

Stress testing examines model behavior under unusually difficult conditions.

```text
id="mv134"
Normal Conditions
       ↓
Stress Conditions
       ↓
Model Behavior
       ↓
Risk Assessment
```

---

### 35. Edge-Case Testing

Validation should include realistic edge cases rather than relying exclusively on average conditions.

Examples:

* Rare events.
* Extreme values.
* Missing fields.
* Unexpected combinations.
* Unusual user behavior.

---

### 36. Out-of-Distribution Testing

The model should be evaluated against data that differs from its training distribution where relevant.

This helps assess whether the model fails safely when encountering unfamiliar conditions.

---

### 37. Explainability Validation

Where explainability is required, validation should assess whether explanations are:

* Available.
* Consistent.
* Understandable.
* Appropriate for the intended users.

Explanations should not automatically be assumed to represent causal reasoning.

---

### 38. Fairness Validation

Where applicable, validation may evaluate whether model outcomes differ materially across relevant populations.

Potential measures include:

* Error rates.
* Selection rates.
* False-positive rates.
* False-negative rates.

The specific approach depends on the use case and applicable requirements.

---

### 39. Privacy Validation

Validation should assess whether the model introduces unacceptable privacy risks.

Potential areas include:

* Training-data exposure.
* Sensitive attribute handling.
* Logging.
* Outputs.
* Access controls.

---

### 40. Core Principle

**Model validation is the structured evidence-based process that determines whether a model is sufficiently reliable, secure, appropriate, and governed for its intended use.**

The central lifecycle is:

```text
id="mv140"
INTENDED PURPOSE
       ↓
DATA ASSESSMENT
       ↓
TECHNICAL TESTING
       ↓
PERFORMANCE VALIDATION
       ↓
ROBUSTNESS & SECURITY
       ↓
RISK ASSESSMENT
       ↓
INDEPENDENT CHALLENGE
       ↓
APPROVAL / REMEDIATION
```

The most important distinction is:

> **A model is not validated simply because it produces a high accuracy score.**

Validation asks whether the **model, data, assumptions, security, operating environment, limitations, and intended use collectively support an acceptable level of risk.**

## Part 2

### 41. Validation Methodology

A model validation methodology should provide a consistent process for assessing models across the enterprise while allowing the depth of validation to vary according to risk.

```text id="mv241"
MODEL IDENTIFICATION
        ↓
SCOPE & PURPOSE
        ↓
DATA ASSESSMENT
        ↓
METHODOLOGY REVIEW
        ↓
PERFORMANCE TESTING
        ↓
ROBUSTNESS TESTING
        ↓
SECURITY / FAIRNESS / PRIVACY
        ↓
LIMITATIONS
        ↓
VALIDATION CONCLUSION
```

---

### 42. Risk-Based Validation

Not every model requires the same level of validation.

Validation depth should consider:

* Model criticality.
* Decision impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Security sensitivity.
* Data sensitivity.
* Complexity.
* Autonomy.
* Potential harm.

```text id="mv242"
LOW RISK
   ↓
Standard Validation

MEDIUM RISK
   ↓
Enhanced Validation

HIGH / CRITICAL RISK
   ↓
Deep Independent Validation
```

---

### 43. Model Complexity

Complexity can increase validation difficulty.

Examples include:

* Deep neural networks.
* Ensemble models.
* Generative AI.
* Large language models.
* Complex pipelines.

Complexity does not automatically mean higher risk, but it may require stronger validation techniques.

---

### 44. Validation Scope

Before validation begins, the scope should be explicitly defined.

It may cover:

* Model artifact.
* Training process.
* Data.
* Features.
* Preprocessing.
* Inference pipeline.
* APIs.
* Security controls.
* Business integration.

---

### 45. Validation Plan

A validation plan should establish:

1. What will be assessed.
2. What evidence is required.
3. Which tests will be performed.
4. What acceptance criteria apply.
5. Who performs validation.
6. How findings are classified.
7. How the final decision is made.

---

### 46. Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria should ideally be established before final validation.

Examples:

```text id="mv246"
Performance
Recall ≥ Required Threshold

Security
No Critical Findings

Data
No Material Integrity Issues

Operations
Latency Within SLA

Governance
Required Documentation Complete
```

Values are illustrative.

---

### 47. Validation Evidence Hierarchy

Not all evidence has equal strength.

For example:

```text id="mv247"
Observed Production Evidence
          ↑
Independent Test Results
          ↑
Controlled Testing
          ↑
Developer Testing
          ↑
Developer Assertion
```

Independent evidence generally provides stronger assurance than unsupported claims.

---

### 48. Reproducibility

A validator should be able to reproduce important validation results where technically feasible.

This requires controlling or documenting:

* Dataset versions.
* Code versions.
* Model versions.
* Configuration.
* Random seeds where applicable.
* Evaluation methodology.

---

### 49. Model Version Control

Validation must be tied to a specific model version.

```text id="mv249"
Model v2.1
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Approved

Model v2.2
    ↓
Changed Model
    ↓
Validation Impact Assessment
```

A validation report should not ambiguously refer to "the model."

---

### 50. Validation Traceability

Each validation result should be traceable to:

* Model version.
* Dataset.
* Test.
* Metric.
* Result.
* Acceptance criterion.

```text id="mv250"
Requirement
    ↓
Test
    ↓
Result
    ↓
Finding
    ↓
Conclusion
```

---

### 51. Independent Data Verification

Where practical, validators should independently verify important dataset characteristics rather than relying entirely on developer-provided descriptions.

Potential checks include:

* Dataset size.
* Missing values.
* Distribution.
* Label quality.
* Sampling.
* Duplicates.

---

### 52. Data Lineage Validation

The validator should understand where important model data originates.

```text id="mv252"
Source
  ↓
Collection
  ↓
Transformation
  ↓
Feature
  ↓
Model
```

Unknown or poorly documented lineage creates validation uncertainty.

---

### 53. Feature Validation

Features should be evaluated for:

* Relevance.
* Quality.
* Stability.
* Availability.
* Leakage.
* Appropriate use.

---

### 54. Feature Leakage

A feature may accidentally contain information about the target that would not actually be available when the model makes its prediction.

```text id="mv254"
Target Outcome
     ↓
Accidentally Included
     ↓
Training
     ↓
Artificial Performance
```

Validation should actively test for this risk.

---

### 55. Label Validation

Where supervised learning is used, validation should consider the quality of labels.

Potential problems include:

* Incorrect labels.
* Inconsistent labeling.
* Missing labels.
* Subjective labeling.
* Historical bias.

---

### 56. Data Quality Thresholds

Data acceptance criteria may be established.

For example:

```text id="mv256"
Missing Values < 2%
Duplicate Records < 0.5%
Schema Violations = 0 Critical
```

Values are examples only.

---

### 57. Statistical Validation

Statistical analysis can determine whether observed performance differences are meaningful.

For example:

```text id="mv257"
Model A Accuracy = 92.1%
Model B Accuracy = 92.5%

Difference = 0.4%

Question:
Is the difference statistically and operationally meaningful?
```

A small numerical improvement does not necessarily justify replacing an existing model.

---

### 58. Confidence Intervals

Validation may use confidence intervals to communicate uncertainty around measured performance.

Instead of reporting only:

> Accuracy = 94%

the report may provide an estimated range around that measurement.

---

### 59. Validation Under Uncertainty

Validation results should acknowledge uncertainty caused by:

* Limited datasets.
* Rare events.
* Delayed outcomes.
* Measurement errors.
* Changing environments.

A mature validation report does not present uncertain results as absolute facts.

---

### 60. Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how model outcomes change when important assumptions or inputs change.

```text id="mv260"
Input / Assumption
       ↓
Change
       ↓
Model Output
       ↓
Impact Assessment
```

---

### 61. Scenario Testing

Validators can evaluate predefined scenarios.

Examples:

* Normal conditions.
* High-volume conditions.
* Poor-quality data.
* Rare events.
* Security attack.
* Infrastructure degradation.

---

### 62. Stress Scenarios

High-impact models may require more severe scenarios.

For example:

```text id="mv262"
Normal
  ↓
Elevated Load
  ↓
Extreme Load
  ↓
Failure Condition
```

The goal is to understand degradation behavior.

---

### 63. Failure Mode Analysis

Validation should consider how the model can fail.

A structured approach can identify:

* Failure mode.
* Cause.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing control.
* Detection method.
* Mitigation.

---

### 64. FMEA for AI Models

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis can be adapted for AI systems.

```text id="mv264"
Failure Mode
     ↓
Cause
     ↓
Effect
     ↓
Risk
     ↓
Control
```

This can complement conventional model performance testing.

---

### 65. Model Assumptions

Every model relies on assumptions.

Examples:

* Data remains representative.
* Features remain available.
* Relationships remain sufficiently stable.
* Labels are reliable.

Validation should document important assumptions.

---

### 66. Assumption Testing

Where practical, assumptions should be challenged.

```text id="mv266"
Assumption
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Test
    ↓
Supported?
```

Unsupported assumptions should become documented risks.

---

### 67. Model Limitations

A validation report should explicitly identify limitations.

Examples:

* Limited training population.
* Poor performance on rare cases.
* Lack of ground truth.
* Limited explainability.
* Sensitivity to data changes.

---

### 68. Material Validation Findings

Findings should be categorized according to risk.

Example:

| Finding  | Severity            | Example                              |
| -------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Critical | Immediate concern   | Major security weakness              |
| High     | Significant concern | Performance below required threshold |
| Medium   | Material weakness   | Documentation or monitoring gap      |
| Low      | Minor issue         | Non-critical improvement             |

---

### 69. Finding Remediation

A model should not necessarily proceed directly to production when material findings exist.

```text id="mv269"
Validation Finding
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Remediation
       ↓
Retesting
       ↓
Validation Closure
```

---

### 70. Conditional Approval

In some cases, a model may be approved with conditions.

Examples:

* Additional monitoring.
* Restricted use.
* Human review.
* Lower transaction limits.
* Time-limited approval.

---

### 71. Restricted Deployment

A model with known limitations may initially be deployed within a restricted scope.

```text id="mv271"
Validation
    ↓
Restricted Deployment
    ↓
Enhanced Monitoring
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Full Approval
```

---

### 72. Validation Conclusion

A validation conclusion should clearly state the outcome.

Possible conclusions include:

* **Approved**
* **Approved with Conditions**
* **Requires Remediation**
* **Not Approved**

Avoid vague conclusions such as "looks acceptable."

---

### 73. Validation Sign-Off

Appropriate stakeholders should formally acknowledge the validation outcome.

Depending on governance structure, this may involve:

* Validator.
* Model owner.
* Business owner.
* Risk/GRC.
* Security.
* Compliance.

---

### 74. Validation Independence Evidence

Organizations should retain evidence demonstrating who performed the validation and their relationship to the development team.

This supports auditability.

---

### 75. Validation Challenge

Independent validation should actively challenge assumptions rather than simply confirm developer documentation.

Questions may include:

> Why was this dataset selected?

> Why is this metric appropriate?

> What happens when the model encounters unfamiliar data?

> What are the consequences of false negatives?

> What evidence supports the stated performance?

---

### 76. Validation vs. Model Approval

Validation provides evidence.

Approval is the governance decision.

```text id="mv276"
Validation
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Risk Decision
    ↓
Approval
```

The validator does not necessarily own the business decision to deploy.

---

### 77. Validation vs. Monitoring

Validation and monitoring operate at different lifecycle points.

| Validation                     | Monitoring          |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------- |
| Establishes assurance          | Maintains assurance |
| Pre-deployment / major changes | Production          |
| Structured assessment          | Continuous/periodic |
| Tests suitability              | Detects deviation   |
| Approval evidence              | Ongoing evidence    |

---

### 78. Revalidation

**Revalidation** is the process of reassessing a model after material changes or at defined intervals to determine whether previous validation conclusions remain valid.

```text id="mv278"
Original Validation
       ↓
Production
       ↓
Change / Time
       ↓
Revalidation
       ↓
Continued Approval?
```

---

### 79. Why Revalidation Matters

A previously validated model can become unsuitable because:

* Data changed.
* The model changed.
* The environment changed.
* The business purpose changed.
* Threats changed.
* Regulations changed.

---

### 80. Core Principle

**Validation establishes whether a model is acceptable for its intended use; revalidation determines whether that conclusion remains valid after change or over time.**

```text id="mv280"
VALIDATE
   ↓
APPROVE
   ↓
DEPLOY
   ↓
MONITOR
   ↓
CHANGE / TIME
   ↓
REVALIDATE
   ↓
CONTINUE / RESTRICT / REMEDIATE / RETIRE
```

The key GRC concept is:

> **Model validation is not a one-time technical certification. It is an evidence-based assurance activity that must be revisited whenever material changes could invalidate the original validation conclusion.**

### 81. Revalidation Triggers

Revalidation should be triggered when there is a reasonable possibility that the original validation conclusion is no longer valid.

Common triggers include:

* Material model changes.
* Significant retraining.
* New training data.
* Feature changes.
* Major data-distribution changes.
* Change in intended use.
* New deployment environment.
* Significant security events.
* Regulatory changes.
* Material performance degradation.

```text id="mv381"
MODEL
  ↓
CHANGE DETECTED
  ↓
MATERIAL?
 ┌──────┴──────┐
 NO           YES
 ↓             ↓
Monitor     Revalidate
```

---

### 82. Model Change

Not every model change requires full revalidation.

The organization should first determine the **materiality of the change**.

Examples:

**Minor change**

* Documentation update.
* Non-material monitoring improvement.

**Material change**

* New algorithm.
* Major retraining.
* Significant feature modification.
* New decision purpose.

---

### 83. Change Impact Assessment

Before deciding on the level of revalidation, perform a change impact assessment.

```text id="mv383"
Change
  ↓
What changed?
  ↓
What could be affected?
  ↓
Risk impact
  ↓
Validation impact
```

---

### 84. Revalidation Tiers

A risk-based organization can define multiple levels.

| Tier   | Trigger           | Response                   |
| ------ | ----------------- | -------------------------- |
| Tier 1 | Minor change      | Documentation review       |
| Tier 2 | Moderate change   | Targeted testing           |
| Tier 3 | Material change   | Full revalidation          |
| Tier 4 | Major risk change | Full validation + approval |

The exact thresholds should be defined by organizational policy.

---

### 85. Retraining as a Trigger

Retraining can alter model behavior even if the model architecture remains unchanged.

```text id="mv385"
Same Architecture
      +
New Training Data
      ↓
Different Model Behavior
```

Therefore, retraining should have a defined validation impact assessment.

---

### 86. New Training Dataset

A new dataset may introduce:

* New populations.
* New biases.
* New errors.
* New correlations.
* New leakage risks.

Revalidation should determine whether the original assumptions remain valid.

---

### 87. Feature Changes

Adding, removing, or transforming features can materially change model behavior.

```text id="mv387"
Feature Change
      ↓
Model Behavior Change
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Targeted / Full Revalidation
```

---

### 88. Model Architecture Change

Changing the underlying algorithm or architecture generally warrants more extensive reassessment.

Examples:

* Random forest → neural network.
* Traditional ML → deep learning.
* Smaller LLM → larger LLM.
* Rule-based system → ML model.

---

### 89. Hyperparameter Changes

Hyperparameter changes may have varying levels of impact.

Examples include:

* Learning rate.
* Tree depth.
* Regularization.
* Number of layers.
* Threshold parameters.

The organization should determine whether the change is material.

---

### 90. Threshold Changes

A model artifact may remain identical while the decision threshold changes.

```text id="mv390"
Same Model
    ↓
Threshold 0.50 → 0.30
    ↓
Different Decisions
    ↓
Potential Risk Change
```

Decision thresholds should therefore be governed and assessed.

---

### 91. Data Pipeline Changes

Changes to preprocessing or feature pipelines can affect model outputs.

Examples:

* New ETL process.
* Changed normalization.
* New data source.
* Changed missing-value treatment.

These changes should be included in revalidation analysis.

---

### 92. Infrastructure Changes

Infrastructure changes may sometimes affect model behavior or performance.

Examples:

* New hardware.
* Runtime upgrade.
* Framework update.
* Container change.
* Dependency update.

---

### 93. Dependency Changes

A library or framework upgrade should be assessed for potential impact.

```text id="mv393"
Dependency Update
      ↓
Runtime Change
      ↓
Potential Model Change
      ↓
Impact Assessment
```

---

### 94. Intended-Use Change

A change in how a model is used can be more significant than a technical model change.

For example:

```text id="mv394"
Fraud Detection
     ↓
Validated

Same Model
     ↓
Credit Eligibility
     ↓
New Use Case
     ↓
Revalidation
```

---

### 95. User Population Change

A model validated for one population may not automatically be appropriate for another.

Potential changes include:

* New geography.
* New demographic population.
* New customer segment.
* New language.
* New business unit.

---

### 96. Geographic Expansion

Expanding a model into another country or region can introduce:

* Different data patterns.
* Different regulations.
* Different customer behavior.
* Different threat environments.

This may require targeted or full revalidation.

---

### 97. Regulatory Change

A new regulation or regulatory interpretation may invalidate aspects of the original validation.

```text id="mv397"
Regulatory Change
      ↓
Requirement Review
      ↓
Validation Impact
      ↓
Revalidation
```

---

### 98. Security Incident as a Trigger

A significant security incident involving a model should trigger a validation impact assessment.

Examples:

* Prompt injection incident.
* Data poisoning.
* Model extraction.
* Unauthorized modification.
* Adversarial attack.

---

### 99. Performance Degradation

Monitoring may reveal that model performance has materially declined.

```text id="mv399"
Monitoring
   ↓
Performance Decline
   ↓
Root-Cause Analysis
   ↓
Validation Impact
   ↓
Revalidation
```

---

### 100. Drift as a Trigger

Significant data or concept drift may invalidate assumptions used during original validation.

However, drift does not automatically mean the model must be revalidated.

The organization should assess:

* Magnitude.
* Duration.
* Business impact.
* Performance impact.

---

### 101. Model Retirement

Revalidation should also support decisions to retire models.

A model may become unsuitable because:

* Better alternatives exist.
* Business requirements changed.
* Risk increased.
* Data sources disappeared.
* Maintenance became impractical.

```text id="mv401"
Model
 ↓
Assessment
 ↓
Continue / Remediate / Replace / Retire
```

---

### 102. Revalidation Planning

A revalidation plan should identify:

* Trigger.
* Scope.
* Tests.
* Evidence.
* Acceptance criteria.
* Responsible parties.
* Approval authority.

---

### 103. Revalidation Evidence

Evidence should demonstrate whether the previous validation conclusion remains appropriate.

Possible evidence:

* New test results.
* Updated datasets.
* Performance comparisons.
* Security assessments.
* Drift analysis.
* Updated documentation.

---

### 104. Regression Testing

Revalidation should determine whether changes introduced unexpected regressions.

```text id="mv404"
Old Model
   ↓
Baseline

New Model
   ↓
Compare

Unexpected Regression?
   ↓
Investigate
```

---

### 105. Backward Compatibility

Where a model interacts with existing systems, revalidation should assess whether the updated model remains compatible.

Potential areas:

* APIs.
* Data schemas.
* Downstream systems.
* Decision workflows.

---

### 106. Comparative Validation

For an updated model, compare:

* Previous version.
* New version.
* Validation baseline.

Example:

```text id="mv406"
Metric             v2.0     v2.1
Recall             94%      96%
Precision           91%      88%
Latency            120ms    190ms
```

An improvement in one metric may introduce deterioration elsewhere.

---

### 107. Trade-Off Analysis

Validation should not focus on a single metric.

For example:

```text id="mv407"
Recall ↑
   +
Precision ↓
   +
Latency ↑
   ↓
Overall Risk Assessment
```

The business context determines whether the change is acceptable.

---

### 108. Champion–Challenger Validation

A **champion model** is the current production model.

A **challenger model** is a candidate replacement.

```text id="mv408"
Production Traffic
      │
 ┌────┴─────┐
 ↓          ↓
Champion  Challenger
 ↓          ↓
Performance / Risk Comparison
```

This approach supports controlled replacement decisions.

---

### 109. Shadow Validation

A challenger can operate in parallel without affecting production decisions.

This allows validation using real-world inputs.

---

### 110. Canary Validation

A new model can receive a small percentage of production traffic.

```text id="mv410"
100% Traffic
     ↓
95% Champion
5% Challenger
     ↓
Compare
     ↓
Decision
```

The percentage is illustrative.

---

### 111. Revalidation After Rollback

A rollback should also be documented and assessed.

If the organization returns to an older model:

* Confirm model integrity.
* Confirm approval status.
* Verify dependencies.
* Reassess known limitations.
* Document the rollback.

---

### 112. Temporary Approval

In urgent circumstances, a model may receive temporary approval subject to conditions.

Such approval should have:

* Defined scope.
* Risk acceptance.
* Expiration date.
* Enhanced monitoring.
* Required follow-up validation.

---

### 113. Emergency Change

Emergency model changes should not bypass governance entirely.

Instead:

```text id="mv413"
Emergency
  ↓
Rapid Risk Assessment
  ↓
Emergency Approval
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Retrospective Validation
```

---

### 114. Continuous Revalidation

For high-risk models, revalidation may be partly continuous.

Monitoring data can continuously provide evidence about whether validation assumptions remain valid.

```text id="mv414"
Validation
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Periodic Revalidation
    ↓
Updated Assurance
```

---

### 115. Periodic Revalidation

Organizations may require formal revalidation at defined intervals.

For example:

* Annual.
* Biennial.
* Risk-based intervals.

The interval should reflect model risk and change velocity.

---

### 116. Event-Based vs. Periodic Revalidation

These approaches complement each other.

**Periodic**

> Revalidate because the scheduled review date has arrived.

**Event-based**

> Revalidate because something material changed.

A high-risk model may require both.

---

### 117. Revalidation Independence

The same independence principles used during initial validation should apply to revalidation.

Material changes should not be approved solely by the team that introduced them.

---

### 118. Revalidation Findings

Findings should be categorized and tracked.

```text id="mv418"
Finding
  ↓
Severity
  ↓
Owner
  ↓
Remediation
  ↓
Retest
  ↓
Closure
```

---

### 119. Continued Approval

Revalidation should result in an explicit conclusion.

Possible outcomes:

```text id="mv419"
Continue Approval
      OR
Continue With Conditions
      OR
Remediate
      OR
Restrict
      OR
Retire
```

---

### 120. Core Principle

**Revalidation is the mechanism that prevents an old validation decision from being treated as permanently valid when the model, data, environment, purpose, threat landscape, or regulatory context has materially changed.**

The lifecycle becomes:

```text id="mv420"
INITIAL VALIDATION
        ↓
APPROVAL
        ↓
DEPLOYMENT
        ↓
MONITORING
        ↓
CHANGE / DRIFT / INCIDENT / TIME
        ↓
IMPACT ASSESSMENT
        ↓
REVALIDATION
        ↓
┌───────┼────────┬─────────┐
↓       ↓        ↓         ↓
KEEP  CONDITION REMEDIATE RETIRE
```

> **The key GRC principle: validation establishes confidence; monitoring provides continuing evidence; revalidation restores formal assurance when that confidence may have been affected.**

### 121. Model Validation Governance Framework

Model validation should operate as a formal governance process connecting technical assessment with organizational risk management.

```text id="mv421"
      ↓
```

The purpose is to ensure that model validation is not an isolated technical exercise.

### 122. Validation Policy

An enterprise policy should define the minimum expectations for model validation.

It should establish:

* Validation depth.
* Required evidence.
* Acceptance criteria.
* Revalidation triggers.
* Finding management.
* Approval authority.
* Exception handling.

A policy states **what must happen**.

A validation standard explains **how it should happen**.

For example:

```text id="mv423"

  ↓

• Required tests
• Evidence
• Metrics
• Acceptance criteria
• Documentation
```

---

### 124. Validation Procedure

The procedure converts the standard into operational steps.

```text id="mv424"

       ↓
```

### 125. Model Validation Committee

For highly consequential models, an organization may establish a model-risk or AI governance committee.

The committee may review:

* Validation results.
* Material findings.
* Risk acceptance.
* Exceptions.

* Revalidation outcomes.

### 126. Three Lines of Defense

A governance model can separate responsibilities.

The model owner should remain accountable for addressing validation findings.

The validator should:

* Evaluate findings.

### 160. Final GRC Framework

The complete **Model Validation & Revalidation** framework can be summarized as:

```text id="mv460"
                 MODEL INVENTORY
                       ↓
                RISK CLASSIFICATION
                       ↓
                 INTENDED PURPOSE
                       ↓
                 INITIAL VALIDATION
                       ↓
             ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             ↓                   ↓
         ACCEPTABLE          FINDINGS
             ↓                   ↓
          APPROVE            REMEDIATE
             ↓                   ↓
         DEPLOYMENT          RETEST
             ↓                   ↓
         MONITORING  ←──── VALIDATE
             ↓
       CHANGE / DRIFT /
       INCIDENT / TIME
             ↓
      IMPACT ASSESSMENT
             ↓
        REVALIDATION
             ↓
     ┌───────┼────────┬─────────┐
     ↓       ↓        ↓         ↓
   KEEP   CONDITION  RESTRICT  RETIRE
```

### The GRC Principle

> **Validation establishes whether an AI system is fit for its intended purpose at a defined point in its lifecycle. Monitoring provides continuing evidence of its behavior. Revalidation determines whether the original assurance remains valid when the model, data, environment, purpose, risk, or regulatory context changes.**

Therefore, the governance objective is not simply:

**“Was this model validated?”**

It is:

**“Can the organization continuously demonstrate that this model remains fit for purpose, appropriately controlled, and within its approved risk boundaries?”**
