# 09.19 — Model Risk Management

## Part 1

### 1. Model Risk Management

**Model Risk Management (MRM)** is the structured process used to identify, assess, control, monitor, and govern risks arising from the use of models.

In an AI governance context, MRM ensures that organizations do not treat a model as merely a technical asset.

A model can create risk through:

* Incorrect predictions.
* Poor-quality data.
* Inappropriate assumptions.
* Model misuse.
* Security weaknesses.
* Bias.
* Model drift.
* Poor implementation.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Human overreliance.

```text id="mrm101"
MODEL
  ↓
IDENTIFY RISK
  ↓
ASSESS RISK
  ↓
CONTROL RISK
  ↓
MONITOR RISK
  ↓
REVIEW / REVALIDATE
```

---

### 2. Why Model Risk Management Matters

A model may be technically functional but still create unacceptable organizational risk.

For example:

```text id="mrm102"
Model Accuracy = High
        ↓
But:
• Wrong use case
• Poor data
• Security weakness
• Uncontrolled deployment
        ↓
HIGH MODEL RISK
```

MRM therefore looks beyond technical performance.

---

### 3. Model Risk vs. Model Performance

These concepts should not be confused.

**Model performance** asks:

> How accurately does the model perform its task?

**Model risk** asks:

> What could happen if the model is wrong, misused, manipulated, or inadequately controlled?

A highly accurate model can still present significant model risk.

---

### 4. Model Risk Sources

Model risk can originate from multiple areas:

```text id="mrm104"
                 MODEL RISK
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
      Data         Model        Usage
       │             │             │
       ↓             ↓             ↓
   Quality       Assumptions     Misuse
   Bias          Complexity      Scope
   Leakage       Errors          Overreliance
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
    Security     Operations    Governance
```

### 6. Model Inventory

Model Risk Management begins with knowing what models exist.

A model inventory should ideally identify:

* Model name.
* Owner.
* Business purpose.
* Model type.
* Risk classification.
* Data sources.
* Deployment environment.
* Dependencies.
* Validation status.
* Monitoring status.

---

### 7. Shadow Models

An organization may discover models that were never formally registered.

Examples include:

* Developer-created ML models.
* Spreadsheet-based scoring models.
* Embedded AI functionality.
* Third-party AI services.
* Experimental models used in production.

These can create **model inventory risk**.

---

### 8. Model Classification

Models should be classified according to risk.

Potential factors include:

* Business impact.
* Decision significance.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Customer impact.
* Security sensitivity.
* Autonomy.
* Data sensitivity.
* Scale of deployment.

---

### 9. Risk-Based Model Tiers

A practical framework may use:

```text id="mrm109"
Tier 1 — Low Risk
        ↓
Tier 2 — Moderate Risk
        ↓
Tier 3 — High Risk
        ↓
Tier 4 — Critical Risk
```

Higher-risk models generally require stronger governance.

---

### 10. Inherent Model Risk

**Inherent model risk** is the risk associated with the model before considering mitigating controls.

For example:

```text id="mrm110"
Highly Complex Model
+
High-Impact Decision
+
Sensitive Data
        ↓
High Inherent Risk
```

---

### 11. Residual Model Risk

**Residual model risk** is the risk remaining after controls are applied.

```text id="mrm111"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Example:

```text id="mrm112"
Inherent Risk = High

Controls:
• Validation
• Human Review
• Monitoring
• Access Control

Residual Risk = Medium
```

Values are illustrative.

---

### 12. Risk Assessment

A model-risk assessment should consider both:

**Likelihood**

> How likely is the model-related failure?

**Impact**

> How serious would the consequences be?

```text id="mrm113"
Likelihood × Impact
        ↓
Risk Rating
```

---

### 13. Model Risk Matrix

A simple matrix may be used:

| Likelihood | Impact | Overall Risk |
| ---------- | ------ | ------------ |
| Low        | Low    | Low          |
| Medium     | Low    | Low/Medium   |
| Medium     | High   | High         |
| High       | High   | Critical     |

The actual scoring methodology should be defined by the organization.

---

### 14. Model Risk Drivers

Risk assessment can consider:

* Complexity.
* Uncertainty.
* Data quality.
* Performance.
* Explainability.
* Autonomy.
* Human oversight.
* Security exposure.
* Business criticality.

---

### 15. Model Complexity Risk

Complexity can make models more difficult to:

* Understand.
* Validate.
* Explain.
* Monitor.
* Troubleshoot.

```text id="mrm115"
Complexity
    ↓
Reduced Transparency
    ↓
Validation Difficulty
    ↓
Higher Potential Risk
```

Complexity alone does not determine risk.

---

### 16. Data Risk

Poor data can produce unreliable models.

Potential issues include:

* Incomplete data.
* Incorrect data.
* Biased data.
* Stale data.
* Unrepresentative data.
* Data leakage.

---

### 17. Assumption Risk

Models rely on assumptions about the environment.

For example:

> Historical relationships will remain sufficiently stable.

If that assumption becomes false, model performance may deteriorate.

---

### 18. Implementation Risk

A well-designed model can still fail because of incorrect implementation.

Examples:

* Wrong feature mapping.
* Incorrect threshold.
* Incorrect API integration.
* Production configuration error.

```text id="mrm118"
Correct Model
     ↓
Incorrect Implementation
     ↓
Incorrect Outcome
```

---

### 19. Usage Risk

A model can create risk when users apply it outside its approved purpose.

Examples:

* Using a fraud model for credit decisions.
* Using a recommendation model for eligibility.
* Using an internal model for external decisions.

---

### 20. Automation Risk

Greater automation can increase potential impact.

```text id="mrm120"
Model Recommendation
       ↓
Human Review
       ↓
Decision

vs.

Model
       ↓
Automatic Decision
```

The second scenario may require stronger controls.

---

### 21. Human Overreliance

Humans may accept model outputs without sufficient challenge.

This is sometimes referred to as **automation bias** or overreliance.

```text id="mrm121"
Model Output
     ↓
Human
     ↓
"No Challenge"
     ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

Human oversight must therefore be meaningful rather than purely procedural.

---

### 22. Human Oversight Risk

A control stating:

> "Human approval required"

is insufficient if the human reviewer:

* Cannot understand the decision.
* Has no authority to override it.
* Has insufficient time.
* Routinely accepts every output.

---

### 23. Model Uncertainty

Models may be uncertain about their predictions.

Risk management should consider:

* Confidence.
* Prediction uncertainty.
* Data uncertainty.
* Environmental uncertainty.

Uncertainty should be communicated appropriately to decision-makers.

---

### 24. Model Risk Appetite

An organization should define how much model risk it is willing to accept.

This is its **model risk appetite**.

For example:

```text id="mrm124"
Risk Appetite
      ↓
Acceptable Boundaries
      ↓
Model Controls
      ↓
Escalation
```

---

### 25. Risk Appetite vs. Risk Tolerance

**Risk appetite** describes the overall amount and type of risk the organization is willing to accept.

**Risk tolerance** defines more specific boundaries around acceptable variation.

Example:

```text id="mrm125"
Risk Appetite
"Low tolerance for critical model failures"

Risk Tolerance
"Critical models must maintain defined
performance and availability thresholds."
```

---

### 26. Model Risk Limits

Risk limits translate appetite into measurable boundaries.

Examples:

* Maximum acceptable error rate.
* Maximum unresolved high-risk findings.
* Maximum downtime.
* Maximum drift.
* Maximum number of unapproved model exceptions.

---

### 27. Model Risk Indicators

Model Risk Indicators (MRIs) can provide early warning.

Examples:

* Increasing false negatives.
* Increasing drift.
* Declining accuracy.
* Rising unexplained predictions.
* Increasing model exceptions.
* Expired validation.

---

### 28. Key Risk Indicators

A GRC dashboard might show:

```text id="mrm128"
Active Exceptions:              2
```

Values are illustrative.

---

### 29. Model Risk Controls

Controls may include:

```text id="mrm129"

       ↓

       ↓
```

---

### 30. Core Principle

**Model Risk Management provides the governance framework for ensuring that model-related risks are understood, controlled, monitored, and kept within the organization's approved risk appetite.**

The central lifecycle is:

```text id="mrm130"
MODEL
  ↓
INVENTORY
  ↓
CLASSIFY
  ↓
IDENTIFY RISKS
  ↓
ASSESS
  ↓
CONTROL
  ↓
VALIDATE
  ↓
APPROVE
  ↓
MONITOR
  ↓
REASSESS
  ↓
REMEDIATE / ACCEPT / RETIRE
```

The key GRC principle is:

> **Model Risk Management is not about proving that a model is perfect. It is about ensuring that the organization understands where the model can fail, understands the consequences of those failures, applies proportionate controls, and makes an explicit decision about the remaining risk.**

### 31. Model Risk Identification

Model Risk Management begins by systematically identifying how a model could create risk.

A useful approach is to examine the model across several dimensions:

```text id="mrm231"
                 MODEL
                   ↓
      ┌────────────┼────────────┐
      ↓            ↓            ↓
     DATA        MODEL        USAGE
      ↓            ↓            ↓
   QUALITY      DESIGN       PURPOSE
   LINEAGE      ASSUMPTIONS   MISUSE
      ↓            ↓            ↓
 SECURITY     IMPLEMENTATION  HUMAN
                              FACTORS
```

The objective is to identify risks **before they become incidents**.

---

### 32. Model Risk Assessment Questions

A GRC assessment should ask:

1. What can go wrong?
2. Why could it go wrong?
3. How likely is the failure?
4. What would happen if it occurred?
5. What controls currently exist?
6. Are the controls effective?
7. What residual risk remains?

---

### 33. Model Risk Taxonomy

An organization should establish a common taxonomy.

Example:

| Risk Category    | Example                            |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Data Risk        | Incomplete or biased training data |
| Model Risk       | Incorrect assumptions              |
| Performance Risk | Poor prediction accuracy           |
| Security Risk    | Model manipulation                 |
| Privacy Risk     | Sensitive data exposure            |
| Operational Risk | Production failure                 |
| Compliance Risk  | Regulatory non-compliance          |
| Governance Risk  | Unapproved model usage             |
| Human Risk       | Excessive reliance on outputs      |

A taxonomy allows risks to be consistently recorded and reported.

---

### 34. Data Risk

Data risk can arise throughout the data lifecycle.

```text id="mrm234"
Collection
   ↓
Storage
   ↓
Transformation
   ↓
Training
   ↓
Inference
   ↓
Output
```

A weakness at any stage can affect model reliability.

---

### 35. Training Data Risk

Training data can introduce:

* Bias.
* Noise.
* Errors.
* Data leakage.
* Poor representation.
* Historical patterns that are no longer appropriate.

The model may reproduce these characteristics.

---

### 36. Production Data Risk

Even if training data was appropriate, production data may differ.

```text id="mrm236"
Training Data
      ↓
Expected Distribution

Production Data
      ↓
Different Distribution
      ↓
Potential Performance Decline
```

This connects model risk to **data drift**.

---

### 37. Model Specification Risk

Model specification risk occurs when the model is designed incorrectly for the problem.

Examples:

* Wrong target variable.
* Wrong objective function.
* Incorrect assumptions.
* Inappropriate features.
* Inappropriate algorithm.

---

### 38. Assumption Risk

Assumptions should be explicitly documented.

For each important assumption:

```text id="mrm238"
ASSUMPTION
    ↓
EVIDENCE
    ↓
VALIDATION
    ↓
MONITORING
```

This converts hidden assumptions into managed risks.

---

### 39. Model Implementation Risk

Implementation risk arises when the production implementation differs from the validated model.

Example:

```text id="mrm239"
Validated Model
       ↓
Production Deployment
       ↓
Different Configuration
       ↓
Different Behavior
```

This is why model version and configuration management are important.

---

### 40. Model Use Risk

A model can be technically correct but used incorrectly.

Example:

```text id="mrm240"
Validated Purpose:
Fraud Detection

Actual Use:
Customer Eligibility

        ↓

Usage Risk
```

Governance should therefore define **approved use cases**.

---

### 41. Unauthorized Model Use

Organizations should detect models being used outside approved governance processes.

Examples:

* An employee connects confidential data to an unapproved AI service.
* A developer deploys an experimental model into production.
* A business team uses an internal model for a new decision without reassessment.

---

### 42. Third-Party Model Risk

Organizations increasingly rely on external AI providers.

Third-party model risk can arise from:

* Limited transparency.
* Unclear training data.
* Provider changes.
* Service availability.
* Security weaknesses.
* Contractual limitations.
* Unclear responsibility.

```text id="mrm242"
Third-Party Model
       ↓
Vendor Risk
       ↓
Model Risk
       ↓
Enterprise Risk
```

---

### 43. Vendor Model Governance

Before adopting a significant external model, organizations should assess:

* Provider reputation.
* Security controls.
* Data handling.
* Model documentation.
* Change management.
* Incident management.
* Availability.
* Contractual obligations.

---

### 44. Fourth-Party Risk

A provider may itself depend on another provider.

For example:

```text id="mrm244"
Your Organization
      ↓
AI Provider
      ↓
Cloud Provider
      ↓
Foundation Model / Data Provider
```

Important dependencies should be understood where practical.

---

### 45. Concentration Risk

Organizations may become heavily dependent on one AI provider or model ecosystem.

Potential consequences include:

* Vendor outage.
* Price changes.
* Model retirement.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Provider policy changes.

---

### 46. Model Dependency Risk

A model may depend on:

* External APIs.
* Data providers.
* Cloud platforms.
* Libraries.
* GPUs.
* Feature stores.

The dependency chain should be considered in risk assessments.

---

### 47. Security Risk

AI systems introduce security-specific model risks.

Examples:

* Model poisoning.
* Model theft.
* Model extraction.
* Adversarial manipulation.
* Prompt injection.
* Unauthorized access.

---

### 48. Model Poisoning

**Model poisoning** involves manipulating training data, training processes, or related inputs to influence model behavior.

```text id="mrm248"
Attacker
   ↓
Manipulated Data
   ↓
Training
   ↓
Compromised Model
```

Controls may include data provenance, integrity checks, access control, and validation.

---

### 49. Model Extraction

An attacker may attempt to reproduce a model by repeatedly querying it.

Potential risks include:

* Intellectual property loss.
* Competitive advantage loss.
* Security exposure.

---

### 50. Adversarial Manipulation

An attacker may deliberately construct inputs that cause incorrect model outputs.

```text id="mrm250"
Normal Input
    ↓
Expected Output

Manipulated Input
    ↓
Unexpected Output
```

Security testing should consider the threat model relevant to the application.

---

### 51. Privacy Risk

Model risk management should consider whether models can expose or infer sensitive information.

Potential risks include:

* Training data memorization.
* Inappropriate logging.
* Excessive data retention.
* Unauthorized access.
* Inference attacks.

---

### 52. Operational Risk

A model can create operational risk through:

* Downtime.
* Latency.
* Capacity limitations.
* Integration failures.
* Dependency failures.

For mission-critical systems, availability can be as important as prediction quality.

---

### 53. Business Continuity

High-impact AI systems should have appropriate continuity arrangements.

```text id="mrm253"
AI System Failure
      ↓
Fallback
      ↓
Manual / Alternative Process
      ↓
Business Continuity
```

---

### 54. Human Risk

Humans remain a major source of model risk.

Potential issues include:

* Automation bias.
* Poor understanding.
* Inadequate training.
* Misinterpretation.
* Unauthorized override.

---

### 55. Human-in-the-Loop

Human review can reduce model risk when properly designed.

```text id="mrm255"
Model
  ↓
Recommendation
  ↓
Qualified Reviewer
  ↓
Challenge / Approve / Reject
  ↓
Decision
```

However, human review must be meaningful.

---

### 56. Human-on-the-Loop

In some systems, humans supervise rather than review every decision.

```text id="mrm256"
Model
  ↓
Automated Decisions
  ↓
Human Monitoring
  ↓
Intervention When Required
```

This can be appropriate for high-volume systems when strong monitoring exists.

---

### 57. Human-out-of-the-Loop

Fully automated decision-making increases the importance of:

* Validation.
* Monitoring.
* Fail-safe mechanisms.
* Escalation.
* Exception handling.

```text id="mrm257"
Input
  ↓
Model
  ↓
Automatic Decision
```

---

### 58. Model Risk Controls

Controls should address identified risks.

A useful structure is:

```text id="mrm258"
Risk
 ↓
Prevent
 ↓
Detect
 ↓
Respond
 ↓
Recover
```

---

### 59. Preventive Controls

Preventive controls attempt to stop model risk before it occurs.

Examples:

* Model approval.
* Access controls.
* Data validation.
* Secure development.
* Change management.
* Segregation of duties.

---

### 60. Detective Controls

Detective controls identify problems after or while they occur.

Examples:

* Model monitoring.
* Drift detection.
* Performance monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Audit logs.
* Periodic reviews.

---

### 61. Corrective Controls

Corrective controls reduce the impact of an identified problem.

Examples:

* Model rollback.
* Retraining.
* Threshold adjustment.
* Restricting model use.
* Remediation.
* Model retirement.

---

### 62. Compensating Controls

If a primary control is unavailable, another control may partially reduce the risk.

Example:

```text id="mrm262"
Automated Model
     ↓
Known Limitation
     ↓
Human Review
     ↓
Compensating Control
```

---

### 63. Control Effectiveness

A control existing on paper does not mean the risk is controlled.

GRC should assess:

**Design effectiveness**

> Is the control appropriately designed?

**Operating effectiveness**

> Does the control actually operate as intended?

---

### 64. Control Testing

Example:

```text id="mrm264"
Policy:
All high-risk models require annual validation.

Testing:
Select sample of high-risk models.

Evidence:
Validation reports + approval records.

Result:
Control Effective / Ineffective
```

---

### 65. Residual Risk Assessment

After controls are evaluated:

```text id="mrm265"
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Control Effectiveness
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Residual risk determines whether additional treatment is required.

---

### 66. Risk Treatment

Organizations generally have several options:

```text id="mrm266"
        RISK
          ↓
 ┌────────┼────────┬─────────┐
 ↓        ↓        ↓         ↓
Avoid   Reduce   Transfer   Accept
```

---

### 67. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means eliminating the activity that creates the risk.

Example:

> Do not deploy the model for a high-impact decision because its limitations cannot be adequately controlled.

---

### 68. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction involves implementing controls.

Examples:

* Human review.
* Additional validation.
* Better data.
* Security controls.
* Restricted scope.

---

### 69. Risk Transfer

Some aspects of risk can be contractually or financially transferred.

Examples:

* Vendor contractual obligations.
* Indemnification.
* Insurance.

However, transferring contractual responsibility does not necessarily eliminate regulatory or operational responsibility.

---

### 70. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means an authorized party consciously accepts the residual risk.

It should be:

* Explicit.
* Documented.
* Authorized.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Based on sufficient information.

---

### 80. Core Principle

**Model Risk Management connects model-level technical risks with enterprise-level governance and decision-making.**

The framework is:

```text id="mrm280"
IDENTIFY
   ↓
ASSESS
   ↓
CONTROL
   ↓
VALIDATE
   ↓
APPROVE
   ↓
MONITOR
   ↓
ESCALATE
   ↓
```

The key GRC principle is:

> **A model becomes a governance concern when its potential failures can create business, security, regulatory, financial, operational, privacy, or human impact. Model Risk Management ensures those risks have an owner, a control strategy, measurable boundaries, and a defined response when those boundaries are exceeded.**

### 81. Model Risk Governance

Model Risk Management requires clear accountability across the model lifecycle.

A governance structure should establish:

* Who owns the model.
* Who owns the associated risk.
* Who validates the model.
* Who approves deployment.
* Who monitors performance.
* Who can suspend or retire the model.

```text id="mrm381"
                 GOVERNANCE
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
 Model Owner     Validator      Risk/GRC
       ↓             ↓             ↓
 Development    Challenge       Oversight
       └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                     ↓
             Approval Authority
```

---

### 82. Accountability Matrix

A RACI-style model can clarify responsibilities.

| Activity            | Model Owner | Validator | GRC | Business |
| ------------------- | ----------- | --------- | --- | -------- |
| Model Development   | R           | I         | I   | A        |
| Risk Assessment     | R           | C         | A   | C        |
| Validation          | C           | R         | A   | I        |
| Deployment Approval | C           | C         | C   | A        |
| Monitoring          | R           | C         | A   | C        |
| Revalidation        | R           | R         | A   | C        |

**R = Responsible, A = Accountable, C = Consulted, I = Informed.**

The exact allocation should reflect the organization's governance model.

---

### 83. Model Owner

The **model owner** is accountable for ensuring that the model remains appropriately managed throughout its lifecycle.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining documentation.
* Managing model changes.
* Addressing findings.
* Ensuring monitoring.
* Initiating revalidation.
* Escalating material risks.

---

### 84. Business Owner

The business owner understands the consequences of model decisions within the business process.

The business owner may determine:

* Whether the model provides sufficient business value.
* Whether residual risk is acceptable.
* Whether alternative processes are available.

---

### 85. Model Validator

The validator provides independent challenge and assurance.

The validator should assess:

* Methodology.
* Data.
* Performance.
* Assumptions.
* Limitations.
* Controls.
* Risk.

---

### 86. GRC Function

GRC provides governance oversight and helps ensure that model risk is managed consistently with enterprise requirements.

Typical responsibilities include:

* Policy.
* Standards.
* Risk classification.
* Control requirements.
* Exception management.
* Reporting.
* Escalation.

---

### 87. Senior Management

Senior management should understand material model risks and ensure that significant risks receive appropriate decisions and resources.

```text id="mrm387"
Technical Findings
       ↓
Risk Interpretation
       ↓
Management Decision
```

---

### 88. Board-Level Oversight

For organizations with significant AI exposure, board or board-committee oversight may be appropriate for material AI/model risks.

The board does not need to understand every technical detail.

## The Core GRC Mental Model

When discussing **Model Risk Management** in a GRC interview or assessment, think in five questions:

### **1. What can go wrong?**

Identify model, data, security, operational, human, compliance, and governance risks.

### **2. How serious is it?**

Assess likelihood, impact, inherent risk, and residual risk.

### **3. What controls exist?**

Evaluate preventive, detective, corrective, and compensating controls.

### **4. Who is accountable?**

Establish model ownership, risk ownership, validation independence, and approval authority.

### **5. What happens when conditions change?**

Monitor, escalate, remediate, revalidate, restrict, suspend, or retire the model.

> **The ultimate objective of Model Risk Management is not to eliminate every possible model error. It is to create a controlled environment in which model risks are identified, measured, owned, monitored, challenged, and kept within the organization's approved risk appetite throughout the entire lifecycle.**
