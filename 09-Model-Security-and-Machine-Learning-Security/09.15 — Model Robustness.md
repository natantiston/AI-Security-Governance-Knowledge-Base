# 09.15 — Model Robustness

## Part 1

### 1. Model Robustness

**Model robustness** is the ability of an AI/ML model to maintain acceptable and reliable behavior when exposed to variations, disturbances, unexpected inputs, or conditions that differ from the ideal conditions used during development.

A robust model should not experience unacceptable degradation simply because its operating environment changes within reasonably foreseeable boundaries.

---

### 2. Robustness as a Security Property

Robustness is not only a machine-learning performance characteristic.

For security-sensitive systems, robustness contributes to:

* Resistance to manipulation.
* Reliable threat detection.
* Stable decision-making.
* Reduced attack success.
* Operational resilience.

```text id="mr101"
Expected Input
      ↓
    MODEL
      ↓
Expected Output

Variation / Disturbance
      ↓
    MODEL
      ↓
Acceptable Behavior
```

---

### 3. Robustness vs. Accuracy

**Accuracy** measures how often a model produces correct predictions under a defined evaluation dataset.

**Robustness** examines how well the model maintains acceptable behavior when conditions change.

A model can therefore have:

```text id="mr102"
High Accuracy
      +
Low Robustness
```

Such a model may perform well in testing but degrade significantly under manipulated or unfamiliar conditions.

---

### 4. Robustness Dimensions

Model robustness can be evaluated across multiple dimensions:

* Input robustness.
* Distribution robustness.
* Adversarial robustness.
* Noise robustness.
* Data robustness.
* Environmental robustness.
* Operational robustness.
* Temporal robustness.

---

### 5. Input Robustness

Input robustness concerns whether the model remains reliable when inputs vary within expected or reasonably foreseeable conditions.

Examples include:

* Formatting differences.
* Minor measurement changes.
* Missing values.
* Noise.
* Different input ordering.

---

### 6. Noise Robustness

Real-world data often contains noise.

Examples include:

* Sensor errors.
* Transmission errors.
* Measurement inaccuracies.
* OCR errors.
* Audio interference.
* Network-related corruption.

A robust model should tolerate reasonable levels of noise.

---

### 7. Example of Noise Sensitivity

Consider an image-classification model.

```text id="mr107"
Original Image
      ↓
   Model
      ↓
Correct Classification
```

A small amount of realistic noise is introduced:

```text id="mr108"
Modified Image
      ↓
   Model
      ↓
Incorrect Classification
```

If minor noise consistently causes severe failures, the model may have a robustness weakness.

---

### 8. Distribution Robustness

A model is trained on a particular data distribution.

Production data may differ.

```text id="mr109"
Training Data
      ↓
    Model
      ↓
Production Data
      ↓
Potential Distribution Difference
```

The model should be evaluated against realistic variations in its production environment.

---

### 9. Distribution Shift

A **distribution shift** occurs when the characteristics of production data differ from those present during training.

Examples include:

* New customer behavior.
* New network traffic patterns.
* Different device types.
* Seasonal changes.
* Changing market conditions.

---

### 10. Robustness to Distribution Shift

A model that performs well only on historical data may become unreliable when the environment changes.

Security monitoring models are particularly sensitive to changing attacker behavior.

---

### 11. Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between inputs and the target outcome changes over time.

For example:

```text id="mr111"
Historical Behavior
      ↓
Model Relationship
      ↓
Environment Changes
      ↓
Old Relationship No Longer Holds
```

This can reduce model effectiveness.

---

### 12. Data Drift vs. Concept Drift

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Data drift:**

The statistical characteristics of the input data change.

**Concept drift:**

The underlying relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

Both can affect robustness.

---

### 13. Adversarial Robustness

Adversarial robustness concerns the model's resistance to intentionally manipulated inputs designed to cause incorrect behavior.

```text id="mr113"
Legitimate Input
      ↓
Attacker Modification
      ↓
Adversarial Input
      ↓
Model
      ↓
Incorrect Output
```

---

### 14. Adversarial Examples

An adversarial example is an input intentionally modified to cause a model to make an incorrect prediction or classification.

The modification may be small or carefully constructed while remaining difficult to detect.

---

### 15. Evasion

An evasion attack attempts to manipulate an input at inference time so that the model fails to recognize malicious activity.

Example:

```text id="mr115"
Malicious Activity
      ↓
Input Manipulation
      ↓
Model
      ↓
"Benign"
```

This is particularly relevant to cybersecurity detection models.

---

### 16. Robustness and Detection Systems

Security models should be evaluated against realistic attacker behavior.

For example, a malware classifier should not be evaluated only against known malware samples.

Testing should also consider whether small or meaningful changes to malicious samples can bypass detection.

---

### 17. Perturbation

A perturbation is a modification applied to an input.

Perturbations may be:

* Random.
* Structured.
* Natural.
* Adversarial.

Security testing should distinguish between ordinary environmental variation and deliberately engineered attacks.

---

### 18. Perturbation Magnitude

The size of a perturbation matters.

A model that fails only after extreme changes may have a different risk profile from one that fails after minimal changes.

```text id="mr118"
Small Change
     ↓
Stable Output
     ↓
Robust

Small Change
     ↓
Major Output Change
     ↓
Potential Weakness
```

---

### 19. Local Robustness

Local robustness examines model behavior around a specific input.

The question is:

> Would reasonably small changes around this input produce an unacceptable change in the prediction?

---

### 20. Global Robustness

Global robustness considers behavior across a broader portion of the input space.

It asks whether the model consistently maintains acceptable behavior across many different conditions.

---

### 21. Certified Robustness

Some techniques attempt to provide formal guarantees about model behavior within defined conditions.

For example, a model may be evaluated to determine whether certain bounded input changes can alter its classification.

Certified robustness can provide stronger assurance than empirical testing alone, although its applicability depends on the model and method.

---

### 22. Empirical Robustness

Empirical robustness is demonstrated through testing.

```text id="mr122"
Test Inputs
   ↓
Variations
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Observed Results
   ↓
Robustness Assessment
```

It provides evidence based on tested scenarios rather than a universal mathematical guarantee.

---

### 23. Robustness Boundaries

No model is infinitely robust.

The objective is to establish reasonable operating boundaries.

Examples include:

* Acceptable input ranges.
* Maximum noise levels.
* Expected data distributions.
* Supported environments.

---

### 24. Operating Envelope

An **operating envelope** defines the conditions under which the model is expected to perform reliably.

```text id="mr124"
             MODEL
        ┌──────────────┐
        │ Valid        │
        │ Operating    │
        │ Envelope     │
        └──────────────┘
```

Inputs outside this envelope may require additional controls.

---

### 25. Out-of-Distribution Detection

Models can be supported by mechanisms that identify inputs substantially different from expected training or operational data.

```text id="mr125"
Input
 ↓
OOD Assessment
 ├── Normal → Model
 └── OOD → Reject / Review / Alternate Path
```

---

### 26. Robustness and Confidence

Confidence scores should not be treated as a complete indicator of robustness.

A model may produce a high-confidence prediction even for an unfamiliar input.

Therefore:

**High confidence ≠ guaranteed robustness.**

---

### 27. Calibration

Calibration concerns whether predicted confidence corresponds reasonably well to actual likelihood of correctness.

A well-calibrated model can provide more useful confidence information.

---

### 28. Robustness Testing Dataset

A robustness test dataset should contain more than ordinary validation examples.

It can include:

* Noisy examples.
* Boundary cases.
* Rare cases.
* Out-of-distribution samples.
* Realistic environmental variations.
* Adversarial samples.

---

### 29. Dataset Diversity

A diverse evaluation dataset helps expose weaknesses that may remain hidden in narrow testing environments.

Diversity can involve:

* Different users.
* Different devices.
* Different environments.
* Different operating conditions.
* Different data sources.

---

### 30. Edge Cases

Edge cases are unusual but plausible scenarios near the boundaries of expected operation.

Security teams should explicitly test them because attackers may deliberately search for unusual conditions.

---

### 31. Stress Cases

Stress cases intentionally push the model toward difficult operating conditions.

Examples include:

* High-volume input.
* Rare combinations.
* Extreme but valid values.
* Significant environmental noise.

---

### 32. Robustness Regression

A model update can unintentionally reduce robustness.

```text id="mr132"
Model v1
   ↓
Robustness Testing
   ↓
Model v2
   ↓
Regression Testing
```

Previously identified robustness tests should therefore be repeated after important changes.

---

### 33. Model Updates

Robustness should be reassessed after:

* Retraining.
* Fine-tuning.
* Architecture changes.
* Dataset changes.
* Feature changes.
* Major dependency changes.

---

### 34. Robustness Baseline

Organizations should establish a baseline for acceptable model behavior.

The baseline can include:

* Minimum accuracy under defined conditions.
* Maximum degradation.
* Maximum acceptable attack success.
* Required confidence behavior.

---

### 35. Degradation Threshold

A model may naturally lose some performance under difficult conditions.

The organization should define how much degradation is acceptable.

```text id="mr135"
Normal Performance
       ↓
Expected Degradation
       ↓
Acceptable

Excessive Degradation
       ↓
Security / Reliability Concern
```

---

### 36. Security-Critical Thresholds

For security-sensitive applications, thresholds should reflect the consequence of model failure.

A small performance reduction may be acceptable for one application but unacceptable for another.

---

### 37. Robustness and Business Risk

Robustness should ultimately be connected to business impact.

```text id="mr137"
Robustness Weakness
      ↓
Model Failure
      ↓
Incorrect Decision
      ↓
Business Impact
```

---

### 38. Model Robustness Risk Assessment

A practical assessment can consider:

| Factor         | Question                                           |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Exposure       | Who can influence inputs?                          |
| Sensitivity    | How much can inputs change outputs?                |
| Criticality    | How important is the decision?                     |
| Exploitability | Can an attacker deliberately trigger the weakness? |
| Impact         | What happens if the model fails?                   |
| Detection      | Can abnormal behavior be detected?                 |

---

### 39. Defense-in-Depth

Robustness should not be the only protection.

Additional controls may include:

* Input validation.
* Rate limiting.
* Authentication.
* Output validation.
* Business rules.
* Human review.
* Monitoring.

---

### 40. Core Principle

**Model robustness is the ability of an AI/ML system to maintain acceptable behavior when confronted with realistic variation, unexpected conditions, or deliberate manipulation within its relevant threat environment.**

```text id="mr140"
EXPECTED CONDITIONS
        ↓
VARIATIONS
        ↓
UNEXPECTED CONDITIONS
        ↓
ADVERSARIAL CONDITIONS
        ↓
ROBUSTNESS ASSESSMENT
```

A security-focused robustness program should therefore evaluate not only **how accurate a model is under normal conditions**, but also **how gracefully and securely it behaves when those conditions change**.

## Part 2

### 41. Robustness Threat Model

Model robustness should be assessed against the conditions and actors capable of causing model behavior to deteriorate.

A practical threat model considers:

```text id="mr201"
Threat Source
     ↓
Input / Environment
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Behavior Change
     ↓
Security / Business Impact
```

Threat sources may include:

* Natural environmental variation.
* Faulty data sources.
* Malicious users.
* Automated attackers.
* Compromised systems.
* Changing business conditions.

---

### 42. Robustness Failure Modes

A robustness failure occurs when the model's behavior becomes unacceptable under a condition that should have been reasonably anticipated.

Examples include:

* Significant accuracy degradation.
* Incorrect classification.
* Unstable predictions.
* Excessive confidence in incorrect outputs.
* Failure to detect malicious activity.
* Unsafe automated decisions.

---

### 43. Failure Severity

Not every robustness failure represents the same level of risk.

Severity should consider:

* Frequency.
* Exploitability.
* Model criticality.
* Affected population.
* Potential financial impact.
* Security consequences.

---

### 44. Natural vs. Adversarial Degradation

Robustness testing should distinguish between:

**Natural degradation**

Caused by normal environmental or operational variation.

**Adversarial degradation**

Caused intentionally by an attacker attempting to manipulate model behavior.

```text id="mr204"
Environmental Change ──→ Natural Degradation

Attacker Action ───────→ Adversarial Degradation
```

The defensive requirements may differ.

---

### 45. Threat Modeling Input Control

The degree of attacker control over inputs is an important factor.

```text id="mr205"
No Input Control
      ↓
Limited Input Control
      ↓
Partial Input Control
      ↓
Full Input Control
```

A model exposed to attacker-controlled inputs generally requires stronger robustness testing.

---

### 46. Direct Input Control

An attacker has direct influence when they can intentionally determine or manipulate the data submitted to the model.

Examples include:

* User-submitted text.
* Uploaded files.
* Network packets.
* Transaction attributes.
* Images.

---

### 47. Indirect Input Control

An attacker may influence model inputs indirectly.

For example:

```text id="mr207"
Attacker
   ↓
External System
   ↓
Data Feed
   ↓
Feature Pipeline
   ↓
Model
```

Indirect influence can be difficult to identify.

---

### 48. Environmental Robustness

A model may operate in environments that differ from its development environment.

Examples include:

* Different hardware.
* Different network conditions.
* Different sensors.
* Different geographic regions.
* Different user populations.

---

### 49. Hardware Variation

Models involving sensors, cameras, or edge devices may experience changes caused by hardware characteristics.

Examples include:

* Sensor calibration.
* Camera quality.
* Microphone characteristics.
* Device generation.

---

### 50. Environmental Noise

Environmental conditions may introduce noise into model inputs.

Examples:

* Background noise.
* Lighting changes.
* Motion.
* Signal interference.
* Sensor degradation.

---

### 51. Robustness to Missing Data

Production systems may encounter missing features.

The model should have defined behavior for missing or incomplete information.

```text id="mr211"
Complete Input
      ↓
    Model
      ↓
Prediction

Incomplete Input
      ↓
Validation / Handling
      ↓
Controlled Behavior
```

---

### 52. Missing-Feature Handling

Possible approaches include:

* Rejecting the input.
* Imputing a value.
* Requesting additional information.
* Routing to an alternative model.
* Escalating to human review.

The appropriate approach depends on the application.

---

### 53. Data Corruption

Corrupted data can cause model performance to deteriorate.

Potential sources include:

* Faulty sensors.
* Transmission errors.
* Software bugs.
* Storage corruption.
* Malicious manipulation.

---

### 54. Data Integrity

Model robustness therefore depends partly on data integrity.

```text id="mr214"
Data Integrity
      ↓
Reliable Features
      ↓
Reliable Model Input
      ↓
More Reliable Output
```

---

### 55. Feature Integrity

Critical features should have appropriate controls against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

* Validation.
* Source authentication.
* Integrity checks.
* Access control.
* Monitoring.

---

### 56. Feature Availability

A feature can also become unavailable.

The model should have a defined response when critical features cannot be obtained.

---

### 57. Feature Dependency

Some models rely heavily on a small number of features.

This creates concentration risk.

```text id="mr217"
Feature A ──┐
Feature B ──┼──→ Model
Feature C ──┘
```

If Feature A is highly influential and attacker-controlled, the model may have a significant robustness weakness.

---

### 58. Feature Sensitivity Testing

Security teams can test how output changes when individual features are modified.

This can identify:

* Highly sensitive features.
* Unexpected dependencies.
* Manipulation opportunities.

---

### 59. Correlated Features

Two or more features may contain similar information.

Removing or changing one feature may therefore have less effect than expected.

Robustness analysis should consider relationships among features rather than examining them only individually.

---

### 60. Feature Interaction

Some vulnerabilities arise from combinations of features rather than one feature alone.

```text id="mr220"
Feature A
    +
Feature B
    ↓
Unexpected Model Behavior
```

Testing should include important feature combinations.

---

### 61. Data Distribution Testing

Robustness assessments should compare training, validation, and production distributions.

```text id="mr221"
Training Distribution
        │
        ├── Validation Distribution
        │
        └── Production Distribution
```

Significant differences should trigger investigation.

---

### 62. Distribution Monitoring

Production systems can monitor statistical characteristics of model inputs.

Potential indicators include:

* Feature frequency.
* Value ranges.
* Category distributions.
* Missing-value rates.

---

### 63. Distribution Shift Detection

A significant change may indicate:

* Legitimate business change.
* Data pipeline change.
* New population.
* Model degradation.
* Attack activity.

The detection itself does not prove malicious activity.

---

### 64. Robustness Under Shift

When a distribution shift is detected, organizations should assess whether model performance remains within acceptable limits.

```text id="mr224"
Distribution Shift
       ↓
Performance Assessment
       ↓
Within Threshold?
   ↙          ↘
 YES          NO
 ↓             ↓
Continue      Remediate
```

---

### 65. Temporal Robustness

Model behavior can change as time passes.

Factors include:

* Changing user behavior.
* New technologies.
* New threats.
* Economic conditions.
* Seasonal effects.

---

### 66. Seasonality

Some models naturally encounter seasonal patterns.

Examples include:

* Retail demand.
* Fraud patterns.
* Network traffic.
* Weather-related activity.

Testing should distinguish expected seasonality from abnormal changes.

---

### 67. Threat Evolution

Security models face an additional challenge: attackers adapt.

```text id="mr227"
Defensive Model
      ↓
Attacker Adapts
      ↓
New Attack Pattern
      ↓
Model Performance Changes
```

A robust security model therefore requires continuing evaluation.

---

### 68. Adaptive Attackers

Attackers may learn from the behavior of the defensive system.

They may:

* Probe responses.
* Identify thresholds.
* Modify inputs.
* Change attack patterns.

This creates an ongoing robustness challenge.

---

### 69. Black-Box Robustness

In a black-box environment, attackers may not have access to:

* Model architecture.
* Parameters.
* Training data.

They may still learn from model outputs.

---

### 70. Query-Based Testing

Repeated queries can reveal how model behavior changes across different inputs.

Security testing should determine whether such interaction can expose exploitable weaknesses.

---

### 71. White-Box Robustness

In a white-box scenario, the tester has greater knowledge of the model.

Potentially available information may include:

* Architecture.
* Parameters.
* Training process.
* Features.

White-box testing can expose weaknesses that black-box testing may not identify.

---

### 72. Gray-Box Testing

Gray-box testing assumes partial knowledge.

This can represent realistic scenarios where an attacker has obtained some information about the system.

---

### 73. Robustness Test Coverage

A mature testing program should cover multiple conditions.

```text id="mr233"
Normal
  ↓
Boundary
  ↓
Noisy
  ↓
Missing Data
  ↓
Distribution Shift
  ↓
Adversarial
```

---

### 74. Test Case Design

Each test should define:

* Input condition.
* Expected behavior.
* Observed behavior.
* Acceptance criteria.
* Security impact.

---

### 75. Golden Test Set

A controlled set of known test cases can provide a stable reference.

The set should contain representative and security-relevant scenarios.

---

### 76. Adversarial Test Set

A separate test set can contain deliberately challenging inputs.

This allows organizations to measure model behavior under attack-oriented conditions.

---

### 77. Holdout Security Dataset

Security testing should avoid relying entirely on data used during training.

A controlled holdout dataset can provide more independent evidence.

---

### 78. Data Leakage in Robustness Testing

Test data should be protected from becoming part of future training datasets unintentionally.

Otherwise, subsequent evaluations may become misleading.

---

### 79. Test Reproducibility

Robustness tests should be reproducible where practical.

Testing should record:

* Model version.
* Dataset version.
* Test configuration.
* Environment.
* Results.

---

### 80. Robustness Benchmark

A benchmark can establish expected performance under defined conditions.

```text id="mr240"
Condition
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Performance
   ↓
Benchmark
   ↓
Pass / Fail
```

---

### 81. Robustness Score

Organizations may develop an internal robustness score based on multiple measurements.

For example:

```text id="mr241"
Input Robustness
       +
Noise Robustness
       +
Distribution Robustness
       +
Adversarial Robustness
       +
Operational Robustness
```

The scoring methodology should be documented and consistently applied.

---

### 82. Weighted Robustness

Different dimensions may carry different importance.

For a security detection model:

```text
Adversarial Robustness
        >
Normal-Condition Accuracy
```

The weighting should reflect the business threat model.

---

### 83. Robustness Acceptance Criteria

A model should have defined conditions for acceptance.

Examples include:

* Maximum performance degradation.
* Maximum false-negative increase.
* Maximum attack success rate.
* Maximum response instability.

---

### 84. Security Margin

Organizations may establish a safety margin rather than accepting performance exactly at the minimum threshold.

```text id="mr244"
Required Minimum
       │
       ↓
Actual Performance
       │
       ↓
Safety Margin
```

A safety margin can provide resilience against small changes.

---

### 85. Robustness vs. Resilience

These concepts are related but different.

**Robustness** concerns maintaining acceptable behavior despite disturbances.

**Resilience** concerns the ability to recover after disruption or failure.

```text id="mr245"
Disturbance
   ↓
Robustness → Continue Operating
   ↓
Failure
   ↓
Resilience → Recover
```

---

### 86. Robustness vs. Reliability

**Reliability** generally concerns consistent correct operation under defined conditions.

**Robustness** focuses more specifically on maintaining acceptable behavior when conditions vary or become challenging.

---

### 87. Robustness vs. Security

Security focuses on protection against malicious actions and unacceptable risk.

Robustness contributes to security but does not replace:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Encryption.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.

---

### 88. Defense-in-Depth

A robust model should still operate within a layered security architecture.

```text id="mr248"
Secure Input
    ↓
Robust Model
    ↓
Output Validation
    ↓
Business Controls
    ↓
Monitoring
```

---

### 89. Robustness Documentation

Model documentation should record relevant robustness characteristics.

Examples include:

* Known limitations.
* Tested conditions.
* Untested conditions.
* Failure modes.
* Performance thresholds.
* Security assumptions.

---

### 90. Core Principle

**Robustness must be defined in relation to the model's intended operating environment and threat model. A model is not simply "robust" or "not robust"; it has specific strengths, limitations, and boundaries under particular conditions.**

```text id="mr250"
MODEL
  ↓
OPERATING ENVIRONMENT
  ↓
EXPECTED VARIATION
  ↓
ADVERSARIAL CONDITIONS
  ↓
OBSERVED BEHAVIOR
  ↓
RISK ASSESSMENT
```

The most meaningful robustness assessment therefore asks:

**How does the model behave when the conditions it encounters are no longer identical to the conditions under which it was developed?**

### 91. Robustness Evaluation Strategy

A mature robustness program should evaluate the model systematically rather than relying on a single test.

```text id="mrt301"
Define Threat Model
        ↓
Define Operating Envelope
        ↓
Create Test Conditions
        ↓
Execute Tests
        ↓
Measure Degradation
        ↓
Assess Risk
        ↓
Remediate
```

---

### 92. Define the Operating Envelope

Before testing robustness, the organization should establish the conditions under which the model is expected to operate.

This may include:

* Input ranges.
* Supported formats.
* Expected data sources.
* Environmental conditions.
* User populations.
* Performance requirements.

---

### 93. Normal Operating Conditions

Testing should establish a baseline under normal conditions.

The baseline provides a reference point for determining whether performance deteriorates under more difficult conditions.

---

### 94. Boundary Conditions

Testing should examine inputs near the limits of the defined operating envelope.

Examples include:

* Minimum values.
* Maximum values.
* Threshold values.
* Rare but valid combinations.

---

### 95. Beyond-Boundary Testing

Testing can also examine what happens immediately outside the expected operating envelope.

The objective is not necessarily to make the model operate everywhere, but to determine whether it fails in a controlled manner.

---

### 96. Graceful Degradation

A robust system should ideally degrade gradually rather than suddenly.

```text id="mrt306"
Increasing Input Difficulty
          ↓
Performance
   ──────────────╲
                  ╲
                   ╲
                    ╲
```

A sudden collapse may indicate a significant robustness weakness.

---

### 97. Catastrophic Failure

A catastrophic failure occurs when a relatively small change produces a disproportionately severe consequence.

For example:

```text id="mrt307"
Minor Input Change
        ↓
Major Prediction Change
        ↓
Critical Business Decision
```

Such behavior requires careful investigation.

---

### 98. Robustness Curves

Organizations can evaluate model performance across increasing levels of disturbance.

For example:

```text id="mrt308"
Disturbance Level
0% → 1% → 2% → 5% → 10%

Performance
100 → 98 → 96 → 90 → 70
```

The pattern can reveal how rapidly the model deteriorates.

---

### 99. Performance Degradation

Performance degradation should be measured using metrics appropriate to the model.

Possible metrics include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* False-positive rate.
* False-negative rate.
* Mean absolute error.

---

### 100. Security-Specific Degradation

For security applications, security outcomes may be more important than general accuracy.

For example:

```text id="mrt310"
Normal Detection Rate
        ↓
Adversarial Detection Rate
        ↓
Difference
        ↓
Security Degradation
```

---

### 101. False-Negative Analysis

For security detection models, robustness testing should examine whether disturbances disproportionately increase false negatives.

A small change that causes many malicious activities to be classified as benign may represent a serious security concern.

---

### 102. False-Positive Analysis

Robustness testing should also determine whether environmental changes cause excessive false positives.

This can overwhelm security operations and reduce trust in the system.

---

### 103. Robustness and Class Imbalance

Security datasets may contain significantly more legitimate events than malicious events.

A model can therefore appear highly accurate while performing poorly against the minority class.

Robustness testing should account for class imbalance.

---

### 104. Recall Under Attack

For detection systems, recall under adversarial or difficult conditions can be particularly important.

```text id="mrt314"
Normal Recall
     ↓
Attack Condition
     ↓
Recall Under Attack
     ↓
Compare
```

---

### 105. Precision Under Attack

Precision should also be measured under changed conditions to determine whether robustness failures cause excessive false alarms.

---

### 106. Robustness Across Populations

Models should be evaluated across relevant populations rather than only aggregate results.

Examples may include:

* Different geographic regions.
* Different device types.
* Different customer segments.
* Different network environments.

---

### 107. Subgroup Robustness

Aggregate performance can hide weaknesses affecting specific groups.

```text id="mrt317"
Overall Performance: High
        ↓
 ┌──────┼──────┐
 ↓      ↓      ↓
Group A Group B Group C
High    Low     High
```

Security and governance teams should investigate material differences.

---

### 108. Robustness Across Data Sources

A model may behave differently depending on where its input originates.

Testing can compare:

* Internal data.
* External data.
* Partner data.
* Different sensors.
* Different collection systems.

---

### 109. Sensor Robustness

Sensor-based models should be tested against realistic changes such as:

* Sensor noise.
* Calibration differences.
* Environmental interference.
* Partial sensor failure.

---

### 110. Image Robustness

Computer-vision systems may need testing against:

* Lighting changes.
* Rotation.
* Scaling.
* Blur.
* Occlusion.
* Compression.

---

### 111. Audio Robustness

Audio models may encounter:

* Background noise.
* Echo.
* Different microphones.
* Speech variation.
* Recording quality differences.

---

### 112. Text Robustness

Natural-language models may encounter:

* Spelling variation.
* Formatting differences.
* Abbreviations.
* Unusual phrasing.
* Multilingual inputs.

Security testing should also consider deliberately crafted inputs.

---

### 113. Tabular Model Robustness

Tabular models can be sensitive to:

* Missing fields.
* Outliers.
* Category changes.
* Numerical scaling.
* Feature manipulation.

---

### 114. Time-Series Robustness

Time-series models should be tested against:

* Missing intervals.
* Delayed data.
* Sudden spikes.
* Seasonal changes.
* Structural changes.

---

### 115. Robustness Across Environments

A model may behave differently in:

* Development.
* Testing.
* Staging.
* Production.

The deployment pipeline should therefore maintain consistency where practical.

---

### 116. Environment Drift

Differences between environments can produce unexpected model behavior.

Examples include:

* Different libraries.
* Different preprocessing.
* Different hardware.
* Different model configuration.

---

### 117. Model Runtime Consistency

Production should use controlled versions of:

* Model artifact.
* Runtime.
* Framework.
* Dependencies.
* Configuration.

This helps reduce unexpected behavioral differences.

---

### 118. Robustness and Reproducibility

If a robustness result cannot be reproduced, it is difficult to establish reliable assurance.

Testing should therefore capture:

* Model version.
* Test dataset.
* Environment.
* Configuration.
* Random seeds where relevant.
* Results.

---

### 119. Repeatability

A robustness test should produce reasonably consistent conclusions when repeated under the same conditions.

Large unexplained variation may itself indicate instability.

---

### 120. Stochastic Models

Some models or inference processes may involve randomness.

Security teams should determine whether randomness can materially change security outcomes.

---

### 121. Randomness Control

Where appropriate, testing can use controlled random seeds to improve reproducibility.

However, production behavior should also be assessed under its actual operating configuration.

---

### 122. Robustness Stress Testing

Stress testing should evaluate the model under extreme but plausible conditions.

```text id="mrt322"
Normal
  ↓
Heavy
  ↓
Extreme
  ↓
Failure Point
```

The objective is to understand the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable operation.

---

### 123. Resource Stress

Resource stress can involve:

* High request rates.
* Large inputs.
* High concurrency.
* Limited compute.
* Memory pressure.

---

### 124. Latency Robustness

A model may remain accurate while becoming too slow to support the business process.

Robustness therefore also has an operational dimension.

---

### 125. Timeout Handling

The system should define what happens when inference exceeds the acceptable response time.

Possible responses include:

* Retry.
* Fallback.
* Queue.
* Human review.
* Controlled failure.

---

### 126. Robustness Under Partial Failure

Critical model systems should be tested when dependencies fail.

Examples include:

```text id="mrt326"
Feature Store Failure
        ↓
Model Input Incomplete

Model Service Failure
        ↓
Fallback Required

External API Failure
        ↓
Alternative Data Source
```

---

### 127. Fallback Models

Some critical applications may maintain an alternative model.

The fallback should itself be evaluated for security and robustness.

---

### 128. Fallback Decision Logic

Fallback mechanisms should be explicit.

```text id="mrt328"
Primary Model
     ↓
Available?
 ┌───┴───┐
YES     NO
 ↓       ↓
Use    Fallback
```

---

### 129. Fallback Security

An attacker should not be able to intentionally trigger a weaker fallback path simply by manipulating inputs.

Fallback logic should therefore be included in security testing.

---

### 130. Human Fallback

Where no alternative model exists, the system may route certain cases to human review.

This should be treated as a designed control rather than an informal workaround.

---

### 131. Robustness and Human Review

Human review can reduce the impact of uncertain predictions, but reviewers may themselves be affected by:

* Automation bias.
* Alert fatigue.
* Excessive workload.
* Poor explanation quality.

---

### 132. Human Override Testing

Organizations should verify that authorized humans can override model decisions when required.

The override process should be:

* Accessible.
* Logged.
* Authorized.
* Auditable.

---

### 133. Model Robustness and Safety Controls

For high-impact systems, robustness should be complemented by independent controls.

Examples include:

* Rule-based validation.
* Transaction limits.
* Approval thresholds.
* Segregation of duties.

---

### 134. Independent Decision Controls

A critical business action should not necessarily depend exclusively on one model prediction.

```text id="mrt334"
Model
  +
Business Rules
  +
Context
  ↓
Decision
```

---

### 135. Ensemble Models

Some systems use multiple models to reduce dependence on a single model.

```text id="mrt335"
Model A ──┐
Model B ──┼──→ Aggregation → Decision
Model C ──┘
```

Ensembles can improve robustness in some situations but introduce additional complexity and dependencies.

---

### 136. Ensemble Failure

If multiple models share the same training data, architecture, or dependency, they may share common weaknesses.

An ensemble does not automatically eliminate systemic vulnerabilities.

---

### 137. Model Diversity

Where appropriate, diversity among models can reduce correlated failure.

Diversity may involve:

* Different algorithms.
* Different training datasets.
* Different feature sets.

---

### 138. Correlated Model Failures

If multiple models fail under the same condition, the apparent redundancy may provide little additional protection.

This should be evaluated during robustness testing.

---

### 139. Robustness Under Distribution Shift

When production data changes, the organization should determine whether:

1. The shift is expected.
2. The model remains within acceptable performance.
3. Security risk has increased.
4. Retraining is required.

---

### 140. Core Principle

**Robustness assurance requires testing the model across realistic variations, operational stresses, adversarial conditions, and failure scenarios while measuring whether degradation remains within acceptable security and business limits.**

```text id="mrt340"
BASELINE
   ↓
BOUNDARY TESTING
   ↓
STRESS TESTING
   ↓
DISTRIBUTION SHIFT
   ↓
ADVERSARIAL TESTING
   ↓
FAILURE / FALLBACK TESTING
   ↓
RISK ASSESSMENT
```

The purpose is not to prove that the model will never fail. The objective is to establish **where it fails, how severely it fails, whether those failures are exploitable, and whether the surrounding controls prevent those failures from becoming unacceptable business or security outcomes.**

### 200. Core Principle

**Model robustness is an assurance discipline focused on maintaining acceptable model behavior across expected variation, environmental change, operational stress, and adversarial conditions.**

```text id="mrg200"
DEFINE
  ↓
TEST
  ↓
MEASURE
  ↓
APPROVE
  ↓
DEPLOY
  ↓
MONITOR
  ↓
DETECT
  ↓
REMEDIATE
  ↓
REVALIDATE
```

The enterprise objective is not to establish that a model can never fail. It is to establish **clear boundaries for acceptable behavior, reliable evidence of robustness within those boundaries, effective detection when those boundaries are crossed, and controlled mechanisms for reducing the resulting risk.**
