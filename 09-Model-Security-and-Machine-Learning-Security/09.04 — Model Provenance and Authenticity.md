# 09.04 — Model Provenance and Authenticity

## Part 1

### 1. Model Provenance and Authenticity

Model provenance and authenticity establish **where a model came from, how it was created or obtained, what components were used to build it, and whether the model can be trusted as the artifact it claims to be**.

These concepts answer two closely related questions:

> **Provenance:** Where did this model come from, and what happened to it?

> **Authenticity:** Is this actually the model from that trusted source?

A model can have strong integrity controls while still having questionable provenance.

---

### 2. Why Provenance Matters

Knowing that a model has not changed since acquisition does not necessarily mean that the model was trustworthy when acquired.

For example:

```text id="k8p4zd"
External Model
      ↓
Hash Recorded
      ↓
Hash Remains Unchanged
      ↓
Production
```

The hash proves that the artifact remained unchanged.

It does **not** prove that the original model was:

* Created by the claimed organization.
* Trained using trustworthy data.
* Free from malicious behavior.
* Obtained through an authorized channel.

This is why provenance and integrity address different trust questions.

---

### 3. Provenance vs Integrity

The distinction is important.

**Provenance asks:**

> Where did the model come from?

**Integrity asks:**

> Has the model changed?

Conceptually:

```text id="x5v9am"
PROVENANCE
    ↓
Where did it originate?

INTEGRITY
    ↓
Has it been modified?
```

Both are required for strong model assurance.

---

### 4. Provenance vs Authenticity

These concepts are also related but different.

**Provenance** describes the model's history.

**Authenticity** establishes whether the model is genuinely associated with the claimed source.

For example:

```text id="c8y3jt"
Model
 ↓
Who created it?
 ↓
Where was it obtained?
 ↓
Who modified it?
 ↓
Who approved it?
```

Authenticity asks whether the answers can be trusted.

---

### 5. Model Provenance

Model provenance is the recorded history of a model and its lifecycle.

It can include:

* Creator.
* Organization.
* Source repository.
* Base model.
* Training datasets.
* Training code.
* Training environment.
* Dependencies.
* Fine-tuning activities.
* Evaluation.
* Security testing.
* Approval.
* Deployment.

---

### 6. Provenance as a Chain

A model can have a provenance chain such as:

```text id="n3c8we"
Training Data
      ↓
Training Code
      ↓
Training Environment
      ↓
Base Model
      ↓
Fine-Tuning
      ↓
Evaluation
      ↓
Model Artifact
      ↓
Registry
      ↓
Production
```

The stronger the chain, the easier it is to establish trust.

---

### 7. Model Origin

The first provenance question is:

> **Where did the model originate?**

Possible origins include:

* Internally developed.
* Third-party vendor.
* Open-source repository.
* Cloud provider.
* Research institution.
* Acquired company.
* Community repository.

Different origins create different security and governance requirements.

---

### 8. Internally Developed Models

An internally developed model may provide stronger visibility into:

* Training process.
* Code.
* Data.
* Dependencies.
* Testing.
* Ownership.

However, internal development does not automatically guarantee authenticity or security.

Internal systems can also be compromised.

---

### 9. Third-Party Models

Third-party models introduce additional provenance questions.

For example:

* Who developed the model?
* Where was it developed?
* What data was used?
* What version is being provided?
* Has it been modified?
* How are updates distributed?
* How is authenticity verified?

---

### 10. Open-Source Models

Open-source models can provide substantial transparency but may also introduce provenance challenges.

Organizations should establish:

* Approved repositories.
* Trusted maintainers.
* Version controls.
* Artifact verification.
* Security review.
* Licensing review.

An artifact downloaded from a public repository should not automatically be considered trustworthy.

---

### 11. Model Acquisition

Model acquisition should be treated as a controlled process.

A simplified workflow is:

```text id="b5s2jq"
Identify Model
      ↓
Identify Source
      ↓
Verify Authenticity
      ↓
Review Provenance
      ↓
Security Assessment
      ↓
Approve
      ↓
Register
      ↓
Deploy
```

---

### 12. Trusted Source

A trusted source is an origin that the organization has determined to be sufficiently reliable for a particular use case.

Examples may include:

* Internal model registry.
* Approved vendor.
* Verified publisher.
* Official repository.
* Controlled artifact repository.

The level of trust should be based on risk.

---

### 13. Source Verification

Source verification determines whether the model actually originated from the claimed source.

For example:

```text id="p6z8xk"
Claim:
"Model created by Organization X"

Verification:
↓
Official Repository
↓
Official Signature
↓
Official Documentation
↓
Trusted Artifact
```

The objective is to avoid relying solely on a filename or webpage description.

---

### 14. Authenticity Claims

A model may claim to be:

> "Official Enterprise Fraud Model v4."

The organization should be able to verify that claim.

Potential evidence includes:

* Digital signatures.
* Trusted certificates.
* Repository ownership.
* Artifact hashes.
* Release records.
* Publisher attestations.

---

### 15. Digital Signatures for Authenticity

Digital signatures can provide strong evidence that an artifact was signed by a trusted key.

Conceptually:

```text id="q7w3mt"
Model Artifact
      ↓
Trusted Publisher
      ↓
Private Signing Key
      ↓
Signature
```

The receiving organization verifies the signature using the trusted public key.

---

### 16. Authenticity Verification

A simplified verification process is:

```text id="e6v1ps"
Receive Model
      ↓
Identify Claimed Publisher
      ↓
Locate Trusted Public Key
      ↓
Verify Signature
      ↓
VALID?
  ↙      ↘
YES       NO
 ↓         ↓
Continue  Reject / Investigate
```

### 18. Model Cards

A model card can document important information about a model.

Depending on the organization, it may contain:

* Training information.
* Risk considerations.

Model cards can contribute to provenance documentation, although they do not by themselves prove authenticity.

### 20. Provenance Record

A provenance record should allow an organization to reconstruct the model's history.

For example:

### 21. Traceability

Traceability means that the organization can follow a model backward and forward through its lifecycle.

**Backward:**

```text id="c2s8nh"
Production Model
      ↓
Training Run
      ↓
Training Code
      ↓
Dataset
      ↓
Source
```

**Forward:**

```text id="m8k1vz"
Model Version
      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Production
      ↓
Business Application
```

---

### 22. Lineage

Model lineage is the relationship between a model and the assets used to create it.

A simplified lineage may be:

```text id="q5c7ya"
Dataset A ─┐
Dataset B ─┼→ Training → Model v2.0
Code X ────┘
```

Lineage becomes particularly important when investigating unexpected model behavior.

---

### 23. Training Run Identity

Each significant training run should ideally have a unique identifier.

For example:

```text id="a4v7dk"
Training Run: TR-2026-00891
Model: Fraud-v4
Dataset: FraudData-v12
Code: Commit-9A82
Environment: ML-Train-03
```

This allows the resulting model to be traced back to its creation process.

---

### 24. Reproducibility

Provenance is strengthened when a model can be reproduced from documented inputs and configuration.

A reproducible training process may require:

* Dataset version.
* Source code version.
* Model architecture.
* Hyperparameters.
* Dependencies.
* Framework versions.
* Environment information.

---

### 25. Model Build Record

A build record can capture how a model artifact was generated.

For example:

```text id="e3m5xq"
Source Code
     +
Dataset
     +
Configuration
     +
Dependencies
     ↓
Training Run
     ↓
Model Artifact
```

This establishes a relationship between inputs and output.

---

### 26. Provenance and Model Integrity

Provenance and integrity reinforce one another.

```text id="k9r6ds"
PROVENANCE
     ↓
Establishes origin and history
     +
INTEGRITY
     ↓
Establishes that artifact has not changed
     =
STRONGER TRUST
```

Neither should be considered a complete substitute for the other.

---

### 27. Provenance and Supply-Chain Security

Model provenance is particularly important in the ML supply chain.

A model may depend on:

* Base models.
* Datasets.
* Libraries.
* Frameworks.
* Containers.
* Plugins.
* External services.

Each dependency can introduce provenance risk.

---

### 28. Base Model Provenance

When fine-tuning an existing model, the organization should record:

* Base model name.
* Base model version.
* Source.
* Publisher.
* License.
* Hash or signature.
* Acquisition date.

For example:

```text id="b8w2zj"
Base Model v1.4
       ↓
Fine-Tuning Dataset v8
       ↓
Enterprise Model v2.0
```

The enterprise model inherits part of its provenance from the base model.

---

### 29. Fine-Tuning Provenance

Fine-tuning should also be traceable.

The record may include:

* Base model.
* Fine-tuning dataset.
* Fine-tuning code.
* Configuration.
* Training run.
* Resulting model version.

This makes it possible to understand how the final model evolved.

---

### 30. Model Transformation History

A model may undergo several transformations:

```text id="c7f4xa"
Base Model
    ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
    ↓
Quantized Model
    ↓
Optimized Model
    ↓
Production Model
```

Each transformation should be traceable.

---

### 31. Quantization Provenance

Quantization can alter the representation of model parameters.

Therefore, the organization should know:

* Which original model was used.
* Which quantization method was applied.
* Which tool performed the transformation.
* Which resulting version was created.

---

### 32. Model Optimization Provenance

Optimization processes may also create new artifacts.

Examples include:

* Compression.
* Pruning.
* Compilation.
* Hardware optimization.
* Runtime conversion.

Each resulting artifact should have a clear relationship to its source model.

---

### 33. Provenance Graph

For complex environments, provenance can be represented as a graph.

```text id="q9x5mn"
Dataset A ─┐
Dataset B ─┼→ Training Run ─→ Model A
Code A ────┘                    ↓
                              Fine-Tuning
                                 ↓
                              Model B
                                 ↓
                              Quantization
                                 ↓
                              Model C
```

This is useful when a production model has many upstream dependencies.

---

### 34. Provenance and Accountability

Provenance establishes responsibility and traceability.

An organization should be able to determine:

* Who created the model.
* Who modified it.
* Who reviewed it.
* Who approved it.
* Who deployed it.

This creates accountability across the lifecycle.

---

### 35. Ownership

Every enterprise model should have an identifiable owner or accountable function.

Ownership may include responsibility for:

* Model lifecycle.
* Risk management.
* Security.
* Documentation.
* Approval.
* Retirement.

---

### 36. Model Stewardship

A model steward may manage operational governance activities.

These can include:

* Maintaining metadata.
* Updating documentation.
* Maintaining provenance.
* Tracking versions.
* Coordinating reviews.

The exact roles should be defined by organizational governance.

---

### 37. Provenance Gaps

A provenance gap occurs when important information about a model's history is unknown or unavailable.

Examples:

```text id="v6m3rq"
Unknown Creator
Unknown Dataset
Unknown Base Model
Unknown Version
Unknown Modification History
```

Such a model should receive additional scrutiny before being trusted.

---

### 38. Unknown-Origin Models

An organization should be cautious with models whose origin cannot be reliably established.

The risk may include:

* Hidden malicious behavior.
* Unknown vulnerabilities.
* Unclear training practices.
* Untrusted dependencies.
* Licensing problems.
* Inability to investigate incidents.

---

### 39. Provenance Confidence

Not every provenance record provides the same level of assurance.

A useful conceptual scale is:

```text id="a2y8mn"
UNKNOWN
   ↓
DOCUMENTED
   ↓
VERIFIED
   ↓
CRYPTOGRAPHICALLY ATTESTED
```

Higher-risk models should generally require stronger assurance.

---

### 40. Model Authenticity as a Trust Decision

Authenticity should ultimately support a business decision:

> **Can this organization trust this model enough to use it for its intended purpose?**

That decision should consider:

* Source.
* Provenance.
* Integrity.
* Security testing.
* Intended use.
* Risk.

---

### 41. Provenance Verification Before Deployment

Before production deployment, the organization can verify:

```text id="m3c8wv"
Source
 ↓
Provenance
 ↓
Version
 ↓
Integrity
 ↓
Security Assessment
 ↓
Approval
 ↓
Deployment
```

This prevents provenance from becoming merely documentation created after deployment.

---

### 42. Provenance During Model Updates

Every new model release should create a new provenance record or extend the existing lineage.

For example:

```text id="z4w6qk"
v2.0
 ↓
New Dataset
 ↓
Retraining
 ↓
v2.1
```

The relationship between versions should remain traceable.

---

### 43. Provenance During Model Retirement

Provenance should remain available after retirement.

The organization may need historical records for:

* Audit.
* Incident investigation.
* Regulatory review.
* Business disputes.
* Model-risk analysis.

Retired models should therefore not simply disappear from the governance record.

---

### 44. Provenance Retention

Retention requirements should reflect:

* Business criticality.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Legal requirements.
* Security risk.
* Audit requirements.

The organization should define what provenance information must be retained and for how long.

---

### 45. Provenance Evidence

Potential evidence includes:

* Model registry records.
* Repository records.
* Build logs.
* Training logs.
* Dataset identifiers.
* Version history.
* Hashes.
* Signatures.
* Approval records.
* Deployment records.

Together, these create a defensible provenance trail.

---

### 46. Provenance and Incident Response

During a security incident, provenance can help answer:

> **Where did the affected model come from?**

Investigators can trace:

```text id="y5n8cf"
Production Model
      ↓
Model Version
      ↓
Training Run
      ↓
Dataset / Code
      ↓
Source
```

This can significantly accelerate root-cause analysis.

---

### 47. Provenance and Model Recall

If a compromised model is identified, provenance allows the organization to determine which other models may share the same origin or components.

For example:

```text id="p8m6za"
Compromised Base Model
       ↓
 ┌─────┼─────┐
 ↓     ↓     ↓
A      B      C
```

The organization can then assess potentially affected downstream models.

---

### 48. Provenance and Dependency Mapping

Dependency mapping helps identify relationships between:

* Models.
* Datasets.
* Libraries.
* Base models.
* Infrastructure.

This is particularly valuable for enterprise-scale AI environments.

---

### 49. Provenance and Model Risk

Poor provenance increases uncertainty.

Conceptually:

```text id="h4n7xc"
Poor Provenance
      ↓
Greater Uncertainty
      ↓
Greater Risk
      ↓
Stronger Due Diligence Required
```

A model with unknown origin should not necessarily be treated the same as a fully documented internal model.

---

### 50. Core Principle

Model provenance and authenticity establish the foundation for trustworthy model acquisition and lifecycle management.

The key principle is:

> **An organization should know where a model came from, what happened to it, what it depends on, and have reliable evidence that the model being used is genuinely the model that was obtained, validated, and approved.**

## Part 2

### 51. Provenance as a Security Control

Model provenance should not be treated merely as documentation.

It can function as a **security control** by establishing traceability between the model, its source, its development process, and its current deployment.

```text
Trusted Source
      ↓
Documented Creation
      ↓
Verified Transformation
      ↓
Approved Artifact
      ↓
Controlled Deployment
```

The objective is to maintain confidence throughout the model lifecycle.

---

### 52. Provenance as Evidence

A provenance record provides evidence that a model followed an authorized process.

For example:

```text
Model ID: MOD-00127
Version: 4.2
Source: Internal ML Platform
Training Run: TR-8821
Dataset: DATA-44
Code Commit: 8F92A
Security Review: Passed
Approval: Approved
```

This allows security, GRC, and audit teams to establish how the model came into existence.

---

### 53. Minimum Provenance Information

An enterprise should establish a minimum provenance dataset for each model.

This may include:

* Model identifier.
* Version.
* Source.
* Owner.
* Creator.
* Creation date.
* Base model.
* Dataset versions.
* Training run.
* Code version.
* Dependencies.
* Validation status.
* Security status.
* Approval status.

The exact fields should reflect the model's risk.

---

### 54. Provenance Completeness

A model may have provenance information without having **complete provenance**.

For example:

```text
Model Source       ✓
Model Version      ✓
Training Dataset   ?
Training Code      ?
Base Model         ?
Transformation     ?
```

Unknown fields represent provenance gaps.

---

### 55. Provenance Quality

Provenance quality can be assessed using criteria such as:

* Completeness.
* Accuracy.
* Timeliness.
* Consistency.
* Verifiability.
* Traceability.

A record that exists but cannot be verified provides weaker assurance.

---

### 56. Provenance Validation

Provenance information should itself be validated where practical.

For example, an organization may verify:

```text
Claimed Source
      ↓
Repository Record
      ↓
Release Record
      ↓
Artifact Hash
      ↓
Signature
```

The objective is to distinguish **documented claims** from **verified facts**.

---

### 57. Self-Reported Provenance

A vendor or model publisher may provide provenance information.

For example:

> "This model was trained using Dataset X and released by Organization Y."

This information can be useful, but it should not automatically be treated as independently verified.

---

### 58. Independent Provenance Verification

For higher-risk models, an organization may independently verify critical provenance claims.

Verification may involve:

* Reviewing source repositories.
* Validating signatures.
* Comparing release artifacts.
* Reviewing documentation.
* Confirming vendor attestations.

---

### 59. Provenance Attestation

An attestation is a statement asserting that a model or process satisfies specified conditions.

For example:

```text
Publisher
   ↓
Attests:
"Artifact X corresponds to Release 4.2"
   ↓
Consumer
   ↓
Verifies Attestation
```

Attestations become stronger when supported by cryptographic mechanisms.

---

### 60. Cryptographic Attestation

Cryptographic mechanisms can strengthen provenance claims by binding information to an identifiable artifact.

For example:

```text
Model Artifact
      +
Metadata
      ↓
Signed Attestation
      ↓
Verification
```

This reduces reliance on editable metadata alone.

---

### 61. Artifact Hash

A cryptographic hash can uniquely represent a particular artifact with very high probability.

For example:

```text
Model v4.2
    ↓
SHA-256
    ↓
9F83...A712
```

The hash can then be recorded in the provenance system.

---

### 62. Hash Does Not Establish Origin

A critical distinction is:

> **A hash identifies an artifact; it does not establish who created it.**

For example:

```text
Unknown Model
      ↓
Hash
      ↓
Known Artifact Identity
```

The hash does not answer:

> "Who should I trust?"

That requires provenance and authenticity mechanisms.

---

### 63. Signature + Hash

Combining hashing and digital signatures provides stronger assurance.

```text
Model
  ↓
Hash
  ↓
Private Key
  ↓
Signature
```

The verifier can establish both:

* The artifact corresponds to the signed content.
* The signature is associated with a trusted signing identity.

---

### 64. Trusted Public Keys

Authenticity verification depends on knowing which public keys are trusted.

Organizations should maintain a trusted-key process covering:

* Key ownership.
* Key lifecycle.
* Key rotation.
* Key revocation.
* Key compromise.
* Approved signing authorities.

---

### 65. Publisher Identity

The identity of the model publisher should be distinguishable from the model name.

For example:

```text
Model Name:
Enterprise-Fraud-v5

Publisher:
Organization A

Signing Identity:
ORG-A-MODEL-SIGNING
```

This reduces ambiguity between similarly named artifacts.

---

### 66. Model Naming

Model naming conventions should support identification.

A useful naming structure may include:

```text
<Organization>-<Model>-<Version>-<Environment>
```

For example:

```text
ORG-A-FRAUD-5.2-PROD
```

Naming conventions alone do not provide authenticity but improve operational traceability.

---

### 67. Unique Model Identifier

A model should ideally have a stable unique identifier independent of its display name.

For example:

```text
Model ID:
MOD-93A72F
```

The model can then have multiple versions:

```text
MOD-93A72F
 ├── v1.0
 ├── v2.0
 ├── v3.0
 └── v4.0
```

---

### 68. Version Identity

Version information should identify a specific release.

For example:

```text
Model ID: MOD-93A72F
Version: 4.2
Artifact Hash: ...
Release ID: REL-2026-088
```

This makes the provenance chain more precise.

---

### 69. Release Provenance

A release record should establish how a particular version became available.

Potential information includes:

* Release identifier.
* Previous version.
* Changes introduced.
* Security testing.
* Approval.
* Release date.
* Release authority.

---

### 70. Model Change Lineage

Model changes should maintain lineage.

```text
v3.0
 ↓
Dataset Update
 ↓
Retraining
 ↓
v3.1
 ↓
Optimization
 ↓
v3.2
```

This makes it possible to determine how a production version evolved.

---

### 71. Parent-Child Model Relationships

Some models are derived from other models.

For example:

```text
Base Model
    ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
    ↓
Domain Model
    ↓
Production Variant
```

These relationships should be documented.

---

### 72. Model Derivation

A derived model should reference its parent artifact.

For example:

```text
Parent:
Foundation Model v3.1

Derived:
Enterprise Model v1.0
```

This allows security teams to identify inherited dependencies.

---

### 73. Shared Base Models

Multiple enterprise models may depend on the same base model.

```text
              Base Model
             /    |     \
            /     |      \
           ↓      ↓       ↓
        Model A Model B Model C
```

If the base model has a security problem, multiple downstream models may require assessment.

---

### 74. Provenance Dependency Graph

A dependency graph can help identify affected models.

```text
Dataset A
    ↓
Base Model
    ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
    ↓
Production Model
    ↓
Application
```

The graph connects technical dependencies with business systems.

---

### 75. Provenance and Blast Radius

When a security issue is discovered, provenance can help determine the **blast radius**.

For example:

```text
Compromised Component
        ↓
Affected Models
        ↓
Affected Applications
        ↓
Affected Business Processes
```

Without lineage information, determining the scope can be significantly harder.

---

### 76. Model Provenance and Recall

An organization may need to recall or disable a model if its provenance becomes questionable.

Possible triggers include:

* Compromised source.
* Malicious upstream model.
* Dataset contamination.
* Invalid release.
* Compromised signing key.
* Vendor security incident.

---

### 77. Provenance-Based Model Quarantine

A model with uncertain provenance can be placed into quarantine.

```text
Unknown / Suspicious Model
          ↓
       QUARANTINE
          ↓
Security Assessment
          ↓
Approved / Rejected
```

This prevents uncertainty from becoming production exposure.

---

### 78. Model Intake Process

Organizations should establish a formal intake process for externally sourced models.

A model intake record may include:

```text
Source
Publisher
Version
Hash
Signature
License
Dependencies
Known Vulnerabilities
Intended Use
Risk Classification
Approval
```

---

### 79. Source Reputation

Source reputation can be considered during model acquisition.

Factors may include:

* Publisher history.
* Repository ownership.
* Security practices.
* Release process.
* Vulnerability disclosure practices.
* Community or enterprise trust.

Reputation should support—not replace—technical verification.

---

### 80. Repository Ownership

When a model is obtained from a repository, the organization should establish whether the repository actually belongs to the claimed publisher.

For example:

```text
Claimed Publisher
       ↓
Official Repository?
       ↓
Verified
```

A similarly named repository may not be legitimate.

---

### 81. Repository Integrity

Even an authentic repository may be compromised.

Therefore, provenance verification should consider:

* Commit history.
* Release artifacts.
* Tags.
* Signing.
* Maintainer identities.
* Unexpected changes.

---

### 82. Release Integrity

A model publisher may provide multiple release mechanisms.

For example:

```text
Repository
    ↓
Release v5.0
    ↓
Artifact
    ↓
Checksum
    ↓
Signature
```

The consumer should verify that the downloaded artifact corresponds to the legitimate release.

---

### 83. Mirror Risk

Organizations may obtain models through mirrors or internal copies.

A mirror can introduce additional integrity and provenance concerns.

```text
Publisher
    ↓
Mirror
    ↓
Enterprise
```

The enterprise should verify that the mirrored artifact corresponds to the trusted original.

---

### 84. Internal Model Mirrors

Internal mirrors can improve operational efficiency but should preserve provenance information.

The organization should retain:

* Original source.
* Original version.
* Original hash.

### 101. Provenance Architecture

Enterprise model provenance should be designed as an information flow connecting the major stages of the model lifecycle.

```text id="p9v4ka"
  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓
```

Each stage should generate sufficient information to establish where the model came from and what happened to it.

---

### 102. Provenance Repository

Organizations may maintain provenance information within:

* Model registries.
* Metadata repositories.
* Data catalogs.
* Configuration-management databases.
* Artifact repositories.
* Governance platforms.

The important requirement is that provenance information remains **accessible, consistent, and protected**.

---

### 103. Centralized Provenance

A centralized provenance repository can provide a common source of information.

For example:

```text id="c4m8xw"
              PROVENANCE REPOSITORY
                     │
       ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
       ↓             ↓             ↓
     Model         Dataset       Code
     Records       Records      Records
       │             │             │
       └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                     ↓
                 Lineage
```

This can simplify audit and investigation activities.

---

### 104. Distributed Provenance

Large organizations may have provenance information distributed across several platforms.

For example:

```text id="v3p7nd"
Git Repository
      +
Data Catalog
      +
ML Platform
      +
Artifact Registry
      +
Deployment Platform
```

In such environments, unique identifiers are important for connecting the records.

---

### 105. Provenance Identifier

A provenance system should use stable identifiers.

For example:

```text id="4j7m2s"

Training Run   → RUN-8821
Dataset        → DATA-721
Code Commit    → 9F82AC
Release        → REL-2026-41
Deployment     → DEP-551
```

These identifiers create relationships between otherwise separate systems.

---

### 106. Metadata Consistency

Provenance becomes weaker when different systems contain conflicting information.

For example:

```text id="7n2q4x"
Registry:
Model v4.2

Deployment:
Model v4.1

Change Record:
Model v4.2
```

Such inconsistencies should be investigated and resolved.

---

### 107. Single Source of Truth

Organizations should define authoritative sources for critical provenance information.

For example:

| Information     | Authoritative Source |
| --------------- | -------------------- |
| Model version   | Model registry       |
| Code version    | Source repository    |
| Dataset version | Data catalog         |
| Deployment      | Deployment platform  |
| Approval        | Governance system    |

This avoids ambiguity.

---

### 108. Provenance Synchronization

Where multiple systems maintain related records, synchronization mechanisms can reduce inconsistencies.

For example:

```text id="2q8k3h"
Model Registry
      ↓
Deployment System
      ↓
Governance Record
```

Important identifiers should remain consistent across systems.

---

### 109. Provenance Data Integrity

Provenance records themselves require integrity protection.

An attacker who changes:

```text id="q5m7as"
Model Source
Model Version
Approval Status
Artifact Hash
```

could make an unauthorized model appear legitimate.

Therefore:

> **The provenance record must itself be treated as a security-sensitive asset.**

---

### 110. Provenance Access Control

Not every user should be able to modify provenance records.

Typical permissions may include:

```text id="d8s4vw"
Read Provenance       → Broad
Create Provenance     → Controlled
Modify Provenance     → Restricted
Approve Provenance    → Highly Restricted
Delete Provenance     → Exceptional
```

---

### 111. Immutable Provenance Records

For important lifecycle events, organizations may use immutable or append-only records.

Examples include:

* Approval events.
* Release events.
* Deployment events.
* Security validation.
* Model retirement.

This helps preserve historical evidence.

---

### 112. Append-Only Audit Trail

An append-only model records events rather than rewriting history.

```text id="j4k9sa"

   ↓

   ↓
Approved
   ↓
Deployed
   ↓
Updated
   ↓
Retired
```

Each event remains part of the historical record.

### 113. Provenance Event

A provenance event can contain:

```text id="q8v2nc"
Action

Version
Source
Previous State

```

This creates a structured lifecycle history.

---

### 114. Time Synchronization

Accurate timestamps are important for reconstructing model history.

Enterprise systems should use consistent time sources so that events can be correlated correctly.

For example:

```text id="f5x8za"

10:03 — Model Change
10:04 — Validation
10:07 — Deployment
```

Accurate timestamps improve investigation quality.

---

### 115. Actor Attribution

Every significant provenance event should identify the responsible actor or system.

Examples:

* User account.
* Service account.
* CI/CD pipeline.
* Automated ML platform.
* Vendor identity.

"System changed model" is weaker evidence than a traceable service identity and event record.

---

### 116. Human vs Machine Actions

Automated systems frequently create or transform model artifacts.

Therefore, provenance should distinguish between:

```text id="b7q3mt"
Human Action
      vs
Automated Action
```

For automated actions, the responsible service identity should be recorded.

---

### 117. Service Account Provenance

A training or deployment pipeline may operate under a service account.

The provenance record should establish:

```text id="e9w4sk"

      ↓

      ↓
Training Run
      ↓
Model Artifact
```

This connects automated activity to an accountable process.

---

### 118. Provenance and Identity Federation

In large organizations, identities may span multiple platforms.

Federated identity can help maintain consistent attribution across:

* Cloud platforms.
* ML platforms.
* Repositories.
* Governance systems.

---

### 119. Model Publisher Verification

For externally acquired models, publisher identity should be verified independently where possible.

Potential evidence includes:

* Official documentation.
* Trusted certificates.
* Digital signatures.
* Verified repositories.
* Publisher-controlled release records.

---

### 120. Publisher Key Management

If a publisher signs model releases, the organization needs confidence in the signing-key lifecycle.

Questions include:

* Who controls the key?
* How is the key protected?
* How is compromise handled?
* How are replacement keys distributed?
* How are old signatures validated?

---

### 121. Trust on First Acquisition

The first time an organization acquires a model, it must establish an initial trust decision.

A conceptual process is:

```text id="g6p2rf"
Acquire
  ↓
Identify Source
  ↓
Verify Publisher
  ↓
Verify Artifact
  ↓
Review Provenance
  ↓
Security Assessment
  ↓
Establish Trusted Baseline
```

This initial baseline becomes important for future integrity verification.

---

### 122. First-Use Risk

If the organization accepts a model without sufficient validation, it may establish an untrusted artifact as its baseline.

This creates a dangerous situation:

```text id="n7v4jc"
Malicious Artifact
      ↓
Accepted as Baseline
      ↓
"Trusted" Model
```

Integrity controls cannot correct a fundamentally untrusted starting point.

---

### 123. Trust Establishment

Trust should therefore be established through multiple forms of evidence.

For example:

```text id="r4k6mz"
Source Verification
       +
Authenticity Verification
       +
Provenance Review
       +
Security Testing
       +
Business Approval
       ↓
TRUSTED MODEL
```

---

### 124. Model Authenticity Levels

Organizations can establish different assurance levels.

For example:

**Level 1 — Claimed**

The publisher claims ownership.

**Level 2 — Documented**

Documentation supports the claim.

**Level 3 — Verified**

The organization independently verifies important information.

**Level 4 — Cryptographically Verified**

Signatures or equivalent mechanisms provide strong artifact-level assurance.

---

### 125. Authenticity Assurance

The required assurance level should depend on:

* Model criticality.
* Business impact.
* Data sensitivity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* External exposure.
* Threat environment.

Higher-risk models generally justify stronger verification.

---

### 126. Model Provenance Risk Classification

Models can be classified according to provenance risk.

For example:

```text id="z2k9pw"
LOW
Internal + Fully Documented

MEDIUM
Trusted Third Party

HIGH
External + Limited Provenance

CRITICAL
Unknown / Unverifiable Origin
```

The classification should drive additional controls.

---

### 127. Unknown Provenance

An unknown-origin model should not automatically receive the same trust level as an internally controlled model.

The organization may:

* Reject it.
* Quarantine it.
* Perform enhanced assessment.
* Restrict it to non-production use.

---

### 128. Provenance Exceptions

Sometimes complete provenance cannot be established.

For example:

* Legacy model.
* Acquired company.
* Historical artifact.
* Vendor limitation.

The organization should formally document the exception rather than silently accepting the gap.

An exception record may contain:

```text id="m7x3qc"
Compensating Controls

Expiration Date
Remediation Plan
```

This converts an unknown condition into a governed risk.

---

### 130. Compensating Controls

Where provenance is incomplete, additional controls may reduce risk.

Examples include:

* Enhanced security testing.
* Restricted deployment.
* Runtime monitoring.
* Human review.
* Limited data access.
* Sandboxed execution.

---

### 131. Model Quarantine

A suspicious or insufficiently verified model can be isolated.

```text id="k5r8za"

      ↓
Quarantine
      ↓
Analysis
      ↓
Verification
      ↓
Approved / Rejected
```

Quarantine prevents uncertain artifacts from entering trusted environments.

---

### 132. Sandboxed Model Evaluation

Models with uncertain provenance may be evaluated in an isolated environment.

The objective is to reduce potential impact while security teams assess:

* Model behavior.
* Dependencies.
* Artifact contents.
* Network activity.
* Runtime requirements.

---

### 133. Provenance and Malicious Model Detection

Provenance information can support security analysis, but it is not a guarantee against malicious behavior.

A model from a legitimate publisher can still contain vulnerabilities or be compromised.

Therefore:

> **Trusted provenance reduces uncertainty; it does not eliminate the need for security testing.**

---

### 134. Provenance and Behavioral Testing

A model can undergo behavioral security testing after provenance verification.

```text id="s7w2xn"

        ↓
Artifact Verification
        ↓
Behavioral Testing
        ↓
Security Assessment
```

This provides multiple layers of assurance.

### 135. Provenance and Model Validation

Model validation should record which exact artifact was tested.

For example:

```text id="e6m9px"

Hash: ABC...
       ↓
Validation
       ↓
PASSED
```

This prevents a different artifact from being deployed under the same version label.

---

### 136. Validation Artifact Binding

The validation record should bind together:

* Model ID.
* Version.
* Hash.
* Test results.
* Validation date.
* Validator.
* Approval status.

This creates strong traceability.

---

### 137. Security Test Binding

Security testing should also identify the exact model artifact tested.

For example:

```text id="y4c8nj"

       ↓
Model MOD-42
       ↓
Version 3.7
       ↓
Hash ABC123
       ↓
PASS
```

This creates defensible evidence.

---

Approval should similarly be tied to the exact model version.

An approval such as:

> "Fraud model approved"

is weaker than:

> "Fraud Model MOD-42 v3.7, hash ABC123, approved."

The latter identifies the exact artifact.

---

### 139. Deployment Artifact Binding

The deployment record should identify the exact approved artifact.

```text id="q3r7wd"

      ↓

      ↓
Deployment
      ↓
Production Instance
```

This closes the provenance chain.


### 141. Runtime Provenance

* Infrastructure identity.
* Configuration version.
* Model artifact hash.

```text id="v6n3yt"
      ↓

      ↓
```

```text id="j8q5cx"
   ↕

   ↕

   ↕

```

Differences should be investigated.

```text id="d7x4ma"
      +

      +

      ↓
```

The principle is:

> **Provenance assurance should be proportional to model risk.**

* Unknown artifacts.

* Deployment discrepancies.
* Missing metadata.
* Unauthorized source changes.

---

### 171. Continuous Provenance Validation

A mature environment can periodically reconcile:

```text id="m5c9ra"
Approved Registry
       ↕
Deployment Inventory
       ↕
Runtime Environment
       ↕
Governance Records
```

The objective is to identify discrepancies between what **should** be running and what **is** running.

---

### 172. Provenance Drift Detection

A provenance drift event could occur when:

```text id="f7x4zb"
Approved:
Model v6.1
Hash ABC

Production:
Model v6.1
Hash XYZ
```

The version is the same, but the artifact differs.

This should be treated as a significant security and governance event.

---

### 173. Authenticity Failure

An authenticity failure may occur when:

* Signature verification fails.
* Publisher identity cannot be established.
* Artifact does not match the trusted release.
* Signing key is revoked.
* Source is compromised.
* Model origin is disputed.

The model should not automatically be treated as trusted.

---

### 174. Provenance Incident Response

A provenance-related incident should follow an established response process.

```text id="z5p8kc"
Detect
 ↓
Validate
 ↓
Contain
 ↓
Identify Origin
 ↓
Trace Dependencies
 ↓
Assess Impact
 ↓
Remediate
 ↓
Document
```

---

### 175. Provenance Incident Questions

Investigators should ask:

1. Which model was affected?
2. Which version?
3. What is the trusted artifact?
4. What is the current artifact?
5. Where did the model originate?
6. Who modified or deployed it?
7. Which upstream components are involved?
8. Which applications use it?
9. Was the model exposed to sensitive data?
10. Are other models affected?

---

### 176. Compromised Publisher

If a trusted publisher is compromised, organizations should not assume that all artifacts from that publisher remain trustworthy.

They may need to determine:

* Which releases were affected.
* Which signing keys were compromised.
* Which models were downloaded.
* Which enterprise deployments use affected versions.

---

### 177. Compromised Signing Key

A compromised signing key can undermine authenticity verification.

A response may require:

```text id="r8m3wx"
Key Compromise
      ↓
Identify Affected Releases
      ↓
Revoke / Replace Key
      ↓
Revalidate Artifacts
      ↓
Redeploy Trusted Versions
```

This demonstrates why key-management governance is part of model authenticity.

---

### 178. Provenance Revocation

Organizations should be able to invalidate trust in a model or release.

Possible triggers include:

* Confirmed compromise.
* Invalid provenance.
* Malicious publisher activity.
* Vulnerable dependency.
* Regulatory concern.
* Incorrect release.

```text id="a7c5kn"
   ↓
```

The status should be visible to systems that control deployment.

```text id="k2x9qm"
      ↓
```

### 181. Provenance and Business Continuity

Trusted historical model versions should be preserved when operationally appropriate.

This can support recovery when:

* A new model is compromised.
* A deployment fails.
* A vendor release is withdrawn.
* An integrity incident occurs.

---

### 182. Trusted Recovery

Recovery should use a previously verified artifact rather than an arbitrary backup.

```text id="w6m4pv"
Incident
   ↓
Identify Last Trusted Version
   ↓
Verify Integrity
   ↓
Verify Provenance
   ↓
Deploy
```

This is stronger than simply restoring the most recent file.

---

### 183. Provenance and Disaster Recovery

Disaster recovery planning should account for:

* Model artifacts.
* Provenance metadata.
* Model registry.
* Signing keys.
* Validation records.
* Approval records.
* Deployment configuration.

Losing provenance information can make recovery more difficult to trust.

---

### 184. Backup Integrity

Backups containing model artifacts and provenance records should themselves be protected.

Otherwise:

```text id="u4s7yc"
Trusted Production
      ↓
Compromised Backup
      ↓
Untrusted Recovery
```

A recovery copy must remain trustworthy.

### 185. Provenance and Business Acceptance

Business stakeholders should understand that provenance is not solely a technical requirement.

It provides confidence that:

* The approved model is actually being used.
* The model's origin is known.
* Changes are traceable.
* Security investigations can reconstruct history.

### 186. Provenance and Legal Defensibility

This may be important during disputes, audits, or regulatory inquiries.

### 196. Provenance Maturity Model

A simple maturity model can be:

**Level 1 — Unknown**

Model origin and history are poorly understood.

**Level 2 — Documented**

Basic source and ownership information exists.

**Level 3 — Traceable**

Versions, lineage, approvals, and deployments are connected.

**Level 4 — Verified**

Authenticity and artifact identity are technically verified.

**Level 5 — Automated**

Provenance is automatically captured, verified, monitored, and reconciled throughout the lifecycle.

### 199. GRC Perspective

From a GRC perspective, model provenance and authenticity answer a fundamental governance question:

> **Can the organization demonstrate that the model currently being used is the legitimate, traceable, and authorized model that it intended to acquire or develop?**

This requires more than a model file and a version number.

It requires a defensible chain of evidence connecting:

* Origin.
* Ownership.
* Development.
* Dependencies.
* Transformation.
* Validation.
* Approval.
* Deployment.

---

### 200. Core Principle

The fundamental principle is:

> **Model trust should be supported by verifiable provenance and authenticity throughout the entire model lifecycle.**

A mature organization should be able to trace a production model backward to its trusted source and forward to its operational deployment while maintaining evidence of what changed, who authorized it, and whether the artifact remains authentic.

```text id="c8m5zx"
TRUSTED ORIGIN
      ↓
VERIFIED PROVENANCE
      ↓
AUTHENTIC ARTIFACT
      ↓
VALIDATED MODEL
      ↓
AUTHORIZED RELEASE
      ↓
TRACEABLE DEPLOYMENT
      ↓
CONTINUOUS ASSURANCE
```

This transforms model provenance from a documentation exercise into a **core AI security, supply-chain, risk-management, and governance capability**.
