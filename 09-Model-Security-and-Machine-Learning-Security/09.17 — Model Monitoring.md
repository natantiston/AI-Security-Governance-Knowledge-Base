# 09.17 — Model Monitoring

## Part 1

### 1. Model Monitoring

**Model monitoring** is the continuous or periodic observation of a deployed model to determine whether it remains reliable, secure, performant, and appropriate for its approved purpose.

Model monitoring extends beyond checking whether the model is technically available.

```text id="mm101"
MODEL DEPLOYMENT
       ↓
   MONITORING
       ↓
 ┌─────┼────────┬─────────┐
 ↓     ↓        ↓         ↓
Data  Model   Security  System
      Output             Health
       ↓
   Risk Assessment
       ↓
 Response / Remediation
```

---

### 2. Why Model Monitoring Matters

A model that passes validation before deployment can still develop problems in production.

Potential causes include:

* Data changes.
* Model drift.
* Data-quality problems.
* Infrastructure changes.
* New attack techniques.
* Dependency changes.
* Unexpected user behavior.

Therefore:

> **Model validation establishes initial confidence; model monitoring maintains that confidence over time.**

---

### 3. Monitoring vs. Validation

These activities serve different purposes.

**Model validation** asks:

> Does the model meet requirements before or during approval?

**Model monitoring** asks:

> Does the deployed model continue to meet requirements?

```text id="mm103"
Validation
     ↓
Initial Assurance
     ↓
Deployment
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Ongoing Assurance
```

---

### 4. Monitoring vs. Model Drift

Model drift is one component of model monitoring.

Monitoring is broader.

```text id="mm104"
MODEL MONITORING
       │
 ┌─────┼──────┬───────┬────────┐
 ↓     ↓      ↓       ↓        ↓
Data  Drift  Model  Security Performance
Quality       Health
```

A monitoring program can detect problems even when there is no measurable distribution drift.

---

### 5. Monitoring Dimensions

An enterprise monitoring program should typically consider:

1. Data health.
2. Model performance.
3. Prediction behavior.
4. Model drift.
5. Security.
6. Infrastructure.
7. Availability.
8. Latency.
9. Resource consumption.
10. Governance status.

---

### 6. Data Monitoring

Data monitoring determines whether the model is receiving appropriate inputs.

Controls may monitor:

* Completeness.
* Accuracy.
* Validity.
* Timeliness.
* Consistency.
* Distribution.

---

### 7. Data Completeness

The organization should monitor whether required model inputs are present.

Example:

```text id="mm107"
Expected Records: 100,000
Received:         99,800
Missing:             200
```

A sudden increase in missing records may indicate a pipeline or operational issue.

---

### 8. Data Validity

Inputs should conform to expected rules.

Examples include:

* Correct data types.
* Valid ranges.
* Valid categories.
* Expected formats.

Invalid inputs can produce unreliable predictions.

---

### 9. Data Timeliness

Some models depend on fresh information.

Monitoring may therefore measure:

```text id="mm109"
Expected Data Time
        ↓
Actual Data Time
        ↓
Data Age / Latency
```

Stale data can produce incorrect decisions even when the model itself has not changed.

---

### 10. Data Consistency

The organization should identify unexpected differences between:

* Data sources.
* Environments.
* Processing stages.
* Historical records.

---

### 11. Input Distribution Monitoring

Input distributions can be monitored to detect changes.

```text id="mm111"
Reference Distribution
        ↓
Current Distribution
        ↓
Comparison
        ↓
Potential Drift
```

This connects directly with the controls discussed in **09.16 — Model Drift**.

---

### 12. Prediction Monitoring

Organizations should monitor what the model is producing.

Examples include:

* Prediction distribution.
* Classification frequency.
* Risk scores.
* Confidence levels.
* Output ranges.

---

### 13. Prediction Distribution

Suppose a model historically generates:

```text id="mm113"
Low Risk       75%
Medium Risk    20%
High Risk       5%
```

If high-risk predictions suddenly become 30%, the organization should investigate the reason.

The change may be legitimate or abnormal.

---

### 14. Output Validation

Model outputs should be checked against expected constraints.

For example:

```text id="mm114"
Expected Score: 0–1
        ↓
Output: 0.82
        ↓
Valid

Output: 4.7
        ↓
Invalid → Investigate
```

---

### 15. Model Performance Monitoring

Performance monitoring determines whether the model continues to achieve its intended objective.

Potential metrics include:

* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* F1 score.
* AUC.
* Mean absolute error.
* Mean squared error.

The correct metric depends on the model.

---

### 16. Security Performance

Security-related models may require specialized monitoring.

Examples include:

* Detection rate.
* False-negative rate.
* False-positive rate.
* Attack detection rate.
* Alert precision.

---

### 17. False-Negative Monitoring

For security models, an increase in false negatives may be particularly concerning.

```text id="mm117"
Malicious Activity
       ↓
Model
       ↓
Incorrectly Classified
       ↓
Security Exposure
```

---

### 18. False-Positive Monitoring

False positives should also be monitored because excessive alerts can overwhelm security teams.

```text id="mm118"
Too Many Alerts
      ↓
Alert Fatigue
      ↓
Analyst Overload
      ↓
Potential Missed Threat
```

---

### 19. Ground-Truth Monitoring

Performance cannot always be measured immediately because actual outcomes may become known later.

For example:

```text id="mm119"
Prediction
   ↓
Real-World Event
   ↓
Investigation
   ↓
Ground Truth
   ↓
Performance Measurement
```

Monitoring systems should account for delayed labels.

---

### 20. Model Confidence Monitoring

Confidence distributions can provide additional insight into model behavior.

A sudden increase in low-confidence predictions may indicate:

* New input patterns.
* Data quality problems.
* Distribution changes.
* Model limitations.

Confidence should be treated as a signal, not proof of correctness.

---

### 21. Calibration Monitoring

A model's predicted probabilities should remain reasonably aligned with actual outcomes where probability estimates are important.

Example:

```text id="mm121"
Predicted:
80% probability

Observed:
~80% outcomes
```

Significant divergence may indicate calibration degradation.

---

### 22. Latency Monitoring

A model may remain accurate but become operationally unusable if inference becomes too slow.

Important metrics can include:

* Average latency.
* P95 latency.
* P99 latency.
* Queue time.
* Processing time.

---

### 23. Availability Monitoring

Critical model services should be monitored for:

* Uptime.
* Service availability.
* Failed requests.
* Timeouts.
* Dependency failures.

---

### 24. Throughput Monitoring

Throughput measures how much inference traffic the system can process.

Unexpected changes can indicate:

* Business changes.
* Application problems.
* Denial-of-service activity.
* Integration issues.

---

### 25. Resource Monitoring

Model infrastructure may require monitoring of:

* CPU.
* GPU.
* Memory.
* Storage.
* Network.
* Accelerator utilization.

Resource exhaustion can affect model availability and latency.

---

### 26. Model Runtime Monitoring

The runtime environment should be monitored for changes affecting model behavior.

Examples include:

* Framework versions.
* Libraries.
* Runtime configuration.
* Hardware.
* Dependencies.

---

### 27. Dependency Monitoring

A deployed model may rely on multiple components.

```text id="mm127"
Model
 ↓
Runtime
 ↓
Libraries
 ↓
Feature Store
 ↓
API
 ↓
Infrastructure
```

A failure or change in any dependency may affect model operation.

---

### 28. API Monitoring

If the model is exposed through an API, monitoring should consider:

* Request volume.
* Error rates.
* Authentication failures.
* Unusual clients.
* Abnormal request patterns.

---

### 29. Model Access Monitoring

Organizations should monitor who and what is accessing the model.

Relevant information can include:

* User identity.
* Application identity.
* API client.
* Timestamp.
* Request type.
* Authorization outcome.

---

### 30. Model Security Monitoring

Security monitoring should identify suspicious behavior such as:

* Unusual query volumes.
* Repeated failed authentication.
* Abnormal request patterns.
* Excessive model queries.
* Unexpected geographic or network sources.
* Attempts to bypass controls.

---

### 31. Model Extraction Signals

Repeated systematic queries can potentially indicate an attempt to reproduce model behavior.

For example:

```text id="mm131"
Large Query Volume
       +
Systematic Input Variation
       ↓
Potential Model Extraction
```

Monitoring can therefore support defenses against **model theft and extraction**, covered earlier in the chapter.

---

### 32. Adversarial Input Monitoring

Organizations may monitor for unusual input patterns associated with adversarial activity.

Examples include:

* Highly unusual feature combinations.
* Repeated boundary-value inputs.
* Systematic input manipulation.
* Abnormal request sequences.

---

### 33. Monitoring Input Constraints

Input validation can prevent malformed or unexpected requests from reaching the model.

```text id="mm133"
Request
  ↓
Authentication
  ↓
Authorization
  ↓
Input Validation
  ↓
Model
```

---

### 34. Monitoring Model Integrity

The organization should verify that the deployed model remains the approved model.

Potential controls include:

* Artifact hashes.
* Digital signatures.
* Registry verification.
* Deployment attestation.

---

### 35. Model Version Monitoring

Every production inference should be traceable to a model version where practical.

For example:

```text id="mm135"
Request
  ↓
Model v3.2
  ↓
Prediction
  ↓
Audit Record
```

This is important when investigating unexpected behavior.

---

### 36. Configuration Monitoring

Model behavior can change even when the model artifact remains unchanged.

Monitoring should therefore consider:

* Thresholds.
* Feature configuration.
* Runtime parameters.
* Routing rules.
* Preprocessing settings.

---

### 37. Model Deployment Monitoring

Deployment systems should track:

* Deployment time.
* Model version.
* Environment.
* Deployment status.
* Rollback status.

Unexpected deployment activity should be investigated.

---

### 38. Canary Monitoring

When a new model is introduced gradually, monitoring should compare the new model against the established production model.

```text id="mm138"
Production Traffic
      │
 ┌────┴─────┐
 ↓          ↓
Current    New
Model      Model
 ↓          ↓
Results Comparison
```

---

### 39. Shadow Monitoring

A new model may receive production inputs without influencing actual decisions.

This allows organizations to compare behavior safely.

---

### 40. Core Principle

**Model monitoring provides continuous assurance that a deployed model remains within its approved operating, performance, security, and governance boundaries.**

```text id="mm140"
DATA
 ↓
MODEL
 ↓
PREDICTIONS
 ↓
PERFORMANCE
 ↓
SECURITY
 ↓
INFRASTRUCTURE
 ↓
GOVERNANCE
 ↓
RISK SIGNALS
 ↓
RESPONSE
```

The central distinction is:

> **Monitoring is not simply checking whether the model is running. It is determining whether the model is still behaving as expected, producing acceptable outcomes, remaining secure, and operating within its approved risk boundaries.**

### 41. Model Monitoring Architecture

A mature enterprise monitoring architecture collects signals from the entire model operating environment rather than observing only model accuracy.

```text
                    MODEL REGISTRY
                          │
                          ↓
                   MODEL METADATA
                          │
                          ↓
 ┌─────────────── PRODUCTION MODEL ───────────────┐
 │                                                │
 ↓                                                ↓
INPUT DATA                                      OUTPUTS
 │                                                │
 ↓                                                ↓
Data Quality                                  Predictions
Drift                                           Scores
Schema                                          Confidence
 │                                                │
 └──────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┘
                    ↓
              MONITORING LAYER
                    │
       ┌────────────┼────────────┐
       ↓            ↓            ↓
   Analytics     Security     Operations
       │            │            │
       └────────────┼────────────┘
                    ↓
              RISK ENGINE
                    ↓
             ALERT / RESPONSE
```

---

### 42. Monitoring Layers

Model monitoring can be divided into several layers:

1. **Infrastructure monitoring**
2. **Application monitoring**
3. **Data monitoring**
4. **Model monitoring**
5. **Security monitoring**
6. **Business monitoring**
7. **Governance monitoring**

Each layer answers a different question.

---

### 43. Infrastructure Monitoring

Infrastructure monitoring asks:

> Is the technical environment operating correctly?

Typical metrics include:

* CPU utilization.
* GPU utilization.
* Memory.
* Storage.
* Network.
* Availability.
* Container health.
* Service health.

---

### 44. Application Monitoring

Application monitoring focuses on the service surrounding the model.

Examples include:

* API availability.
* Request failures.
* Response times.
* Authentication errors.
* Queue depth.
* Service dependencies.

---

### 45. Data Monitoring

Data monitoring asks:

> Is the model receiving the data it expects?

Monitoring can include:

* Completeness.
* Validity.
* Freshness.
* Schema.
* Distribution.
* Missing values.
* Outliers.

---

### 46. Model Monitoring

Model monitoring asks:

> Is the model behaving as expected?

Potential measurements include:

* Prediction distribution.
* Confidence.
* Accuracy.
* Precision.
* Recall.
* Error rate.
* Calibration.

---

### 47. Security Monitoring

Security monitoring asks:

> Is the model or its surrounding environment being abused or attacked?

Examples include:

* Abnormal query patterns.
* Model extraction indicators.
* Adversarial inputs.
* Unauthorized access.
* Model integrity changes.

---

### 48. Business Monitoring

Business monitoring asks:

> Is the model still producing useful business outcomes?

Examples include:

* Fraud prevented.
* Revenue impact.
* Conversion rate.
* Risk reduction.
* Operational efficiency.

---

### 49. Governance Monitoring

Governance monitoring asks:

> Is the model still operating within its approved governance requirements?

This can include:

* Approved model version.
* Approved use case.
* Required reviews.
* Risk classification.
* Control status.
* Exception status.

---

### 50. Monitoring Hierarchy

These layers can be visualized as:

```text id="mm250"
Governance
    ↑
Business
    ↑
Security
    ↑
Model
    ↑
Data
    ↑
Application
    ↑
Infrastructure
```

A model can appear healthy at one layer while experiencing problems at another.

---

### 51. Golden Signals for Model Services

Traditional application monitoring often focuses on:

* Latency.
* Traffic.
* Errors.
* Saturation.

These remain relevant to ML systems but should be supplemented with model-specific signals.

---

### 52. ML-Specific Golden Signals

A model monitoring program may add:

```text id="mm252"
Traffic
Latency
Errors
Saturation
     +
Drift
Prediction Distribution
Confidence
Performance
Security Signals
```

---

### 53. Monitoring Frequency

Different signals require different monitoring frequencies.

| Signal               | Possible Frequency |
| -------------------- | ------------------ |
| Service availability | Real time          |
| API errors           | Real time          |
| Suspicious access    | Real time          |
| Input quality        | Hourly/Daily       |
| Drift                | Daily/Continuous   |
| Accuracy             | Periodic           |
| Calibration          | Periodic           |
| Business outcome     | Weekly/Monthly     |

The actual frequency should depend on risk and operational requirements.

---

### 54. Real-Time Monitoring

Real-time monitoring is appropriate when rapid detection is important.

Examples include:

* Cybersecurity detection.
* Fraud detection.
* Safety systems.
* High-value transactions.

---

### 55. Periodic Monitoring

Periodic monitoring can be appropriate where outcomes are slow to materialize.

For example:

```text id="mm255"
Daily Predictions
       ↓
Weekly Outcomes
       ↓
Weekly Performance Analysis
```

---

### 56. Event-Based Monitoring

Monitoring can also be triggered by specific events.

Examples:

* New model deployment.
* Significant configuration change.
* Security incident.
* Data-source change.
* Major business event.

---

### 57. Monitoring Baselines

A monitoring system needs reference values.

Baselines may represent:

* Expected traffic.
* Normal latency.
* Normal prediction distribution.
* Expected error rates.
* Normal feature distributions.

---

### 58. Baseline Windows

Different metrics may require different historical windows.

For example:

```text id="mm258"
Latency → Recent 7 days

Prediction Distribution → Recent 30 days

Seasonal Business Metric → Same period last year
```

There is no universal baseline period.

---

### 59. Static vs. Dynamic Baselines

As discussed in model drift:

**Static baseline**

```text id="mm259"
Fixed Reference
     ↓
Current Production
```

**Dynamic baseline**

```text id="mm260"
Recent Production
     ↓
Updated Reference
     ↓
Current Production
```

Dynamic baselines can adapt but require governance to prevent abnormal behavior from becoming normalized.

---

### 60. Monitoring Thresholds

Thresholds define when an observation becomes actionable.

Examples:

```text id="mm261"
Normal
  ↓
Warning
  ↓
Critical
```

Thresholds should reflect the risk of the model rather than arbitrary technical values.

---

### 61. Static Thresholds

A static threshold remains fixed.

Example:

```text id="mm262"
False Negative Rate > 5%
          ↓
Alert
```

Static thresholds are simple but may not account for changing conditions.

---

### 62. Dynamic Thresholds

Dynamic thresholds adapt to historical behavior.

For example:

```text id="mm263"
Historical Range
       ↓
Expected Boundary
       ↓
Current Observation
```

They can be useful for variable workloads.

---

### 63. Threshold Risk

Thresholds that are too sensitive can generate excessive alerts.

Thresholds that are too permissive can delay detection.

```text id="mm265"
Too Sensitive
     ↓
Alert Fatigue

Too Permissive
     ↓
Missed Problems
```

---

### 64. Model-Specific Thresholds

Different models require different thresholds.

A fraud model, recommendation model, and cybersecurity detection model should not necessarily use the same monitoring criteria.

---

### 65. Critical Model Thresholds

High-impact models should generally have tighter controls around:

* Availability.
* Performance.
* Security.
* Drift.
* Prediction anomalies.

---

### 66. Monitoring Data Collection

Monitoring systems should collect sufficient telemetry to support investigation.

Examples include:

* Timestamp.
* Model version.
* Request identifier.
* Application.
* Input metadata.
* Output metadata.
* Confidence.
* Latency.
* Decision.

---

### 67. Privacy-Aware Monitoring

Monitoring does not require unrestricted collection of raw model inputs.

Where possible, organizations should use:

* Aggregated metrics.
* Pseudonymized identifiers.
* Feature statistics.
* Redacted logs.

---

### 68. Monitoring Logs

Logs should support troubleshooting and auditability.

Important information can include:

```text id="mm268"
Timestamp
Model Version
Request ID
Result
Latency
Status
Security Event
```

---

### 69. Correlation IDs

A correlation identifier can connect:

```text id="mm269"
User Request
    ↓
API
    ↓
Model
    ↓
Prediction
    ↓
Business Action
```

This is valuable during investigations.

---

### 70. Model Observability

**Model observability** refers to the organization's ability to understand model behavior using available telemetry.

A model is highly observable when teams can determine:

* What happened.
* When it happened.
* Which model version was involved.
* Which inputs were affected.
* What output was produced.
* What environmental conditions existed.

---

### 71. Observability vs. Monitoring

Monitoring identifies predefined conditions.

Observability provides the information needed to investigate conditions that may not have been anticipated.

```text id="mm271"
Monitoring
"What happened?"

Observability
"Why did it happen?"
```

Both are valuable.

---

### 72. Model Explainability in Monitoring

Explainability information can help investigate changes in model behavior.

For example:

```text id="mm272"
Prediction Change
      ↓
Feature Contribution
      ↓
Changed Feature
      ↓
Investigation
```

Explainability should be treated as an investigative aid rather than an absolute explanation of causality.

---

### 73. Feature Attribution Monitoring

For selected models, organizations can monitor whether feature contributions change significantly over time.

Unexpected changes may indicate:

* Data changes.
* Model changes.
* Concept drift.
* Pipeline issues.

---

### 74. Input-to-Output Monitoring

The relationship between inputs and outputs can also be monitored.

```text id="mm274"
Input Pattern
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Output Pattern
```

Unexpected relationships may reveal model degradation or unusual activity.

---

### 75. Behavioral Monitoring

Behavioral monitoring focuses on whether the model acts consistently with its intended purpose.

This is particularly important where conventional accuracy metrics are insufficient.

---

### 76. Business Rule Monitoring

Models often operate alongside business rules.

For example:

```text id="mm276"
Model Score
     +
Business Rule
     ↓
Final Decision
```

Both components should be monitored.

---

### 77. Decision Monitoring

The final business or security decision may be more important than the model prediction itself.

Monitoring should therefore consider:

```text id="mm277"
Input
 ↓
Model Prediction
 ↓
Business Logic
 ↓
Final Decision
 ↓
Real-World Outcome
```

---

### 78. Outcome Monitoring

Outcome monitoring closes the feedback loop.

```text id="mm278"
Prediction
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Outcome
    ↓
Compare
    ↓
Model Performance
```

---

### 79. Monitoring Feedback Loop

The complete lifecycle becomes:

```text id="mm279"
Production
    ↓
Telemetry
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Alert
    ↓
Investigation
    ↓
Remediation
    ↓
Validation
    ↓
Updated Monitoring
```

---

### 80. Core Principle

**Effective model monitoring requires visibility across the entire AI operating environment—not just the model itself.**

```text id="mm280"
INFRASTRUCTURE
       ↓
APPLICATION
       ↓
DATA
       ↓
MODEL
       ↓
PREDICTION
       ↓
SECURITY
       ↓
BUSINESS OUTCOME
       ↓
GOVERNANCE
```

The objective is to create sufficient visibility to answer four fundamental questions:

> **Is the model available?**

> **Is the model behaving correctly?**

> **Is the model remaining secure?**

> **Is the model still producing acceptable outcomes within its approved risk boundaries?**

## Part 3

### 81. Model Monitoring Alerting

Monitoring becomes operationally useful when detected anomalies can generate appropriate alerts.

A monitoring system should distinguish between:

* Informational events.
* Warnings.
* Material deviations.
* Critical conditions.

```text id="mm381"
Telemetry
   ↓
Detection
   ↓
Severity Assessment
   ↓
Alert
   ↓
Investigation / Response
```

---

### 82. Alert Quality

The objective is not to generate as many alerts as possible.

The objective is to generate **actionable alerts**.

Poorly designed monitoring can produce:

```text id="mm382"
Too Many Alerts
      ↓
Alert Fatigue
      ↓
Ignored Alerts
      ↓
Reduced Security
```

---

### 83. Alert Severity

A practical model can use:

| Level         | Meaning                 | Typical Action     |
| ------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------ |
| Informational | Expected change         | Record             |
| Low           | Minor deviation         | Monitor            |
| Medium        | Material deviation      | Investigate        |
| High          | Significant risk        | Escalate           |
| Critical      | Immediate material risk | Immediate response |

Thresholds should be tailored to the model.

---

### 84. Alert Deduplication

The same underlying issue may generate thousands of technical alerts.

Monitoring systems should correlate related alerts where possible.

```text id="mm384"
1 Root Cause
   ↓
1000 Alerts
   ↓
Correlation
   ↓
1 Investigation
```

---

### 85. Alert Correlation

Model alerts can be correlated with:

* SIEM events.
* Application incidents.
* Infrastructure alerts.
* Change-management records.
* Data-quality events.

This provides operational context.

---

### 86. Alert Enrichment

An alert should contain enough information for an analyst to understand its significance.

Useful enrichment includes:

* Model name.
* Model version.
* Criticality.
* Metric breached.
* Current value.
* Expected range.
* Time detected.
* Affected segment.
* Recent changes.
* Owner.

---

### 87. Example Alert

```text id="mm387"
MODEL ALERT

Model: Fraud Detection
Version: 4.2
Severity: High

Metric:
False-negative rate

Expected:
< 5%

Observed:
11.8%

Detected:
14:32 UTC

Potential impact:
Reduced fraud detection

Recent change:
Feature pipeline updated 2 hours earlier
```

The example illustrates how technical telemetry can become actionable information.

---

### 88. Alert Routing

Alerts should reach the team capable of responding.

For example:

```text id="mm388"
Data Quality Issue
      ↓
Data Engineering

Model Performance Issue
      ↓
Model Owner / Data Science

Security Anomaly
      ↓
SOC / Security

Governance Issue
      ↓
Risk / GRC
```

---

### 89. Escalation Rules

Critical alerts should have predefined escalation paths.

For example:

```text id="mm389"
Alert
 ↓
Primary Owner
 ↓
Security / Risk
 ↓
Management
```

Escalation should depend on severity and business impact.

---

### 90. Alert Suppression

Known, approved conditions may temporarily suppress alerts.

Examples include:

* Planned maintenance.
* Approved migration.
* Controlled model deployment.

Suppression should be:

* Authorized.
* Documented.
* Time-limited.
* Auditable.

---

### 91. Alert Acknowledgement

The monitoring system should record whether an alert was acknowledged.

Possible states:

```text id="mm391"
New
 ↓
Acknowledged
 ↓
Investigating
 ↓
Resolved
 ↓
Closed
```

---

### 92. Alert Investigation

An investigation should determine:

1. What changed?
2. Is the signal genuine?
3. What caused the change?
4. What model is affected?
5. What users or systems are affected?
6. Is security involved?
7. Is remediation necessary?

---

### 93. Monitoring Runbooks

Critical monitoring alerts should have associated runbooks.

A runbook defines:

* Initial checks.
* Evidence to collect.
* Teams to contact.
* Containment options.
* Escalation criteria.
* Recovery procedures.

---

### 94. Example Runbook

```text id="mm394"
High Model Error Rate
        ↓
1. Verify metric
        ↓
2. Check recent deployment
        ↓
3. Check data pipeline
        ↓
4. Check infrastructure
        ↓
5. Check model version
        ↓
6. Assess security indicators
        ↓
7. Determine remediation
```

---

### 95. Automated Response

Some monitoring conditions may justify automated action.

Examples:

* Restarting an unhealthy service.
* Removing an invalid data source.
* Blocking an unauthorized API client.
* Routing traffic to a healthy model instance.

Automated actions should be carefully controlled.

---

### 96. Automated Model Containment

For highly critical models, predefined controls may restrict model operation when severe conditions occur.

For example:

```text id="mm396"
Critical Failure
      ↓
Model Restricted
      ↓
Fallback / Human Review
      ↓
Investigation
```

---

### 97. Human-in-the-Loop Fallback

Where model failure could create significant consequences, the organization may use human review as a fallback.

```text id="mm397"
Model
 ↓
Normal → Automated Decision

Failure / Uncertainty
 ↓
Human Review
```

---

### 98. Fail-Safe vs. Fail-Open

Model-dependent systems should define how they behave when the model becomes unavailable or unreliable.

**Fail-safe** generally means restricting action to reduce potential harm.

**Fail-open** allows the process to continue despite model failure.

The appropriate approach depends on the use case and risk.

---

### 99. Model Monitoring During Incidents

Monitoring becomes particularly important during security or operational incidents.

For example:

```text id="mm399"
Security Incident
      ↓
Model Behavior
      ↓
Prediction Changes
      ↓
Potential Secondary Impact
```

Incident response should consider dependent AI systems.

---

### 100. Monitoring During Cyberattacks

Security teams should consider whether an attack is:

* Affecting model inputs.
* Affecting model infrastructure.
* Manipulating outputs.
* Increasing false positives.
* Increasing false negatives.
* Attempting model extraction.

---

### 101. Model Integrity Monitoring

Organizations should continuously or periodically verify that the deployed model artifact has not been altered.

Controls may include:

* Hash verification.
* Digital signatures.
* Trusted registries.
* Deployment attestation.

---

### 102. Unexpected Model Change

An unexpected model version change should be treated as a potentially significant event.

```text id="mm402"
Approved Version
       ↓
Production

Unexpected Version
       ↓
Integrity / Change Investigation
```

---

### 103. Model Registry Reconciliation

The production model should correspond to an approved model in the registry.

```text id="mm403"
Production
   ↓
Model v5.1
   ↓
Registry
   ↓
Approved?
```

If no approved record exists, investigation may be required.

---

### 104. Monitoring Model Dependencies

Monitoring should also cover critical dependencies.

For example:

```text id="mm404"
Model
 ├── Feature Store
 ├── Database
 ├── API
 ├── Runtime
 └── Infrastructure
```

A dependency failure can appear as a model problem.

---

### 105. Feature Store Monitoring

Where a feature store is used, monitoring may include:

* Feature freshness.
* Missing features.
* Unexpected distributions.
* Feature availability.
* Feature version.

---

### 106. Training–Serving Consistency

A critical monitoring concern is whether production feature processing matches training-time processing.

```text id="mm406"
Training
  ↓
Feature Transformation A

Production
  ↓
Feature Transformation B

A ≠ B
  ↓
Potential Model Degradation
```

---

### 107. Training–Serving Skew

**Training–serving skew** occurs when the data or feature transformation used during inference differs materially from what the model encountered during training.

This can produce degraded predictions without an obvious infrastructure failure.

---

### 108. Monitoring for Skew

Organizations can compare:

* Feature definitions.
* Transformation logic.
* Data types.
* Statistical distributions.
* Missing-value behavior.

---

### 109. Monitoring Model Dependencies Over Time

Dependencies should be monitored after updates.

For example:

```text id="mm409"
Library Update
      ↓
Model Runtime
      ↓
Prediction Behavior
      ↓
Performance Monitoring
```

A seemingly harmless dependency update can alter model behavior.

---

### 110. Model Performance Regression

A new model version may appear better in offline testing but perform worse in production.

Monitoring should therefore compare:

```text id="mm410"
Offline Performance
        ↕
Production Performance
```

Significant differences require investigation.

---

### 111. Production vs. Validation Gap

A gap between validation and production performance may indicate:

* Data differences.
* Environmental changes.
* Sampling differences.
* Pipeline problems.
* Overfitting.
* Concept drift.

---

### 112. Online Evaluation

Where practical, models can be evaluated against real production outcomes.

Possible techniques include:

* Controlled experiments.
* Shadow deployment.
* A/B testing.
* Canary deployment.

---

### 113. A/B Testing

Two model versions can receive controlled traffic.

```text id="mm413"
Production Traffic
      │
 ┌────┴─────┐
 ↓          ↓
Model A    Model B
 ↓          ↓
Outcomes Compared
```

A/B testing should consider business and security metrics, not just accuracy.

---

### 114. Monitoring Fairness

Where fairness is relevant to the approved use case, organizations may monitor outcome differences across appropriate groups.

Potential measures include:

* Selection rates.
* Error rates.
* False-positive rates.
* False-negative rates.

The monitoring approach should align with the applicable legal, ethical, and business requirements.

---

### 115. Fairness Drift

Fairness characteristics can change over time even when overall model accuracy remains stable.

```text id="mm415"
Overall Accuracy
      ↓
Stable

Group-Level Outcomes
      ↓
Material Change
```

This illustrates why aggregate performance alone may be insufficient.

---

### 116. Privacy Monitoring

Monitoring should also consider whether model operations are creating unexpected privacy risks.

Potential signals include:

* Excessive data collection.
* Unauthorized access.
* Unexpected output exposure.
* Sensitive information appearing in logs.

---

### 117. Monitoring Sensitive Outputs

Outputs should be monitored where they could inadvertently expose sensitive information.

Controls can include:

* Output filtering.
* Logging restrictions.
* Access controls.
* Data-loss prevention.

---

### 118. Monitoring Model Usage

A model may be used differently from its original approved purpose.

For example:

```text id="mm418"
Approved Use Case
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Production

New Usage
      ↓
Outside Approved Scope?
      ↓
Governance Review
```

---

### 119. Use-Case Drift

**Use-case drift** occurs when the way a model is used changes from its originally approved purpose.

This is distinct from statistical model drift.

Examples:

* A model approved for recommendations is used for eligibility decisions.
* A security model is reused in a different environment without reassessment.

---

### 120. Core Principle

**Effective monitoring must observe not only the model's technical performance but also the conditions under which the model operates and the way its outputs are used.**

```text id="mm420"
MODEL
 ↓
DATA
 ↓
PIPELINE
 ↓
INFRASTRUCTURE
 ↓
SECURITY
 ↓
USAGE
 ↓
BUSINESS OUTCOME
 ↓
GOVERNANCE
```

A mature monitoring program therefore asks:

> **Is the model still the approved model?**

> **Is it receiving appropriate data?**

> **Is it producing reliable outputs?**

> **Is the surrounding system secure and healthy?**

> **Is the model still being used for the purpose for which it was approved?**

### 121. Model Monitoring Governance

Model monitoring should be governed as a formal lifecycle control rather than treated only as an operational dashboard.

The governance objective is to ensure that monitoring results lead to appropriate decisions.

### 123. Model Criticality and Monitoring

### 159. Monitoring Maturity

A practical maturity model is:

```text id="mm459"
Level 1 — Reactive
       ↓
Level 2 — Defined
       ↓
Level 3 — Monitored
       ↓
Level 4 — Integrated
       ↓
Level 5 — Adaptive
```

**Level 1 — Reactive**

Problems are discovered primarily after users report them.

**Level 2 — Defined**

Monitoring requirements and ownership exist on paper.

**Level 3 — Monitored**

Production models have operational monitoring and alerting.

**Level 4 — Integrated**

Monitoring is connected to MLOps, cybersecurity, GRC, incident response, and change management.

**Level 5 — Adaptive**

The organization continuously improves monitoring based on incidents, drift, emerging threats, and operational experience.

### 160. Final Principle

**Model monitoring is the continuous assurance mechanism that keeps an approved AI system within its expected technical, security, performance, and governance boundaries after deployment.**

The complete control loop is:

```text id="mm460"
        APPROVE
           ↓
        DEPLOY
           ↓
        MONITOR
           ↓
         DETECT
           ↓
        ANALYZE
           ↓
      RISK ASSESSMENT
           ↓
     ┌─────┴─────┐
     ↓           ↓
  ACCEPT      REMEDIATE
     │           │
     └─────┬─────┘
           ↓
       VALIDATE
           ↓
       DOCUMENT
           ↓
       CONTINUE
       MONITORING
```

The key GRC principle is:

> **Deployment is not the end of model assurance. It is the point at which continuous assurance begins.**

A model should remain trustworthy only as long as the organization can demonstrate that its **data, behavior, performance, security, infrastructure, usage, and governance status remain within approved boundaries**.
