# 03.18 — AI Governance Reporting

## AI Governance Reporting Framework, Information Flows, Management Reporting, and Decision Support

# 1. Introduction to AI Governance Reporting

AI governance reporting is the structured communication of AI governance information to the people responsible for oversight, risk management, decision-making, and accountability.

The purpose of AI governance reporting is to ensure that relevant stakeholders understand:

* The organization's AI portfolio.
* AI risk exposure.
* Governance performance.
* Control effectiveness.
* Significant AI incidents.
* Exceptions and risk acceptances.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Governance weaknesses.
* Emerging AI risks.
* Required management actions.
* Decisions requiring escalation.

Reporting transforms governance information into actionable management intelligence.

A governance framework without effective reporting may have policies, committees, controls, and responsibilities but still fail to provide leadership with sufficient visibility into AI risk.

---

# 2. Purpose of AI Governance Reporting

The primary purposes of AI governance reporting are to:

1. Provide transparency.
2. Support informed decision-making.
3. Demonstrate accountability.
4. Communicate AI risk exposure.
5. Monitor governance performance.
6. Identify emerging risks.
7. Escalate material issues.
8. Support regulatory compliance.
9. Demonstrate control effectiveness.
10. Enable continuous improvement.

Reporting should therefore be considered an integral component of the AI governance operating model rather than an administrative activity.

---

# 3. AI Governance Reporting Principles

An effective reporting framework should follow several principles.

### Accuracy

Information should be factually reliable and based on validated data.

### Relevance

Reports should contain information relevant to the intended audience.

### Timeliness

Material information should reach decision-makers quickly enough to support action.

### Completeness

Important risks and exceptions should not be omitted.

### Consistency

Metrics and reporting definitions should remain consistent across reporting periods.

### Transparency

Material assumptions, limitations, and uncertainties should be disclosed.

### Risk-Based Reporting

Reporting depth should correspond to AI risk and business impact.

### Action Orientation

Reports should clearly identify what action is required.

---

# 4. Reporting Is Not the Same as Data Collection

AI governance reporting should not simply reproduce information collected from governance systems.

There is an important distinction:

```text
Data
  ↓
Information
  ↓
Analysis
  ↓
Insight
  ↓
Decision
```

For example:

### Data

> 250 AI systems are registered.

### Information

> 230 of 250 systems have completed required risk classification.

### Insight

> High-risk AI classification coverage has declined because AI adoption increased faster than governance capacity.

### Decision

> Management should increase governance assessment capacity and prioritize the remaining high-risk systems.

The value of reporting comes from the progression from data to decision.

---

# 5. AI Governance Reporting Hierarchy

AI governance reporting should operate at multiple organizational levels.

A typical hierarchy is:

```text
Operational Reporting
        ↓
Management Reporting
        ↓
Governance Committee Reporting
        ↓
Executive Reporting
        ↓
Board Reporting
```

Each level should receive information appropriate to its responsibilities.

Operational teams require detailed information, while Boards generally require concise information focused on material risk, strategic implications, and decisions.

* Significant incidents.

* Strategic risks.
* Decisions requiring executive approval.

---

# 10. Board-Level AI Governance Reporting

Board reporting should focus on matters that fall within Board oversight responsibilities.

This may include:

* Material AI risks.
* Strategic AI opportunities and risks.
* Risk appetite.
* Significant AI incidents.
* Regulatory exposure.
* AI-related business resilience.
* Material legal or reputational risks.
* Governance effectiveness.
* Management response to significant AI risks.

The Board should not normally receive every individual AI governance metric.

Instead, reporting should provide a concise view of whether management is effectively governing AI within approved risk boundaries.

# 12. Need-to-Know Reporting

AI governance reporting should follow a need-to-know principle.

A stakeholder should receive information necessary to fulfill their governance responsibilities.

This reduces:

* Information overload.
* Unnecessary disclosure.
* Confusion.
* Duplicate reporting.
* Misinterpretation.

It also helps protect sensitive AI information.

# 31. Limitations of Traffic-Light Reporting

Traffic lights can oversimplify complex risks.

For example:

> Green

does not necessarily mean:

> No AI risk exists.

It may only mean:

> The metric is currently within the approved threshold.

Reports should therefore provide sufficient context for material areas.

# 33. AI Governance Composite Scores

Organizations may create composite governance scores.

However, composite scores should be used cautiously.

A single score can hide:

* Critical weaknesses.
* Risk concentrations.
* Severe individual control failures.
* Important exceptions.

For example:

> Overall AI Governance Score = 94%

could conceal one critical AI system with unacceptable risk.

Material risk should therefore always remain visible even when aggregate performance appears strong.
