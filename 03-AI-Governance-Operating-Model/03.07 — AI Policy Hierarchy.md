# 03.07 — AI Policy Hierarchy

## Introduction

An AI Policy Hierarchy defines how an organization's AI governance requirements are structured from high-level principles and executive direction down to detailed operational requirements.

As organizations adopt AI across multiple business units, technologies, and jurisdictions, a single AI policy is rarely sufficient. Different levels of governance documentation are required to translate strategic intent into specific, actionable requirements.

A mature AI Policy Hierarchy creates a controlled relationship between:

**Principles → Policies → Standards → Procedures → Guidelines → Controls → Evidence**

This hierarchy ensures that AI governance requirements are:

* Clearly defined.
* Consistently interpreted.
* Assigned to accountable owners.
* Implemented through operational controls.
* Measurable.
* Auditable.
* Reviewable.
* Adaptable to changing AI technologies and regulations.

The AI Policy Hierarchy therefore serves as the **documentary architecture of AI governance**.

---

# Purpose of an AI Policy Hierarchy

The primary purpose of an AI Policy Hierarchy is to establish a clear structure for translating enterprise AI governance objectives into operational requirements.

It should help the organization answer:

* What does the organization require?
* Why is the requirement necessary?
* Who is responsible?
* How must the requirement be implemented?
* What evidence demonstrates compliance?
* What happens when the requirement cannot be met?

Without a defined hierarchy, organizations may create overlapping, contradictory, or excessively detailed policies that are difficult to maintain.

---

# Why AI Requires a Structured Policy Hierarchy

AI introduces governance requirements across many disciplines.

These may include:

* AI governance.
* AI security.
* AI risk.
* Privacy.
* Data governance.
* Responsible AI.
* Model governance.
* Software development.
* Third-party risk.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Business continuity.

Each domain may require its own policies, standards, procedures, and controls.

For example:

**AI Governance Policy**

may establish the organization's overall requirements for AI.

Supporting documents may then define:

**AI Security Standard**

which specifies minimum cybersecurity requirements.

An operational document may define:

**AI Security Procedure**

which explains how those requirements are implemented.

This creates a structured chain from governance intent to operational execution.

---

# The AI Policy Hierarchy

A typical hierarchy can be represented as:

```text
Enterprise Governance
        │
        ▼
AI Governance Principles
        │
        ▼
AI Policies
        │
        ▼
AI Standards
        │
        ▼
AI Procedures
        │
        ▼
AI Guidelines
        │
        ▼
AI Controls
        │
        ▼
Evidence and Records
```

Each level has a different purpose.

---

# 1. AI Governance Principles

Principles represent the highest-level expectations for AI governance.

They communicate fundamental organizational positions such as:

* AI must be governed according to risk.
* AI systems must have accountable owners.
* AI security must be incorporated throughout the AI lifecycle.
* Sensitive data must be appropriately protected.
* High-risk AI systems require enhanced oversight.
* Material AI risks must be escalated.
* AI decisions must remain accountable to authorized humans where required.

Principles should be stable and strategic.

They should not contain excessive operational detail.

---

# 2. AI Policies

Policies translate governance principles into mandatory organizational requirements.

An AI policy may establish requirements concerning:

* AI system approval.
* AI risk management.
* AI security.
* AI data usage.
* AI development.
* Third-party AI services.
* AI monitoring.
* AI incident management.
* AI accountability.

Policies should answer:

**What must the organization do?**

They generally should not attempt to explain every technical implementation detail.

---

# 3. AI Standards

Standards define specific mandatory requirements that support policy implementation.

For example, an AI Security Policy may state:

> AI systems must implement appropriate access controls.

An AI Security Standard may specify:

* Strong authentication.
* Least privilege.
* Role-based access.
* Privileged access controls.
* Access reviews.
* Service identity requirements.

Standards therefore translate broad policy requirements into measurable expectations.

---

# 4. AI Procedures

Procedures explain how organizational requirements are executed.

A procedure may describe:

* Who performs an activity.
* What steps must be followed.
* What systems are used.
* What approvals are required.
* What evidence must be retained.
* What happens when an exception occurs.

For example:

**AI Risk Assessment Procedure**

may define the workflow for:

**Register → Classify → Assess → Treat → Approve → Monitor**

Procedures should be operational and actionable.

---

# 5. AI Guidelines

Guidelines provide recommended practices and practical direction.

Unlike mandatory policies and standards, guidelines may allow greater flexibility.

Examples include:

* Recommended prompt security practices.
* Recommended AI development practices.
* Suggested documentation methods.
* Recommended model evaluation approaches.
* Recommended AI architecture patterns.

Guidelines can help users make better decisions without creating unnecessary mandatory requirements.

---

# 6. AI Controls

Controls are the mechanisms used to manage identified risks and enforce governance requirements.

Controls may be:

### Preventive

Designed to prevent an undesirable event.

Example:

**AI systems cannot access production databases without authorization.**

### Detective

Designed to identify undesirable activity.

Example:

**AI agent activity is continuously monitored.**

### Corrective

Designed to respond to or recover from an event.

Example:

**Compromised AI credentials are automatically revoked.**

Controls should be traceable to the requirements they support.

---

# 7. Evidence and Records

Evidence demonstrates that governance requirements and controls are operating.

Examples include:

* Risk assessments.
* Approval records.
* Security assessment reports.
* Model documentation.
* Access reviews.
* Audit logs.
* Exception records.
* Training records.
* Incident reports.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence allows the organization to demonstrate that governance exists not only on paper but in practice.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Governance Authority

The hierarchy should also establish different levels of authority.

For example:

| Level      | Primary Purpose                                 |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Principles | Establish strategic direction                   |
| Policies   | Establish mandatory organizational requirements |
| Standards  | Define mandatory implementation requirements    |
| Procedures | Define execution steps                          |
| Guidelines | Provide recommended practices                   |
| Controls   | Manage risk and enforce requirements            |
| Evidence   | Demonstrate implementation                      |

Higher-level documents should generally have greater governance authority.

Lower-level documents should implement rather than contradict higher-level requirements.

---

# Mandatory Versus Advisory Documents

One important characteristic of the hierarchy is distinguishing mandatory requirements from recommendations.

### Mandatory

* Policies.
* Standards.
* Required procedures.
* Mandatory controls.

### Advisory

* Guidelines.
* Recommendations.
* Reference practices.
* Examples.

This distinction should be explicitly stated within the governance framework.

Ambiguous language such as "should," "may," and "where appropriate" should be carefully controlled in mandatory documents.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and AI Risk

The policy hierarchy should be risk-sensitive.

Not every AI system requires the same level of control.

For example:

### Low-Risk AI

May require:

* Basic policy compliance.
* Standard security controls.
* Basic documentation.

### Moderate-Risk AI

May require:

* Formal risk assessment.
* Additional standards.
* Enhanced monitoring.

### High-Risk AI

May require:

* Formal governance approval.
* Enhanced security controls.
* Independent assessment.
* Human oversight.
* Formal risk acceptance where applicable.

The policy hierarchy therefore provides the foundation for applying **proportionate governance**.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and the AI Lifecycle

AI policies should apply across the complete AI lifecycle.

A simplified relationship is:

**Idea → Design → Development → Testing → Approval → Deployment → Operation → Monitoring → Change → Retirement**

Each stage may require specific governance documents and controls.

For example:

**AI Policy**

defines overall governance.

**AI Development Standard**

defines development requirements.

**AI Testing Procedure**

defines testing activities.

**AI Monitoring Standard**

defines operational monitoring.

**AI Retirement Procedure**

defines requirements for decommissioning.

This creates lifecycle-based governance rather than governance concentrated only at deployment.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Regulatory Requirements

AI policies should also provide a mechanism for translating external obligations into internal requirements.

The relationship can be represented as:

**Law / Regulation / Contract**

↓

**Enterprise Governance Requirement**

↓

**AI Policy**

↓

**AI Standard**

↓

**AI Procedure**

↓

**Control**

↓

**Evidence**

This allows regulatory obligations to be converted into practical organizational requirements.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Control Traceability

Every important AI governance requirement should ideally be traceable through the hierarchy.

For example:

**AI Governance Principle**

↓

**AI Security Policy**

↓

**AI Security Standard**

↓

**AI Access Control Procedure**

↓

**Access Control**

↓

**Access Review Evidence**

This traceability helps demonstrate that strategic governance requirements are actually implemented.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Accountability

Every document within the hierarchy should have a clearly defined owner.

Possible ownership may include:

* AI Governance.
* Cybersecurity.
* Enterprise Risk.
* Data Governance.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Technology.
* AI Engineering.
* Business functions.

Ownership should define who is responsible for:

* Maintaining the document.
* Reviewing changes.
* Approving updates.
* Communicating requirements.
* Monitoring implementation.

A policy without an accountable owner can quickly become outdated.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Document Governance

AI governance documents should themselves be governed.

Document governance should include:

* Document owner.
* Approver.
* Version.
* Effective date.
* Review date.
* Classification.
* Change history.
* Scope.
* Applicability.
* Exception process.

This ensures that AI governance documentation remains controlled and reliable.

---

# Policy Hierarchy and Exceptions

Not every requirement can always be implemented exactly as written.

The hierarchy should therefore define an exception mechanism.

A typical process is:

**Exception Request → Risk Assessment → Compensating Controls → Approval → Expiration → Review**

Exceptions should not silently override policies or standards.

They should be formally documented and approved by the appropriate authority.

---

# Policy Hierarchy as a Governance Control

The policy hierarchy itself should be considered a governance control.

It provides:

* Consistency.
* Accountability.
* Traceability.
* Standardization.
* Clarity.
* Auditability.
* Risk alignment.

Without a structured hierarchy, AI governance can become fragmented across different business and technology functions.

---

# Core Principle

An effective AI Policy Hierarchy ensures that enterprise AI governance can move from **high-level intent to enforceable operational practice**.

The fundamental relationship is:

**Principles define direction.**

**Policies define requirements.**

**Standards define expectations.**

**Procedures define execution.**

**Guidelines provide practical direction.**

**Controls manage risk.**

**Evidence demonstrates compliance.**

This hierarchy creates the documentary foundation necessary for a scalable, consistent, and auditable AI Governance Operating Model.
