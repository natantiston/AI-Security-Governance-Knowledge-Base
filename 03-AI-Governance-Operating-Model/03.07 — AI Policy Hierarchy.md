# 03.07 — AI Policy Hierarchy

## Introduction

An AI Policy Hierarchy defines how an organization's AI governance requirements are structured from high-level principles and executive direction down to detailed operational requirements.

As organizations adopt AI across multiple business units, technologies, and jurisdictions, a single AI policy is rarely sufficient. Different levels of governance documentation are required to translate strategic intent into specific, actionable requirements.

A mature AI Policy Hierarchy creates a controlled relationship between:

**Principles → Policies → Standards → Procedures → Guidelines → Controls → Evidence**

This hierarchy ensures that AI governance requirements are:

* Clearly defined.
* Consistently interpreted.
* Assigned to accountable owners.
* Implemented through operational controls.
* Measurable.
* Auditable.
* Reviewable.
* Adaptable to changing AI technologies and regulations.

The AI Policy Hierarchy therefore serves as the **documentary architecture of AI governance**.

---

# Purpose of an AI Policy Hierarchy

The primary purpose of an AI Policy Hierarchy is to establish a clear structure for translating enterprise AI governance objectives into operational requirements.

It should help the organization answer:

* What does the organization require?
* Why is the requirement necessary?
* Who is responsible?
* How must the requirement be implemented?
* What evidence demonstrates compliance?
* What happens when the requirement cannot be met?

Without a defined hierarchy, organizations may create overlapping, contradictory, or excessively detailed policies that are difficult to maintain.

---

# Why AI Requires a Structured Policy Hierarchy

AI introduces governance requirements across many disciplines.

These may include:

* AI governance.
* AI security.
* AI risk.
* Privacy.
* Data governance.
* Responsible AI.
* Model governance.
* Software development.
* Third-party risk.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Business continuity.

Each domain may require its own policies, standards, procedures, and controls.

For example:

**AI Governance Policy**

may establish the organization's overall requirements for AI.

Supporting documents may then define:

**AI Security Standard**

which specifies minimum cybersecurity requirements.

An operational document may define:

**AI Security Procedure**

which explains how those requirements are implemented.

This creates a structured chain from governance intent to operational execution.

# 1. AI Governance Principles

Principles represent the highest-level expectations for AI governance.

They communicate fundamental organizational positions such as:

* AI must be governed according to risk.
* AI systems must have accountable owners.
* AI security must be incorporated throughout the AI lifecycle.
* Sensitive data must be appropriately protected.
* High-risk AI systems require enhanced oversight.
* Material AI risks must be escalated.
* AI decisions must remain accountable to authorized humans where required.

Principles should be stable and strategic.

They should not contain excessive operational detail.

---

# 2. AI Policies

Policies translate governance principles into mandatory organizational requirements.

An AI policy may establish requirements concerning:

* AI system approval.
* AI risk management.
* AI security.
* AI data usage.
* AI development.
* Third-party AI services.
* AI monitoring.
* AI incident management.
* AI accountability.

Policies should answer:

**What must the organization do?**

They generally should not attempt to explain every technical implementation detail.

---

# 3. AI Standards

Standards define specific mandatory requirements that support policy implementation.

For example, an AI Security Policy may state:

> AI systems must implement appropriate access controls.

An AI Security Standard may specify:

* Strong authentication.
* Least privilege.
* Role-based access.
* Privileged access controls.
* Access reviews.
* Service identity requirements.

Standards therefore translate broad policy requirements into measurable expectations.

---

# 4. AI Procedures

Procedures explain how organizational requirements are executed.

A procedure may describe:

* Who performs an activity.
* What steps must be followed.
* What systems are used.
* What approvals are required.
* What evidence must be retained.
* What happens when an exception occurs.

For example:

**AI Risk Assessment Procedure**

may define the workflow for:

**Register → Classify → Assess → Treat → Approve → Monitor**

Procedures should be operational and actionable.

---

# 5. AI Guidelines

Guidelines provide recommended practices and practical direction.

Unlike mandatory policies and standards, guidelines may allow greater flexibility.

Examples include:

* Recommended prompt security practices.
* Recommended AI development practices.
* Suggested documentation methods.
* Recommended model evaluation approaches.
* Recommended AI architecture patterns.

Guidelines can help users make better decisions without creating unnecessary mandatory requirements.

---

# 6. AI Controls

Controls are the mechanisms used to manage identified risks and enforce governance requirements.

Controls may be:

### Preventive

Designed to prevent an undesirable event.

Example:

**AI systems cannot access production databases without authorization.**

### Detective

Designed to identify undesirable activity.

Example:

**AI agent activity is continuously monitored.**

### Corrective

Designed to respond to or recover from an event.

Example:

**Compromised AI credentials are automatically revoked.**

Controls should be traceable to the requirements they support.

---

# 7. Evidence and Records

Evidence demonstrates that governance requirements and controls are operating.

Examples include:

* Risk assessments.
* Approval records.
* Security assessment reports.
* Model documentation.
* Access reviews.
* Audit logs.
* Exception records.
* Training records.
* Incident reports.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence allows the organization to demonstrate that governance exists not only on paper but in practice.

# Policy Hierarchy and Governance Authority

The hierarchy should also establish different levels of authority.

For example:

| Level      | Primary Purpose                                 |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Principles | Establish strategic direction                   |
| Policies   | Establish mandatory organizational requirements |
| Standards  | Define mandatory implementation requirements    |
| Procedures | Define execution steps                          |
| Guidelines | Provide recommended practices                   |
| Controls   | Manage risk and enforce requirements            |
| Evidence   | Demonstrate implementation                      |

Higher-level documents should generally have greater governance authority.

Lower-level documents should implement rather than contradict higher-level requirements.

# Mandatory Versus Advisory Documents

One important characteristic of the hierarchy is distinguishing mandatory requirements from recommendations.

### Mandatory

* Policies.
* Standards.
* Required procedures.
* Mandatory controls.

### Advisory

* Guidelines.
* Recommendations.
* Reference practices.
* Examples.

This distinction should be explicitly stated within the governance framework.

Ambiguous language such as "should," "may," and "where appropriate" should be carefully controlled in mandatory documents.

# Policy Hierarchy and AI Risk

The policy hierarchy should be risk-sensitive.

Not every AI system requires the same level of control.

For example:

### Low-Risk AI

May require:

* Basic policy compliance.
* Standard security controls.
* Basic documentation.

### Moderate-Risk AI

May require:

* Formal risk assessment.
* Additional standards.
* Enhanced monitoring.

### High-Risk AI

May require:

* Formal governance approval.
* Enhanced security controls.
* Independent assessment.
* Human oversight.
* Formal risk acceptance where applicable.

The policy hierarchy therefore provides the foundation for applying **proportionate governance**.

# Policy Hierarchy and Exceptions

Not every requirement can always be implemented exactly as written.

The hierarchy should therefore define an exception mechanism.

A typical process is:

**Exception Request → Risk Assessment → Compensating Controls → Approval → Expiration → Review**

Exceptions should not silently override policies or standards.

They should be formally documented and approved by the appropriate authority.

An effective AI Policy Hierarchy ensures that enterprise AI governance can move from **high-level intent to enforceable operational practice**.

**Principles define direction.**

**Policies define requirements.**

**Procedures define execution.**

**Guidelines provide practical direction.**

# AI Policy Hierarchy — Part 2

## Policy Hierarchy Structure and Governance Relationships

A mature AI Policy Hierarchy should establish a clear relationship between strategic governance requirements and operational implementation. The objective is not simply to create multiple documents, but to ensure that each document has a defined purpose, authority, owner, audience, and relationship to the documents above and below it.

The hierarchy should prevent two common problems:

1. **Governance gaps** — where an important requirement has no supporting policy or control.
2. **Governance duplication** — where multiple documents establish conflicting or repetitive requirements.

The hierarchy should therefore function as an integrated governance system.

---

A typical AI governance documentation structure may be organized as:

```text
Enterprise Governance

        ▼

        │
        ▼
AI Policies
        │

AI Standards
        │

        │

        │

```

Each layer should have a clearly defined role.

The hierarchy should be sufficiently detailed to support enterprise governance while remaining practical for business and technology teams.

---

## 2. Principles as the Strategic Foundation

AI governance principles establish the organization's fundamental expectations.

Examples include:

* AI must be governed according to risk.
* AI systems must have accountable ownership.
* AI security must be incorporated throughout the lifecycle.
* AI usage must comply with applicable requirements.
* Material AI risks must be identified and managed.
* High-risk AI systems require enhanced oversight.
* AI decisions must have appropriate accountability.

Principles should generally remain stable even when specific technologies change.

---

## 3. Policies as Mandatory Governance Requirements

Policies convert principles into mandatory organizational requirements.

For example:

**Principle:**

> AI systems must be governed according to risk.

**Policy:**

> AI systems must undergo risk assessment before deployment when required by their classification.

The policy establishes the requirement without prescribing every implementation detail.

---

## 4. Standards as Measurable Requirements

Standards should translate policy statements into specific requirements.

For example:

**AI Security Policy**

> AI systems must implement appropriate access controls.

**AI Access Control Standard**

may specify:

* Authentication requirements.
* Authorization requirements.
* Least-privilege requirements.
* Privileged-access requirements.
* Access review frequency.
* Service account requirements.

Standards should be sufficiently precise to support objective assessment.

---

## 5. Procedures as Operational Instructions

Procedures should describe how requirements are performed.

For example, an:

**AI Risk Assessment Procedure**

may define:

1. Register the AI system.
2. Identify the use case.
3. Determine risk classification.
4. Identify applicable risks.
5. Assess inherent risk.
6. Identify controls.
7. Assess residual risk.
8. Obtain required approval.
9. Record the result.
10. Establish monitoring.

Procedures should focus on execution rather than policy interpretation.

---

## 6. Guidelines as Flexible Guidance

Guidelines provide recommended approaches where strict standardization may not be necessary.

Examples include:

* Recommended AI development practices.
* Recommended prompt security practices.
* Suggested model documentation.
* Recommended AI architecture patterns.
* Recommended testing approaches.

Guidelines should clearly indicate that they are advisory unless a particular guideline has been formally incorporated into a mandatory requirement.

---

## 7. Controls as Risk Management Mechanisms

Controls translate governance requirements into practical mechanisms.

For example:

**Policy Requirement:**

AI systems must protect sensitive information.

**Standard:**

Sensitive AI data must be encrypted in transit and at rest.

**Control:**

Encryption is enabled and technically enforced.

**Evidence:**

Encryption configuration and assessment records.

This creates a direct relationship between governance and implementation.

---

## 8. Evidence as Proof of Implementation

Evidence demonstrates that controls are operating.

Evidence may include:

* Approval records.
* Risk assessments.
* Security test reports.
* Access reviews.
* Configuration records.
* Audit logs.
* Training records.
* Incident reports.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence should be retained according to organizational record-management requirements.

## 10. Establish Document Ownership

Every AI governance document should have a named owner.

The owner should be accountable for:

* Content accuracy.
* Periodic review.
* Regulatory alignment.
* Stakeholder consultation.
* Change management.
* Version control.
* Communication.

Ownership should not be assigned merely to a generic department without identifying accountability.

For example:

**AI Governance Policy**

may require executive approval.

**AI Security Standard**

**AI Risk Procedure**

Approval authority should correspond to the significance and scope of the document.

Every document should clearly define its applicability.

Scope may specify:

* Business units.
* Geographic regions.
* AI system types.
* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Third parties.
* Development environments.
* Production environments.

A policy with unclear scope can lead to inconsistent interpretation.

---

## 13. Establish Policy Applicability

The organization should define which AI systems are subject to which requirements.

Applicability may depend on:

* Risk classification.
* Data sensitivity.
* Business criticality.
* Geographic location.
* Regulatory requirements.
* AI autonomy.
* External exposure.

This supports proportional governance.

---

## 14. Establish Policy Relationships

Each document should identify its relationship to related governance documents.

For example:

**AI Governance Policy**

may be supported by:

* AI Risk Management Policy.
* AI Security Policy.
* AI Data Governance Policy.
* AI Third-Party AI Policy.
* AI Model Governance Policy.

These policies should operate as a coherent framework rather than independent documents.

---

## 15. Avoid Policy Conflicts

Multiple AI policies can create conflicting requirements.

For example:

**AI Security Policy**

may require strict access controls.

While:

**AI Innovation Policy**

may encourage rapid experimentation.

The policy hierarchy should establish precedence and mechanisms for resolving conflicts.

A higher-level policy should generally take precedence unless a formally approved exception exists.

---

## 16. Establish Policy Precedence

The organization should define what happens when two governance documents appear to conflict.

A typical precedence model may be:

**Law / Regulation**

↓

**Contractual Requirement**

↓

**Enterprise Policy**

↓

**AI Policy**

↓

**AI Standard**

↓

**AI Procedure**

↓

**AI Guideline**

However, legal and contractual requirements may apply independently of internal documentation hierarchy.

The hierarchy should therefore never be interpreted as allowing an internal document to override applicable law or binding obligations.

---

## 17. Establish Mandatory Language

Mandatory documents should use controlled terminology.

For example:

### "Must"

Indicates a mandatory requirement.

### "Must Not"

Indicates a prohibited activity.

### "Should"

Indicates a recommended practice unless otherwise defined.

### "May"

Indicates an optional activity.

### "Can"

Generally describes capability rather than a requirement.

Consistent terminology reduces ambiguity.

---

## 18. Establish Policy Exceptions

The hierarchy should provide a controlled exception mechanism.

An exception request should normally contain:

* Requirement being excepted.
* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Compensating controls.
* Risk owner.
* Approval authority.
* Start date.
* Expiration date.
* Review requirements.

Exceptions should not be used to permanently bypass governance requirements.

Review triggers may include:

* Major AI incidents.
* Significant technology changes.

A document should remain valid only while its requirements remain appropriate.

## 20. Establish Change Management

Changes to AI governance documentation should follow a controlled process.

A typical workflow is:

**Change Request → Impact Assessment → Review → Approval → Publication → Communication → Implementation**

Changes should be recorded in the document's revision history.

Governance documents should be formally retired when they are no longer needed.

Retirement may occur because:

* The AI technology is obsolete.
* The requirement has been incorporated elsewhere.
* A new policy replaces it.
* The organization has changed its operating model.
* Regulatory requirements have changed.

Retired documents should remain available where required for historical or audit purposes.

# Policy Hierarchy Design Principle

A well-designed AI Policy Hierarchy should establish a logical chain:

**Why → What → How → With What Control → What Evidence**

Where:

**Principles = Why**

**Policies = What**

**Standards = What Specifically**

**Procedures = How**

**Controls = With What Mechanism**

**Evidence = How We Demonstrate It**

This structure allows AI governance to remain strategically aligned while still providing practical implementation requirements.

# Core Governance Rule

The AI Policy Hierarchy should follow one fundamental rule:

> **Every mandatory AI governance requirement should have a clear source, owner, implementation mechanism, and means of demonstrating compliance.**

This creates a governance environment where policies are not isolated documents but interconnected components of the enterprise AI governance operating model.

# AI Policy Hierarchy — Part 3

## Policy Lifecycle, Governance Integration, and Control Traceability

The AI Policy Hierarchy must be managed as a living governance system. AI policies should not be treated as static documents that are created once and then stored in a repository. They must evolve as AI technology, organizational practices, risks, regulations, and business requirements change.

Effective policy governance therefore requires a controlled lifecycle covering **creation, approval, publication, implementation, monitoring, review, revision, exception management, and retirement**.

---

## 1. Establish the AI Policy Lifecycle

A controlled policy lifecycle should generally follow:

**Identify → Draft → Review → Approve → Publish → Implement → Monitor → Review → Update → Retire**

Each stage should have defined responsibilities and decision criteria.

The lifecycle should ensure that policies remain:

* Relevant.
* Current.
* Consistent.
* Enforceable.
* Risk-aligned.
* Auditable.

---

## 2. Policy Identification

The need for a new or revised AI policy may originate from:

* New AI technology.
* New business use cases.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Risk assessments.
* Security incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Emerging threats.
* Organizational changes.
* Third-party dependencies.
* Changes in enterprise strategy.

Policy development should be driven by actual governance requirements rather than document creation for its own sake.

---

## 3. Policy Development

Policy development should involve the appropriate subject matter experts.

Depending on the policy, contributors may include:

* AI Governance.
* Cybersecurity.
* Enterprise Risk.
* Legal.
* Privacy.
* Data Governance.
* Compliance.

Cross-functional participation helps prevent policies from being technically correct but operationally impractical.

Draft policies should undergo structured review before approval.

Review should assess:

* Business applicability.
* Security implications.
* Risk implications.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Privacy requirements.
* Operational feasibility.
* Technical feasibility.
* Control requirements.
* Existing policy conflicts.

The review process should identify problems before the policy becomes mandatory.

## 5. Establish Policy Approval

Approval authority should correspond to the policy's significance.

For example:

**Enterprise AI Policy**

may require executive approval.

**AI Security Standard**

**AI Risk Procedure**

Communication may include:

* Employee announcements.
* Governance newsletters.
* Training.
* AI developer briefings.
* Business-unit communications.

Communication should be appropriate to the policy's scope.

**AI Governance Policy**

↓

**AI Risk Standard**

↓

**AI Risk Assessment Procedure**

↓

**AI Risk Assessment Control**

↓

**AI Risk Assessment Evidence**

## 9. Establish Policy Implementation Ownership

Implementation responsibility should be assigned to appropriate stakeholders.

For example:

* Business owners implement business requirements.
* Security implements security controls.
* Data governance implements data requirements.
* Privacy implements privacy controls.
* Risk functions manage risk requirements.
* Technology teams implement technical controls.

Policy ownership and control ownership do not necessarily have to belong to the same person or function.

---

## 10. Establish Policy Compliance Monitoring

The organization should determine whether policies are being followed.

Monitoring approaches may include:

* Self-assessment.
* Control testing.
* Compliance reviews.
* Automated validation.
* Internal audit.
* Risk assessments.
* Exception reporting.

The level of monitoring should reflect the importance and risk associated with the policy.

## 11. Establish Policy Effectiveness Assessment

Compliance alone does not demonstrate that a policy is effective.

The organization should also ask:

* Is the policy reducing the intended risk?
* Are controls effective?
* Are exceptions increasing?
* Are incidents occurring despite compliance?
* Are users able to understand the requirements?
* Are requirements creating unintended consequences?

This distinguishes:

**Policy Compliance**

from:

**Policy Effectiveness**

## 14. Establish Policy Change Classification

### Moderate

The organization should periodically determine whether:

* The compensating controls remain effective.
* The requirement should be changed.

## 7. Establish Policy Inheritance

The AI Policy Hierarchy should establish a direct connection between **enterprise intent, risk management, operational requirements, and demonstrable control effectiveness**.

The ultimate objective is to ensure that AI governance requirements are **clear, authoritative, risk-based, enforceable, measurable, traceable, and continuously maintained**.
