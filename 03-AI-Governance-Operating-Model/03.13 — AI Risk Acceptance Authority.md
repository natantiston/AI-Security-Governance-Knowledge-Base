# 03.13 — AI Risk Acceptance Authority

## AI Risk Acceptance Authority and Governance Accountability

AI systems inevitably introduce risk. Not every AI security, operational, compliance, privacy, model, data, or business risk can be completely eliminated. Effective AI governance therefore requires an explicit mechanism for determining **who has the authority to accept residual risk** when a risk cannot be fully mitigated.

AI Risk Acceptance Authority defines the organizational authority responsible for making that decision.

The fundamental principle is:

> **The person or committee that accepts AI risk must have sufficient authority, competence, independence, and accountability to accept the potential consequences of that risk.**

Risk acceptance is therefore not simply an administrative approval. It is a formal governance decision that determines whether the organization is willing to operate an AI system despite identified residual risk.

---

# 1. Purpose of AI Risk Acceptance Authority

The purpose of establishing AI Risk Acceptance Authority is to ensure that:

* AI risks have accountable owners.
* Residual risk is explicitly recognized.
* Risk acceptance is performed at the appropriate organizational level.
* Individuals cannot accept risks beyond their authority.
* High-impact risks are escalated appropriately.
* Risk decisions are documented.
* Risk acceptance has defined validity periods.
* Accepted risks remain subject to monitoring and review.

Without defined acceptance authority, organizations may unintentionally allow material AI risks to remain unresolved.

---

# 2. Risk Acceptance as a Governance Decision

Risk acceptance should be distinguished from ordinary risk management activities.

Risk management may involve:

* Identifying risk.
* Analyzing risk.
* Evaluating risk.
* Treating risk.
* Monitoring risk.

Risk acceptance is the decision to knowingly retain residual risk.

A simplified model is:

```text
AI Risk Identified
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Risk Treatment
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Can Risk Be Accepted?
       ↓
Authorized Decision
       ↓
Risk Accepted / Rejected / Escalated
```

The decision should be made by an appropriately authorized party.

---

# 3. Residual AI Risk

Residual risk is the risk that remains after reasonable controls and treatments have been implemented.

For example:

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Security Controls
      ↓
Risk Treatment
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

An organization should not assume that implementing security controls eliminates all risk.

Residual AI risk may remain because of:

* Technical limitations.
* Model uncertainty.
* Data limitations.
* Business constraints.
* Cost considerations.
* Third-party dependencies.
* Regulatory uncertainty.
* Operational limitations.
* Emerging threats.

---

# 4. Risk Acceptance Versus Risk Approval

Risk acceptance should not be confused with general project approval.

A business executive may approve an AI project because it provides business value.

That does not necessarily mean that the same person has authority to accept its cybersecurity or regulatory risks.

Therefore:

> **Project approval and risk acceptance should be treated as separate governance decisions where appropriate.**

The organization should define how these decisions interact.

---

# 5. Risk Acceptance Authority Model

A risk acceptance model may use organizational authority levels.

For example:

```text
Low Risk
   ↓
AI System Owner

Moderate Risk
   ↓
Business / Risk Management

High Risk
   ↓
Senior Management / AI Risk Committee

Critical Risk
   ↓
Executive Committee / Appropriate Executive Authority
```

The exact thresholds should be defined by the organization's risk framework.

---

# 6. Risk Appetite

Risk acceptance authority must operate within the organization's AI and enterprise risk appetite.

Risk appetite describes the amount and type of risk the organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

For AI governance, risk appetite may address:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.
* Operational resilience.
* Financial impact.
* Reputation.
* Customer impact.
* Model risk.

Risk acceptance should not exceed established risk appetite without appropriate escalation.

---

# 7. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance defines acceptable variation around established risk objectives.

For example, an organization may have:

* Zero tolerance for certain regulatory violations.
* Very low tolerance for unauthorized disclosure of highly sensitive information.
* Moderate tolerance for limited operational disruption.
* Defined tolerance for non-critical model performance degradation.

Risk acceptance authority should consider both risk appetite and risk tolerance.

---

# 8. Authority Must Match Risk

A fundamental governance principle is:

> **The greater the potential impact of an AI risk, the higher the level of authority required to accept it.**

A junior manager should not be able to accept a risk capable of causing:

* Major financial loss.
* Significant data exposure.
* Regulatory breach.
* Critical service disruption.
* Material customer harm.
* Significant reputational damage.

Authority limits should reflect potential consequences.

---

# 9. Risk Acceptance Thresholds

The organization should establish measurable thresholds for risk acceptance.

Thresholds may consider:

* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Risk score.
* Data sensitivity.
* AI criticality.
* Business process criticality.
* Regulatory significance.
* Security severity.

For example:

| Risk Level | Potential Authority             |
| ---------- | ------------------------------- |
| Low        | Operational Owner               |
| Moderate   | Business / Risk Manager         |
| High       | Senior Management               |
| Critical   | Executive / Committee Authority |

These are illustrative and should be adapted to the enterprise risk framework.

---

# 10. Risk Acceptance Hierarchy

An organization may establish a formal hierarchy:

```text
Operational Risk Owner
        ↓
Business Management
        ↓
AI Governance / Risk Committee
        ↓
Executive Management
        ↓
Board-Level Oversight
```

Not every risk needs to reach the highest level.

Escalation should be proportional to the potential impact and organizational authority.

---

# 11. AI System Owner and Risk Acceptance

The AI System Owner may be responsible for identifying and managing system-level risks.

However, the AI System Owner should only accept residual risk when organizational policy explicitly grants that authority.

This prevents the same person from effectively declaring:

> "My system has a risk, and I have decided that the risk is acceptable."

Risk acceptance should follow formally defined authority limits.

---

# 12. Business Owner and Risk Acceptance

The Business Owner may have authority to accept certain business risks.

However, business ownership alone should not automatically confer authority to accept:

* Mandatory security requirements.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Legal restrictions.
* Critical cybersecurity risks.
* Privacy violations.

The Business Owner should operate within the organization's established risk acceptance framework.


# AI Risk Acceptance Authority

## Risk Acceptance Roles, Authority Levels, and Decision Criteria

AI Risk Acceptance Authority requires more than identifying an individual who can sign a risk acceptance record. The organization must establish a structured authority model that connects risk severity, business impact, regulatory obligations, AI criticality, and decision-making authority.

Risk acceptance should therefore operate as a controlled governance process in which the organization can demonstrate **who made the decision, what risk was accepted, why it was accepted, what controls were considered, how long the acceptance remains valid, and when the decision must be reconsidered**.

---

# 13. AI Security Owner and Risk Acceptance

The AI Security Owner should provide security expertise and identify security-related residual risk.

The AI Security Owner may:

* Assess security exposure.
* Recommend remediation.
* Identify compensating controls.
* Evaluate residual security risk.
* Recommend escalation.
* Challenge inappropriate risk acceptance.

However, the AI Security Owner should not automatically be the person who accepts the risk.

This separation helps prevent conflicts between **risk assessment and risk acceptance**.

---

# 14. Risk Owner

The Risk Owner is accountable for managing a specific risk.

The Risk Owner may be responsible for:

* Understanding the risk.
* Ensuring treatment is implemented.
* Monitoring residual risk.
* Ensuring risk remains within tolerance.
* Escalating material changes.
* Recommending acceptance where appropriate.

The Risk Owner should have sufficient authority and knowledge to manage the risk effectively.

---

# 15. Risk Acceptance Authority

The Risk Acceptance Authority is the individual or governance body authorized to formally accept residual risk.

The authority should be determined by:

* Risk severity.
* Potential impact.
* AI classification.
* Business criticality.
* Regulatory significance.
* Security implications.
* Financial exposure.
* Organizational risk appetite.

The authority should be explicitly documented.

---

# 16. AI Risk Committee

An AI Risk Committee may be responsible for reviewing risks that exceed operational management authority.

The committee may evaluate:

* High-risk AI systems.
* Material residual risk.
* Cross-functional risks.
* Security exceptions.
* Regulatory concerns.
* Significant third-party risks.
* Risks affecting multiple business units.

The committee should operate within formally delegated authority.

---

# 17. Executive Risk Acceptance

Some AI risks may require executive-level acceptance.

Examples may include:

* Critical AI systems.
* Significant regulatory exposure.
* Material financial risk.
* Major cybersecurity exposure.
* Significant customer impact.
* Enterprise-wide AI dependencies.

Executive acceptance should be supported by sufficient evidence and documented rationale.

---

# 18. Board-Level Risk Acceptance

The Board should generally focus on risks that exceed management's authority or materially affect enterprise risk.

Board involvement may be appropriate when:

* Risk exceeds established enterprise risk appetite.
* The potential impact is exceptionally high.
* Regulatory obligations require Board oversight.
* The risk could materially affect the organization's viability.
* The organization's governance framework explicitly assigns Board authority.

The Board should not normally be used as an operational risk approval mechanism.

---

# 19. Delegated Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance authority may be delegated.

A delegation framework should specify:

* Delegated authority.
* Maximum risk level.
* Scope.
* Conditions.
* Duration.
* Escalation requirements.
* Documentation requirements.

Delegation should never create authority beyond the delegating body's own authority.

---

# 20. Authority Limits

Organizations should define explicit authority limits.

For example:

| Authority Level      | Illustrative Scope                    |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| AI System Owner      | Low-risk operational issues           |
| Business Management  | Moderate business risks               |
| Risk Management      | Defined enterprise risks              |
| AI Risk Committee    | High and cross-functional risks       |
| Executive Management | Material enterprise risks             |
| Board                | Risks requiring Board-level oversight |

The actual thresholds should be established by the organization's risk framework.

---

# 21. Risk Acceptance Decision Criteria

Before accepting AI risk, the authority should consider:

* Nature of the risk.
* Likelihood.
* Potential impact.
* Inherent risk.
* Residual risk.
* Existing controls.
* Compensating controls.
* Business necessity.
* Alternatives.
* Risk appetite.
* Risk tolerance.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Security implications.
* Customer impact.

Risk acceptance should be evidence-based.

---

# 22. Business Justification

A risk acceptance request should explain why the organization is willing to retain the risk.

Valid business considerations may include:

* Critical business requirement.
* Lack of viable alternative.
* Time-sensitive business need.
* Temporary technical limitation.
* Cost disproportionate to risk reduction.
* Required transition period.

Business justification should not be used to automatically override mandatory legal or regulatory requirements.

---

# 23. Alternatives Considered

Risk acceptance should document whether alternatives were considered.

Alternatives may include:

* Implementing additional controls.
* Reducing system functionality.
* Restricting users.
* Reducing data access.
* Reducing AI autonomy.
* Changing the model.
* Changing the provider.
* Delaying deployment.
* Discontinuing the use case.

This demonstrates that acceptance was a deliberate decision rather than a default outcome.

---

# 24. Compensating Controls

When the preferred security or governance control cannot be implemented, compensating controls may reduce residual risk.

Examples include:

* Additional monitoring.
* Restricted access.
* Manual approval.
* Network isolation.
* Reduced privileges.
* Additional testing.
* Increased logging.
* Limited deployment scope.

Compensating controls should have identified owners and measurable requirements.

---

# 25. Temporary Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance may be temporary.

Temporary acceptance may be appropriate when:

* A remediation project is underway.
* A replacement system is being implemented.
* A vendor fix is pending.
* A technical limitation is temporary.
* A compensating control is being deployed.

Temporary acceptance should include a defined expiration date.

---

# 26. Permanent Risk Acceptance

Some residual risks may be inherently difficult or impractical to eliminate.

Permanent or long-term acceptance may be permitted when:

* The risk is understood.
* The risk is within tolerance.
* Reasonable controls are implemented.
* The business justification is valid.
* Appropriate authority approves the risk.

Even permanent acceptance should remain subject to periodic review.

---

# 27. Risk Acceptance Expiration

Every risk acceptance should have a defined review or expiration mechanism where appropriate.

For example:

```text id="y4r9ku"
Risk Accepted
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Review Date
     ↓
Reassess
  ↙     ↘
Accept   Remediate
Again
```

An expired acceptance should not automatically remain valid indefinitely.

---

# 28. Risk Acceptance Conditions

Acceptance may be conditional.

Conditions may include:

* Complete remediation by a specified date.
* Implement compensating controls.
* Restrict system access.
* Maintain additional monitoring.
* Conduct periodic security testing.
* Report material changes.

Failure to satisfy conditions should trigger reassessment or escalation.

---

---

# 30. Risk Acceptance Evidence

Evidence supporting acceptance may include:

* Risk assessment.
* Threat model.
* Security assessment.
* Architecture review.
* Vulnerability assessment.
* Penetration test.
* Business impact analysis.
* Regulatory analysis.
* Control assessment.
* Compensating control documentation.

The evidence should be sufficient for the authority to make an informed decision.

---

# 31. Risk Acceptance Workflow

A standardized workflow may be:

```text id="m8v5jt"
Risk Identified
      ↓
Risk Assessed
      ↓
Controls Evaluated
      ↓
Residual Risk Determined
      ↓
Treatment Options Considered
      ↓
Business Justification
      ↓
Authority Determined
      ↓
Risk Acceptance Review
      ↓
Decision
   ↙       ↘
Accept    Reject
  ↓          ↓
Monitor    Remediate
```

The workflow should be integrated into the enterprise GRC process.

---

# 32. Risk Acceptance Decision Outcomes

A risk authority should have multiple possible decisions.

### Accept

Residual risk is accepted within authorized limits.

### Reject

The risk cannot be accepted at the proposed level.

### Remediate

Additional controls are required.

### Defer

A decision is postponed pending additional information.

### Escalate

The risk exceeds the authority's decision rights.

### Restrict

The AI system may operate only under defined limitations.

These outcomes should be explicitly recorded.

# AI Risk Acceptance Authority

## Risk Acceptance Governance, Escalation, Monitoring, and Assurance

AI risk acceptance is not a one-time administrative approval. Once residual risk has been accepted, the organization must continue to monitor the risk, verify that acceptance conditions remain valid, and reassess the decision when circumstances change.

An effective AI Risk Acceptance Authority model therefore connects **risk identification, authority, decision-making, conditions, monitoring, escalation, reassessment, and assurance** into a continuous governance process.

---

# 33. Risk Acceptance and Risk Treatment

Risk acceptance should normally occur after reasonable treatment options have been evaluated.

The sequence should generally be:

```text
Identify Risk
     ↓
Assess Risk
     ↓
Avoid / Reduce / Transfer
     ↓
Evaluate Residual Risk
     ↓
Determine Acceptance Authority
     ↓
Accept or Escalate
```

Risk acceptance should not become the default alternative to implementing reasonable controls.

---

# 34. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means changing or eliminating the AI activity so that the risk no longer exists or is materially reduced.

Examples include:

* Not deploying the AI system.
* Removing a high-risk capability.
* Discontinuing an unsafe integration.
* Avoiding processing of highly sensitive data.
* Removing autonomous actions.

Risk avoidance may be appropriate where the potential impact is unacceptable.

---

# 35. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction involves implementing controls that lower likelihood, impact, or both.

Examples include:

* Stronger authentication.
* Least-privilege access.
* Human approval.
* Network segmentation.
* Additional monitoring.
* Security testing.
* Data minimization.
* Restricted model capabilities.

The residual risk after these measures may then be considered for acceptance.

---

# 36. Risk Transfer

Risk transfer involves shifting some financial or contractual consequences to another party.

Examples include:

* Cyber insurance.
* Contractual indemnification.
* Vendor liability provisions.
* Outsourcing arrangements.

Risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying accountability.

An organization may still remain responsible for:

* Regulatory compliance.
* Customer obligations.
* Security oversight.
* Business continuity.
* Governance.

---

# 37. Risk Avoidance, Reduction, Transfer, and Acceptance

A mature AI governance model should distinguish the major risk treatment options:

| Treatment | Meaning                                        |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid     | Eliminate the activity causing the risk        |
| Reduce    | Implement controls to lower risk               |
| Transfer  | Shift defined consequences or responsibilities |
| Accept    | Retain the residual risk                       |
| Share     | Distribute risk among multiple parties         |

The selected treatment should be documented.

---

# 38. Risk Acceptance and Mandatory Requirements

Some requirements cannot simply be accepted as risk.

For example, an organization should not assume that a manager can formally "accept" a requirement that is:

* Legally mandatory.
* Regulatory.
* Contractually binding.
* Prohibited by policy.
* Required by a binding security standard.

Where a requirement is mandatory, the organization should determine the appropriate compliance response rather than treating non-compliance as an ordinary discretionary risk.

---

# 39. Risk Acceptance and Security Exceptions

A security exception may be related to risk acceptance but they are not necessarily identical.

A **security exception** generally permits deviation from an established control or requirement.

A **risk acceptance** acknowledges and accepts the resulting residual risk.

Therefore:

```text
Control Requirement
       ↓
Exception Requested
       ↓
Security / Risk Assessment
       ↓
Residual Risk
       ↓
Authorized Risk Acceptance
```

Both processes should be coordinated.

---

# 40. Risk Acceptance and AI Security Exceptions

AI systems may require exceptions because of:

* Legacy architecture.
* Third-party limitations.
* Model provider constraints.
* Temporary technical limitations.
* Deployment deadlines.
* Incompatible security controls.

The exception should identify the resulting AI security risk and the authority responsible for accepting it.

---

# 41. Risk Acceptance and Risk Appetite Breach

If residual risk exceeds the organization's established risk appetite, the risk should normally be escalated.

The organization should not allow an operational manager to override enterprise risk appetite simply through a local approval.

A possible process is:

```text
Residual Risk
     ↓
Within Risk Appetite?
   ↙          ↘
 Yes           No
 ↓              ↓
Normal        Escalate
Authority     Higher Authority
```

---

# 42. Risk Acceptance and Risk Tolerance Breach

A risk may be within broad organizational appetite but outside a specific risk tolerance.

For example, an organization may tolerate limited AI service disruption but have a very low tolerance for disruption affecting a critical business process.

Tolerance thresholds should therefore be considered separately from general risk appetite.

---

# 43. Risk Acceptance and Risk Aggregation

Individual AI risks may appear acceptable when considered separately but become material when aggregated.

For example:

```text
AI System A → Moderate Risk
AI System B → Moderate Risk
AI System C → Moderate Risk
AI System D → Moderate Risk
       ↓
Enterprise AI Risk Concentration
       ↓
Potential Material Exposure
```

AI governance should therefore consider both **system-level and enterprise-level risk**.

---

# 44. Portfolio-Level Risk Acceptance

Organizations with many AI systems should monitor aggregate exposure.

Portfolio-level considerations may include:

* Number of high-risk AI systems.
* Shared model providers.
* Shared cloud platforms.
* Shared datasets.
* Common vulnerabilities.
* Concentration of AI vendors.
* Common security dependencies.

A risk that is acceptable for one AI system may become material when multiplied across the enterprise.

---

# 45. Third-Party AI Risk Acceptance

Third-party AI introduces additional acceptance considerations.

The organization should assess:

* Provider security controls.
* Data handling.
* Model provenance.
* Availability.
* Subprocessors.
* Incident notification.
* Contractual protections.
* Service dependencies.
* Exit options.

Third-party use should not automatically transfer the organization's risk acceptance responsibility to the provider.

---

# 46. Vendor Risk Acceptance

If a third-party provider cannot satisfy a required control, the organization should determine:

* Whether an exception is permitted.
* Whether compensating controls exist.
* Whether residual risk is acceptable.
* Who has authority to accept it.
* Whether contractual protections are sufficient.

Material vendor risks should be escalated according to established thresholds.

---

# 47. AI Supply Chain Risk Acceptance

AI supply chains may involve:

* Foundation model providers.
* Cloud providers.
* Open-source models.
* Data providers.
* Model repositories.
* AI development platforms.
* APIs.
* Software dependencies.

The organization should understand material dependencies before accepting associated risks.

---

# 48. Model Risk Acceptance

Model-related residual risks may involve:

* Model performance.
* Model robustness.
* Model behavior.
* Model drift.
* Model explainability.
* Model security.
* Model limitations.

The appropriate authority should understand the consequences of operating the model despite these limitations.

---

# 49. Data Risk Acceptance

AI systems may create risks associated with:

* Sensitive data.
* Data quality.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized access.
* Data provenance.
* Data retention.
* Cross-border transfers.

Data-related risk acceptance should involve the appropriate Data, Privacy, Security, and Risk functions where applicable.

---

# 50. Privacy Risk Acceptance

Privacy risk should be separately considered where AI processes personal information.

The organization should determine whether:

* The processing is permitted.
* Required privacy controls exist.
* Privacy risks are understood.
* Appropriate privacy assessments have been completed.
* Any residual risk can legitimately be accepted.

A business owner should not unilaterally accept a legal or regulatory privacy obligation.

---

# 51. Operational Risk Acceptance

AI systems can create operational risks involving:

* Availability.
* Reliability.
* Recovery.
* Performance.
* Dependency on external providers.
* Human oversight.
* Automation failures.

The Operational Risk Owner should assess whether the residual risk remains within established tolerance.

---

# 52. Business Continuity Risk Acceptance

For critical AI systems, risk acceptance should consider continuity requirements.

Questions may include:

* What happens if the model provider becomes unavailable?
* What happens if the AI system is compromised?
* Is there a manual fallback?
* Can the system be restored?
* How quickly can service be recovered?
* Can the organization operate without the AI?

Continuity limitations may create material residual risk.

---

# 53. Risk Acceptance and Human Oversight

Where human oversight is required, the acceptance authority should consider whether the proposed oversight is realistic.

Factors may include:

* Human availability.
* Response time.
* Expertise.
* Workload.
* Escalation capability.
* Ability to override AI decisions.

A theoretical human-in-the-loop process should not be treated as an effective control if humans cannot realistically intervene.

---

# 54. Risk Acceptance and AI Autonomy

Higher AI autonomy may require higher acceptance authority.

For example:

```text
Recommendation
     ↓
Human Approval
     ↓
Automated Execution
     ↓
Autonomous Multi-Step Execution
```

As autonomy increases, the potential impact of AI failure or compromise may also increase.

The risk acceptance model should reflect this.

---

# 55. Risk Acceptance and Privileged AI Actions

AI systems with privileged access should receive enhanced scrutiny.

Examples include AI systems capable of:

* Changing infrastructure.
* Creating accounts.
* Modifying security policies.
* Executing production commands.
* Accessing sensitive databases.
* Sending external communications.

The authority accepting such risk should understand the consequences of compromise or misuse.

---

# 56. Risk Acceptance and Critical Infrastructure

AI systems supporting critical infrastructure or critical business operations may require enhanced governance.

Risk acceptance may require:

* Senior management approval.
* Formal security assessment.
* Resilience assessment.
* Incident response planning.
* Independent assurance.
* Periodic executive review.

The organization should determine the applicable requirements based on criticality.

---

# 57. Risk Acceptance and Customer Impact

AI systems affecting customers may create additional risk.

Examples include AI used for:

* Customer support.
* Financial decisions.
* Fraud detection.
* Service eligibility.
* Recommendations.
* Automated communications.

Risk acceptance should consider potential customer consequences.

---

# 58. Risk Acceptance and Financial Impact

Financial impact should be included in risk assessment.

Potential impacts include:

* Direct financial loss.
* Fraud.
* Revenue loss.
* Contractual penalties.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Recovery costs.
* Litigation costs.

Financial impact may determine the appropriate acceptance authority.

---

# 59. Risk Acceptance and Reputational Impact

AI incidents can create significant reputational consequences.

Risk acceptance should consider:

* Public visibility.
* Customer trust.
* Brand impact.
* Media exposure.
* Stakeholder confidence.

A low-probability risk may still require escalation if the potential reputational impact is severe.

---

# 60. Risk Acceptance and Regulatory Impact

Regulatory impact should be explicitly assessed.

Questions include:

* Does the AI system fall under specific regulatory requirements?
* Could a security failure create a reporting obligation?
* Are there restrictions on the AI use case?
* Are records required?
* Are customers or regulators entitled to information?

Regulatory significance should influence risk acceptance authority.

---

# 61. Risk Acceptance and Legal Review

Legal review may be required when risks involve:

* Regulatory obligations.
* Contracts.
* Liability.
* Intellectual property.
* Data protection.
* Customer rights.
* Cross-border processing.

Legal advice should inform the risk decision but should not automatically replace the formal risk acceptance process.

---

# 62. Risk Acceptance and Security Review

Security should provide an independent assessment of security implications.

The security review may identify:

* Threats.
* Vulnerabilities.
* Control gaps.
* Exploitation scenarios.
* Compensating controls.
* Residual security risk.

The acceptance authority should consider this information before making a decision.
