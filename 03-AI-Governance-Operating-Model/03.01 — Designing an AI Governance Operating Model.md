# 03.01 — Designing an AI Governance Operating Model

## Introduction

An AI Governance Operating Model defines how an organization organizes, assigns, executes, oversees, and continuously improves the governance of artificial intelligence across the enterprise. It translates AI governance principles, policies, risk requirements, regulatory obligations, and strategic objectives into a practical organizational structure that determines **who makes decisions, who owns AI-related risks, who performs governance activities, how decisions are escalated, and how accountability is maintained**.

As organizations increasingly adopt AI across business functions, technology platforms, products, services, operations, and decision-making processes, governance can no longer depend solely on a central policy document or a single AI committee. Effective AI governance requires an operating model that connects executive leadership, business owners, AI system owners, data owners, technology teams, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance, risk management, internal audit, procurement, and other relevant functions.

The operating model therefore serves as the organizational mechanism through which AI governance becomes operational.

A well-designed AI Governance Operating Model establishes a structured relationship between **governance authority, accountability, decision rights, risk management, controls, oversight, and execution**. It should provide sufficient centralized oversight to maintain consistency and control while allowing business and technical teams to manage AI systems within clearly defined boundaries.

The objective is not to create bureaucracy around every AI use case. Instead, the operating model should enable the organization to distinguish between different levels of AI risk and apply governance proportionately.

## Purpose of an AI Governance Operating Model

The primary purpose of an AI Governance Operating Model is to establish an enterprise-wide mechanism for governing AI throughout its lifecycle.

It should enable the organization to:

* Establish clear accountability for AI governance.
* Define who has authority to make AI-related decisions.
* Assign ownership for AI systems, models, data, security, compliance, and risks.
* Establish governance forums and decision-making bodies.
* Define escalation and exception-management mechanisms.
* Integrate AI governance with existing enterprise governance structures.
* Coordinate business, technology, security, risk, legal, privacy, and compliance functions.
* Apply governance proportionately according to AI risk.
* Establish consistent governance processes across business units.
* Support regulatory and contractual compliance.
* Provide management and the board with appropriate AI governance visibility.
* Enable continuous monitoring and improvement of the AI governance program.

The operating model should ultimately answer a fundamental question:

> **How does the organization govern AI in practice?**

A governance framework may describe **what should be governed**, while the operating model establishes **how governance is actually performed**.

## Operating Model Versus Governance Framework

An AI Governance Framework and an AI Governance Operating Model are closely related but serve different purposes.

The **AI Governance Framework** establishes the overarching principles, requirements, policies, standards, controls, processes, and governance expectations that apply to AI.

The **AI Governance Operating Model** determines how those requirements are implemented organizationally.

For example, an AI Governance Framework may require that high-risk AI systems receive formal approval before production deployment. The Operating Model determines:

* Who identifies the system as high risk.
* Who performs the risk assessment.
* Which committee reviews the assessment.
* Who can approve deployment.
* Which functions must provide assurance.
* Who owns residual risk.
* What happens when approval conditions are not met.
* Who receives escalation when a disagreement occurs.
* How the decision is documented.
* How ongoing compliance is monitored.

The framework establishes the governance requirement; the operating model establishes the organizational mechanism that makes the requirement actionable.

## Core Design Principle

The central design principle is **clear accountability supported by appropriate authority and proportional oversight**.

An organization should avoid creating governance structures in which responsibility is assigned to one function while decision authority remains somewhere else.

For example, assigning an AI Risk Manager responsibility for managing an AI risk without giving the appropriate governance body authority to require remediation would create an accountability gap.

Similarly, assigning a business owner responsibility for an AI system without providing the owner with sufficient visibility into security, privacy, model, data, and regulatory risks would create an ownership gap.

An effective operating model therefore aligns:

**Responsibility → Authority → Decision Rights → Accountability → Oversight**

These elements should reinforce one another.

## Key Design Considerations

Designing an AI Governance Operating Model requires consideration of several organizational dimensions.

### 1. Governance Scope

The organization should first define what falls within the scope of AI governance.

The scope may include:

* Traditional machine learning systems.
* Generative AI systems.
* Large language models.
* AI-enabled applications.
* AI agents and autonomous systems.
* Predictive analytics involving machine learning.
* AI services obtained from third parties.
* Embedded AI capabilities within enterprise software.
* Internally developed AI models.
* Third-party foundation models.
* AI APIs and external AI platforms.
* AI-enabled products and customer services.

The scope should also clarify whether governance applies to experimentation, proof-of-concept activities, development environments, production systems, and retired systems.

### 2. Governance Structure

The operating model should establish the organizational bodies responsible for AI governance.

Depending on organizational size and complexity, these may include:

* Board of Directors.
* Executive Management.
* AI Governance Committee.
* AI Steering Committee.
* AI Security Council.
* AI Risk Committee.
* Data Governance Committee.
* Privacy Governance function.
* Enterprise Risk Management.
* Internal Audit.
* Business and technology governance forums.

Not every organization requires every committee. The structure should reflect the organization's size, AI risk profile, regulatory environment, operating model, and level of AI adoption.

### 3. Roles and Accountability

The operating model should explicitly define the roles responsible for AI governance activities.

Typical stakeholders may include:

* Executive AI sponsor.
* AI governance lead.
* Business owner.
* AI system owner.
* Model owner.
* Data owner.
* AI security owner.
* Privacy owner.
* Risk owner.
* Compliance owner.
* Legal counsel.
* AI development team.
* Data science team.
* Cybersecurity team.
* Internal audit.
* Procurement and third-party risk management.

Clear ownership prevents governance responsibilities from becoming distributed so broadly that no individual or function is ultimately accountable.

### 4. Decision Rights

The operating model should define who has authority to make specific AI-related decisions.

Examples include decisions concerning:

* AI system approval.
* Risk classification.
* Production deployment.
* Use of restricted AI technologies.
* Acceptance of residual risk.
* Policy exceptions.
* Third-party AI providers.
* High-risk use cases.
* Significant model changes.
* Suspension or retirement of AI systems.

Decision rights should be explicit rather than assumed.

### 5. Risk-Based Governance

AI governance should be proportionate to the level of risk.

A low-risk internal productivity tool should generally not require the same governance process as an AI system that influences employment, credit, healthcare, critical infrastructure, security operations, or other high-impact decisions.

A risk-based operating model may therefore establish governance tiers such as:

* Low risk.
* Moderate risk.
* High risk.
* Critical or prohibited use.

Each tier can have different requirements for assessment, approval, documentation, monitoring, testing, and executive oversight.

### 6. Three Lines of Accountability

AI governance can also incorporate the principles of the Three Lines Model.

The first line generally consists of business and technology teams that own and operate AI systems and associated risks.

The second line provides independent risk, security, compliance, privacy, and governance oversight.

The third line provides independent assurance, typically through internal audit.

This separation helps prevent the same team from simultaneously developing, approving, operating, and independently assuring its own AI controls.

## Centralized, Federated, and Hybrid Models

One of the most important design decisions is determining how centralized AI governance should be.

### Centralized Model

A centralized model places most AI governance authority within a central enterprise function.

Advantages may include:

* Consistent governance.
* Centralized expertise.
* Standardized processes.
* Strong executive visibility.
* Easier enterprise-wide reporting.

However, excessive centralization can create bottlenecks and may reduce business ownership.

### Federated Model

A federated model distributes governance responsibilities across business units while maintaining common enterprise principles and standards.

Advantages may include:

* Greater business ownership.
* Faster decision-making.
* Better understanding of local risks.
* Flexibility across business units.

However, federated governance can result in inconsistent practices if enterprise standards and oversight are weak.

### Hybrid Model

A hybrid model combines centralized oversight with distributed execution.

Under this model, an enterprise AI governance function may establish:

* Enterprise policies.
* Minimum control requirements.
* Risk classification methodology.
* Governance standards.
* Reporting requirements.
* Escalation mechanisms.

Business units may then retain responsibility for:

* AI use-case identification.
* Business ownership.
* Operational implementation.
* Local risk management.
* Day-to-day AI system management.

For many large enterprises, a hybrid model provides an effective balance between consistency and operational flexibility.

## Lifecycle Orientation

The operating model should govern AI across its complete lifecycle rather than focusing only on deployment approval.

Relevant lifecycle stages may include:

1. AI idea or use-case identification.
2. Initial screening.
3. Risk classification.
4. Business justification.
5. Data assessment.
6. Design and development.
7. Security and privacy assessment.
8. Model validation and testing.
9. Governance approval.
10. Production deployment.
11. Continuous monitoring.
12. Change management.
13. Incident and issue management.
14. Periodic reassessment.
15. Decommissioning or retirement.

This lifecycle orientation ensures that governance remains active after an AI system has been approved and deployed.

## Governance as an Enterprise Capability

AI governance should ultimately be treated as an enterprise capability rather than a one-time compliance project.

A mature operating model enables the organization to repeatedly and consistently answer:

* What AI systems do we have?
* Who owns them?
* What risks do they create?
* Who is accountable for those risks?
* What controls are required?
* Who can approve their use?
* Who can accept residual risk?
* How are exceptions handled?
* How are incidents escalated?
* How is management informed?
* How does the board receive appropriate oversight?
* How do we know whether AI governance is effective?

The answers should not depend on informal relationships or individual knowledge. They should be embedded into the organization's governance structure, documented processes, decision rights, and accountability mechanisms.

A properly designed AI Governance Operating Model therefore transforms AI governance from a collection of policies and committees into a **repeatable enterprise management capability** capable of supporting responsible AI adoption, security, risk management, regulatory compliance, and business objectives.

# Designing an AI Governance Operating Model — Part 2

## Operating Model Design Components

Designing an effective AI Governance Operating Model requires more than establishing committees or assigning governance responsibilities. The model should define the complete organizational mechanism through which AI-related decisions, risks, controls, oversight, and accountability operate.

A practical operating model can be designed around several interconnected components:

1. Governance authority
2. Organizational structure
3. Roles and responsibilities
4. Decision rights
5. Risk and control mechanisms
6. Governance processes
7. Information and reporting
8. Escalation mechanisms
9. Assurance
10. Continuous improvement

These components should operate as an integrated system rather than as independent governance activities.

## 1. Governance Authority

Governance authority establishes where enterprise authority for AI governance resides.

The organization should determine the highest level of management accountable for AI governance and how that authority is delegated throughout the organization.

Governance authority may originate from:

* The Board of Directors.
* Chief Executive Officer.
* Executive Committee.
* Chief Information Officer.
* Chief Technology Officer.
* Chief Information Security Officer.
* Chief Risk Officer.
* Chief Data Officer.
* Chief Compliance Officer.
* A designated AI executive sponsor.
* A formally established AI governance body.

The specific organizational owner will vary by enterprise. The important requirement is that authority is explicitly established.

The governance structure should make it clear who has authority to:

* Establish enterprise AI governance requirements.
* Approve AI policies.
* Approve high-risk AI systems.
* Accept residual AI risk.
* Approve governance exceptions.
* Require remediation.
* Suspend AI systems.
* Escalate significant AI issues.
* Report AI risk to executive management and the board.

Without clearly established authority, AI governance committees may become advisory groups without meaningful decision-making power.

## 2. Organizational Structure

The organizational structure determines how AI governance responsibilities are distributed across the enterprise.

A mature structure commonly includes three levels:

### Strategic Level

The strategic level provides executive and board oversight.

Responsibilities may include:

* Establishing AI governance expectations.
* Aligning AI governance with corporate strategy.
* Reviewing significant AI risks.
* Approving enterprise-level risk appetite.
* Reviewing material AI incidents.
* Providing executive sponsorship.

### Tactical Level

The tactical level translates strategic direction into governance programs, standards, processes, and controls.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining the AI governance framework.
* Establishing risk assessment methodologies.
* Managing AI governance processes.
* Reviewing significant AI use cases.
* Coordinating security, privacy, compliance, and risk assessments.
* Monitoring governance performance.
* Preparing management reports.

### Operational Level

The operational level implements and maintains governance requirements within AI systems and business processes.

Responsibilities may include:

* Registering AI use cases.
* Performing assessments.
* Implementing controls.
* Maintaining documentation.
* Monitoring AI systems.
* Managing incidents.
* Performing periodic reviews.
* Maintaining evidence of compliance.

This structure creates a connection between executive oversight and day-to-day AI governance execution.

## 3. Governance Layers

AI governance can also be structured into multiple governance layers.

A typical enterprise model may contain:

**Board Oversight → Executive Governance → Enterprise AI Governance → Business Unit Governance → AI System Governance**

Each layer should have clearly defined responsibilities.

### Board Oversight

The board provides oversight of significant AI-related strategic, financial, regulatory, ethical, security, and reputational risks.

### Executive Governance

Executive management establishes organizational priorities, risk appetite, investment direction, and accountability.

### Enterprise AI Governance

The enterprise AI governance function establishes common policies, standards, processes, methodologies, and reporting.

### Business Unit Governance

Business units apply enterprise requirements to their specific AI use cases and operational environments.

### AI System Governance

Individual AI systems are managed through defined ownership, lifecycle controls, monitoring, security requirements, risk management, and performance management.

This layered structure helps prevent both excessive centralization and uncontrolled decentralization.

## 4. Roles and Responsibilities

AI governance requires explicit allocation of responsibilities.

A governance operating model should distinguish between:

* Who performs an activity.
* Who owns the outcome.
* Who approves the decision.
* Who provides advice.
* Who must be consulted.
* Who must be informed.
* Who provides independent assurance.

For example, an AI development team may be responsible for implementing technical controls, while the AI system owner remains accountable for ensuring that the system complies with organizational requirements.

Similarly, the cybersecurity function may assess AI security risks, but the designated risk owner may retain accountability for accepting residual risk.

This distinction is fundamental to effective governance.

## 5. Decision Rights

Decision rights define who is authorized to make specific decisions.

A governance operating model should establish decision rights for material AI decisions rather than relying on organizational hierarchy alone.

Examples include:

| Decision                                  | Typical Authority                                |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Register AI use case                      | AI Governance / Business Owner                   |
| Determine preliminary risk classification | AI Governance / Risk Function                    |
| Approve low-risk AI use                   | Delegated Business Authority                     |
| Approve high-risk AI use                  | AI Governance Committee / Executive Authority    |
| Accept significant residual risk          | Designated Risk Authority                        |
| Approve policy exception                  | Appropriate Governance Authority                 |
| Approve production deployment             | System Owner + Required Control Functions        |
| Suspend an unsafe AI system               | Designated Executive / Security / Risk Authority |
| Approve retirement                        | System Owner / Business Authority                |

The exact authorities should be customized to the organization's governance structure.

Decision rights should also include thresholds.

For example, the authority required to approve a low-risk internal AI tool may differ significantly from the authority required to approve an AI system that affects customers, employees, financial decisions, or regulated activities.

## 6. Governance Processes

The operating model should convert governance requirements into repeatable processes.

Core processes may include:

* AI use-case intake.
* AI inventory management.
* AI risk classification.
* AI impact assessment.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Data assessment.
* Model validation.
* Third-party AI assessment.
* AI approval.
* Exception management.
* Change management.
* AI incident management.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Periodic reassessment.
* AI retirement.

Each process should have defined:

* Inputs.
* Activities.
* Outputs.
* Owners.
* Approvers.
* Control requirements.
* Evidence requirements.
* Escalation criteria.

This allows AI governance to become operational rather than remaining conceptual.

## 7. Risk-Based Governance Tiers

A governance operating model should establish different governance requirements based on risk.

For example:

### Tier 1 — Low Risk

Typical characteristics may include:

* Internal productivity use.
* Limited business impact.
* No sensitive decision-making.
* Limited data sensitivity.
* Low external impact.

Governance may consist of registration, basic assessment, acceptable-use controls, and user awareness.

### Tier 2 — Moderate Risk

Characteristics may include:

* Business process integration.
* Moderate operational impact.
* Sensitive organizational information.
* Meaningful dependency on AI outputs.

Additional controls may include security assessment, privacy review, documented ownership, enhanced monitoring, and formal approval.

### Tier 3 — High Risk

Characteristics may include:

* Significant impact on individuals.
* Regulated processes.
* Critical business functions.
* Significant financial or operational consequences.
* Material security or privacy implications.

Governance may require formal risk assessment, independent review, model validation, security testing, executive approval, enhanced monitoring, and documented residual risk acceptance.

### Tier 4 — Critical or Restricted

This category may include use cases presenting unacceptable or exceptionally high organizational risk.

Such use cases may require:

* Executive review.
* Specialized legal or regulatory assessment.
* Board visibility.
* Additional control requirements.
* Strict limitations.
* Prohibition where the organization's risk appetite or applicable requirements do not permit the use.

The classification methodology should be formally documented and consistently applied.

## 8. Governance Forums

Governance forums provide structured mechanisms for decision-making and oversight.

Different forums should exist for different types of decisions.

For example:

* **AI Governance Committee** — enterprise governance and oversight.
* **AI Steering Committee** — strategic direction and portfolio decisions.
* **AI Security Council** — AI cybersecurity and security architecture.
* **AI Risk Committee** — AI risk assessment and risk decisions.
* **Business Unit Governance Forum** — local implementation.
* **Technical Review Board** — architecture and engineering decisions.

The organization should avoid creating multiple committees with overlapping authority.

Each forum should have a clearly defined:

* Mandate.
* Membership.
* Chair.
* Decision authority.
* Meeting frequency.
* Quorum.
* Agenda.
* Escalation mechanism.
* Recordkeeping requirement.

## 9. Integration With Existing Enterprise Governance

AI governance should not operate as an isolated governance universe.

Where appropriate, the AI Governance Operating Model should integrate with existing:

* Enterprise Risk Management.
* Information Security Governance.
* Data Governance.
* Privacy Governance.
* IT Governance.
* Architecture Governance.
* Third-Party Risk Management.
* Compliance Governance.
* Business Continuity.
* Internal Control.
* Change Management.
* Internal Audit.

This reduces duplication and allows organizations to reuse established governance capabilities.

For example, an organization may already have a third-party risk process. Rather than creating a completely separate vendor governance process for AI providers, AI-specific requirements can be incorporated into the existing third-party risk methodology.

## 10. Governance Evidence

A mature operating model should define what evidence must be produced to demonstrate that governance activities occurred.

Examples include:

* AI inventory records.
* Risk assessments.
* Impact assessments.
* Security assessments.
* Privacy assessments.
* Model validation results.
* Approval records.
* Committee decisions.
* Risk acceptance records.
* Exception approvals.
* Monitoring reports.
* Incident records.
* Periodic review records.
* Retirement documentation.

Governance evidence should be retained according to organizational records-management requirements and applicable regulatory obligations.

Evidence is particularly important for demonstrating that governance exists not only on paper but also in practice.

## 11. Escalation and Exception Management

No governance operating model can eliminate all disagreements, exceptions, or unexpected risks.

The model should therefore define escalation mechanisms.

Escalation may be triggered by:

* Significant AI risk.
* Control failure.
* Security incident.
* Privacy incident.
* Regulatory concern.
* Model degradation.
* Unacceptable AI behavior.
* Business disagreement.
* Policy exception request.
* Risk exceeding approved tolerance.
* Failure to complete required governance activities.

Escalation paths should define:

**Trigger → Initial Owner → Escalation Authority → Decision → Required Action → Closure**

This creates a controlled mechanism for resolving governance issues.

## 12. Independent Assurance

The operating model should include independent assurance mechanisms.

Assurance may be provided through:

* Internal audit.
* Independent security assessments.
* Model validation.
* Compliance reviews.
* Privacy assessments.
* External assurance providers.
* Regulatory examinations.

Independent assurance should evaluate whether governance requirements are appropriately designed and whether they operate effectively.

The purpose is not simply to determine whether documentation exists. Assurance should examine whether governance produces the intended outcomes.

## 13. Continuous Improvement

AI governance must evolve as AI technologies, organizational practices, threats, regulations, and business models change.

The operating model should therefore establish mechanisms for continuous improvement.

Inputs to improvement may include:

* AI incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Risk assessments.
* Regulatory changes.
* New AI technologies.
* Security threats.
* Business feedback.
* Control performance.
* Governance metrics.
* Industry developments.
* Lessons learned.

The organization should periodically evaluate whether its governance structure remains appropriate for its AI risk profile.

## Operating Model Design Principle

A strong AI Governance Operating Model should create a clear chain from **enterprise strategy to operational execution**.

The relationship can be represented conceptually as:

**Strategy → Governance Authority → Policies → Roles → Decision Rights → Processes → Controls → Monitoring → Reporting → Assurance → Improvement**

Each element should connect to the next.

If policies exist without assigned owners, governance becomes theoretical.

If responsibilities exist without decision rights, accountability becomes ineffective.

If controls exist without monitoring, management cannot determine whether they remain effective.

If monitoring exists without reporting, leadership may lack visibility.

If reporting exists without decision authority, governance information may not result in action.

The operating model is effective when these components operate together as a coherent governance system.

# Designing an AI Governance Operating Model — Part 3

## Implementing the AI Governance Operating Model

Designing the operating model establishes the intended governance structure, but the model becomes effective only when it is translated into operational processes, assigned responsibilities, measurable controls, and repeatable decision mechanisms.

Implementation should therefore focus on turning the operating model into a functioning enterprise capability.

The implementation approach should be structured, risk-based, and aligned with the organization's existing governance environment.

## 1. Establish Executive Sponsorship

The first implementation requirement is clear executive sponsorship.

AI governance affects multiple organizational functions and may require decisions that cross business, technology, security, risk, legal, privacy, and compliance boundaries.

Executive sponsorship provides the authority necessary to:

* Establish enterprise AI governance expectations.
* Allocate resources.
* Resolve organizational conflicts.
* Approve governance structures.
* Establish accountability.
* Enforce governance requirements.
* Escalate significant AI risks.
* Maintain alignment with enterprise strategy.

The executive sponsor should have sufficient organizational authority to ensure that AI governance is not treated as an optional activity.

## 2. Define the AI Governance Mandate

The organization should formally define the mandate of the AI governance capability.

The mandate should establish:

* Why AI governance exists.
* What AI systems and activities are in scope.
* Which organizational functions are covered.
* What governance objectives must be achieved.
* Which governance bodies have authority.
* What decisions require formal governance.
* How risk and compliance requirements are incorporated.
* How exceptions are handled.
* How governance performance is measured.

The mandate should be documented and communicated across the organization.

## 3. Establish the Governance Structure

The next step is to establish the governance bodies required to operate the model.

Depending on organizational complexity, this may include:

* Executive AI governance authority.
* AI Governance Committee.
* AI Steering Committee.
* AI Security Council.
* AI Risk Committee.
* Business-unit AI governance forums.
* Technical review forums.

The organization should avoid establishing committees simply because they appear in industry frameworks.

Each governance body should exist because it performs a necessary governance function.

The structure should clearly distinguish between:

* Strategic decision-making.
* Risk decision-making.
* Security oversight.
* Operational governance.
* Technical review.
* Independent assurance.

## 4. Define Governance Charters

Each governance body should have a formal charter.

A charter should normally define:

* Purpose.
* Scope.
* Authority.
* Responsibilities.
* Membership.
* Chair.
* Voting or decision mechanisms.
* Quorum.
* Meeting frequency.
* Required inputs.
* Required outputs.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Documentation requirements.

A committee without a defined mandate can easily become an information-sharing meeting rather than a governance body.

## 5. Define Roles and Accountability

The organization should identify all significant AI governance roles and formally assign accountability.

A practical role structure may include:

### Executive AI Sponsor

Provides executive sponsorship and ensures that AI governance aligns with organizational strategy.

### AI Governance Lead

Coordinates the enterprise AI governance program and maintains governance processes and requirements.

### Business Owner

Owns the business purpose, value, and operational outcomes associated with an AI use case.

### AI System Owner

Maintains accountability for the AI system throughout its lifecycle.

### Model Owner

Maintains accountability for model-related performance, documentation, validation, and lifecycle activities.

### Data Owner

Maintains accountability for the governance and appropriate use of data supporting the AI system.

### AI Security Owner

Ensures that AI-specific cybersecurity risks and security requirements are addressed.

### Risk Owner

Owns identified AI risks and determines whether residual risk is acceptable within delegated authority.

### Privacy Owner

Provides oversight of privacy and personal-data considerations where applicable.

### Compliance Owner

Assesses applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal compliance requirements.

### Internal Audit

Provides independent assurance over governance design and effectiveness.

The exact titles may vary, but the underlying accountabilities should remain clear.

## 6. Build the AI Inventory

An operating model cannot effectively govern AI systems that the organization does not know exist.

An enterprise AI inventory should therefore be established.

The inventory may capture:

* AI system name.
* Business purpose.
* Business owner.
* System owner.
* Model information.
* Data sources.
* AI technology or model provider.
* Deployment environment.
* Business unit.
* Risk classification.
* Regulatory applicability.
* Security classification.
* Lifecycle status.
* Approval status.
* Date of last assessment.
* Monitoring status.
* Third-party dependencies.

The inventory should become a central governance information source.

It should be maintained throughout the AI lifecycle rather than created only during initial registration.

## 7. Establish AI Intake and Registration

New AI use cases should enter the governance process through a defined intake mechanism.

The intake process should capture enough information to determine whether governance requirements apply.

Typical intake questions may address:

* What is the intended use?
* Who will use the AI system?
* What business process will it support?
* Who may be affected?
* What data will it process?
* Will personal or sensitive information be involved?
* Will the AI make or support consequential decisions?
* Is the AI system internally developed or externally provided?
* Will the system interact with customers or external parties?
* What level of autonomy will the system have?
* What business impact could result from incorrect outputs?

The answers provide the foundation for risk classification and governance routing.

## 8. Establish Risk Classification

Every AI use case should undergo an appropriate risk classification.

The classification process should consider factors such as:

* Impact on individuals.
* Business criticality.
* Data sensitivity.
* Security implications.
* Privacy implications.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Financial impact.
* Operational impact.
* Model complexity.
* Degree of autonomy.
* External exposure.
* Potential for harmful or discriminatory outcomes.
* Dependence on third-party providers.

The classification should determine the level of governance required.

Higher-risk AI should receive greater scrutiny, stronger controls, more extensive documentation, and higher approval authority.

## 9. Establish Governance Gates

Governance gates provide formal control points throughout the AI lifecycle.

Potential gates include:

### Gate 1 — Use-Case Approval

Determines whether the proposed AI use case is permissible and appropriate.

### Gate 2 — Risk and Impact Assessment

Determines the risk profile and required controls.

### Gate 3 — Design and Development Review

Evaluates architecture, data, security, privacy, model, and control requirements.

### Gate 4 — Pre-Production Approval

Determines whether the system is sufficiently controlled for production deployment.

### Gate 5 — Production Monitoring

Evaluates ongoing performance, risk, security, compliance, and control effectiveness.

### Gate 6 — Periodic Reassessment

Determines whether the system remains appropriate as circumstances change.

### Gate 7 — Retirement

Ensures that the AI system is safely decommissioned and associated data, models, credentials, integrations, and dependencies are appropriately managed.

Governance gates should be proportionate to risk.

## 10. Integrate Security Into the Operating Model

AI security should not be treated as an activity performed only after AI development is complete.

Security requirements should be incorporated throughout the lifecycle.

The operating model should address areas such as:

* Identity and access management.
* Model and API security.
* Data protection.
* Prompt and input security.
* Output validation.
* AI application security.
* Supply-chain security.
* Third-party AI services.
* Model integrity.
* Adversarial threats.
* AI-specific abuse scenarios.
* Logging and monitoring.
* Incident response.
* Secure AI development.

Security governance should be integrated with the organization's existing cybersecurity governance while addressing risks unique to AI.

## 11. Integrate Risk, Privacy, Legal, and Compliance

AI governance should establish mechanisms for cross-functional review.

Depending on the risk profile, an AI system may require assessment by:

* Enterprise risk management.
* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* Data governance.
* Procurement.
* Third-party risk management.
* Business continuity.
* Records management.

The operating model should define when these functions must participate.

This avoids two common failures:

1. Requiring every AI use case to undergo excessive review.
2. Allowing significant AI risks to bypass specialist oversight.

A risk-based routing mechanism should determine which functions must be involved.

## 12. Establish Risk Acceptance

Not every AI risk can be eliminated.

The operating model should therefore define how residual risk is evaluated and accepted.

Risk acceptance should identify:

* The risk.
* Potential impact.
* Existing controls.
* Residual exposure.
* Risk owner.
* Acceptance authority.
* Expiration or review date.
* Required compensating controls.
* Conditions attached to acceptance.

The individual or committee accepting risk should have authority appropriate to the magnitude of the risk.

A system owner should not automatically be permitted to accept a risk that exceeds the organization's defined risk appetite or delegated authority.

## 13. Establish Exception Management

Organizations will sometimes encounter situations where an AI system cannot fully comply with an established governance requirement.

The operating model should provide a controlled exception process.

An exception should include:

* Requirement being bypassed.
* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Compensating controls.
* Exception owner.
* Approval authority.
* Effective period.
* Expiration date.
* Review requirements.

Exceptions should not become permanent alternatives to governance requirements.

They should be periodically reviewed and closed when the underlying condition no longer exists.

## 14. Establish Monitoring

Governance does not end when an AI system is approved.

The organization should monitor:

* AI performance.
* Model drift.
* Data quality.
* Security events.
* Privacy issues.
* Policy violations.
* AI incidents.
* Control effectiveness.
* Regulatory changes.
* Third-party provider changes.
* User complaints.
* Unexpected system behavior.

Monitoring requirements should be proportional to the risk classification.

High-risk systems should generally receive more comprehensive monitoring than low-risk systems.

## 15. Establish Incident and Escalation Management

AI incidents should be integrated into existing enterprise incident-management processes while incorporating AI-specific scenarios.

Potential incidents include:

* Harmful AI outputs.
* Data leakage.
* Prompt injection.
* Unauthorized model access.
* Model manipulation.
* Security compromise.
* Privacy violations.
* Significant model degradation.
* Unacceptable automated decisions.
* Third-party AI service failure.
* Regulatory non-compliance.

The operating model should define:

**Detection → Triage → Containment → Investigation → Escalation → Remediation → Recovery → Lessons Learned**

Significant incidents should be escalated according to predefined thresholds.

## 16. Establish Reporting

The operating model should produce information appropriate to different management levels.

Operational reporting may include:

* AI inventory status.
* Assessment completion.
* Open governance actions.
* Control exceptions.
* AI incidents.
* Risk findings.

Management reporting may include:

* AI risk profile.
* High-risk AI systems.
* Material exceptions.
* Control effectiveness.
* Significant incidents.
* Governance performance.

Executive and board reporting may focus on:

* Strategic AI risk.
* Material regulatory exposure.
* Significant security risks.
* Major AI incidents.
* Risk appetite breaches.
* Overall governance effectiveness.

Reporting should support decisions rather than simply produce information.

## 17. Establish Governance Metrics

The organization should measure whether the operating model is functioning effectively.

Potential measures include:

* Percentage of AI systems registered.
* Percentage of AI systems risk-classified.
* Percentage completing required assessments.
* Percentage with assigned owners.
* Number of overdue governance reviews.
* Number of policy exceptions.
* Number of unresolved high-risk findings.
* AI incident frequency.
* Average remediation time.
* Percentage of high-risk systems with continuous monitoring.
* Percentage of AI systems reviewed within required intervals.

Metrics should measure both **governance activity** and **governance effectiveness**.

High activity does not necessarily mean effective governance.

## 18. Establish Documentation and Evidence Management

Governance activities should produce traceable evidence.

Documentation should allow the organization to demonstrate:

* What decision was made.
* Who made it.
* What information was considered.
* Which risks were identified.
* Which controls were required.
* What exceptions were approved.
* Who accepted residual risk.
* When the decision occurred.
* When the decision should be reviewed.

This creates an auditable decision trail.

## 19. Validate the Operating Model

Before declaring the operating model operational, the organization should test whether it works in practice.

Validation may include scenario-based exercises such as:

* Registering a new AI system.
* Classifying a high-risk use case.
* Escalating a security incident.
* Requesting a policy exception.
* Accepting residual risk.
* Suspending an AI system.
* Reporting a material AI issue to executives.
* Conducting a periodic AI review.

These exercises can identify gaps between documented governance and actual organizational behavior.

## 20. Institutionalize Continuous Improvement

Once operational, the governance model should be periodically reviewed.

The organization should evaluate:

* Whether decision rights remain appropriate.
* Whether committees remain effective.
* Whether responsibilities remain clear.
* Whether governance processes create unnecessary delays.
* Whether high-risk AI receives sufficient oversight.
* Whether business units comply with enterprise requirements.
* Whether metrics demonstrate meaningful outcomes.
* Whether new AI technologies require changes.
* Whether regulatory developments require updates.
* Whether incidents reveal weaknesses in governance.

The operating model should evolve as the organization's AI maturity increases.

## Practical Implementation Principle

A successful implementation should move progressively from **authority to structure, from structure to accountability, from accountability to process, and from process to measurable governance outcomes**.

The implementation should not attempt to create every governance capability simultaneously.

A practical sequence is:

**Executive Sponsorship → Governance Mandate → Governance Structure → Roles and Decision Rights → AI Inventory → Risk Classification → Governance Processes → Controls → Monitoring → Reporting → Assurance → Continuous Improvement**

This sequence provides a controlled path from governance design to operational capability.

The ultimate measure of success is not the number of committees, policies, assessments, or governance documents created.

The measure of success is whether the organization can consistently make **informed, accountable, risk-based, and traceable decisions about AI throughout the AI lifecycle**.
