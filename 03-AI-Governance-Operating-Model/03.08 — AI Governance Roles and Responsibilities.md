# 03.08 — AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities

## Introduction

AI governance requires clearly defined roles and responsibilities to ensure that accountability is established across the entire AI lifecycle.

AI systems typically involve multiple stakeholders, including business leaders, AI developers, data owners, cybersecurity teams, risk functions, privacy specialists, legal teams, compliance functions, technology teams, and third-party providers. Without clearly defined responsibilities, critical governance activities can be overlooked, duplicated, or assumed to be someone else's responsibility.

AI governance roles and responsibilities establish **who is accountable, who performs activities, who provides oversight, who provides expertise, and who has authority to make decisions**.

The objective is not to create unnecessary organizational complexity. It is to ensure that every material AI governance responsibility has a clearly identified owner.

---

# Purpose of AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities

The purpose of defining AI governance roles is to establish clear accountability for:

* AI strategy.
* AI governance.
* AI risk.
* AI security.
* AI systems.
* AI models.
* AI data.
* AI privacy.
* AI compliance.
* AI development.
* AI deployment.
* AI monitoring.
* AI incidents.
* AI third-party relationships.
* AI risk acceptance.
* AI governance decisions.

A mature operating model should ensure that responsibilities exist from **AI conception through retirement**.

---

# Why Clear Accountability Is Necessary

AI governance often crosses organizational boundaries.

For example, an AI system may involve:

**Business Owner**

who determines why the AI system is needed.

**AI Engineering**

which develops or integrates the system.

**Data Owner**

who governs the data used by the system.

**Cybersecurity**

which manages security risks.

**Privacy**

which assesses personal-data implications.

**Risk**

which evaluates enterprise risk.

**Legal**

which evaluates legal obligations.

**AI Governance**

which coordinates governance requirements.

No single function necessarily owns all aspects of the AI system.

Therefore, AI governance must establish **distributed accountability with clearly defined boundaries**.

---

# Core AI Governance Accountability Model

A useful model separates responsibilities into several categories:

### Strategic Accountability

Determines the organization's AI direction and risk posture.

### Governance Accountability

Defines governance requirements and oversees their implementation.

### Business Accountability

Owns the business purpose and outcomes of AI systems.

### Technical Accountability

Owns the technical design, development, operation, and maintenance.

### Risk Accountability

Identifies, evaluates, and monitors AI-related risk.

### Control Accountability

Owns the operation of specific governance and security controls.

### Assurance Accountability

Provides independent assessment of governance and control effectiveness.

This separation helps prevent conflicts and unclear ownership.

---

# 1. Board of Directors

The Board provides the highest level of organizational oversight for material AI governance and risk.

Responsibilities may include:

* Understanding material AI risks.
* Approving or overseeing AI risk appetite where applicable.
* Providing strategic oversight.
* Challenging management on significant AI risks.
* Reviewing material AI incidents.
* Understanding regulatory and reputational exposure.
* Ensuring management has appropriate AI governance structures.

The Board generally should not manage operational AI activities.

Its role is strategic oversight and accountability.

---

# 2. Executive Management

Executive management translates organizational strategy into AI direction and governance expectations.

Responsibilities may include:

* Approving AI strategy.
* Establishing organizational priorities.
* Allocating resources.
* Supporting governance structures.
* Reviewing material AI risks.
* Making significant risk decisions.
* Ensuring accountability across business functions.

Executive management should ensure that AI adoption remains aligned with organizational objectives and risk appetite.

---

# 3. AI Governance Executive

The AI Governance Executive provides leadership for the enterprise AI governance program.

Depending on organizational structure, this role may be performed by:

* Chief AI Officer.
* Chief Risk Officer.
* Chief Information Officer.
* Chief Digital Officer.
* AI Governance Executive.

Responsibilities may include:

* Establishing AI governance strategy.
* Coordinating governance functions.
* Overseeing AI governance policies.
* Reporting governance status.
* Escalating material issues.
* Coordinating governance committees.
* Supporting executive decision-making.

The role should have sufficient authority to coordinate cross-functional governance.

---

# 4. AI Governance Function

The AI Governance function provides the central coordination mechanism for AI governance.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining the AI governance framework.
* Maintaining AI governance policies.
* Coordinating governance committees.
* Managing the AI governance lifecycle.
* Maintaining governance requirements.
* Coordinating risk classification.
* Monitoring governance compliance.
* Maintaining governance metrics.
* Coordinating reporting.

The AI Governance function should not automatically become the owner of every AI risk.

Its role is primarily **governance coordination, oversight, and enablement**.

---

# 5. AI Governance Committee

The AI Governance Committee provides enterprise-level governance oversight.

Responsibilities may include:

* Reviewing AI governance strategy.
* Approving governance requirements.
* Reviewing material AI risks.
* Reviewing governance performance.
* Resolving cross-functional governance issues.
* Reviewing major AI initiatives.
* Escalating significant matters.

The committee provides collective governance rather than replacing individual accountability.

---

# 6. AI Steering Committee

The AI Steering Committee typically focuses on strategic AI investment, prioritization, and execution.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI portfolio prioritization.
* Investment decisions.
* Strategic AI initiatives.

* Resource allocation.
* Major implementation decisions.

The Steering Committee should coordinate with risk and governance bodies where strategic initiatives create material risk.

# 7. AI Security Council

Responsibilities may include:

* AI security strategy.
* AI threat management.
* AI security architecture.
* AI security standards.
* AI security assessments.
* AI security incidents.

The AI Security Council should work closely with cybersecurity leadership and broader AI risk governance.

# 8. AI Risk Committee

Responsibilities may include:

* Reviewing material AI risks.
* Challenging risk assessments.
* Monitoring risk appetite.
* Reviewing risk treatment.
* Reviewing risk acceptance.
* Monitoring emerging risks.
* Escalating material risks.

Risk ownership should remain with the appropriate business or functional owner.

# 9. Business / AI System Owner

The AI System Owner is accountable for the business use and lifecycle of a specific AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

* Defining the business purpose.
* Ensuring appropriate authorization.

* Ensuring risks are managed.
* Ensuring controls are implemented.

* Managing business impact.
* Coordinating system changes.
* Supporting retirement.

The AI System Owner should have sufficient authority and knowledge to manage the system responsibly.

The Business Owner may be different from the technical System Owner.

* AI architecture.

Internal Audit should maintain appropriate independence from management and operational ownership.

Employees and users of AI systems also have governance responsibilities.

# 27. Establish Clear Accountability Boundaries

Each role should have defined boundaries.

For example:

**Business Owner**

owns the business outcome.

**System Owner**

owns the system lifecycle.

**Model Owner**

owns model governance.

**Data Owner**

owns data governance.

**Risk Owner**

owns the risk.

**Control Owner**

owns the control.

**AI Governance**

owns governance coordination.

**Security**

owns security expertise and controls.

This separation prevents accountability gaps.

---

# 28. Establish the Three-Lines Perspective

AI governance can be aligned with the Three Lines Model.

### First Line — Management and Operations

Own and manage AI risks.

Examples:

* Business Owners.
* System Owners.
* AI Engineers.
* Product Owners.

### Second Line — Risk and Governance

Provide oversight, challenge, policies, and monitoring.

Examples:

* AI Governance.
* Enterprise Risk.
* Cybersecurity Governance.
* Compliance.
* Privacy.

### Third Line — Internal Audit

Provides independent assurance.

This model reinforces the principle that **risk ownership should remain with management rather than being transferred to the risk or governance function**.

# 29. Establish Role Documentation

Every material AI governance role should have documented:

* Role name.
* Purpose.
* Authority.
* Responsibilities.
* Decision rights.
* Required competencies.
* Escalation responsibilities.
* Reporting responsibilities.

# 30. Establish the Principle of Explicit Accountability

The most important principle of AI governance roles is:

> **Every material AI governance responsibility should have a clearly identified accountable role, even when multiple functions participate in the activity.**

Participation does not automatically equal accountability.

An AI governance operating model should therefore distinguish between:

**Who performs the work.**

**Who owns the outcome.**

**Who provides oversight.**

**Who provides expertise.**

**Who approves the decision.**

**Who provides independent assurance.**

This distinction forms the foundation for effective AI governance accountability.

# AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities — Part 2

## Role Architecture and Accountability Design

Defining AI governance roles is only the first step. An effective operating model must also establish how those roles interact, where authority resides, how responsibilities are divided, and how accountability is maintained when multiple functions participate in the same AI activity.

AI governance should therefore use a structured **role architecture** that connects organizational authority with operational responsibility.

---

## 1. Establish an AI Governance Role Architecture

An enterprise AI governance role architecture should organize responsibilities across several layers.

A practical model is:

```text
Board / Executive Oversight
          │
          ▼
AI Governance Leadership
          │
          ├── AI Governance
          ├── AI Risk
          ├── AI Security
          ├── AI Privacy
          ├── AI Compliance
          └── AI Legal
          │
          ▼
Business and Technology Ownership
          │
          ├── Business Owner
          ├── AI System Owner
          ├── AI Product Owner
          ├── Model Owner
          ├── Data Owner
          └── Platform Owner
          │
          ▼
Operational Teams
          │
          ├── AI Engineers
          ├── Developers
          ├── Architects
          ├── Operations
          └── AI Users
          │
          ▼
Independent Assurance
          │
          └── Internal Audit
```

The structure should be adapted to the organization's size and operating model.

---

# 2. Separate Accountability From Responsibility

One of the most important principles is distinguishing **accountability** from **responsibility**.

### Accountability

Means ownership of the outcome and final answerability.

### Responsibility

Means performing or coordinating the activity.

For example:

**AI System Owner**

may be accountable for ensuring a system completes required risk assessment.

**AI Risk Function**

may be responsible for performing or reviewing the risk assessment.

**Security**

may be responsible for performing the security assessment.

The System Owner remains accountable for ensuring the governance requirement is satisfied.

---

# 3. Separate Ownership From Oversight

The organization should avoid assigning operational ownership to oversight functions simply because those functions monitor compliance.

For example:

**Business Owner**

should normally own business risk.

**Risk Function**

should provide oversight and challenge.

Similarly:

**System Owner**

should own the AI system.

**AI Governance**

should oversee governance compliance.

This preserves management accountability.

---

# 4. Establish a Single Accountable Role

For every material governance activity, there should normally be one clearly accountable role.

For example:

| Activity              | Accountable Role  |
| --------------------- | ----------------- |
| Business use approval | Business Owner    |
| AI system lifecycle   | AI System Owner   |
| Model lifecycle       | Model Owner       |
| Data governance       | Data Owner        |
| AI security           | AI Security Owner |
| AI risk               | Risk Owner        |
| Governance framework  | AI Governance     |
| Independent assurance | Internal Audit    |

Multiple people can contribute, but accountability should remain clear.

---

# 5. Avoid Shared Accountability Without Governance Rules

Statements such as:

> "The AI Governance team and Business Owner are jointly accountable."

can create ambiguity.

Shared accountability may be appropriate in specific circumstances, but the operating model should still identify:

* Primary accountable role.
* Supporting roles.
* Decision authority.
* Escalation authority.

Otherwise, important decisions may be delayed or avoided.

---

# 6. Establish Role Authority

Responsibility without authority is ineffective.

Each major role should have sufficient authority to perform its responsibilities.

For example, an AI System Owner should have authority to:

* Request assessments.
* Allocate resources.
* Suspend a system where necessary.
* Initiate remediation.
* Escalate risk.
* Request changes.

Similarly, AI Security should have sufficient authority to identify and escalate unacceptable security risks.

---

# 7. Establish Role Competency Requirements

AI governance roles should require appropriate knowledge and skills.

Depending on the role, competencies may include:

* AI technology.
* Cybersecurity.
* Risk management.
* Data governance.
* Privacy.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Model governance.
* Business processes.
* Internal controls.

The level of competency should be proportional to the role's decision authority and risk exposure.

---

# 8. Establish Role Independence

Certain governance activities require independence from the team being assessed.

Examples include:

* Independent model validation.
* Security assessment.
* Compliance assessment.
* Internal audit.
* High-risk AI review.

Independence does not necessarily require a completely separate organizational department.

It requires sufficient separation to provide objective challenge.

---

# 9. Establish Segregation of Duties

AI governance should identify activities where one individual should not control the entire process.

For example:

```text
Development
     ↓
Testing
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Approval
     ↓
Production Deployment
```

Where risk warrants it, different individuals or functions should perform these activities.

This reduces the possibility of:

* Conflicts of interest.
* Unauthorized deployment.
* Control bypass.
* Unchallenged decisions.

---

# 10. Establish Role Interaction

Roles should be designed as an interconnected system.

For example:

**Business Owner**

defines the business objective.

↓

**AI System Owner**

manages system lifecycle.

↓

**AI Engineering**

builds or integrates the solution.

↓

**Data Owner**

approves data usage.

↓

**Security**

assesses security.

↓

**Risk**

assesses risk.

↓

**AI Governance**

confirms governance requirements.

↓

**Approving Authority**

makes the required decision.

This provides a controlled governance pathway.

---

# 11. Establish Business Ownership

Business ownership should remain close to the business process affected by AI.

The Business Owner should understand:

* Why AI is being used.
* What business process is affected.
* What could happen if the AI fails.
* What level of business risk is acceptable.
* What business outcomes are expected.

Business ownership prevents AI governance from becoming exclusively a technical exercise.

---

# 12. Establish Technical Ownership

Technical ownership should ensure that the AI system can be securely and reliably operated.

Technical responsibilities may include:

* Architecture.
* Configuration.
* Deployment.
* Integration.
* Monitoring.
* Availability.
* Performance.
* Vulnerability management.
* Technical change management.

Technical ownership should remain aligned with business ownership.

---

# 13. Establish Model Ownership

Where AI models are material to business outcomes, model ownership should be explicitly assigned.

The Model Owner may be responsible for:

* Model documentation.
* Model development lifecycle.
* Validation.
* Performance.
* Drift monitoring.
* Model changes.
* Model retirement.

Model ownership is particularly important when several models are used within one AI system.

---

# 14. Establish Data Ownership

AI systems frequently depend on multiple categories of data.

Data ownership should define accountability for:

* Data quality.
* Data classification.
* Data access.
* Data lineage.
* Data usage.
* Data retention.
* Data protection.

Where multiple datasets are used, different Data Owners may exist.

---

# 15. Establish Security Ownership

AI security responsibilities should not be left undefined simply because the system is owned by the business.

Security ownership should address:

* Threat modeling.
* Identity.
* Access control.
* Infrastructure security.
* Application security.
* Model security.
* Data security.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.

The appropriate security authority should have the ability to challenge unsafe designs.

---

# 16. Establish Risk Ownership

Risk ownership should be assigned to the party capable of managing the underlying risk.

For example:

**Business risk**

may belong to the Business Owner.

**Cybersecurity risk**

may belong to the relevant technology or security risk owner.

**Privacy risk**

may involve the appropriate privacy and business ownership structure.

The Risk function should generally provide oversight and challenge rather than automatically accepting ownership of operational risks.

---

# 17. Establish Control Ownership

Each material governance control should have a defined owner.

For example:

**AI Access Control**

Control Owner: IAM / Security Operations.

**AI Risk Assessment**

Control Owner: AI Risk Management.

**AI System Registration**

Control Owner: AI Governance.

**AI Data Classification**

Control Owner: Data Governance.

This makes control performance measurable.

---

# 18. Establish Governance Ownership

The AI Governance function should own the governance framework itself.

Responsibilities may include:

* Governance policies.
* Governance standards.
* Governance processes.
* Governance committee coordination.
* AI inventory requirements.
* Governance metrics.
* Governance reporting.
* Governance issue tracking.

However, AI Governance should not automatically own every underlying risk.

---

# 19. Establish Committee Relationships

AI governance roles should connect clearly to governance committees.

For example:

```text
Board
  │
  ▼
Executive Management
  │
  ▼
AI Governance Committee
  │
  ├── AI Steering Committee
  ├── AI Risk Committee
  └── AI Security Council
  │
  ▼
Operational AI Governance
```

Each committee should have defined authority and membership.

Committees should not duplicate one another.

---

# 20. Establish Escalation Responsibilities

Roles should define when issues must be escalated.

Examples include:

* High-risk AI deployment.
* Significant security vulnerabilities.
* Material regulatory concerns.
* Major AI incidents.
* Unresolved control failures.
* Risk exceeding appetite.
* Expired exceptions.
* Unapproved AI systems.

Escalation should occur according to predefined thresholds.

---

# 21. Establish Decision Rights

Roles should identify which decisions each role can make.

For example:

| Decision                 | Potential Authority             |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Approve business use     | Business Owner                  |
| Approve technical design | Architecture Authority          |
| Approve security posture | Security Authority              |
| Accept business risk     | Authorized Risk Owner           |
| Approve high-risk AI     | Designated Governance Authority |
| Approve policy exception | Appropriate Risk Authority      |
| Approve retirement       | System Owner / Business Owner   |

The exact authority should be defined by organizational policy.

---

# 22. Establish Temporary or Delegated Roles

Organizations may need temporary delegation.

For example:

* Acting AI System Owner.
* Delegated risk approver.
* Temporary committee authority.

Delegations should define:

* Delegating authority.
* Delegate.
* Scope.
* Duration.
* Limitations.

Delegation should not eliminate accountability.

---

# 23. Establish Role Substitution Rules

Small organizations may combine multiple roles.

For example:

**AI System Owner + Product Owner**

may be the same individual.

However, roles that require independence should remain appropriately separated.

For example, the person responsible for developing a high-risk model should not automatically be the sole independent validator of that model.

---

# 24. Establish Role Conflicts

Potential conflicts should be explicitly identified.

Examples include:

* Developer approving own production deployment.
* Business owner accepting risk without sufficient authority.
* Security team operating controls while independently assessing those same controls.
* Governance team owning operational business risk.
* Vendor controlling its own independent assurance.

Conflict identification should be part of governance design.

---

# 25. Establish Role Escalation Paths

Every important role should know where unresolved issues go.

For example:

```text
AI Engineer
     ↓
AI System Owner
     ↓
Business Owner
     ↓
AI Governance / Risk
     ↓
AI Governance Committee
     ↓
Executive Management
     ↓
Board
```

Not every issue needs to reach the Board.

Escalation should be proportional to risk and decision authority.

---

# 26. Establish Role Documentation in the AI Inventory

The AI inventory should contain ownership information for each material AI system.

Possible fields include:

* AI System Owner.

* Data Owner.

This creates an operational link between governance roles and actual AI systems.

Roles should be mapped to lifecycle stages.

Example:

| Lifecycle Stage | Primary Roles                 |
| --------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Ideation        | Business Owner                |
| Assessment      | Business / Risk / Security    |
| Design          | Architect / Engineering       |
| Development     | AI Engineering                |

| Operation       | System / Platform Owner       |

This prevents accountability from disappearing after deployment.

---

# 28. Establish Role Accountability for Incidents

AI incidents should have clearly defined responsibilities.

Possible roles include:

* Incident Owner.
* AI System Owner.
* Security Incident Manager.
* Risk Owner.
* Privacy Officer.
* Legal.
* Communications.
* Executive Management.

The organization should define who coordinates the response and who makes escalation decisions.

---

# 29. Establish Role Accountability for Third Parties

When an AI system depends on an external provider, internal accountability should not disappear.

The organization should maintain internal ownership even when:

* The model is externally hosted.
* The AI platform is SaaS.
* The provider manages infrastructure.
* The vendor performs monitoring.

A third party may operate a service, but the organization should retain appropriate accountability for its own use of that service.

---

# 30. Establish the Accountability Chain

A mature AI governance model should be able to answer five questions for every significant AI activity:

1. **Who owns the outcome?**
2. **Who performs the activity?**
3. **Who provides specialist oversight?**
4. **Who has authority to approve or reject the decision?**
5. **Who provides independent assurance?**

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, the governance model has an accountability gap.

---

# Role Architecture Principle

AI governance roles should be designed around the principle of:

**Clear ownership + appropriate authority + defined responsibility + effective oversight + independent assurance**

The organization should avoid both extremes:

**Too little governance**

where nobody clearly owns AI decisions.

and:

**Too much governance**

where excessive committees and overlapping responsibilities prevent effective decision-making.

The objective is a governance structure in which every significant AI activity has a **clearly accountable owner, appropriately assigned responsibilities, defined decision rights, and an effective escalation path**.

# AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities — Part 3

## Role Governance Across the AI Lifecycle

AI governance roles must remain effective throughout the complete lifecycle of an AI system. Assigning an owner during initial development is insufficient if ownership becomes unclear when the system moves into production, changes models, processes new data, introduces autonomous capabilities, or is eventually retired.

A mature role model therefore establishes **continuous accountability across AI planning, development, deployment, operation, monitoring, change, incident management, and retirement**.

---

## 1. Map Roles to the AI Lifecycle

The organization should explicitly map governance roles to lifecycle activities.

A simplified lifecycle is:

```text
Ideation
   ↓
Business Assessment
   ↓
Risk Classification
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Testing & Validation
   ↓
Approval
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Monitoring
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

Each stage should have defined accountable and responsible roles.

AI governance roles should create a continuous line of accountability from **business intent to operational outcome**.

A mature operating model should ensure that:

# Core Governance Principles

The AI Governance Roles and Responsibilities model should be built around several fundamental principles:

### 1. Accountability must be explicit

Every material AI activity must have an identifiable accountable owner.

### 2. Responsibility must be assigned

The individuals performing governance activities must be clearly identified.

### 3. Authority must match accountability

People cannot be held accountable for decisions they do not have the authority to make.

### 4. Oversight must remain independent where required

High-risk activities should receive appropriate independent challenge and assurance.

### 5. Risk ownership remains with management

Governance and risk functions provide oversight and challenge but should not automatically assume operational risk ownership.

### 6. Roles must cover the entire lifecycle

Accountability must continue from AI conception through retirement.

### 7. Committees do not eliminate individual accountability

Governance committees provide collective oversight, but individual owners remain accountable for their responsibilities.

### 8. Third parties do not eliminate internal accountability

External providers may operate AI services, but the organization remains accountable for its own use of those services.

---

# Final Governance Principle

An effective AI Governance Operating Model should make it possible to answer, for every material AI system and governance activity:

> **Who owns it? Who operates it? Who manages the risk? Who owns the control? Who can approve it? Who can stop it? Who must be consulted? Who must be informed? And who independently assures it?**

When these questions have clear answers, AI governance becomes operational rather than merely conceptual.

The ultimate objective of AI governance roles and responsibilities is therefore to establish a **transparent, accountable, risk-based, and enforceable organizational structure in which AI decisions and outcomes have clearly defined ownership throughout the enterprise AI lifecycle**.
