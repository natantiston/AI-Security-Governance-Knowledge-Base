# 03.02 — AI Governance Framework Structure

## Introduction

An AI Governance Framework provides the structured foundation through which an organization establishes, communicates, implements, and maintains its governance requirements for artificial intelligence. It defines the principles, policies, standards, processes, controls, roles, decision mechanisms, and oversight activities required to govern AI consistently across the enterprise.

While an AI Governance Operating Model establishes **how governance is organized and executed**, the AI Governance Framework establishes the **structured body of governance requirements that the operating model implements**.

A well-designed framework creates a common governance language across business, technology, cybersecurity, risk, privacy, legal, compliance, data, procurement, and executive functions. It provides the structure needed to translate organizational objectives and external requirements into practical governance expectations.

The framework should also provide sufficient flexibility to accommodate different AI technologies, business models, jurisdictions, risk levels, and stages of AI maturity.

## Purpose of an AI Governance Framework

The primary purpose of an AI Governance Framework is to establish a coherent and controlled structure for managing AI throughout its lifecycle.

It should enable the organization to:

* Establish enterprise AI governance principles.
* Define the scope of AI governance.
* Translate strategic objectives into governance requirements.
* Establish AI policies and standards.
* Define governance processes and controls.
* Establish accountability and ownership.
* Integrate AI risk management with enterprise risk management.
* Address AI security and privacy requirements.
* Support regulatory and contractual compliance.
* Establish lifecycle governance.
* Define monitoring and assurance requirements.
* Provide consistent governance across business units.
* Support management and board oversight.
* Enable continuous improvement of AI governance.

The framework should answer a fundamental question:

> **What governance structure and requirements does the organization use to control and oversee AI?**

## Framework Versus Operating Model

The AI Governance Framework and AI Governance Operating Model should be clearly distinguished.

The **framework** defines the governance architecture and requirements.

The **operating model** defines how those requirements are organized, assigned, executed, monitored, and enforced.

For example, the framework may establish a requirement that every high-risk AI system must undergo a formal risk assessment before deployment.

The operating model determines:

* Who performs the assessment.
* Which methodology is used.
* Which function reviews the assessment.
* Who approves the system.
* Who can accept residual risk.
* How the decision is recorded.
* How the system is monitored after approval.
* How exceptions are escalated.

The framework and operating model should therefore function together.

A framework without an operating model may remain theoretical.

An operating model without a structured framework may lack consistent governance requirements.

## Framework Architecture

An enterprise AI Governance Framework can be organized into multiple layers.

A practical conceptual structure is:

**Governance Principles → Governance Policies → Standards → Procedures → Controls → Processes → Evidence → Monitoring → Assurance**

Each layer has a different purpose.

### Governance Principles

Principles establish fundamental expectations for responsible and controlled AI use.

Examples include:

* Accountability.
* Transparency.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Fairness.
* Human oversight.
* Risk proportionality.
* Traceability.
* Resilience.
* Regulatory compliance.

Principles provide strategic direction but generally do not contain detailed implementation instructions.

### Policies

Policies translate principles into mandatory organizational requirements.

Examples include:

* AI Governance Policy.
* AI Security Policy.
* Generative AI Policy.
* AI Acceptable Use Policy.
* AI Risk Management Policy.
* AI Data Governance Policy.
* AI Third-Party Risk Policy.

Policies establish what the organization requires.

### Standards

Standards provide more specific and measurable requirements.

Examples may include requirements for:

* Model documentation.
* Security testing.
* Data classification.
* Access control.
* Logging.
* Model validation.
* AI monitoring.
* Third-party assessments.

Standards establish a consistent minimum level of control.

### Procedures

Procedures describe how governance requirements are performed.

Examples include:

* AI risk assessment procedure.
* AI approval procedure.
* AI incident response procedure.
* AI exception procedure.
* AI model review procedure.
* AI retirement procedure.

Procedures convert requirements into repeatable operational activities.

### Controls

Controls provide mechanisms for preventing, detecting, or correcting governance failures.

Controls may include:

* Approval controls.
* Access controls.
* Monitoring controls.
* Validation controls.
* Segregation-of-duties controls.
* Change controls.
* Audit controls.

Controls should be measurable and testable where appropriate.

### Evidence

Governance activities should generate evidence demonstrating that requirements have been implemented.

Evidence may include:

* Risk assessments.
* Approval records.
* Security reviews.
* Model validation reports.
* Monitoring records.
* Exception approvals.
* Committee decisions.
* Audit findings.
* Remediation records.

Evidence supports accountability, assurance, and regulatory examination.

## Hierarchical Structure

A mature framework should maintain a clear hierarchy.

One possible structure is:

**Level 1 — Governance Framework**

Defines the overall AI governance architecture.

**Level 2 — Policies**

Establish mandatory enterprise requirements.

**Level 3 — Standards**

Define measurable minimum requirements.

**Level 4 — Procedures**

Define how requirements are implemented.

**Level 5 — Controls**

Provide specific mechanisms for managing risk.

**Level 6 — Evidence**

Demonstrates that controls and processes were performed.

This hierarchy prevents policies, standards, procedures, and controls from becoming an unstructured collection of documents.

## Relationship With Enterprise Governance

The AI Governance Framework should not exist independently from the broader enterprise governance environment.

It should integrate with existing:

* Corporate governance.
* Enterprise risk management.
* Information security governance.
* Data governance.
* Privacy governance.
* IT governance.
* Compliance management.
* Third-party risk management.
* Internal controls.
* Internal audit.

AI governance should add AI-specific requirements where necessary while leveraging established enterprise governance capabilities.

For example, an organization may already have an enterprise information-security framework. The AI Governance Framework should reference and extend that capability for AI-specific security risks rather than unnecessarily duplicating the entire cybersecurity framework.

## Risk-Based Framework Design

The framework should support differentiated governance according to AI risk.

A single governance requirement applied identically to every AI system can create unnecessary administrative burden.

Instead, the framework can establish baseline requirements applicable to all AI systems and additional requirements for higher-risk systems.

For example:

**All AI Systems**

* Registration.
* Ownership.
* Basic risk assessment.
* Acceptable-use requirements.
* Security baseline.
* Monitoring appropriate to risk.

**Moderate-Risk AI**

* Enhanced risk assessment.
* Privacy review where applicable.
* Security assessment.
* Formal approval.
* Enhanced monitoring.

**High-Risk AI**

* Formal impact assessment.
* Independent review.
* Model validation.
* Enhanced security testing.
* Executive approval.
* Formal residual-risk acceptance.
* Continuous monitoring.

**Restricted or Prohibited AI**

* Additional executive or legal review.
* Specific restrictions.
* Prohibition where required.

This approach allows governance to remain both rigorous and practical.

## Lifecycle-Based Framework Structure

The framework should also map governance requirements to the AI lifecycle.

A lifecycle-oriented structure may include:

**Discover → Assess → Design → Develop → Validate → Approve → Deploy → Monitor → Change → Retire**

Each stage should have defined governance requirements.

For example, the development stage may require:

* Secure development practices.
* Data governance.
* Model documentation.
* Security testing.
* Privacy controls.

The deployment stage may require:

* Formal approval.
* Risk acceptance.
* Production security controls.
* Monitoring.
* Defined ownership.

The monitoring stage may require:

* Performance monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Risk reassessment.
* Incident management.
* Regulatory review.

This lifecycle structure ensures that governance remains active throughout the existence of an AI system.

## Framework as a Common Enterprise Language

One of the most important functions of the framework is to establish common terminology.

Different organizational functions may otherwise use different definitions for concepts such as:

* AI system.
* Model.
* AI risk.
* High-risk AI.
* AI owner.
* Model owner.
* AI incident.
* AI exception.
* AI approval.
* AI validation.

The framework should establish authoritative definitions and terminology.

This improves communication between technical, business, risk, security, legal, compliance, and executive stakeholders.

## Framework Governance

The AI Governance Framework itself should be governed.

The organization should define:

* Framework owner.
* Approval authority.
* Review frequency.
* Change-management process.
* Version control.
* Exception mechanism.
* Communication requirements.
* Regulatory-change process.

The framework should be reviewed when there are significant changes in:

* AI technology.
* Organizational strategy.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Threat landscape.
* Business operations.
* Risk appetite.
* AI maturity.

This prevents the framework from becoming outdated while AI capabilities continue to evolve.

## Characteristics of an Effective Framework

An effective AI Governance Framework should be:

### Clear

Requirements should be understandable to both technical and non-technical stakeholders.

### Consistent

Common governance principles should apply across the enterprise.

### Risk-Based

Governance requirements should reflect the level of AI risk.

### Proportionate

Controls should not create unnecessary bureaucracy for low-risk activities.

### Enforceable

Requirements should have identifiable owners and mechanisms for enforcement.

### Measurable

Controls and governance outcomes should be capable of assessment.

### Auditable

The organization should be able to demonstrate that governance requirements are operating.

### Adaptable

The framework should accommodate changes in AI technology, regulation, and organizational maturity.

### Integrated

The framework should connect with existing enterprise GRC capabilities.

### Lifecycle-Oriented

Governance should cover AI from initial concept through retirement.

## Fundamental Design Principle

The AI Governance Framework should establish a **structured hierarchy of governance requirements that connects enterprise strategy with operational controls**.

The conceptual relationship can be represented as:

**Enterprise Strategy → AI Governance Principles → Policies → Standards → Procedures → Controls → Evidence → Monitoring → Assurance**

This structure provides the foundation for consistent enterprise governance.

The framework should not be treated as a static collection of documents. It should function as a living governance architecture that evolves as the organization's use of AI develops.

A mature AI Governance Framework ultimately enables the organization to govern AI systematically rather than managing each AI system as an isolated technology initiative.

# AI Governance Framework Structure — Part 2

## Framework Architecture and Structural Components

An effective AI Governance Framework should provide a logical structure through which governance requirements can be organized, communicated, implemented, and maintained.

The framework should not be designed as a single document containing every AI-related requirement. A mature framework uses a structured hierarchy in which high-level principles are progressively translated into specific and actionable requirements.

This structure allows executives to understand governance expectations at a strategic level while enabling operational teams to implement detailed requirements.

## 1. Governance Framework Layer

The top layer establishes the overall AI governance architecture.

It should define:

* Purpose.
* Scope.
* Governance objectives.
* Governing principles.
* Applicability.
* Governance authority.
* Accountability.
* Relationship with enterprise governance.
* Relationship with applicable external requirements.

The framework layer provides the foundation for the governance system.

It should answer:

> What does the organization intend to achieve through AI governance?

The framework should also establish the boundaries within which more detailed policies, standards, procedures, and controls operate.

## 2. Principle Layer

Governance principles establish the fundamental expectations that guide AI use and decision-making.

Typical principles may include:

### Accountability

Individuals and organizational functions must have clearly defined responsibility for AI systems and associated risks.

### Transparency

Relevant stakeholders should have sufficient information to understand how AI is being used and how significant decisions are made.

### Security

AI systems and associated data, models, infrastructure, and interfaces should be protected against applicable cybersecurity threats.

### Privacy

AI systems processing personal information should comply with applicable privacy requirements and organizational privacy principles.

### Human Oversight

AI systems should have appropriate human oversight based on their risk, impact, and level of autonomy.

### Fairness

AI systems should be designed and operated to identify and manage inappropriate bias and discriminatory outcomes where applicable.

### Risk Proportionality

Governance requirements should correspond to the potential impact and risk associated with the AI system.

### Traceability

Important AI decisions, approvals, changes, and governance activities should be appropriately documented.

### Resilience

AI systems should remain sufficiently reliable, secure, and recoverable for their intended business purpose.

Principles provide direction but should ultimately be translated into enforceable requirements.

## 3. Policy Layer

Policies convert governance principles into mandatory organizational requirements.

An enterprise may establish a central AI Governance Policy supported by specialized policies.

Examples include:

* AI Governance Policy.
* AI Security Policy.
* Generative AI Policy.
* AI Acceptable Use Policy.
* AI Risk Management Policy.
* AI Data Governance Policy.
* AI Privacy Policy.
* AI Model Governance Policy.
* AI Third-Party AI Policy.
* AI Incident Management Policy.

The organization does not necessarily need a separate policy for every topic.

Policies should be created where a distinct governance requirement, accountability structure, or risk area warrants formal treatment.

## 4. Standard Layer

Standards provide specific requirements that enable consistent implementation of policies.

For example, an AI Governance Policy may require that high-risk AI systems undergo security assessment.

An AI Security Standard could specify:

* Required security testing.
* Authentication requirements.
* Authorization controls.
* Logging requirements.
* Vulnerability management.
* Secrets management.
* API protection.
* Monitoring.
* Incident requirements.

Standards should generally be more specific and measurable than policies.

## 5. Procedure Layer

Procedures explain how governance requirements are performed.

Examples include:

* AI registration procedure.
* AI risk classification procedure.
* AI impact assessment procedure.
* AI security assessment procedure.
* AI approval procedure.
* AI exception procedure.
* AI incident procedure.
* AI monitoring procedure.
* AI retirement procedure.

Procedures should define:

* Trigger.
* Inputs.
* Activities.
* Responsible roles.
* Approvals.
* Outputs.
* Evidence.
* Escalation.

This makes governance repeatable.

## 6. Control Layer

Controls are the mechanisms used to manage governance risks.

Controls may be:

### Preventive

Designed to prevent undesirable activity.

Examples:

* Approval before production deployment.
* Restricted access to sensitive AI systems.
* Approved AI technology lists.
* Data-use restrictions.

### Detective

Designed to identify undesirable activity.

Examples:

* AI activity monitoring.
* Security monitoring.
* Model performance monitoring.
* Compliance reviews.

### Corrective

Designed to restore appropriate conditions after an issue occurs.

Examples:

* Remediation.
* Model rollback.
* Access removal.
* System suspension.
* Corrective configuration.

Controls should be linked to specific governance requirements and risks.

## 7. Evidence Layer

Every important governance activity should generate appropriate evidence.

Evidence can demonstrate:

* Who performed an assessment.
* What risk was identified.
* Which controls were applied.
* Who approved a decision.
* Who accepted residual risk.
* When a review occurred.
* Whether corrective actions were completed.

Examples include:

* Assessment records.
* Approval records.
* Committee minutes.
* Risk acceptance forms.
* Exception records.
* Security testing results.
* Monitoring reports.
* Audit reports.

Evidence should be retained according to applicable organizational and regulatory requirements.

## 8. Assurance Layer

The framework should establish how the organization determines whether governance is working effectively.

Assurance may include:

* Management reviews.
* Control testing.
* Compliance assessments.
* Internal audits.
* Independent model validation.
* Security assessments.
* External assessments.

Assurance should examine both:

**Design effectiveness** — whether the governance requirement is appropriately designed.

**Operating effectiveness** — whether the requirement actually operates as intended.

A documented control that is never implemented is not effective governance.

## 9. Reporting Layer

The framework should define how governance information is communicated.

Different stakeholders require different levels of information.

### Operational Reporting

May include:

* Assessment status.
* Open actions.
* Control exceptions.
* AI inventory changes.
* Monitoring issues.

### Management Reporting

May include:

* AI risk profile.
* High-risk systems.
* Significant findings.
* Incidents.
* Exceptions.
* Remediation status.

### Executive Reporting

May include:

* Material AI risks.
* Risk appetite concerns.
* Significant regulatory exposure.
* Major incidents.
* Strategic AI governance performance.

### Board Reporting

May focus on:

* Enterprise AI risk.
* Material AI-related exposures.
* Strategic opportunities and risks.
* Significant incidents.
* Governance effectiveness.
* Regulatory developments.

The framework should prevent excessive reporting that does not support meaningful decisions.

## Framework Mapping

An effective framework should provide traceability between governance requirements.

For example:

**Principle → Policy → Standard → Procedure → Control → Evidence → Metric**

Consider an AI security example:

**Security Principle**

AI systems should be protected against applicable security threats.

↓

**AI Security Policy**

AI systems must meet defined enterprise security requirements.

↓

**AI Security Standard**

High-risk AI systems must undergo defined security testing before production deployment.

↓

**Security Assessment Procedure**

Security teams perform the required assessment and document findings.

↓

**Security Control**

Production deployment is blocked until critical security findings are resolved or formally accepted by the appropriate authority.

↓

**Evidence**

Security assessment and approval records are retained.

↓

**Metric**

Percentage of high-risk AI systems completing required security assessments before production deployment.

This traceability allows organizations to demonstrate how broad governance principles translate into operational controls.

## Framework Domains and Cross-Functional Integration

AI governance intersects with many established governance disciplines.

The framework should therefore identify relationships with:

* Enterprise risk.
* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Data governance.
* Model risk management.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* Procurement.
* Third-party risk.
* Business continuity.
* Internal audit.
* Enterprise architecture.

This creates a cross-functional governance structure rather than treating AI as an isolated technology category.

## Baseline and Supplemental Requirements

A useful framework structure distinguishes between baseline requirements and supplemental requirements.

### Baseline Requirements

These apply to most or all AI systems.

Examples include:

* Registration.
* Ownership.
* Risk classification.
* Security baseline.
* Acceptable-use requirements.
* Documentation.
* Monitoring appropriate to risk.

### Supplemental Requirements

These apply only when specific conditions exist.

Examples include:

* Enhanced privacy assessment.
* Model validation.
* High-risk impact assessment.
* Independent security testing.
* Executive approval.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Regulatory reporting.

This structure improves proportionality and reduces unnecessary governance overhead.

## Mandatory and Advisory Requirements

The framework should distinguish between requirements that are mandatory and those that provide guidance.

### Mandatory

Failure to comply requires remediation, escalation, or formal exception approval.

Examples:

* Required security controls.
* Mandatory risk assessments.
* Required approvals.
* Regulatory obligations.

### Advisory

Provides recommended practices but may allow organizational discretion.

Examples:

* Recommended documentation formats.
* Recommended testing techniques.
* Suggested implementation approaches.

The distinction should be explicit.

Otherwise, users may misunderstand whether a requirement is compulsory.

## Enterprise-Wide and Local Requirements

Large organizations may have both enterprise-wide and business-unit-specific requirements.

The framework should define the relationship between them.

Enterprise requirements should normally establish a minimum baseline.

Business units may introduce stricter requirements when justified by:

* Local regulation.
* Business risk.
* Customer requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Industry-specific requirements.
* Data sensitivity.

Local requirements should not normally weaken mandatory enterprise requirements unless a formally approved exception mechanism permits it.

## External Framework Alignment

The AI Governance Framework may be aligned with relevant external frameworks and standards.

Depending on the organization's environment, these may include:

* NIST AI Risk Management Framework.
* ISO/IEC 42001.
* ISO/IEC 23894.
* ISO/IEC 27001.
* ISO/IEC 27701.
* Applicable AI legislation.
* Sector-specific regulations.
* Internal control frameworks.

External frameworks should be used to inform governance design rather than copied without considering the organization's actual risks and operating environment.

The organization should maintain a clear mapping between external requirements and internal governance requirements where such mapping is useful.

## Governance Requirement Traceability

Traceability is particularly important in complex enterprises.

The framework should allow an organization to determine:

**External Requirement → Internal Policy → Standard → Control → Evidence → Responsible Owner**

For example, when a regulatory requirement changes, the organization should be able to identify:

* Which internal policy is affected.
* Which standard requires modification.
* Which controls must change.
* Which AI systems are affected.
* Which owners are responsible.
* Which evidence must be updated.
* Which governance bodies must approve the changes.

This makes regulatory change management significantly more effective.

## Framework Versioning

The framework should use formal version control.

Each major framework release should identify:

* Version number.
* Effective date.
* Owner.
* Approval authority.
* Summary of changes.
* Affected policies or standards.
* Required implementation actions.

Significant changes should be communicated to affected stakeholders.

Historical versions should be retained where required to demonstrate the governance requirements that applied at a particular point in time.

## Framework Exceptions

A governance framework should define how exceptions are managed.

An exception should not simply remove a requirement.

It should document:

* Requirement being excepted.
* Reason for exception.
* Risk created.
* Compensating controls.
* Responsible owner.
* Approval authority.
* Expiration date.
* Review requirements.

Exceptions should be temporary and controlled whenever practical.

## Framework Design Objective

The ultimate objective of the framework structure is to create a governance system in which every significant AI requirement can be traced from strategic intent to operational implementation.

A mature structure therefore creates a chain of accountability:

**Governance Objective → Principle → Policy → Standard → Procedure → Control → Evidence → Monitoring → Assurance**

This structure gives the organization a consistent mechanism for governing AI while allowing requirements to be adapted according to risk, business context, technology, and regulatory obligations.

The framework becomes most effective when it is understandable to executives, actionable for operational teams, measurable for management, and auditable by independent assurance functions.

# AI Governance Framework Structure — Part 3

## Implementing the AI Governance Framework

A well-designed AI Governance Framework becomes valuable only when it is translated into operational requirements that business and technology teams can consistently apply.

Implementation should establish a controlled relationship between governance requirements, organizational responsibilities, AI lifecycle activities, risk management, controls, evidence, and assurance.

The objective is to move from a framework that describes governance expectations to a framework that actively governs AI activities across the enterprise.

## 1. Establish Framework Ownership

The organization should assign a formal owner for the AI Governance Framework.

The framework owner is responsible for ensuring that the framework remains:

* Current.
* Relevant.
* Consistent.
* Aligned with enterprise strategy.
* Aligned with applicable requirements.
* Integrated with related governance frameworks.
* Communicated to relevant stakeholders.

The framework owner may be an AI Governance Lead, Chief AI Officer, Risk function, Compliance function, or another formally designated authority.

Ownership should include both responsibility for maintaining the framework and authority to coordinate changes.

## 2. Define the Framework Scope

Before implementation, the organization should clearly define what the framework covers.

Scope considerations may include:

* AI systems.
* Machine learning models.
* Generative AI.
* AI agents.
* AI-enabled applications.
* Internally developed AI.
* Third-party AI services.
* AI APIs.
* Embedded AI functionality.
* Experimental AI.
* Production AI.
* AI used by employees.
* AI used in customer-facing services.

The scope should also identify organizational boundaries.

For example, the framework may apply to:

* Corporate functions.
* Business units.
* Subsidiaries.
* Regional operations.
* Joint ventures.
* Contractors.
* Third-party service providers where contractual requirements apply.

Clear scope prevents uncertainty about when governance requirements must be applied.

## 3. Establish Applicability Rules

Not every AI capability will require the same governance treatment.

The framework should define applicability criteria.

For example, governance requirements may be triggered when an AI system:

* Processes personal data.
* Influences consequential decisions.
* Supports critical business processes.
* Interacts directly with customers.
* Performs autonomous actions.
* Uses sensitive information.
* Operates in a regulated environment.
* Creates material financial or operational impact.
* Introduces significant cybersecurity risk.

Applicability rules should be objective enough to support consistent interpretation.

## 4. Create a Governance Requirement Catalog

The organization should maintain a centralized catalog of governance requirements.

The catalog can identify:

* Requirement ID.
* Requirement description.
* Source.
* Applicability.
* Risk level.
* Responsible owner.
* Related policy.
* Related standard.
* Related control.
* Evidence requirement.
* Review frequency.

This provides a structured repository for governance requirements.

It also supports traceability between high-level governance expectations and operational controls.

## 5. Map Requirements to AI Lifecycle Stages

Each framework requirement should be associated with relevant AI lifecycle stages.

For example:

| Lifecycle Stage | Example Governance Requirements                         |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ideation        | Business justification, preliminary risk screening      |
| Assessment      | Risk classification, impact assessment                  |
| Design          | Security, privacy, data, architecture requirements      |
| Development     | Secure development, documentation, testing              |
| Validation      | Model validation, security testing, performance testing |
| Approval        | Governance approval, risk acceptance                    |
| Deployment      | Production controls, access management, monitoring      |
| Operation       | Performance, security, compliance monitoring            |
| Change          | Change assessment, regression testing, reapproval       |
| Retirement      | Decommissioning, data handling, access removal          |

This ensures that governance requirements are applied when they are most relevant.

## 6. Establish a Minimum Governance Baseline

The organization should establish a minimum governance baseline applicable to AI systems within scope.

The baseline may require:

* Identified owner.
* AI inventory registration.
* Risk classification.
* Applicable policy determination.
* Security assessment.
* Data assessment.
* Privacy assessment where applicable.
* Documentation.
* Approval before production.
* Monitoring.
* Periodic reassessment.

The baseline provides consistency across the enterprise.

Additional requirements can then be applied based on risk.

## 7. Implement Risk-Tiered Requirements

The framework should translate risk classifications into specific governance requirements.

For example:

### Low Risk

May require:

* Registration.
* Basic risk assessment.
* Ownership.
* Acceptable-use compliance.
* Baseline security controls.

### Moderate Risk

May additionally require:

* Formal risk assessment.
* Privacy review where applicable.
* Security review.
* Enhanced documentation.
* Formal approval.
* Defined monitoring.

### High Risk

May additionally require:

* Impact assessment.
* Independent validation.
* Enhanced security testing.
* Executive approval.
* Formal residual-risk acceptance.
* Continuous monitoring.
* Periodic reassessment.

The framework should document exactly which requirements apply to each risk tier.

## 8. Translate Policies Into Controls

Policies should not remain abstract statements.

Each significant policy requirement should be mapped to one or more controls.

For example:

**Policy Requirement**

High-risk AI systems must undergo security assessment before production.

**Control**

The AI deployment process prevents production release until the required security assessment has been completed and approved.

**Evidence**

* Security assessment report.
* Approval record.
* Deployment authorization.

**Metric**

Percentage of high-risk AI systems with completed security assessments before production deployment.

This structure connects policy requirements to measurable implementation.

## 9. Assign Control Ownership

Every significant AI governance control should have an identified owner.

Control ownership should specify:

* Control owner.
* Control performer.
* Control reviewer.
* Evidence owner.
* Frequency.
* Testing method.
* Escalation mechanism.

Control ownership should not automatically be assigned to the AI governance function.

Business, technology, security, risk, privacy, and compliance teams may own different controls according to their responsibilities.

## 10. Establish Evidence Requirements

For each governance requirement, the framework should identify what evidence is expected.

Evidence should be:

* Relevant.
* Sufficient.
* Traceable.
* Reliable.
* Retained appropriately.
* Accessible to authorized reviewers.

Examples include:

* Assessment forms.
* Approval records.
* Risk registers.
* Security reports.
* Model validation reports.
* Monitoring dashboards.
* Incident records.
* Committee minutes.
* Exception approvals.

Evidence requirements should be defined before implementation rather than added only when an audit occurs.

## 11. Integrate With AI Intake

The framework should become part of the AI intake process.

When a new AI use case is submitted, the intake mechanism should identify:

1. Whether the use case is within framework scope.
2. Which risk tier applies.
3. Which governance domains are relevant.
4. Which assessments are required.
5. Which approvals are necessary.
6. Which controls must be implemented.
7. What evidence must be produced.

This allows the framework to influence AI decisions from the beginning.

## 12. Establish Governance Gate Criteria

Framework requirements should be connected to governance gates.

For example:

### Development Gate

Required conditions may include:

* Approved architecture.
* Data assessment.
* Security requirements.
* Privacy requirements.

### Validation Gate

Required conditions may include:

* Testing completed.
* Model validation completed.
* Security findings addressed.
* Required documentation completed.

### Production Gate

Required conditions may include:

* Governance approval.
* Risk acceptance.
* Required controls implemented.
* Monitoring established.

### Operational Review Gate

Required conditions may include:

* Performance acceptable.
* No unresolved material findings.
* Monitoring functioning.
* Regulatory requirements satisfied.

The framework should specify which requirements must be satisfied before an AI system can proceed to the next lifecycle stage.

## 13. Establish Control Testing

Controls should periodically be tested to determine whether they operate effectively.

Testing may evaluate:

* Design effectiveness.
* Operating effectiveness.
* Evidence quality.
* Frequency.
* Exceptions.
* Control failures.

For example, an organization may test whether high-risk AI systems consistently received required approval before production deployment.

Control testing should produce findings where weaknesses are identified.

## 14. Manage Non-Compliance

The framework should define what happens when requirements are not met.

Potential responses include:

* Remediation.
* Compensating controls.
* Temporary exception.
* Risk acceptance.
* Escalation.
* Suspension.
* Restriction of AI functionality.
* Retirement.

The response should depend on the severity and nature of the non-compliance.

A minor documentation issue should not necessarily receive the same response as an AI system operating without required security controls.

## 15. Integrate Framework Compliance Into Change Management

AI systems frequently change after initial approval.

Changes may involve:

* Model versions.
* Training data.
* Prompts.
* AI providers.
* APIs.
* Architecture.
* Intended use.
* User population.
* Decision authority.
* Autonomy level.

The framework should establish when changes trigger reassessment or reapproval.

A material change should not bypass governance simply because the original system was approved.

## 16. Establish Regulatory Mapping

Where the organization operates across multiple jurisdictions, the framework should support regulatory mapping.

The organization should identify:

* Applicable jurisdictions.
* Relevant regulatory requirements.
* Affected AI systems.
* Applicable controls.
* Responsible owners.
* Compliance evidence.

This allows the organization to maintain a consistent enterprise framework while addressing jurisdiction-specific obligations.

## 17. Establish Framework Metrics

Framework performance should be measurable.

Useful metrics may include:

* Percentage of AI systems covered by the framework.
* Percentage with assigned owners.
* Percentage risk-classified.
* Percentage completing required assessments.
* Percentage meeting minimum control requirements.
* Number of overdue reviews.
* Number of framework exceptions.
* Number of control failures.
* Percentage of remediation actions completed on time.
* Percentage of AI systems with current governance documentation.

Metrics should identify both coverage and effectiveness.

## 18. Establish Framework Review Cycles

The framework should be reviewed periodically.

Reviews may occur:

* Annually.
* Following significant regulatory changes.
* Following major AI incidents.
* Following material organizational changes.
* Following significant technology changes.
* Following major audit findings.

The review should assess whether:

* Requirements remain relevant.
* Controls remain effective.
* Governance remains proportionate.
* New AI capabilities require additional controls.
* Existing controls create unnecessary duplication.
* Risk classifications remain appropriate.

## Implementation Operating Cycle

A mature implementation can be represented as a continuous cycle:

**Define → Apply → Control → Evidence → Monitor → Assess → Improve**

### Define

Establish principles, policies, standards, procedures, and controls.

### Apply

Determine which requirements apply to each AI system.

### Control

Implement and operate the required controls.

### Evidence

Capture evidence that governance activities occurred.

### Monitor

Monitor compliance, risk, performance, and control effectiveness.

### Assess

Evaluate governance effectiveness through reviews, testing, audits, and metrics.

### Improve

Update the framework based on findings, changes, and lessons learned.

This cycle prevents the framework from becoming a static documentation exercise.

## Framework Implementation Success Criteria

The implementation can be considered effective when the organization can demonstrate that:

* AI systems within scope are identifiable.
* AI owners are assigned.
* Risk classifications are consistently applied.
* Applicable governance requirements are identifiable.
* Required assessments are completed.
* Controls are implemented.
* Governance decisions are documented.
* Exceptions are controlled.
* Significant risks are escalated.
* AI systems are monitored.
* Governance performance is measured.
* Independent assurance can evaluate effectiveness.

The goal is not simply to demonstrate that a framework exists.

The goal is to demonstrate that the framework **changes how AI is governed and managed throughout the enterprise**.

# AI Governance Framework Structure — Part 4

## Advanced Framework Design Considerations

An AI Governance Framework must remain useful as AI adoption expands, technologies evolve, regulatory requirements change, and new risk scenarios emerge.

A framework that is overly rigid may become a barrier to innovation. A framework that is too general may fail to provide meaningful control. The objective is therefore to establish a framework that is **structured, risk-based, scalable, measurable, adaptable, and enforceable**.

## 1. Design for Enterprise Scalability

The framework should support organizations ranging from a small number of AI use cases to large portfolios of AI systems operating across multiple business units and jurisdictions.

Scalability can be achieved through:

* Standardized governance requirements.
* Risk-based control tiers.
* Delegated approval authority.
* Automated workflows.
* Reusable control libraries.
* Centralized AI inventories.
* Standardized assessment questionnaires.
* Pre-approved technologies.
* Automated monitoring.
* Governance tooling.

Low-risk AI should be capable of moving through streamlined governance processes, while high-risk AI should receive progressively stronger oversight.

## 2. Establish a Common Control Framework

Organizations with multiple governance domains should consider establishing a common control framework.

AI governance controls can be mapped to existing:

* Cybersecurity controls.
* Privacy controls.
* Data governance controls.
* IT controls.
* Risk controls.
* Compliance controls.
* Third-party controls.

This avoids creating separate control environments for every governance discipline.

For example, an AI system processing personal information may already be subject to enterprise access-control, encryption, logging, and privacy controls. The AI Governance Framework should identify how these existing controls satisfy AI governance requirements rather than unnecessarily duplicating them.

## 3. Use Control Inheritance

Control inheritance can significantly improve efficiency.

If an AI system operates within an environment that already implements approved enterprise controls, those controls may satisfy applicable AI governance requirements.

For example, an AI application hosted on an approved cloud platform may inherit certain:

* Identity controls.
* Network security controls.
* Logging controls.
* Encryption controls.
* Vulnerability-management controls.

However, inherited controls should be formally assessed for applicability and effectiveness.

Control inheritance should not become an assumption that all underlying controls automatically address AI-specific risks.

## 4. Establish AI-Specific Control Requirements

While existing enterprise controls should be reused where appropriate, AI introduces risks that may require specialized controls.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection protection.
* Model integrity protection.
* AI output validation.
* Model drift monitoring.
* Training-data governance.
* AI red teaming.
* Model version control.
* AI-specific abuse monitoring.
* Agent action restrictions.
* Human oversight.
* AI-generated content controls.

The framework should clearly identify where existing enterprise controls are sufficient and where AI-specific controls are required.

## 5. Establish a Control Taxonomy

A control taxonomy helps organize AI governance requirements.

Controls can be categorized by domains such as:

* Governance.
* Risk.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Data.
* Model management.
* Third-party risk.
* Compliance.
* Human oversight.
* Monitoring.
* Incident management.
* Business continuity.

Controls can also be categorized by lifecycle stage:

* Pre-development.
* Development.
* Validation.
* Deployment.
* Operation.
* Change.
* Retirement.

A structured taxonomy makes control mapping, testing, reporting, and assurance easier.

## 6. Design for Regulatory and Jurisdictional Variation

Multinational organizations may operate under different legal and regulatory requirements.

The framework should therefore distinguish between:

**Global Baseline Requirements**

Minimum requirements that apply throughout the enterprise.

**Jurisdiction-Specific Requirements**

Additional requirements applicable to particular countries or regions.

**Business-Specific Requirements**

Additional requirements created by the risk profile or regulatory environment of a specific business unit.

The enterprise baseline should normally establish the minimum level of governance, while local requirements may impose additional controls where necessary.

## 7. Maintain Regulatory Traceability

The framework should allow regulatory obligations to be traced into internal governance requirements.

A useful structure is:

**Regulation → Obligation → Policy Requirement → Control → Evidence → Responsible Owner**

This allows the organization to determine what changes when a regulatory requirement changes.

It also helps demonstrate compliance during regulatory examinations and audits.

## 8. Establish a Governance Exception Hierarchy

Not all exceptions should be approved at the same organizational level.

The framework should establish thresholds for exception authority.

For example:

* Low-impact exception → delegated management authority.
* Moderate-risk exception → AI governance or risk authority.
* High-risk exception → executive authority.
* Material regulatory or risk appetite exception → appropriate senior governance authority.

The authority should correspond to the risk created by the exception.

This prevents inappropriate delegation of significant governance decisions.

## 9. Prevent Framework Fragmentation

Large organizations may gradually create multiple AI-related policies, standards, procedures, and control requirements.

Without centralized framework management, this can result in:

* Conflicting requirements.
* Duplicate controls.
* Different definitions.
* Inconsistent risk classifications.
* Multiple approval processes.
* Confusing ownership.

Framework governance should therefore include mechanisms for identifying and resolving overlapping requirements.

A central governance repository can help maintain consistency.

## 10. Establish a Single Source of Truth

The organization should maintain an authoritative location for AI governance requirements.

The repository may contain:

* AI Governance Framework.
* Policies.
* Standards.
* Procedures.
* Control library.
* Definitions.
* Templates.
* Assessment methodologies.
* Governance forms.
* Guidance.
* Version history.

A single source of truth reduces the likelihood that employees rely on obsolete governance documents.

## 11. Separate Requirements From Guidance

The framework should clearly distinguish between mandatory requirements and recommended practices.

For example:

**Requirement**

High-risk AI systems must undergo documented security assessment before production deployment.

**Guidance**

Organizations should consider threat modeling techniques appropriate to the architecture and AI technology.

This distinction improves usability and prevents guidance from being incorrectly interpreted as a mandatory control.

## 12. Establish Governance Metadata

Each framework requirement should ideally contain structured metadata.

Useful metadata may include:

* Requirement identifier.
* Requirement title.
* Requirement description.
* Owner.
* Applicability.
* Risk tier.
* Lifecycle stage.
* Control type.
* Evidence requirement.
* Testing frequency.
* Related regulations.
* Related policies.
* Related standards.
* Effective date.
* Review date.

Metadata enables automation and improves governance reporting.

## 13. Automate Where Practical

Technology can reduce the administrative burden associated with framework implementation.

Potential automation includes:

* AI inventory registration.
* Risk classification.
* Assessment workflows.
* Approval routing.
* Evidence collection.
* Control monitoring.
* Exception management.
* Regulatory mapping.
* Reporting.
* Review reminders.

Automation should support governance rather than replace human judgment for significant decisions.

High-impact decisions should retain appropriate human oversight.

## 14. Integrate Framework Requirements With Technology Architecture

The framework should be reflected in the organization's technology architecture.

For example, approved architecture patterns can embed:

* Identity controls.
* Data protection.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Secure API integration.
* Model access controls.
* Network segmentation.

This shifts governance from a purely documentation-driven model toward governance embedded within technical implementation.

## 15. Establish AI Governance by Design

Governance should be incorporated as early as possible in AI initiatives.

Instead of waiting until deployment to determine whether governance requirements have been met, the framework should influence AI projects from the beginning.

This may include:

* Early risk screening.
* Security requirements during design.
* Privacy considerations during architecture.
* Data governance during development.
* Model governance during validation.
* Approval requirements before deployment.

This approach is sometimes described as **governance by design**.

## 16. Establish Evidence by Design

Evidence requirements should also be considered during process design.

For every significant governance requirement, the organization should determine:

* What evidence is required.
* Who produces it.
* Where it is stored.
* How long it is retained.
* Who can access it.
* How it will be reviewed.

Evidence should be generated as part of normal operations rather than reconstructed after an audit or incident.

## 17. Design for Auditability

A mature framework should support auditability from the beginning.

Auditors and assurance functions should be able to trace:

**AI System → Owner → Risk Classification → Applicable Requirements → Controls → Evidence → Exceptions → Monitoring → Governance Decisions**

This traceability provides confidence that governance is systematic and repeatable.

## 18. Evaluate Framework Effectiveness

Framework effectiveness should be evaluated through both quantitative and qualitative measures.

### Quantitative Measures

Examples include:

* Framework coverage.
* Assessment completion rates.
* Control compliance rates.
* Exception volumes.
* Remediation times.
* Number of material findings.
* Number of AI incidents.

### Qualitative Measures

Examples include:

* Stakeholder feedback.
* Governance usability.
* Decision-making quality.
* Clarity of responsibilities.
* Effectiveness of escalation.
* Business alignment.

A framework can achieve high compliance rates while still being ineffective if it does not support appropriate AI decisions.

## 19. Avoid Common Framework Failures

Several weaknesses can undermine an AI Governance Framework.

### Framework as Documentation Only

The framework exists but does not influence operational decisions.

### Excessive Complexity

The framework becomes too difficult for business and technical teams to understand.

### One-Size-Fits-All Controls

Every AI system is subjected to the same governance requirements regardless of risk.

### Undefined Ownership

Requirements exist without clearly assigned accountability.

### Duplicate Governance

AI requirements duplicate existing cybersecurity, privacy, or risk controls without adding meaningful value.

### Static Framework

The framework is not updated as AI technologies and regulations evolve.

### Committee Dependency

The organization relies on meetings rather than embedded processes and controls.

### Poor Traceability

The organization cannot demonstrate how governance requirements connect to controls and evidence.

These failures should be considered during framework design and periodic maturity assessments.

## 20. Framework Maturity

The maturity of an AI Governance Framework can be assessed progressively.

### Level 1 — Initial

AI governance is informal and fragmented.

### Level 2 — Developing

Basic policies, terminology, and governance requirements exist.

### Level 3 — Defined

The framework is formally structured, documented, approved, and implemented across the enterprise.

### Level 4 — Managed

Governance performance is measured, controls are tested, and requirements are continuously monitored.

### Level 5 — Optimized

Governance is risk-based, integrated, automated where appropriate, continuously improved, and embedded into AI development and business processes.

Maturity should be assessed based on actual governance capability rather than the quantity of documentation.

## Framework Design Principles

A mature AI Governance Framework should follow these principles:

1. **Enterprise alignment** — AI governance supports organizational strategy.
2. **Risk proportionality** — governance intensity reflects AI risk.
3. **Clear accountability** — every significant requirement has an owner.
4. **Traceability** — requirements can be traced to controls and evidence.
5. **Lifecycle coverage** — governance applies throughout the AI lifecycle.
6. **Integration** — AI governance works with existing enterprise GRC capabilities.
7. **Scalability** — governance can support increasing AI adoption.
8. **Adaptability** — the framework evolves with technology and regulation.
9. **Auditability** — governance decisions and control activities are demonstrable.
10. **Operational usability** — requirements can realistically be implemented.
11. **Independence** — appropriate governance and assurance separation is maintained.
12. **Continuous improvement** — governance evolves based on evidence and experience.

## Final Perspective

An AI Governance Framework should function as the **architectural backbone of enterprise AI governance**.

Its value comes from connecting high-level governance principles with practical requirements, operational controls, evidence, monitoring, reporting, and assurance.

The strongest frameworks avoid two extremes.

The first is a framework that is so general that it provides little practical direction.

The second is a framework that is so detailed and rigid that it becomes an administrative burden and prevents proportional governance.

A mature framework establishes a clear hierarchy while allowing implementation to adapt according to risk, business context, technology, and regulatory requirements.

The fundamental governance chain remains:

**Principles → Policies → Standards → Procedures → Controls → Evidence → Monitoring → Assurance → Improvement**

When this chain is clearly established and operationalized, the AI Governance Framework becomes more than a collection of documents. It becomes a structured enterprise mechanism for maintaining **accountability, consistency, risk visibility, control effectiveness, regulatory alignment, and trustworthy AI adoption**.
