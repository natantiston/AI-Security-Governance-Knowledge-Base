# 03.04 — AI Steering Committee

## Introduction

An AI Steering Committee is a senior governance body responsible for providing strategic direction, prioritization, coordination, and executive oversight of an organization's AI portfolio and transformation initiatives.

While an AI Governance Committee primarily focuses on governance requirements, risk oversight, controls, and accountability, an AI Steering Committee typically focuses more heavily on **AI strategy, investment, prioritization, business alignment, organizational adoption, and strategic execution**.

The distinction is important because AI governance and AI strategy are closely related but serve different purposes.

AI governance establishes the boundaries within which AI should operate.

AI steering establishes how the organization should prioritize and direct its AI ambitions within those boundaries.

An effective AI Steering Committee helps ensure that AI initiatives are not developed as isolated technology projects. Instead, they are coordinated as an enterprise portfolio aligned with strategic objectives, business value, risk appetite, organizational capabilities, and long-term transformation goals.

## Purpose of the AI Steering Committee

The primary purpose of an AI Steering Committee is to provide strategic leadership and executive coordination for enterprise AI initiatives.

The committee may be responsible for:

* Aligning AI initiatives with business strategy.
* Establishing AI strategic priorities.
* Reviewing the enterprise AI roadmap.
* Prioritizing AI investments.
* Resolving cross-business dependencies.
* Allocating or recommending resources.
* Monitoring strategic AI programs.
* Supporting enterprise AI adoption.
* Reviewing business value and outcomes.
* Coordinating major AI transformation initiatives.
* Ensuring strategic initiatives operate within approved governance boundaries.

The committee should provide direction without taking over the operational responsibilities of AI program teams or the control responsibilities of specialized governance functions.

## AI Steering Committee Versus AI Governance Committee

The two committees may coexist, particularly in larger organizations, but their responsibilities should be clearly differentiated.

### AI Steering Committee

Primarily focuses on:

* Strategy.
* Portfolio.
* Investment.
* Prioritization.
* Business value.
* Transformation.
* Organizational adoption.
* Executive coordination.

### AI Governance Committee

Primarily focuses on:

* Governance requirements.
* Risk.
* Compliance.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Controls.
* Exceptions.
* Governance decisions.
* Assurance.

The relationship can be represented as:

**AI Strategy → AI Steering Committee → Strategic Execution**

and:

**AI Governance → AI Governance Committee → Risk and Control Oversight**

The two governance bodies should coordinate closely but should not unnecessarily duplicate one another.

## Strategic Role

The AI Steering Committee should help translate enterprise strategy into an actionable AI portfolio.

This may involve identifying:

* Strategic AI opportunities.
* Priority business capabilities.
* Transformation opportunities.
* AI investment areas.
* Critical AI dependencies.
* Required organizational capabilities.
* Workforce requirements.
* Technology priorities.

The committee should ensure that AI investments support measurable business objectives rather than simply increasing the number of AI initiatives.

## Enterprise AI Portfolio Oversight

Organizations may have numerous AI initiatives operating simultaneously.

Without centralized strategic oversight, this can result in:

* Duplicate investments.
* Conflicting technology choices.
* Fragmented architectures.
* Competing priorities.
* Inconsistent business objectives.
* Excessive vendor concentration.
* Insufficient resources.
* Unmanaged dependencies.

The AI Steering Committee should maintain visibility across the enterprise AI portfolio.

Portfolio oversight may consider:

* Number of initiatives.
* Business owners.
* Strategic objectives.
* Investment levels.
* Expected benefits.
* Delivery status.
* Dependencies.
* Risks.
* Resource requirements.
* Governance status.
* Business outcomes.

## Strategic Prioritization

Not every AI initiative should receive equal organizational attention or investment.

The committee should establish prioritization criteria.

Potential criteria include:

* Strategic alignment.
* Business value.
* Customer impact.
* Operational efficiency.
* Revenue potential.
* Risk reduction.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Competitive advantage.
* Implementation feasibility.
* Technology readiness.
* Data availability.
* Resource requirements.

A structured prioritization process helps ensure that limited organizational resources are directed toward the most valuable and strategically important AI initiatives.

## AI Investment Oversight

* Infrastructure.
* Cloud services.
* AI platforms.
* Foundation models.
* Data.
* Cybersecurity.
* Talent.
* Integration.
* Training.
* Governance.
* Monitoring.

The AI Steering Committee may review major AI investment proposals to determine whether they support strategic objectives and provide sufficient expected value.

Financial approval authority should remain consistent with the organization's broader financial governance framework.

## Business Value

The committee should ensure that AI initiatives have clearly defined business outcomes.

Potential outcomes may include:

* Revenue growth.
* Cost reduction.
* Productivity improvement.
* Customer experience improvement.
* Risk reduction.
* Operational resilience.
* Faster decision-making.
* Improved service quality.
* New products or services.

AI adoption should not be considered successful merely because a technology has been deployed.

The committee should ask whether the AI initiative actually produces the intended business outcome.

## AI Roadmap Oversight

The AI Steering Committee should oversee the enterprise AI roadmap.

The roadmap may identify:

* Strategic initiatives.
* Major milestones.
* Technology dependencies.
* Data requirements.
* Organizational capabilities.
* Investment phases.
* Target outcomes.
* Governance milestones.
* Deployment priorities.

The roadmap should be reviewed periodically as business priorities, technology capabilities, and external conditions change.

Dependencies may include:

* Data platforms.
* Cloud infrastructure.
* Cybersecurity.
* Identity management.
* Enterprise architecture.
* Legal and compliance.
* Procurement.
* Business process transformation.
* Talent.

The steering committee can help resolve dependencies that cannot be effectively addressed within an individual project.

## Organizational Alignment

The AI Steering Committee should promote alignment between:

* Technology.

* Human resources.
* Finance.

## AI Adoption and Change Management

AI transformation is not solely a technology initiative.

Successful adoption may require changes to:

* Business processes.
* Organizational roles.
* Workforce skills.
* Operating procedures.
* Performance measures.
* Management practices.
* Employee responsibilities.

The AI Steering Committee should therefore provide strategic oversight of organizational change associated with AI adoption.

This may include:

* Workforce readiness.
* Training.
* Communication.
* Stakeholder engagement.
* Adoption measurement.
* Change resistance.
* Process redesign.

## Relationship With AI Governance

The AI Steering Committee should operate within the organization's AI Governance Framework.

It should not bypass governance requirements in order to accelerate strategic initiatives.

For example, an AI initiative may be strategically important but still require:

* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* AI risk assessment.
* Regulatory review.
* Model validation.
* Appropriate approval.

Strategic importance does not eliminate governance obligations.

Instead, the steering committee should work with the AI Governance Committee and relevant control functions to ensure that strategic initiatives can proceed within acceptable risk boundaries.

## Relationship With AI Governance Committee

Where both committees exist, their relationship should be explicitly defined.

The AI Steering Committee may:

* Set strategic priorities.
* Prioritize investments.
* Review portfolio performance.
* Resolve business dependencies.
* Sponsor strategic AI initiatives.

The AI Governance Committee may:

* Establish governance requirements.
* Review AI risk.
* Oversee controls.
* Review exceptions.
* Monitor compliance.
* Escalate material governance concerns.

For significant initiatives, the two committees may interact through formal reporting and escalation mechanisms.

## Executive Sponsorship

The AI Steering Committee should have sufficient executive sponsorship to influence enterprise priorities.

Executive sponsorship provides:

* Strategic authority.
* Cross-functional coordination.
* Resource prioritization.
* Organizational visibility.
* Decision-making capability.

Without appropriate executive sponsorship, the committee may struggle to resolve cross-business conflicts or secure resources for strategic initiatives.

## Committee Chair

The chair should have sufficient organizational authority to coordinate strategic AI decisions.

Potential chair roles may include:

* Chief Executive Officer.
* Chief Operating Officer.
* Chief Information Officer.
* Chief Digital Officer.
* Chief Technology Officer.
* Chief AI Officer.

The appropriate role depends on the organization's structure and AI strategy.

The chair should not necessarily be the person with the deepest technical AI expertise.

The role requires the ability to align stakeholders, prioritize enterprise interests, and make or facilitate strategic decisions.

Membership should reflect the strategic and cross-functional nature of AI.

Potential permanent members may include representatives from:

### Transformation

### Coordination

* Resolve cross-functional issues.
* Coordinate enterprise dependencies.
* Align major initiatives.

### Escalation

* Escalate material strategic issues.
* Refer governance risks to appropriate authorities.
* Escalate decisions beyond delegated authority.

## Decision Rights

The committee should have clearly defined decision rights.

These may include authority to:

* Prioritize AI initiatives.
* Recommend investment decisions.
* Approve strategic AI programs within delegated authority.
* Resolve portfolio conflicts.
* Establish strategic priorities.
* Request changes to AI roadmaps.
* Require business cases.
* Escalate significant strategic risks.

The committee should not assume authority over areas that belong to other governance bodies.

For example, it should not independently override cybersecurity, privacy, legal, or regulatory requirements simply because an AI initiative is strategically important.

## Strategic Governance Principle

The AI Steering Committee should provide **strategic direction and enterprise coordination**, while ensuring that AI initiatives remain subject to appropriate governance and risk controls.

Its central question is:

> **Are we investing in and scaling the right AI capabilities, in the right way, at the right level of risk, to achieve the organization's strategic objectives?**

A mature AI Steering Committee therefore connects **enterprise strategy, AI investment, portfolio prioritization, organizational transformation, business value, and governance alignment** into a coordinated strategic decision-making mechanism.

# AI Steering Committee — Part 2

## Strategic Mandate and Responsibilities

The AI Steering Committee should operate under a clearly defined strategic mandate that establishes its role in directing and coordinating the organization's AI portfolio.

Its purpose is not to manage individual AI projects on a day-to-day basis. Instead, it should provide the strategic oversight necessary to ensure that AI investments, programs, capabilities, and adoption efforts collectively support enterprise objectives.

A strong mandate establishes the boundaries between **strategic direction, portfolio management, operational execution, and governance oversight**.

## 1. Define the Strategic Mandate

The AI Steering Committee's mandate should formally describe its responsibilities.

The mandate may include:

* Establishing AI strategic priorities.
* Reviewing the enterprise AI strategy.
* Overseeing the AI portfolio.
* Prioritizing major AI initiatives.
* Reviewing strategic investments.
* Monitoring business value.
* Resolving cross-functional dependencies.
* Supporting enterprise AI adoption.
* Reviewing organizational AI capabilities.
* Monitoring strategic risks.
* Escalating material matters.

The mandate should be approved by the appropriate executive authority.

## 2. Define What the Committee Decides

The committee should have clearly defined decision rights.

Potential decisions may include:

* Which AI initiatives receive strategic priority.
* Which initiatives should be accelerated.
* Which initiatives should be delayed.
* Which initiatives should be consolidated.
* Which initiatives should be discontinued.
* Which capabilities require enterprise investment.
* Which major dependencies require executive intervention.

Decision rights should be aligned with the organization's broader delegation of authority.

## 3. Define What the Committee Recommends

Some decisions may exceed the committee's authority.

In these situations, the committee should make recommendations rather than attempting to make the final decision.

Examples include:

* Major capital investments.
* Enterprise-wide transformation commitments.
* Changes to corporate strategy.
* Significant risk appetite decisions.
* Material organizational restructuring.

Recommendations should include sufficient analysis for the receiving authority to make an informed decision.

## 4. Define What Remains With Management

The committee should not become responsible for operational project management.

Routine responsibilities should generally remain with:

* AI program managers.
* Product owners.
* Business owners.
* Technology teams.
* Engineering teams.
* Data teams.

Examples include:

* Sprint planning.
* Routine technical decisions.
* Individual employee assignments.
* Routine procurement.
* Standard project execution.

The steering committee should focus on strategic matters that require executive coordination.

## 5. Establish Strategic Prioritization Criteria

The committee should use consistent criteria when prioritizing AI initiatives.

Potential criteria include:

### Strategic Alignment

Does the initiative support an established enterprise priority?

### Business Value

Does it produce measurable financial, operational, customer, or strategic value?

### Risk Reduction

Does it reduce an existing organizational risk?

### Customer Impact

Does it materially improve customer experience or outcomes?

### Feasibility

Does the organization have the required technology, data, talent, and resources?

### Time to Value

How quickly can meaningful benefits be realized?

### Scalability

Can the solution be expanded across the organization?

### Differentiation

Does the initiative create meaningful competitive advantage?

### Governance Readiness

Can the initiative satisfy applicable governance, security, privacy, legal, and regulatory requirements?

A consistent scoring model can help reduce subjective prioritization.

## 6. Establish Portfolio Categories

The AI portfolio may be divided into strategic categories.

For example:

* Enterprise transformation.
* Customer experience.
* Operational efficiency.
* Revenue generation.
* Risk reduction.
* Regulatory enablement.
* Internal productivity.
* Research and experimentation.

Portfolio categorization allows the committee to understand where organizational AI investment is concentrated.

## 7. Establish Portfolio Balancing

The committee should avoid concentrating all AI investment in a single category.

A balanced portfolio may include:

**Core AI**

Established technologies delivering measurable business value.

**Emerging AI**

New technologies with potentially significant future value.

**Experimental AI**

Controlled experimentation intended to validate uncertain opportunities.

**Strategic AI**

Initiatives with significant long-term transformation potential.

Portfolio balancing helps prevent excessive investment in either mature technologies or speculative opportunities.

## 8. Review AI Business Cases

Major AI initiatives should be supported by business cases.

A business case may include:

* Problem statement.
* Strategic objective.
* Proposed AI capability.
* Alternatives considered.
* Expected benefits.
* Costs.
* Risks.
* Dependencies.
* Required capabilities.
* Implementation timeline.
* Governance requirements.
* Success criteria.

The steering committee should challenge unrealistic assumptions.

## 9. Evaluate Benefits Realization

Approval of an AI initiative should not represent the end of strategic oversight.

The committee should periodically determine whether expected benefits are actually being achieved.

Potential measures include:

* Cost savings.
* Revenue generated.
* Productivity gains.
* Processing time reduction.
* Quality improvement.
* Customer satisfaction.
* Risk reduction.
* Adoption rates.

If expected benefits are not being achieved, the committee should consider whether to:

* Continue.
* Modify.
* Scale.
* Pause.
* Reallocate resources.
* Terminate the initiative.

## 10. Establish AI Investment Gates

Large AI initiatives can be managed through strategic investment gates.

A possible model is:

**Concept → Assessment → Business Case → Pilot → Validation → Scale → Benefits Review**

At each gate, the committee may determine whether the initiative should proceed.

This allows investment decisions to evolve as evidence becomes available.

## 11. Establish Stage-Gate Governance

Stage-gate governance reduces the risk of making large investments before sufficient evidence exists.

For example:

### Gate 1 — Strategic Fit

Does the idea align with strategy?

### Gate 2 — Feasibility

Can the organization realistically implement it?

### Gate 3 — Pilot

Does the solution demonstrate sufficient value and acceptable risk?

### Gate 4 — Scale

Is there sufficient evidence to expand deployment?

### Gate 5 — Benefits

Is the initiative producing the expected business outcomes?

This approach supports disciplined AI investment.

## 12. Manage Strategic Dependencies

Large AI programs may depend on multiple organizational capabilities.

Examples include:

* Data modernization.
* Cloud migration.
* Cybersecurity transformation.
* Identity modernization.
* Enterprise architecture.
* Procurement.
* Legal review.
* Workforce development.

The steering committee should maintain visibility of critical dependencies and intervene where they threaten strategic outcomes.

## 13. Resolve Cross-Functional Conflicts

AI initiatives may create competing priorities.

For example:

* Business wants rapid deployment.
* Security requires additional controls.
* Privacy requires additional assessment.
* Architecture requires platform standardization.
* Finance requires cost reduction.

The steering committee can provide strategic coordination while respecting the formal authority of specialized governance functions.

Conflicts should be resolved through evidence, enterprise priorities, risk considerations, and clearly defined decision rights.

## 14. Establish Resource Prioritization

Strategic AI initiatives compete for scarce resources.

These may include:

* AI engineers.
* Data scientists.
* Security specialists.
* Cloud engineers.
* Data engineers.
* Product managers.
* AI governance specialists.

The steering committee should identify resource constraints that threaten strategic priorities and recommend appropriate allocation.

## 15. Monitor AI Capability Development

AI transformation requires more than technology investment.

The organization may need to develop capabilities in:

* AI engineering.
* Data management.
* AI security.
* Model risk.
* AI governance.
* AI product management.
* AI literacy.
* Change management.

The steering committee should periodically assess whether the organization's capabilities are sufficient to execute its AI strategy.

## 16. Oversee AI Workforce Strategy

AI may change existing jobs and create new roles.

Strategic workforce considerations may include:

* AI literacy.
* Workforce training.
* New AI roles.
* Reskilling.
* Upskilling.
* Workforce capacity.
* Organizational design.
* Human-AI collaboration.

The committee should work with the appropriate human resources and business functions rather than directly managing individual workforce decisions.

## 17. Monitor Enterprise AI Adoption

AI adoption should be measured across the organization.

Potential indicators include:

* Number of active AI users.
* Adoption by business unit.
* Usage of approved AI platforms.
* Productivity improvements.
* Employee training completion.
* Business process adoption.
* Customer adoption.
* AI-generated business value.

High deployment with low adoption may indicate that the initiative is not achieving its intended outcome.

## 18. Monitor Strategic AI Risk

Although the AI Governance Committee may have primary responsibility for detailed AI risk oversight, the AI Steering Committee should understand strategic risk.

Strategic AI risks may include:

* Failure to achieve expected benefits.
* Excessive vendor dependency.
* Insufficient organizational capability.
* Rapid technology obsolescence.
* Competitive disadvantage.
* Regulatory disruption.
* Poor AI adoption.
* Excessive investment without measurable return.

These risks should be incorporated into strategic decision-making.

## 19. Establish Strategic Escalation

The steering committee should establish clear escalation paths.

Issues may require escalation when:

* Strategic objectives cannot be achieved.
* Major investments exceed authority.
* Business units cannot agree on priorities.
* Critical dependencies remain unresolved.
* Significant resource conflicts arise.
* Material risks threaten strategic outcomes.
* Governance requirements prevent planned deployment.

Escalation should identify the appropriate executive authority.

## 20. Coordinate With the AI Governance Committee

Strategic and governance decisions should remain connected.

The AI Steering Committee may identify a strategic initiative.

The AI Governance Committee may determine the governance requirements necessary to proceed.

The business owner and delivery teams then execute the initiative within those requirements.

This creates a practical relationship:

**Strategic Priority → Governance Assessment → Controlled Execution → Business Outcome**

Neither committee should operate in isolation.

## 21. Strategic Approval Does Not Equal Governance Approval

A strategically approved initiative is not automatically authorized for deployment.

An AI Steering Committee may determine:

> "This initiative is strategically important and should proceed."

The appropriate governance functions may still need to determine:

* Whether security requirements are satisfied.
* Whether privacy requirements are satisfied.
* Whether regulatory requirements are satisfied.
* Whether AI risk is acceptable.
* Whether technical controls are adequate.

This distinction is essential for maintaining independent governance.

## 22. Establish Strategic Reporting

The committee should receive concise portfolio-level reporting.

Reporting may include:

* Strategic initiative status.
* Investment status.
* Benefits realization.
* Major risks.
* Dependencies.
* Resource constraints.
* Adoption.
* Governance status.
* Key decisions required.

Reports should focus on exceptions, trends, and decisions rather than operational detail.

## 23. Use Executive Dashboards

A strategic AI dashboard may provide visibility into:

| Dimension    | Example Measures                          |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Portfolio    | Number and status of initiatives          |
| Investment   | Planned versus actual investment          |
| Value        | Benefits realized                         |
| Adoption     | Active users and business adoption        |
| Delivery     | Milestone performance                     |
| Risk         | Material strategic risks                  |
| Governance   | High-risk initiatives requiring attention |
| Resources    | Critical capability gaps                  |
| Dependencies | Major unresolved dependencies             |

Dashboards should allow executives to quickly understand whether the AI portfolio is delivering strategic value.

## 24. Review Underperforming Initiatives

The committee should establish criteria for identifying underperforming initiatives.

Indicators may include:

* Repeated missed milestones.
* Benefits below expectations.
* Increasing costs.
* Low adoption.
* Technical limitations.
* Persistent governance problems.
* Changing business priorities.

The committee should be willing to discontinue initiatives that no longer justify continued investment.

Stopping an unsuccessful initiative can be a sign of effective governance rather than failure.

## 25. Establish Strategic Portfolio Lifecycle

The AI portfolio should be continuously managed through a lifecycle:

**Identify → Assess → Prioritize → Fund → Execute → Scale → Measure → Optimize → Retire**

Each stage should have appropriate decision criteria.

This prevents AI initiatives from continuing indefinitely without evidence of strategic value.

## 26. Establish Strategic Retirement Criteria

AI systems and initiatives should not remain active indefinitely.

Retirement may be appropriate when:

* Business value is no longer sufficient.
* Technology becomes obsolete.
* A better solution becomes available.
* Risk becomes unacceptable.
* The business process changes.
* Regulatory requirements change.
* Operating costs exceed benefits.

The steering committee should ensure that strategic retirement decisions are incorporated into portfolio management.

## 27. Monitor External AI Developments

The steering committee should maintain awareness of developments that could affect AI strategy.

These may include:

* New AI technologies.
* Foundation model developments.
* Competitor adoption.
* Regulatory changes.
* Market trends.
* AI security developments.
* Vendor changes.
* Emerging business models.

The objective is not to chase every new technology but to understand developments that may materially affect enterprise strategy.

## 28. Maintain Strategic Flexibility

AI strategy should not become permanently tied to a specific:

* Model.
* Vendor.
* Cloud platform.
* Architecture.
* Technology stack.

The committee should consider portability, interoperability, and strategic flexibility when making major investments.

This can reduce technology lock-in and improve long-term resilience.

## 29. Establish Strategic Decision Records

Important strategic decisions should be documented.

Records may include:

* Decision.
* Strategic rationale.
* Options considered.
* Investment implications.
* Risks.
* Expected benefits.
* Accountable owner.
* Conditions.
* Review date.

These records provide continuity when executive leadership or committee membership changes.

## 30. Strategic Governance Principle

The AI Steering Committee should ensure that enterprise AI remains focused on **strategic value rather than technology adoption for its own sake**.

Its operating model can be summarized as:

**Strategy → Portfolio → Prioritization → Investment → Execution → Adoption → Benefits → Strategic Review**

The committee should continuously ask:

* Are we investing in the right AI opportunities?
* Are resources aligned with strategic priorities?
* Are expected benefits being realized?
* Are major dependencies being addressed?
* Are emerging risks understood?
* Are governance requirements being respected?
* Should initiatives be scaled, changed, paused, or retired?

A mature AI Steering Committee therefore acts as the strategic coordination mechanism that connects **enterprise objectives, AI investment, organizational capability, portfolio execution, and measurable business value** while operating within the organization's established AI governance and risk boundaries.

# AI Steering Committee — Part 3

## Committee Operating Model

An AI Steering Committee requires a disciplined operating model to convert strategic priorities into coordinated enterprise action. Strategic direction alone is insufficient if initiatives are not prioritized, funded, monitored, measured, and adjusted based on changing conditions.

The operating model should establish how the committee receives information, evaluates initiatives, makes decisions, monitors execution, manages dependencies, and communicates strategic direction.

## 1. Establish a Formal Steering Committee Charter

The committee should operate under a formally approved charter.

The charter should define:

* Purpose.
* Strategic mandate.
* Scope.
* Authority.
* Membership.
* Chair.
* Secretariat.
* Quorum.
* Meeting frequency.
* Decision rights.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Reporting requirements.
* Record-keeping requirements.
* Review frequency.

The charter should clearly distinguish strategic responsibilities from operational responsibilities.

## 2. Establish a Strategic Governance Calendar

The committee should maintain an annual governance calendar.

Recurring topics may include:

* AI strategy review.
* AI portfolio review.
* Investment planning.
* Benefits realization.
* AI adoption.
* Organizational capability.
* Major technology developments.
* Strategic risks.
* Vendor concentration.
* Regulatory developments.
* AI roadmap.
* Portfolio retirement.

A defined calendar reduces the risk that strategic oversight becomes reactive.

## 3. Establish a Standard Meeting Agenda

A consistent agenda structure can improve executive decision-making.

A typical meeting may include:

1. Opening and quorum.
2. Review of previous decisions.
3. Review of strategic actions.
4. AI portfolio dashboard.
5. Investment and financial performance.
6. Benefits realization.
7. Major strategic initiatives.
8. Resource and capability constraints.
9. Strategic risks and dependencies.
10. Emerging AI developments.
11. Decisions required.
12. Escalations.
13. Closing actions.

The majority of meeting time should be directed toward strategic decisions and material exceptions.

## 4. Establish Portfolio Review Procedures

The committee should periodically review the entire AI portfolio.

The review should consider:

* Active initiatives.
* Proposed initiatives.
* Pilot programs.
* Strategic programs.
* Underperforming initiatives.
* Retiring initiatives.

Portfolio review should consider both individual initiative performance and aggregate portfolio characteristics.

## 5. Establish a Standard Initiative Submission

Major initiatives should use a consistent submission format.

A strategic AI proposal may include:

* Business problem.
* Strategic objective.
* Proposed AI capability.
* Business owner.
* Expected value.
* Investment requirement.
* Resource requirement.
* Timeline.
* Dependencies.
* Technology requirements.
* Governance requirements.
* Strategic risks.
* Success criteria.

Standardization enables more consistent prioritization.

## 6. Establish Strategic Decision Papers

Decision papers should clearly state what the committee needs to decide.

A useful structure is:

### Decision Requested

What decision is required?

### Strategic Context

Why does the initiative matter?

### Business Value

What benefits are expected?

### Investment

What resources are required?

### Alternatives

What alternatives were considered?

### Risks

What strategic risks exist?

### Recommendation

What action is recommended?

### Decision Impact

What happens if the committee approves, delays, or rejects the proposal?

This allows executive members to focus on the decision rather than reviewing excessive operational detail.

## 7. Establish Portfolio Scoring

The committee may use a standardized scoring model to compare initiatives.

Potential dimensions include:

* Strategic alignment.
* Business value.
* Customer impact.
* Risk reduction.
* Feasibility.
* Time to value.
* Scalability.
* Resource requirements.
* Technology readiness.
* Governance readiness.

A scoring model should support executive judgment rather than replace it.

## 8. Establish Investment Gates

Major AI investments should pass through defined decision gates.

For example:

**Gate 1 — Strategic Fit**

Does the initiative support enterprise strategy?

**Gate 2 — Business Case**

Is the expected value sufficient?

**Gate 3 — Feasibility**

Can the organization execute the initiative?

**Gate 4 — Pilot**

Has sufficient evidence been generated?

**Gate 5 — Scale**

Is the initiative ready for broader deployment?

**Gate 6 — Benefits Review**

Has the expected business value been realized?

This allows investment decisions to be adjusted as evidence develops.

## 9. Establish Portfolio Health Indicators

The committee should maintain a portfolio health view.

Potential indicators include:

* Percentage of initiatives on schedule.
* Percentage within budget.
* Benefits realization.
* Adoption rate.
* Resource utilization.
* Number of high-risk initiatives.
* Number of delayed initiatives.
* Number of blocked initiatives.
* Number of initiatives requiring executive intervention.

Portfolio health should be assessed using trends rather than isolated data points.

## 10. Track Strategic Dependencies

The committee should maintain visibility of dependencies that affect multiple AI initiatives.

Examples include:

* Common data platforms.
* AI infrastructure.
* Cloud services.
* Identity systems.
* Security platforms.
* Procurement frameworks.
* Vendor contracts.
* Shared AI platforms.
* Regulatory approvals.

Critical dependencies should have clearly identified owners and resolution plans.

## 11. Maintain a Strategic Dependency Register

A dependency register may contain:

| Field       | Description                     |
| ----------- | ------------------------------- |
| Dependency  | Required capability or decision |
| Initiative  | Affected AI program             |
| Owner       | Responsible party               |
| Impact      | Consequence of delay            |
| Priority    | Strategic importance            |
| Target Date | Required completion             |
| Status      | Current state                   |
| Escalation  | Required executive action       |

This allows the committee to focus on dependencies that threaten strategic outcomes.

## 12. Establish Resource Escalation

Resource shortages may prevent strategically important AI initiatives from progressing.

The committee should monitor critical resource constraints involving:

* AI specialists.
* Data scientists.
* Data engineers.
* Security specialists.
* Cloud engineers.
* AI governance professionals.
* Product managers.

Where necessary, the committee can recommend:

* Reallocation.
* Hiring.
* External support.
* Training.
* Prioritization changes.
* Initiative deferral.

## 13. Monitor Benefits Realization

# AI Steering Committee — Part 4

## Advanced Strategic Governance Considerations

An AI Steering Committee becomes increasingly important as AI moves from isolated experimentation into enterprise-wide transformation. At this stage, the committee must manage not only individual initiatives but also the strategic consequences of AI adoption across the organization.

The committee should therefore evolve from simply reviewing projects to actively managing **enterprise AI direction, investment concentration, organizational capability, strategic dependencies, transformation risk, and long-term value creation**.

## 1. Move From Project Oversight to Portfolio Governance

A mature AI Steering Committee should avoid managing AI as a collection of independent projects.

Instead, it should view AI as an enterprise portfolio.

Portfolio governance considers:

* Strategic objectives.
* Investment allocation.
* Shared capabilities.
* Technology dependencies.
* Risk concentration.
* Business outcomes.
* Organizational capacity.
* Long-term sustainability.

This perspective allows the committee to identify relationships between initiatives that may not be visible at project level.

## 2. Establish Enterprise AI Investment Principles

The committee should establish principles that guide AI investment decisions.

Examples include:

* Prioritize measurable business value.
* Avoid unnecessary duplication.
* Prefer reusable enterprise capabilities.
* Consider long-term operating costs.
* Assess vendor dependency.
* Consider security and governance requirements.
* Avoid investments that create excessive technology lock-in.
* Scale only after sufficient evidence exists.

These principles help create consistency across investment decisions.

## 3. Manage AI Investment Concentration

AI investments can become concentrated around a small number of technologies or providers.

The committee should monitor concentration involving:

* Foundation models.
* Cloud providers.
* AI platforms.
* Data providers.
* Consulting partners.
* AI SaaS providers.

High concentration may create strategic dependency.

The committee should therefore consider alternatives, portability, interoperability, and exit strategies when appropriate.

## 4. Establish Strategic AI Architecture Principles

The steering committee should work with enterprise architecture to ensure that major AI investments support the target technology environment.

Strategic architecture considerations may include:

* Interoperability.
* Reusability.
* Scalability.
* Data portability.
* Model portability.
* Security.
* Integration.
* Resilience.
* Cost efficiency.

The committee should not replace the Architecture Review Board or technical architecture authority.

Instead, it should ensure that strategic AI investments remain aligned with enterprise architecture direction.

## 5. Manage Technology Obsolescence

AI technology evolves rapidly.

A strategic investment that is appropriate today may become less attractive as:

* New models emerge.
* Costs decline.
* Performance improves.
* Open-source alternatives mature.
* New architectures appear.

The committee should therefore periodically reassess major AI investments.

Strategic decisions should remain evidence-based rather than becoming permanent commitments to technologies that have lost their relevance.

## 6. Manage Vendor Lock-In

Vendor dependency can create strategic risks.

The committee should consider:

* Data portability.
* Model portability.
* API dependencies.
* Contractual restrictions.
* Migration complexity.
* Switching costs.
* Proprietary technology.
* Vendor financial stability.

Where appropriate, strategic initiatives should incorporate exit or migration strategies.

## 7. Establish AI Platform Strategy

Organizations may benefit from standardized enterprise AI platforms.

The steering committee may oversee decisions regarding:

* Approved AI platforms.
* Enterprise AI services.
* Shared model infrastructure.
* AI development environments.
* Common data services.
* AI monitoring platforms.

Standardization can reduce duplication and improve security and governance.

However, excessive standardization can limit innovation.

The committee should therefore balance enterprise consistency with appropriate technological flexibility.

## 8. Establish Reusable AI Capabilities

The committee should identify capabilities that can be reused across multiple AI initiatives.

Examples include:

* AI gateways.
* Model access platforms.
* Identity services.
* Monitoring.
* Logging.
* Evaluation frameworks.
* Data pipelines.
* Security controls.
* Governance workflows.

Reusable capabilities can reduce cost and accelerate future AI initiatives.

## 9. Establish Strategic AI Capability Ownership

Enterprise capabilities should have clearly assigned owners.

For example:

| Capability    | Potential Owner                     |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| AI Platform   | Technology                          |
| AI Security   | Cybersecurity                       |
| AI Data       | Data Governance                     |
| AI Risk       | Enterprise Risk                     |
| AI Governance | AI Governance Function              |
| AI Strategy   | Executive / Transformation Function |
| AI Products   | Business Owner                      |

Ownership should be explicit to prevent capability gaps.

## 10. Manage AI Transformation Risk

Large-scale AI transformation introduces organizational risks.

These may include:

* Workforce disruption.
* Process failures.
* Poor adoption.
* Overdependence on automation.
* Inadequate skills.
* Excessive transformation pace.
* Insufficient change management.

The steering committee should ensure these risks are considered within strategic planning.

## 11. Balance Automation and Human Oversight

AI initiatives may gradually move from:

**Decision Support → Assisted Decision-Making → Automated Decision-Making → Autonomous Action**

Each stage may create different strategic and operational consequences.

The committee should ensure that increased autonomy is supported by:

* Appropriate controls.
* Human oversight.
* Clear accountability.
* Monitoring.
* Intervention mechanisms.
* Defined boundaries.

Strategic pressure to automate should not override appropriate risk controls.

## 12. Evaluate AI Operating Model Implications

Enterprise AI adoption may require changes to organizational structures.

Potential changes include:

* New AI teams.
* Centralized AI platforms.
* Federated AI development.
* AI product teams.
* AI centers of excellence.
* New governance roles.
* New security capabilities.

The steering committee should periodically assess whether the organizational operating model remains appropriate.

## 13. Centralized Versus Federated AI

Organizations may choose between centralized and federated AI operating models.

### Centralized Model

AI capabilities are concentrated within a central enterprise function.

Advantages may include:

* Standardization.
* Central expertise.
* Consistent governance.
* Economies of scale.

### Federated Model

Business units develop AI capabilities while following enterprise standards.

Advantages may include:

* Business responsiveness.
* Local expertise.
* Faster experimentation.
* Greater business ownership.

### Hybrid Model

A hybrid model combines:

**Centralized Platforms and Standards + Federated Business Delivery**

This can provide enterprise consistency while maintaining business flexibility.

The steering committee should ensure that the selected model supports enterprise strategy.

## 14. Manage AI Capability Duplication

Multiple business units may independently build similar AI capabilities.

This can create:

* Duplicate investments.
* Higher operating costs.
* Inconsistent security.
* Different governance practices.
* Multiple vendor relationships.

The committee should identify opportunities for consolidation or reuse.

## 17. Use Evidence-Based Scaling

Scaling decisions should consider:

* Business value.
* Technical performance.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Reliability.
* Cost.
* User adoption.
* Governance readiness.
* Operational support.

Evidence-based scaling reduces the risk of expanding ineffective or poorly controlled AI systems.

## 18. Establish Strategic Stop Criteria

Examples include:

* Benefits materially below expectations.
* Costs materially exceeding assumptions.

* Unacceptable risk.

* Regulatory concerns.
* Strategic priorities changing.
* Better alternatives becoming available.

A mature steering committee should be willing to stop initiatives when evidence no longer supports continued investment.

## 30. Final Strategic Principle

The AI Steering Committee should ensure that AI becomes an **enterprise capability rather than a collection of disconnected technology initiatives**.

Its strategic responsibility can be summarized as:

**Set Direction → Prioritize → Invest → Coordinate → Execute → Measure → Adapt**

A mature committee should be capable of answering five fundamental questions:

1. **Why are we using AI?**
2. **Where should we invest?**
3. **What capabilities do we need?**
4. **Are we achieving the intended value within acceptable risk?**
5. **What should we change as circumstances evolve?**

The ultimate objective of an AI Steering Committee is not to maximize the number of AI systems deployed.

It is to ensure that AI investments produce **sustainable enterprise value, strategic advantage, organizational capability, and measurable outcomes while remaining aligned with the organization's governance, risk, security, legal, privacy, and regulatory obligations**.
