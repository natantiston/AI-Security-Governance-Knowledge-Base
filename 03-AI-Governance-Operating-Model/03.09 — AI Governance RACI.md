# 03.09 — AI Governance RACI

## Introduction

An AI Governance RACI establishes a structured method for assigning accountability and responsibility across AI governance activities.

AI governance involves numerous stakeholders, including business owners, AI governance teams, cybersecurity, risk management, privacy, legal, data governance, technology, engineering, compliance, internal audit, and executive leadership. Without a clearly defined responsibility model, organizations may encounter duplicated activities, unclear decision authority, delayed approvals, or governance gaps.

The RACI model provides a practical mechanism for translating the organization's AI governance roles and responsibilities into an operational structure.

RACI stands for:

* **R — Responsible**
* **A — Accountable**
* **C — Consulted**
* **I — Informed**

A well-designed AI Governance RACI establishes **who performs an activity, who owns the outcome, who provides input, and who needs visibility**.

---

# Purpose of an AI Governance RACI

The primary purpose of an AI Governance RACI is to clarify accountability across the AI lifecycle.

It can be used to define responsibilities for:

* AI strategy.
* AI governance.
* AI policy.
* AI risk assessment.
* AI security.
* AI privacy.
* AI data governance.
* AI development.
* AI validation.
* AI approval.
* AI deployment.
* AI monitoring.
* AI incident management.
* AI change management.
* AI retirement.
* AI third-party governance.
* AI risk acceptance.

The RACI should provide an operational representation of the organization's governance model.

---

# 1. Understanding the RACI Model

The four RACI designations have distinct meanings.

## Responsible — R

The person or function that performs the work.

Responsible parties may:

* Execute an activity.
* Prepare documentation.
* Perform assessments.
* Implement controls.
* Produce evidence.

There may be more than one Responsible party when multiple functions perform different portions of the activity.

---

## Accountable — A

The person who ultimately owns the outcome and is answerable for the activity.

The Accountable role:

* Owns the final outcome.
* Ensures the activity is completed.
* Has appropriate authority.
* Approves the result where required.
* Accepts accountability for the decision.

As a governance principle, each significant activity should normally have **one clearly identifiable Accountable role**.

---

## Consulted — C

The person or function that provides expertise or input before a decision or activity is completed.

Consulted parties may include:

* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Risk.
* Data Governance.
* Architecture.
* Compliance.

Consultation should occur where the expertise is materially relevant.

---

## Informed — I

The person or function that needs to receive information about the activity or decision but does not actively participate in making it.

Examples may include:

* Executive management.
* Governance committees.
* Business stakeholders.
* Audit.
* Affected operational teams.

Being Informed does not imply approval authority.

---

# 2. RACI and AI Governance Accountability

RACI should translate organizational governance requirements into operational accountability.

For example:

**AI Security Assessment**

could be structured as:

| Role            | RACI |
| --------------- | ---- |
| AI System Owner | A    |
| Security        | R    |
| Risk            | C    |
| Privacy         | C    |
| AI Governance   | C    |
| Business Owner  | I    |

This establishes that Security performs the assessment, while the System Owner remains accountable for ensuring that the assessment is completed.

---

# 3. RACI Is Not an Organizational Chart

A RACI does not describe the organization's hierarchy.

An organizational chart shows:

* Reporting relationships.
* Management hierarchy.
* Organizational structure.

A RACI shows:

* Activity ownership.
* Responsibility.
* Consultation.
* Information flow.

A single role may therefore appear across multiple organizational areas without changing the reporting structure.

---

# 4. RACI Is Not a Risk Register

A RACI should not replace the AI risk register.

The:

**Risk Register**

identifies and manages risks.

The:

**RACI**

identifies who performs and owns governance activities.

The two should work together.

For example:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Risk Treatment
   ↓
Control
   ↓
Control Owner
   ↓
RACI
```

---

# 5. RACI Is Not a Policy

A policy establishes mandatory organizational requirements.

A RACI translates those requirements into responsibility assignments.

For example:

**Policy**

> High-risk AI systems must undergo formal security assessment before production deployment.

**RACI**

identifies:

* Who performs the assessment.
* Who is accountable.
* Who must be consulted.
* Who must be informed.

---

# 6. Establish a Single Accountable Role

One of the most important RACI principles is establishing clear accountability.

A poorly designed matrix may contain:

| Activity    | Business | Security | Risk | Governance |
| ----------- | -------- | -------- | ---- | ---------- |
| AI approval | A        | A        | A    | A          |

This creates ambiguity.

A better model identifies one primary Accountable role while assigning supporting responsibilities appropriately.

For example:

| Activity    | Business | Security | Risk | Governance |
| ----------- | -------- | -------- | ---- | ---------- |
| AI approval | A        | C        | C    | R          |

The exact assignment should reflect the organization's actual decision authority.

---

# 7. Avoid Activities Without Accountability

Every material AI governance activity should have an Accountable role.

Examples include:

* AI risk assessment.
* AI security assessment.
* AI system registration.
* AI deployment approval.
* AI risk acceptance.
* AI incident escalation.
* AI retirement.

A RACI matrix should be reviewed specifically for activities that have no **A**.

An activity without accountability represents a governance weakness.

---

# 8. Avoid Excessive Accountability

Assigning multiple Accountable roles to every activity can make governance ineffective.

For example:

```text
AI Governance = A
Risk = A
Security = A
Business = A
Legal = A
```

may appear comprehensive but often results in unclear decision authority.

Where multiple stakeholders have significant interests, the organization should still define who has final accountability.

---

# 9. Establish Appropriate Responsibility

Responsible assignments should identify the party performing the actual work.

For example:

**AI Risk Assessment**

Risk may be **R**.

**AI Security Assessment**

Security may be **R**.

**AI Privacy Assessment**

Privacy may be **R**.

**AI System Registration**

AI Governance may be **R**.

The Accountable role should ensure that the activity is completed appropriately.

---

# 10. Establish Appropriate Consultation

Consultation should be meaningful rather than excessive.

For example, an AI system processing personal information may require consultation with:

* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Data Governance.
* Security.
* Risk.

However, involving every governance function in every AI activity can create unnecessary delays.

Consultation should therefore be **risk-based and relevant**.

---

# 11. Establish Appropriate Information Flow

The **I** designation should be used for stakeholders who need visibility but do not need to participate in the activity.

Examples include:

* Executive management.
* Governance committees.
* Business stakeholders.
* Affected users.
* Internal Audit where appropriate.

Information requirements should reflect materiality.

# RACI Design Principles

An effective AI Governance RACI should follow several principles:

### 1. Every material activity should have an Accountable role.

### 2. Accountability should normally be singular.

### 3. Responsible roles should perform the actual work.

### 4. Consultation should be risk-based.

### 5. Information should reach the appropriate stakeholders.

### 6. Decision authority should align with accountability.

### 7. Independent assurance should remain independent.

### 8. RACI assignments should cover the complete AI lifecycle.

### 9. RACI should be connected to policies, controls, and evidence.

### 10. RACI should be reviewed when the operating model changes.

---

# Final Governance Principle

An AI Governance RACI should transform organizational role definitions into an **operational accountability mechanism**.

Its fundamental purpose is to eliminate ambiguity by making clear:

> **Who performs the activity, who owns the outcome, who provides expert input, and who needs visibility.**

A well-designed RACI enables AI governance to move from conceptual responsibility statements to **measurable execution, controlled decision-making, effective escalation, and demonstrable accountability throughout the AI lifecycle**.

# AI Governance RACI — Part 2

## Designing the AI Governance RACI

A RACI matrix is effective only when it is deliberately designed around the organization's governance structure, decision rights, AI lifecycle, and risk model.

Simply assigning R, A, C, and I to a list of activities does not create effective governance. The matrix must accurately represent **who performs the work, who owns the outcome, who has decision authority, who provides specialist challenge, and who requires visibility**.

A common RACI problem is creating too many columns.


# 9. Use Functional Roles Where Appropriate

Instead of assigning responsibility to a specific employee, the RACI should normally identify a role or function.

For example:

**AI Security**

rather than:

**John Smith — Security Architect**

This allows responsibilities to remain stable when employees change.

---

# 10. Define RACI Semantics

The organization should establish formal definitions for R, A, C, and I.

For example:

| Letter | Meaning                       |
| ------ | ----------------------------- |
| R      | Performs the activity         |
| A      | Owns the final outcome        |
| C      | Provides required input       |
| I      | Receives relevant information |

These definitions should remain consistent across the organization.

---

# 11. Establish the Relationship Between A and R

The **A** and **R** roles should be intentionally assigned.

The same person or function may sometimes be both.

For example:

| Activity              | Business Owner | AI System Owner |
| --------------------- | -------------- | --------------- |
| Business use approval | A/R            | C               |

This may be appropriate for smaller organizations.

In other circumstances:

| Activity               | Business Owner | AI System Owner |
| ---------------------- | -------------- | --------------- |
| AI system registration | A              | R               |

The System Owner performs the activity while the Business Owner remains accountable.

---

# 12. Establish the Single-A Principle

A useful governance rule is:

> **Each material RACI activity should normally have one Accountable role.**

This creates clear decision ownership.

If two functions appear to be equally accountable, the organization should determine which function has final authority.

---

# 13. Establish the No-A Rule

A RACI review should identify every activity without an Accountable role.

For example:

| Activity           | Business | Governance | Security | Risk |
| ------------------ | -------- | ---------- | -------- | ---- |
| AI risk acceptance | C        | C          | C        | R    |

There is no **A**.

This is an accountability gap.

The matrix should be corrected before approval.

---

# 14. Establish the No-R Rule

Activities should also normally have at least one Responsible party.

For example:

| Activity               | Business | Governance | Security | Risk |
| ---------------------- | -------- | ---------- | -------- | ---- |
| AI security assessment | A        | C          | —        | C    |

There is no **R**.

This means nobody has been assigned to actually perform the activity.

---

# 15. Avoid Excessive R Assignments

An activity with too many Responsible roles may become difficult to coordinate.

For example:

```text
Business = R
Security = R
Risk = R
Privacy = R
Legal = R
Governance = R
Engineering = R
```

This may indicate that the activity should be broken into smaller activities.

For example:

* Perform security assessment.
* Perform privacy assessment.
* Perform risk assessment.
* Consolidate governance assessment.

Each can have its own RACI.

---

# 16. Limit Consultation

Too many **C** assignments can create governance bottlenecks.

If every activity requires consultation with ten functions, decision-making becomes slow.

Consultation should therefore be:

* Relevant.
* Risk-based.
* Proportionate.
* Timely.

Specialist consultation should occur when the subject matter requires it.

---

# 17. Distinguish Consultation From Approval

Being **Consulted** does not mean having approval authority.

For example:

| Role           | RACI |
| -------------- | ---- |
| Security       | C    |
| Business Owner | A    |

Security can identify security concerns and challenge the decision without necessarily being the final approval authority.

If Security has formal veto authority, the governance model should document that authority explicitly rather than relying on the C designation.

---

# 18. Distinguish Information From Consultation

Being **Informed** means receiving information.

It does not mean:

* Reviewing the activity.
* Approving the activity.
* Providing mandatory input.

For example, Internal Audit may be informed of significant governance developments while maintaining its independence from operational decisions.

---

# 19. Connect RACI to Decision Rights

RACI should align with the organization's decision-rights framework.

For example:

```text id="a2x9qh"
AI Security Review
       ↓
Security = R
       ↓
System Owner = A
       ↓
Approval Authority = defined by governance policy
```

If the RACI and decision-rights model conflict, the decision-rights model should be formally reconciled.

---

# 20. Connect RACI to Risk Ownership

The RACI should not accidentally transfer risk ownership to the Risk function.

For example:

**Business Risk Assessment**

could be:

* Risk = R.
* Business Owner = A.

This means Risk performs or facilitates the assessment while the Business Owner remains accountable for the underlying business risk.

---

# 21. Connect RACI to Control Ownership

Controls should have clearly defined ownership.

For example:

**AI Access Review**

* Security = R.
* Control Owner = A.
* System Owner = C.

The RACI should be consistent with the organization's control framework.

# RACI Quality Review

Before approving an AI Governance RACI, the organization should ask:

### Accountability

* Does every material activity have an **A**?
* Is accountability clear?
* Is there unnecessary shared accountability?

### Responsibility

* Does every activity have an **R**?
* Is the R role capable of performing the work?
* Are there excessive R assignments?

### Consultation

* Are C roles genuinely necessary?
* Could excessive consultation create delays?

### Information

* Are the right stakeholders informed?
* Are I assignments being mistaken for approval?

### Authority

* Does the A role actually have decision authority?
* Are veto or override powers explicitly defined?

### Independence

* Are assurance activities sufficiently independent?
* Are conflicts of interest addressed?

### Lifecycle

* Does the RACI cover the entire AI lifecycle?

# RACI Design Rule

A strong AI Governance RACI should satisfy the following basic logic:

```text id="s5l5j4"
Every Material Activity
        │
        ├── One Primary Accountable Role
        │
        ├── One or More Responsible Roles
        │
        ├── Relevant Consulted Roles
        │
        └── Appropriate Informed Roles
```

This creates a practical accountability structure without unnecessarily increasing governance complexity.

---

# Final Governance Principle

The AI Governance RACI should function as the **operational translation of the AI governance operating model**.

It should connect:

**Roles**

→ **Responsibilities**

→ **Activities**

→ **Decision Rights**

→ **Controls**

→ **Evidence**

→ **Assurance**

The effectiveness of the RACI is therefore not determined by how detailed the matrix looks, but by whether it accurately answers a fundamental governance question:

> **For every material AI activity, who is expected to perform the work, who is ultimately accountable for the outcome, who must provide meaningful input, and who needs to know?**

When these relationships are clearly defined and consistently maintained, the RACI becomes a practical mechanism for preventing accountability gaps, reducing duplicated responsibilities, strengthening segregation of duties, and enabling effective AI governance across the enterprise.

# AI Governance RACI — Part 4

## RACI Governance, Maintenance, Assurance, and Continuous Improvement

An AI Governance RACI should be treated as a controlled governance artifact rather than a static responsibility table. As AI technologies, organizational structures, regulations, business processes, and governance requirements evolve, the RACI must also evolve.

A RACI that no longer reflects actual authority or operational responsibilities can create a false sense of accountability. Therefore, the organization should establish mechanisms to govern, validate, monitor, and continuously improve the RACI.

---

# 1. Establish RACI Ownership

The organization should designate an owner for the enterprise AI Governance RACI.

The RACI Owner should be responsible for:

* Maintaining the matrix.
* Coordinating changes.
* Resolving responsibility conflicts.
* Ensuring consistency with governance policies.
* Coordinating periodic reviews.
* Maintaining version history.
* Ensuring stakeholder approval.

The RACI Owner is not necessarily accountable for every activity contained within the matrix.

---

# 2. Establish RACI Governance Authority

The organization should determine who has authority to approve changes to the RACI.

Depending on the operating model, this may be:

* AI Governance Leadership.
* AI Governance Committee.
* AI Steering Committee.
* Executive Management.

The approval authority should be formally documented.

---

# 3. Establish RACI Version Control

The RACI should have formal version control.

Each version should identify:

* Version number.
* Effective date.
* Owner.
* Approver.
* Change description.
* Affected roles.
* Affected processes.

For example:

```text id="k0f8tt"
AI Governance RACI
Version: 2.0
Effective Date: 2026-08-01
Owner: AI Governance
Approved By: AI Governance Committee
```

This creates traceability over time.

---

# 4. Establish RACI Change Management

Changes to organizational responsibilities should trigger RACI review.

Examples include:

* New AI governance function.
* New AI committee.
* Organizational restructuring.
* New AI security function.
* New risk management structure.
* Outsourcing.
* New AI technology.
* New regulatory requirements.

The RACI should be updated when responsibility changes become effective.

---

# 5. Establish RACI Review Frequency

The organization should establish a formal review cycle.

For example:

* Annual formal review.
* Quarterly review for high-risk environments.
* Event-driven review following major changes.

The review frequency should reflect the organization's AI risk profile and rate of change.

---

# 6. Establish Event-Driven RACI Reviews

Certain events should automatically trigger a RACI review.

Examples include:

* Major AI incident.
* Material audit finding.
* Regulatory change.
* Significant organizational restructuring.
* New AI governance committee.
* Major outsourcing arrangement.
* New autonomous AI capability.
* Significant change in AI risk appetite.

Event-driven reviews help ensure that governance remains aligned with reality.

---

# 7. Establish RACI Conflict Management

Responsibility conflicts may occur when multiple functions believe they own the same activity.

Examples include:

* Security versus System Owner.
* Risk versus Business Owner.
* AI Governance versus Business.
* Data Owner versus System Owner.

The organization should define a mechanism for resolving these conflicts.

Possible escalation:

```text id="q7z2hs"
Role Conflict
     ↓
RACI Owner
     ↓
AI Governance Leadership
     ↓
Governance Committee
     ↓
Executive Authority
```

The escalation level should depend on the significance of the conflict.

---

# 8. Establish RACI Gap Management

The organization should also identify activities with missing ownership.

Typical gaps include:

* AI system without a System Owner.
* AI risk without a Risk Owner.
* Control without a Control Owner.
* Model without a Model Owner.
* Data without a Data Owner.
* Governance process without an accountable function.

These gaps should be formally recorded and remediated.

---

# 9. Establish RACI Exception Management

Sometimes the standard RACI cannot be applied.

For example:

* Small business units.
* Temporary projects.
* Mergers and acquisitions.
* Outsourced operations.
* Emergency deployments.

Exceptions should be:

* Documented.
* Justified.
* Approved.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Reviewed periodically.

Temporary responsibility arrangements should not become permanent through neglect.

---

# 10. Establish RACI Delegation

Accountability and responsibility may sometimes be delegated.

However, delegation should be controlled.

A delegation should identify:

* Delegating authority.
* Delegate.
* Scope.
* Duration.
* Decision authority.
* Limitations.

Delegation should not create uncertainty about who ultimately remains accountable.

---

# 11. Distinguish Accountability From Delegation

A key principle is:

> **Delegating an activity does not necessarily eliminate the accountability of the original owner.**

For example:

```text id="m1o7kf"
System Owner
     │
     │ delegates assessment coordination
     ▼
AI Governance Analyst
     │
     ▼
Assessment performed
```

The System Owner may remain accountable even though the analyst performs the activity.

---

# 12. Establish RACI Segregation of Duties

The RACI should periodically be reviewed for conflicts of interest.

Examples of potentially problematic combinations include:

* Developer + independent validator.
* Control operator + control assessor.
* Risk assessor + sole risk acceptance authority.
* System developer + sole production approval authority.

The higher the AI risk, the greater the importance of appropriate segregation.

---

# 13. Establish Independent Challenge

Certain RACI assignments should deliberately preserve independent challenge.

For example:

```text id="j5r4wr"
System Owner
     │
     ▼
Risk Assessment
     │
     ▼
Risk Function
     │
     ▼
Independent Challenge
```

The purpose is not to prevent management from making decisions but to ensure that significant decisions receive appropriate challenge.

---

# 14. Establish RACI Assurance

The organization should periodically test whether the RACI is operating as designed.

Assurance activities may verify:

* Owners exist.
* Assigned roles understand their responsibilities.
* Activities are actually performed.
* Approvals are performed by authorized roles.
* Escalations reach the correct authority.
* Evidence supports RACI assignments.

Assurance should compare the documented RACI against actual practice.

---

# 15. Establish RACI Auditability

A mature RACI should create evidence that responsibilities were actually exercised.

Evidence may include:

* Approval records.
* Risk assessments.
* Security assessments.
* Meeting minutes.
* Governance decisions.
* Exception approvals.
* Incident records.

# 36. RACI Anti-Patterns

Organizations should actively identify common RACI problems.

### Everyone Is Accountable

Creates unclear decision authority.

### Nobody Is Responsible

Creates execution gaps.

### Everyone Is Consulted

Creates excessive governance overhead.

### Everyone Is Informed

Creates unnecessary communication.

### RACI Reflects Job Titles Instead of Responsibilities

Makes the matrix fragile when personnel change.

### RACI Does Not Match Actual Practice

Creates a false governance record.

### RACI Conflicts With Policy

Creates contradictory governance requirements.

### RACI Conflicts With Decision Rights

Creates uncertainty over who can make decisions.

# Core Governance Principles

The AI Governance RACI should ultimately enforce several fundamental principles:

### 1. Accountability must be explicit.

Every material AI governance activity should have a clearly identified accountable role.

### 2. Responsibility must be executable.

The Responsible role must have the capability, resources, and authority required to perform the activity.

### 3. Decision rights must align with accountability.

The person accountable for an outcome should have appropriate authority to influence that outcome.

### 4. Risk ownership must remain clear.

Risk, Governance, Security, and Compliance functions should not unintentionally assume management's operational accountability.

### 5. Independence must be preserved.

Validation, assurance, and audit activities should maintain appropriate independence from the activities being assessed.

### 6. RACI must reflect actual operations.

The documented matrix should correspond to how governance decisions and activities are actually performed.

### 7. RACI must cover the complete lifecycle.

Responsibilities should remain clear from AI strategy and ideation through development, deployment, operation, monitoring, change, incident response, and retirement.

### 8. RACI must remain current.

Organizational, technological, regulatory, and governance changes should trigger appropriate review.

### 9. RACI should be integrated.

The RACI should connect to policies, processes, controls, risks, AI inventory, decision rights, workflows, and assurance.

### 10. Accountability should be demonstrable.

Organizations should retain sufficient evidence to demonstrate that assigned responsibilities were actually exercised.

---

# Final Governance Principle

The AI Governance RACI is ultimately a mechanism for transforming **organizational accountability into operational accountability**.

A mature RACI allows the organization to answer, for every material AI governance activity:

> **Who is responsible for performing the work? Who is accountable for the outcome? Who must provide expert input? Who must be informed? Who has authority to decide? Who can escalate the matter? And what evidence demonstrates that the responsibility was actually exercised?**

When these relationships are formally defined, implemented, monitored, and periodically reassessed, the RACI becomes a foundational component of the AI Governance Operating Model.

Its ultimate purpose is to ensure that **AI governance decisions, risks, controls, and outcomes have clear ownership throughout the enterprise and across the complete AI lifecycle**.
