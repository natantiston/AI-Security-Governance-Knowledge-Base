# 03.15 — AI Governance Charter

## Introduction

An AI Governance Charter is the formal document that establishes the **mandate, authority, scope, responsibilities, decision rights, membership, operating principles, and accountability structure** of an organization's AI governance function.

The charter provides the organizational foundation through which AI governance operates. Policies define what the organization requires; standards define how requirements are implemented; procedures define how activities are performed; the governance charter establishes **who has the authority to govern, what they are responsible for, and how governance decisions are made**.

An AI Governance Charter is therefore not simply a committee terms-of-reference document. In a mature AI Governance Operating Model, it serves as an authoritative governance instrument that connects executive sponsorship, AI strategy, risk management, security, privacy, compliance, technology, business ownership, and operational execution.

The charter should establish sufficient authority for governance bodies to:

* Review AI initiatives.
* Establish governance requirements.
* Approve or reject defined AI activities.
* Review AI risks.
* Escalate material matters.
* Challenge management decisions.
* Approve defined exceptions.
* Require remediation.
* Monitor governance performance.
* Report significant matters to executive management and the Board where appropriate.

The charter should also make clear that AI governance is an **enterprise responsibility**, rather than an activity belonging exclusively to the technology, cybersecurity, data, legal, or compliance functions.

---

# 1. Purpose of an AI Governance Charter

The primary purpose of an AI Governance Charter is to formally establish the governance authority and operating structure for managing AI across the enterprise.

The charter should provide clarity regarding:

* Why AI governance exists.
* What AI governance covers.
* Who owns AI governance.
* Who participates in governance.
* What decisions governance bodies can make.
* What matters must be escalated.
* How governance decisions are documented.
* How governance performance is monitored.
* How governance bodies interact with executive management and the Board.

Without a formal charter, AI governance may depend excessively on informal relationships, individual influence, or organizational assumptions.

---

# 2. Charter as a Governance Authority

The charter should establish the formal authority of the AI governance function.

For example, the charter may authorize the relevant governance body to:

```text id="j8f3mp"
Review AI Initiatives
        ↓
Assess Governance Requirements
        ↓
Challenge Risks and Controls
        ↓
Make Authorized Decisions
        ↓
Require Remediation
        ↓
Escalate Material Matters
        ↓
Report to Executive Management
```

The authority granted by the charter should be consistent with the organization's overall corporate governance structure.

---

# 3. Relationship Between Charter and AI Governance Framework

The AI Governance Charter should operate as part of the broader AI Governance Framework.

A simplified hierarchy may be represented as:

```text id="q5v7xn"
Corporate Governance
        ↓
AI Governance Charter
        ↓
AI Governance Framework
        ↓
AI Policies
        ↓
AI Standards
        ↓
AI Procedures
        ↓
Operational Controls
        ↓
Evidence and Records
```

The charter establishes governance authority, while the framework and supporting documents establish the requirements and mechanisms through which that authority operates.

---

# 4. Charter Versus Policy

An AI Governance Charter and an AI Governance Policy serve different purposes.

### Charter

Defines:

* Governance mandate.
* Authority.
* Membership.
* Decision rights.
* Responsibilities.
* Reporting relationships.
* Operating structure.

### Policy

Defines:

* Organizational requirements.
* Mandatory principles.
* Prohibited activities.
* Control expectations.
* Compliance obligations.

The charter answers:

> **Who governs and with what authority?**

The policy answers:

> **What does the organization require?**

Both should work together.

---

# 5. Charter Versus Committee Terms of Reference

An AI Governance Charter may establish the overall governance structure, while individual committees may have their own Terms of Reference.

For example:

```text id="m4z8rc"
AI Governance Charter
        ↓
Enterprise AI Governance Authority
        ↓
 ┌──────────────┬──────────────┬──────────────┐
 ↓              ↓              ↓
AI Steering   AI Security    AI Risk
Committee     Council        Committee
 ↓              ↓              ↓
Terms of       Terms of       Terms of
Reference      Reference      Reference
```

The individual Terms of Reference should remain consistent with the overarching charter.

---

# 6. Charter Scope

The charter should clearly define what falls within the scope of AI governance.

Scope may include:

* AI systems.
* Machine learning systems.
* Generative AI.
* AI agents.
* Predictive models.
* AI-enabled applications.
* Third-party AI services.
* AI development.
* AI acquisition.
* AI deployment.
* AI operations.
* AI modification.
* AI retirement.

The organization should determine whether experimental, research, or proof-of-concept AI activities are also covered.

---

# 7. Enterprise Scope

AI governance should generally apply across the enterprise rather than only to the central technology function.

The charter may cover:

* Business units.
* Corporate functions.
* IT.
* Engineering.
* Cybersecurity.
* Data and analytics.
* Research and development.
* Procurement.
* Third parties.
* Subsidiaries.
* Joint ventures where applicable.

Enterprise-wide scope prevents fragmented AI governance.

---

# 8. Geographic Scope

Multinational organizations should define the geographic scope of the charter.

The charter may apply to:

* All countries.
* Specific legal entities.
* Specific jurisdictions.
* Corporate headquarters.
* Regional operations.
* Global AI platforms.

Where local laws impose additional requirements, the charter should allow appropriate local governance requirements to supplement enterprise requirements.

---

# 9. Organizational Scope

The charter should identify which organizational entities are subject to AI governance.

For example:

```text id="y7k2pv"
Enterprise
   ├── Business Units
   ├── Technology
   ├── Engineering
   ├── Security
   ├── Data
   ├── Legal
   ├── Privacy
   ├── Risk
   ├── Compliance
   └── Procurement
```

Each function may have different responsibilities while operating under the same governance structure.

---

# 10. AI Lifecycle Scope

The charter should cover the relevant AI lifecycle.

A typical lifecycle may include:

1. Ideation.
2. Business justification.
3. Use-case classification.
4. Design.
5. Development.
6. Testing.
7. Validation.
8. Approval.
9. Deployment.
10. Operation.
11. Monitoring.
12. Modification.
13. Incident response.
14. Retirement.

Governance responsibilities should be defined across these lifecycle stages.

---

# 11. AI System Scope

The charter should define what constitutes an AI system for governance purposes.

Depending on the organization's definition, this may include:

* Traditional machine learning.
* Deep learning.
* Generative AI.
* Large language models.
* Computer vision.
* Natural language processing.
* Recommendation engines.
* Autonomous agents.
* AI-enabled decision systems.
* AI embedded within commercial software.

Clear definitions prevent ambiguity about which technologies require governance.

---

# 12. Third-Party AI Scope

The charter should address AI obtained from external providers.

This may include:

* SaaS platforms.
* Cloud AI services.
* External APIs.
* Foundation models.
* AI-powered applications.
* Managed AI services.
* AI embedded in vendor products.

Third-party AI should not automatically be excluded from governance simply because the organization does not develop the underlying model.

---

# 13. AI Governance Mandate

The charter should formally state the mandate of the AI governance function.

A typical mandate may include:

> Establish and oversee the enterprise framework for responsible, secure, risk-managed, compliant, and strategically aligned use of artificial intelligence.

The mandate should reflect the organization's business strategy and risk environment.

---

# 14. AI Governance Objectives

The charter should establish clear governance objectives.

Examples include:

* Enable responsible AI adoption.
* Protect organizational information and systems.
* Manage AI-related risks.
* Support regulatory compliance.
* Protect customers and stakeholders.
* Establish accountability.
* Promote consistent governance.
* Enable informed AI investment decisions.
* Provide executive oversight.
* Maintain appropriate human oversight.

Objectives should be measurable where practical.

---

# 15. Governance Principles

The charter should establish principles that guide AI governance decisions.

Potential principles include:

### Accountability

AI decisions and outcomes must have accountable owners.

### Risk Proportionality

Governance should correspond to AI risk.

### Security

AI systems should be protected throughout their lifecycle.

### Privacy

Personal and sensitive information should be appropriately protected.

### Transparency

Governance decisions should be sufficiently explainable to authorized stakeholders.

### Human Oversight

Appropriate human oversight should be maintained for significant AI decisions.

### Compliance

AI activities should comply with applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, and organizational requirements.

---

# 16. Executive Sponsorship

The charter should identify the executive sponsor responsible for supporting the AI governance function.

The sponsor may be:

* Chief Executive Officer.
* Chief Information Officer.
* Chief Technology Officer.
* Chief Risk Officer.
* Chief Information Security Officer.
* Chief Data Officer.
* Other designated executive.

The appropriate sponsor depends on the organization's structure.

Executive sponsorship provides authority and organizational visibility.

---

# 17. Governance Chair

The charter should identify who chairs the primary AI governance body.

The Chair may be responsible for:

* Setting meeting agendas.
* Approving meeting priorities.
* Facilitating decisions.
* Resolving procedural disputes.
* Escalating material matters.
* Reporting to executive management.

Chair responsibilities should be formally documented.

---

# 18. Membership

The charter should define the required membership of the AI governance body.

Potential members may include representatives from:

* Business.
* Technology.
* Engineering.
* Cybersecurity.
* Risk.
* Legal.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.
* Data Governance.
* Internal Audit as an independent observer where appropriate.
* Procurement.
* Human Resources.

Membership should reflect the organization's AI risk profile.

---

# 19. Permanent and Advisory Members

The charter may distinguish between:

### Permanent Members

Individuals with standing governance responsibilities.

### Advisory Members

Subject matter experts invited when their expertise is relevant.

For example, a highly technical AI architecture issue may require an AI engineering specialist who is not a permanent committee member.

This allows governance bodies to remain manageable while maintaining access to necessary expertise.

---

# 20. Roles of Governance Members

The charter should clarify what members are expected to do.

Members may be responsible for:

* Reviewing proposals.
* Challenging assumptions.
* Assessing risks.
* Providing functional expertise.
* Making decisions within their authority.
* Escalating concerns.
* Monitoring actions.
* Supporting governance objectives.

Committee membership should represent an active governance responsibility rather than simply an attendance requirement.

---

# 21. Quorum Requirements

The charter should define quorum requirements for formal decisions.

Quorum may specify:

* Minimum number of members.
* Required representation.
* Required Chair or delegate.
* Required risk or security representation for certain decisions.

For example, a high-risk AI approval may require representation from business, security, risk, and legal or privacy functions.

---

# 22. Decision-Making Rules

The charter should establish how decisions are made.

Possible mechanisms include:

* Consensus.
* Majority vote.
* Chair decision.
* Delegated authority.
* Executive decision.

The organization should select the method appropriate to its governance culture and risk profile.

---

# 23. Decision Authority

The charter should specify what the governance body can decide.

Possible authority includes:

* Approving AI governance requirements.
* Approving defined AI use cases.
* Reviewing high-risk AI systems.
* Approving defined exceptions.
* Accepting risk within delegated limits.
* Requiring remediation.
* Suspending defined AI activities.
* Escalating material matters.

Authority should have clearly defined boundaries.

---

# 24. Limits of Authority

The charter should also specify what the governance body **cannot** decide.

For example, it may not have authority to:

* Override applicable law.
* Accept risk beyond delegated authority.
* Override Board decisions.
* Approve matters reserved for executive management.
* Waive mandatory regulatory requirements.

Defining limits is as important as defining authority.

---

# 25. Escalation Authority

The charter should establish when the governance body must escalate an issue.

Triggers may include:

* Risk exceeding appetite.
* Decision exceeding delegated authority.
* Major AI incident.
* Significant regulatory exposure.
* Material customer impact.
* Major security compromise.
* Strategic enterprise impact.

The escalation mechanism should connect with the organization's broader AI escalation framework.

---

# 26. Risk Acceptance Authority

The charter should define whether the governance body can accept AI risk.

If it can, the charter should specify:

* Risk categories.
* Maximum risk levels.
* Delegated authority.
* Documentation requirements.
* Review requirements.
* Escalation thresholds.

Risk acceptance should never occur implicitly.

---

# 27. Exception Authority

The governance body may have authority to approve defined exceptions to AI governance requirements.

Exceptions should normally include:

* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Compensating controls.
* Expiration date.
* Responsible owner.
* Approval authority.

Material exceptions should be escalated according to the organization's risk framework.

---

# 28. Reporting Responsibilities

The charter should define reporting responsibilities.

The governance body may report:

* AI risk profile.
* Significant AI incidents.
* Major governance decisions.
* Policy exceptions.
* Compliance concerns.
* AI portfolio status.
* Governance performance.
* Emerging AI risks.

Reporting should be directed to the appropriate executive or Board-level authority.

---

# 29. Board Reporting

Where appropriate, the charter should establish a formal relationship with Board-level governance.

The AI governance function may provide the Board with:

* Material AI risks.
* Significant incidents.
* Strategic AI developments.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Risk appetite issues.
* Governance performance.

The Board should receive information necessary to fulfill its oversight responsibilities.

---

# 30. Management Reporting

The AI governance body should also report to executive management.

Management reporting may focus on:

* AI portfolio.
* AI risk.
* Governance decisions.
* Security posture.
* Compliance status.
* Major projects.
* Exceptions.
* Remediation.

Management reporting should be more operational than Board-level reporting while remaining focused on material governance matters.
