# 03.15 — AI Governance Charter

## Introduction

An AI Governance Charter is the formal document that establishes the **mandate, authority, scope, responsibilities, decision rights, membership, operating principles, and accountability structure** of an organization's AI governance function.

The charter provides the organizational foundation through which AI governance operates. Policies define what the organization requires; standards define how requirements are implemented; procedures define how activities are performed; the governance charter establishes **who has the authority to govern, what they are responsible for, and how governance decisions are made**.

An AI Governance Charter is therefore not simply a committee terms-of-reference document. In a mature AI Governance Operating Model, it serves as an authoritative governance instrument that connects executive sponsorship, AI strategy, risk management, security, privacy, compliance, technology, business ownership, and operational execution.

The charter should establish sufficient authority for governance bodies to:

* Review AI initiatives.
* Establish governance requirements.
* Approve or reject defined AI activities.
* Review AI risks.
* Escalate material matters.
* Challenge management decisions.
* Approve defined exceptions.
* Require remediation.
* Monitor governance performance.
* Report significant matters to executive management and the Board where appropriate.

The charter should also make clear that AI governance is an **enterprise responsibility**, rather than an activity belonging exclusively to the technology, cybersecurity, data, legal, or compliance functions.

---

# 1. Purpose of an AI Governance Charter

The primary purpose of an AI Governance Charter is to formally establish the governance authority and operating structure for managing AI across the enterprise.

The charter should provide clarity regarding:

* Why AI governance exists.
* What AI governance covers.
* Who owns AI governance.
* Who participates in governance.
* What decisions governance bodies can make.
* What matters must be escalated.
* How governance decisions are documented.
* How governance performance is monitored.
* How governance bodies interact with executive management and the Board.

Without a formal charter, AI governance may depend excessively on informal relationships, individual influence, or organizational assumptions.

---

The charter should establish the formal authority of the AI governance function.

For example, the charter may authorize the relevant governance body to:

```text id="j8f3mp"

Make Authorized Decisions

        ↓

```

The authority granted by the charter should be consistent with the organization's overall corporate governance structure.

# 4. Charter Versus Policy

An AI Governance Charter and an AI Governance Policy serve different purposes.

### Charter

Defines:

* Governance mandate.
* Authority.
* Membership.
* Decision rights.
* Responsibilities.
* Reporting relationships.
* Operating structure.

### Policy

Defines:

* Organizational requirements.
* Mandatory principles.
* Prohibited activities.
* Control expectations.
* Compliance obligations.

The charter answers:

> **Who governs and with what authority?**

The policy answers:

> **What does the organization require?**

Both should work together.

The mandate should reflect the organization's business strategy and risk environment.

The charter should establish clear governance objectives.

Examples include:

* Enable responsible AI adoption.
* Protect organizational information and systems.
* Manage AI-related risks.
* Support regulatory compliance.
* Protect customers and stakeholders.
* Establish accountability.
* Promote consistent governance.

# 15. Governance Principles

The charter should establish principles that guide AI governance decisions.

Potential principles include:

### Accountability

AI decisions and outcomes must have accountable owners.

### Risk Proportionality

Governance should correspond to AI risk.

### Security

AI systems should be protected throughout their lifecycle.

### Privacy

Personal and sensitive information should be appropriately protected.

### Transparency

Governance decisions should be sufficiently explainable to authorized stakeholders.

### Human Oversight

Appropriate human oversight should be maintained for significant AI decisions.

### Compliance

AI activities should comply with applicable laws, regulations, contractual obligations, and organizational requirements.

# 17. Governance Chair

The charter should identify who chairs the primary AI governance body.

The Chair may be responsible for:

* Setting meeting agendas.
* Approving meeting priorities.
* Facilitating decisions.
* Resolving procedural disputes.
* Escalating material matters.

# 18. Membership

The charter should define the required membership of the AI governance body.

Potential members may include representatives from:

* Business.
* Technology.
* Engineering.
* Cybersecurity.
* Risk.
* Legal.
* Privacy.
* Compliance.
* Data Governance.
* Internal Audit as an independent observer where appropriate.

Membership should reflect the organization's AI risk profile.

---

# 19. Permanent and Advisory Members

The charter may distinguish between:

### Permanent Members

Individuals with standing governance responsibilities.

### Advisory Members

Subject matter experts invited when their expertise is relevant.

For example, a highly technical AI architecture issue may require an AI engineering specialist who is not a permanent committee member.

This allows governance bodies to remain manageable while maintaining access to necessary expertise.

---

# 20. Roles of Governance Members

The charter should clarify what members are expected to do.

Members may be responsible for:

* Reviewing proposals.
* Challenging assumptions.
* Assessing risks.
* Providing functional expertise.
* Making decisions within their authority.
* Escalating concerns.
* Monitoring actions.
* Supporting governance objectives.

Committee membership should represent an active governance responsibility rather than simply an attendance requirement.

---

# 21. Quorum Requirements

The charter should define quorum requirements for formal decisions.

Quorum may specify:

* Minimum number of members.
* Required representation.
* Required Chair or delegate.
* Required risk or security representation for certain decisions.

For example, a high-risk AI approval may require representation from business, security, risk, and legal or privacy functions.

---

# 22. Decision-Making Rules

The charter should establish how decisions are made.

Possible mechanisms include:

* Consensus.
* Majority vote.
* Chair decision.
* Delegated authority.
* Executive decision.

The organization should select the method appropriate to its governance culture and risk profile.

---

# 23. Decision Authority

The charter should specify what the governance body can decide.

Possible authority includes:

* Approving AI governance requirements.
* Approving defined AI use cases.
* Reviewing high-risk AI systems.
* Approving defined exceptions.
* Accepting risk within delegated limits.
* Requiring remediation.
* Suspending defined AI activities.
* Escalating material matters.

Authority should have clearly defined boundaries.

---

# 24. Limits of Authority

The charter should also specify what the governance body **cannot** decide.

For example, it may not have authority to:

* Override applicable law.
* Accept risk beyond delegated authority.
* Override Board decisions.
* Approve matters reserved for executive management.
* Waive mandatory regulatory requirements.

Defining limits is as important as defining authority.

---

# 25. Escalation Authority

The charter should establish when the governance body must escalate an issue.

Triggers may include:

* Risk exceeding appetite.
* Decision exceeding delegated authority.
* Major AI incident.
* Significant regulatory exposure.
* Material customer impact.
* Major security compromise.
* Strategic enterprise impact.

The escalation mechanism should connect with the organization's broader AI escalation framework.

---

# 26. Risk Acceptance Authority

The charter should define whether the governance body can accept AI risk.

If it can, the charter should specify:

* Risk categories.
* Maximum risk levels.
* Delegated authority.
* Documentation requirements.
* Review requirements.
* Escalation thresholds.

Risk acceptance should never occur implicitly.

---

# 27. Exception Authority

The governance body may have authority to approve defined exceptions to AI governance requirements.

Exceptions should normally include:

* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Compensating controls.
* Expiration date.
* Responsible owner.
* Approval authority.

Material exceptions should be escalated according to the organization's risk framework.

---

# 28. Reporting Responsibilities

The charter should define reporting responsibilities.

The governance body may report:

* AI risk profile.
* Significant AI incidents.
* Major governance decisions.
* Policy exceptions.
* Compliance concerns.
* AI portfolio status.
* Governance performance.
* Emerging AI risks.

Reporting should be directed to the appropriate executive or Board-level authority.

---

# 29. Board Reporting

Where appropriate, the charter should establish a formal relationship with Board-level governance.

The AI governance function may provide the Board with:

* Material AI risks.
* Significant incidents.
* Strategic AI developments.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Risk appetite issues.
* Governance performance.

The Board should receive information necessary to fulfill its oversight responsibilities.

---

# 30. Management Reporting

The AI governance body should also report to executive management.

Management reporting may focus on:

* AI portfolio.
* AI risk.
* Governance decisions.
* Security posture.
* Compliance status.
* Major projects.
* Exceptions.
* Remediation.

# AI Governance Charter

## Governance Structure, Responsibilities, Authority, and Operating Requirements

An AI Governance Charter becomes effective only when its mandate is translated into a clearly defined organizational structure. The charter should establish not only **who participates in AI governance**, but also how governance bodies interact with one another, how responsibilities are distributed, and how authority is exercised.

A well-designed charter prevents common governance problems such as duplicated responsibilities, unclear ownership, unauthorized decisions, committee conflicts, and gaps between policy and execution.

---

# 31. Governance Organizational Structure

The AI Governance Charter should define the organizational structure through which AI governance operates.

A representative structure may be:

```text id="k7p4mz"
Board / Board Committee
          ↓
Executive Management
          ↓
Enterprise AI Governance
          ↓
 ┌────────┬──────────┬──────────┐
 ↓        ↓          ↓
AI       AI         AI
Steering Security   Risk
Committee Council   Committee
 ↓        ↓          ↓
Business  Security   Risk /
Functions Functions  Compliance
          ↓
     AI System Owners
          ↓
     AI Delivery Teams
```

The actual structure should be adapted to organizational size, complexity, regulatory environment, and AI risk profile.

---

# 32. Governance Layers

The charter should distinguish between different governance layers.

### Strategic Layer

Responsible for enterprise AI strategy, investment, and major organizational decisions.

### Enterprise Governance Layer

Responsible for establishing governance requirements and overseeing AI risk.

### Functional Governance Layer

Responsible for specialized areas such as security, privacy, legal, data, and compliance.

### Operational Layer

Responsible for implementing and operating AI systems.

These layers should work together without duplicating responsibilities.

---

# 33. Strategic Governance

Strategic AI governance determines how AI supports enterprise objectives.

Strategic responsibilities may include:

* AI investment priorities.
* Enterprise AI strategy.
* Major transformation programs.
* AI operating model.
* Strategic partnerships.
* Major AI capability development.

Strategic governance should remain aligned with corporate strategy.

---

# 34. Enterprise Governance

Enterprise AI governance provides organization-wide oversight.

Responsibilities may include:

* AI governance framework.
* Enterprise AI policy.
* Risk governance.
* Governance standards.
* Decision rights.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Governance metrics.
* Executive reporting.

This layer ensures consistency across business units.

---

# 35. Functional Governance

AI governance requires specialized expertise.

Functional governance may cover:

* AI security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Data governance.
* Model risk.
* Technology architecture.
* Procurement.
* Business continuity.

Functional governance should provide challenge and expertise without unnecessarily taking ownership away from the business.

---

# 36. Operational Governance

Operational teams are responsible for applying governance requirements to individual AI systems.

Operational responsibilities may include:

* Implementing controls.
* Monitoring AI systems.
* Maintaining documentation.
* Managing incidents.
* Conducting testing.
* Reporting risks.
* Implementing remediation.

The charter should clearly distinguish operational execution from governance oversight.

---

# 37. Governance Body Mandates

Each governance body should have a clearly defined mandate.

For example:

| Governance Body         | Primary Mandate                       |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| AI Steering Committee   | Strategy and enterprise direction     |
| AI Governance Committee | Enterprise governance and oversight   |
| AI Security Council     | AI security and cybersecurity         |
| AI Risk Committee       | AI risk and risk decisions            |
| Business Governance     | Business ownership and outcomes       |
| Executive Management    | Material enterprise decisions         |
| Board                   | Strategic oversight and material risk |

The charter should define how these bodies interact.

---

# 38. Avoiding Governance Duplication

AI governance structures can become ineffective when multiple committees perform the same activities.

The charter should clearly distinguish:

* Strategy.
* Governance.
* Risk.
* Security.
* Operations.
* Assurance.

For example, the AI Security Council should not duplicate the entire work of the AI Risk Committee.

Instead, it should provide specialized security governance input.

---

# 39. Committee Interaction

Governance committees should have defined interaction mechanisms.

For example:

        ↓

The interaction model should define information flow, escalation, and decision boundaries.

---

# 40. Committee Escalation

The charter should define how matters move between governance bodies.

An issue may be escalated because:

* It exceeds the committee's authority.
* It requires specialized expertise.
* It has become materially more significant.
* A conflict exists between governance functions.
* Executive intervention is required.

Escalation should not require the issue to restart the entire governance process.

---

# 41. Governance Secretariat

A governance body may require a dedicated secretariat or governance coordinator.

The secretariat may be responsible for:

* Scheduling meetings.
* Preparing agendas.
* Collecting submissions.
* Maintaining minutes.
* Tracking decisions.
* Tracking action items.
* Maintaining the decision register.
* Coordinating reporting.

A strong secretariat improves governance discipline.

---

# 42. Meeting Frequency

The charter should establish the expected frequency of governance meetings.

For example:

* Monthly governance committee.
* Quarterly executive review.
* Quarterly Board reporting.
* Ad hoc meetings for material matters.
* Emergency meetings for critical incidents.

Meeting frequency should reflect AI activity and risk.

---

Governance meetings should follow a structured agenda.

Typical items may include:

The agenda should focus governance bodies on matters requiring oversight and decisions.

# 44. Governance Meeting Inputs

Members should receive appropriate information before meetings.

Inputs may include:

* Risk assessments.
* AI inventory.
* Security assessments.
* Privacy assessments.
* Business cases.
* Exception requests.
* Incident reports.
* Performance metrics.
* Regulatory developments.

Pre-reading requirements improve decision quality.

# 45. Governance Meeting Minutes

Material governance meetings should produce formal records.

Minutes should normally capture:

* Date.
* Participants.
* Matters discussed.
* Decisions.
* Actions.
* Owners.
* Deadlines.
* Escalations.
* Significant dissent where appropriate.

Minutes should not become a verbatim transcript. They should provide an authoritative record of governance activity.

# 47. Action Register

The action register should identify:

* Evidence.

Overdue material actions should be escalated.

# 48. Governance Records

The charter should define what governance records must be retained.

Potential records include:

* Meeting agendas.
* Minutes.
* Decision records.
* Risk assessments.
* Exception approvals.
* Escalation records.
* Action records.
* Governance reports.
* Supporting evidence.

Retention requirements should align with legal, regulatory, and organizational requirements.

---

# 49. Records Classification

AI governance records may contain sensitive information.

Records may contain:

* Security information.
* Personal information.
* Proprietary business information.
* Model information.
* Vendor information.
* Risk information.

The organization should therefore define appropriate classification and access requirements.

---

# 50. Conflict of Interest

The charter should establish requirements for conflicts of interest.

Members should disclose conflicts when they have:

* Personal interests.
* Financial interests.
* Business relationships.
* Direct ownership of the AI system under review.
* Other circumstances that could compromise impartiality.

The charter should define how conflicts are managed.

---

# 51. Recusal

Where appropriate, a member with a material conflict should recuse themselves from the relevant decision.

The governance record should document the recusal when material.

This protects the integrity of governance decisions.

---

# 52. Independence

Certain governance decisions require appropriate independence from the team responsible for delivering the AI system.

For example:

```text id="x4r7ns"
AI Delivery Team
       ↓
Develops AI System
       ↓
Independent Security Review
       ↓
Risk / Governance Review
       ↓
Approval
```

The charter should establish appropriate separation of duties.

---

# 53. Separation of Duties

The charter should prevent inappropriate concentration of authority.

For example, the same individual should not necessarily:

* Develop the AI system.
* Assess its own security.
* Accept unlimited risk.
* Approve its own exception.
* Perform independent assurance.

Separation of duties should be proportionate to risk.

---

# 54. Delegated Authority

Governance bodies may delegate certain responsibilities.

Delegation should be:

* Explicit.
* Documented.
* Limited.
* Time-bound where appropriate.
* Subject to reporting.
* Subject to escalation.

Delegation does not eliminate the accountability of the delegating authority.

---

# 55. Delegation Register

Organizations may maintain a delegation register identifying:

* Delegated authority.
* Delegating body.
* Delegate.
* Scope.
* Effective date.
* Expiration date.
* Conditions.

This makes delegated decision rights transparent.

---

# 56. Governance Authority During Absence

The charter should define who acts when key governance personnel are unavailable.

For example:

```text id="r8m3kp"
Committee Chair
      ↓
Unavailable
      ↓
Designated Deputy
      ↓
Exercises Defined Authority
      ↓
Reports Back to Chair
```

This is particularly important for critical AI decisions and emergency situations.

---

# 57. Emergency Governance

The charter should establish mechanisms for urgent decisions outside normal meeting schedules.

Emergency mechanisms may include:

* Emergency Chair authority.
* Executive escalation.
* Electronic approval.
* Emergency committee meeting.
* Incident command structure.

Emergency authority should be limited to situations requiring immediate action.

---

# 58. Emergency Decision Documentation

Emergency decisions should be documented even when normal governance procedures cannot be followed.

At minimum, the record should capture:

* What happened.
* Why immediate action was required.
* Who made the decision.
* What authority was used.
* What action was taken.
* Who was notified.
* What follow-up review is required.

---

# 59. Post-Emergency Governance Review

Emergency decisions should be reviewed afterward.

The review should determine whether:

* The decision was justified.
* The correct authority acted.
* Governance procedures were followed as far as practical.
* Additional controls are needed.
* The charter should be updated.

This ensures that emergency mechanisms do not become informal permanent practices.

---

# 60. Governance Performance Responsibilities

The charter should establish responsibility for measuring governance effectiveness.

Metrics may include:

* Number of AI systems governed.
* Number of high-risk AI systems.
* Decision cycle time.
* Open governance actions.
* Overdue actions.
* Policy exceptions.
* Risk acceptance.
* Incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Governance maturity.

Performance measurement should focus on effectiveness rather than simply committee activity.

# AI Governance Charter

## Governance Operations, Accountability, Assurance, and Charter Management

The AI Governance Charter should provide enough operational detail to ensure that governance does not remain a theoretical structure. It must establish how the governance body performs its responsibilities, how decisions are controlled, how accountability is maintained, and how the charter itself is kept current as the organization's AI environment evolves.

A mature charter should therefore operate as a **living governance instrument** rather than a static document created once and rarely reviewed.

---

# 61. Governance Responsibilities

The charter should explicitly define the responsibilities of the primary AI governance body.

These responsibilities may include:

* Establishing enterprise AI governance direction.
* Reviewing the AI governance framework.
* Overseeing significant AI risks.
* Reviewing high-risk AI initiatives.
* Monitoring compliance with AI governance requirements.
* Reviewing material exceptions.
* Reviewing significant AI incidents.
* Resolving governance conflicts.
* Escalating matters outside delegated authority.
* Monitoring governance performance.
* Reporting material matters to executive management.

Responsibilities should be specific enough to prevent ambiguity.

---

# 62. Accountability for Governance Outcomes

The governance body should have clearly defined accountability for the outcomes within its mandate.

However, governance accountability should not be confused with operational ownership.

For example:

```text
Governance Body
      ↓
Provides Oversight
      ↓
Challenges and Decides
      ↓
Business / System Owner
      ↓
Implements
      ↓
Operations
      ↓
Maintains Controls
```

The governance body governs and oversees; operational owners remain accountable for operating their AI systems.

---

# 63. AI System Owner Accountability

The charter should recognize the accountability of AI System Owners.

AI System Owners should generally be responsible for:

* Maintaining system documentation.
* Managing identified risks.
* Implementing required controls.
* Monitoring performance.
* Reporting material changes.
* Managing incidents.
* Completing remediation.
* Supporting governance reviews.

Governance approval does not transfer system ownership to the governance committee.

---

# 64. Business Owner Accountability

Where AI supports a business process, the Business Owner should remain accountable for the business outcome.

Responsibilities may include:

* Business justification.
* Business impact assessment.
* Appropriate use of AI.
* Operational effectiveness.
* Business continuity.
* Resource allocation.
* Acceptance of business consequences.

Business ownership should remain distinct from technical ownership.

---

# 65. Security Accountability

The charter should establish the role of cybersecurity and AI security functions.

Security responsibilities may include:

* AI threat assessment.
* Security architecture.
* Security control requirements.
* Vulnerability management.
* AI security monitoring.
* Security incident escalation.
* Security risk challenge.

Security should have sufficient independence to challenge AI deployments where material security concerns exist.

---

# 66. Risk Management Accountability

Risk Management should provide independent oversight of AI risk where applicable.

Responsibilities may include:

* Risk methodology.
* Risk classification.
* Risk aggregation.
* Risk appetite alignment.
* Risk reporting.
* Challenge of risk assessments.
* Risk escalation.

Risk Management should not become the operational owner of every AI risk.

---

# 67. Legal and Compliance Accountability

Legal and Compliance functions should provide guidance regarding:

* Applicable laws.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Regulatory developments.
* Compliance exceptions.
* Legal interpretation.

The charter should establish when Legal or Compliance participation is mandatory.

---

# 68. Privacy Accountability

Where AI involves personal data, privacy governance should have defined responsibilities.

These may include:

* Privacy impact assessments.
* Data protection requirements.
* Privacy risk assessment.
* Data subject considerations.
* Privacy incidents.
* Regulatory privacy obligations.

Privacy responsibilities should be integrated into AI governance rather than treated as an unrelated process.

---

# 69. Data Governance Accountability

Data Governance should support decisions concerning:

* Data quality.
* Data ownership.
* Data classification.
* Data lineage.
* Data usage.
* Data retention.
* Data access.

The charter should define how Data Governance interacts with AI System Owners and Business Owners.

---

# 70. Technology and Architecture Accountability

Technology and Architecture functions may be responsible for:

* AI architecture.
* Technology standards.
* Infrastructure requirements.
* Integration.
* Resilience.
* Scalability.
* Technical lifecycle management.

Architecture governance should be integrated with AI governance for systems with significant technical or enterprise impact.

---

# 71. Procurement Accountability

Procurement should participate in governance where external AI products or services are acquired.

Responsibilities may include:

* Vendor due diligence.
* Contract requirements.
* AI supplier assessment.
* Third-party risk.
* Commercial terms.
* Service requirements.

The charter should ensure that procurement does not introduce AI capabilities outside the governance framework.

---

# 72. Human Resources Accountability

Where AI affects employees, Human Resources may need to participate in governance.

Relevant matters may include:

* Employee-facing AI.
* Workforce analytics.
* AI-assisted employment decisions.
* Employee monitoring.
* AI-related training.
* Workforce impact.

The charter should define when HR review is required.

---

# 73. Internal Audit Relationship

Internal Audit should maintain appropriate independence.

The charter may establish that Internal Audit:

* Provides independent assurance.
* Reviews governance effectiveness.
* Assesses control design and operation.
* Reports significant findings through established audit channels.

Internal Audit should generally not become an operational member responsible for managing AI governance controls.

---

# 74. First-Line, Second-Line, and Third-Line Roles

The charter should align AI governance with the organization's assurance model.

```text
First Line
Business / Technology / AI Operations
        ↓
Own and Manage AI Risk

Second Line
Risk / Security / Privacy / Compliance
        ↓
Challenge and Oversight

Third Line
Internal Audit
        ↓
Independent Assurance
```

This structure helps maintain accountability while preserving appropriate independence.

---

# 75. Governance Authority Boundaries

The charter should establish clear boundaries between governance authority and operational management.

The governance body may:

* Establish requirements.
* Approve defined matters.
* Challenge decisions.
* Escalate risks.
* Require remediation.

It should not unnecessarily:

* Manage day-to-day AI operations.
* Perform engineering tasks.
* Operate security tools.
* Manage individual system configurations.

Governance should remain focused on oversight and decision-making.

---

# 76. Governance Decision Rights

The charter should explicitly identify important decision rights.

Examples include authority to:

* Approve high-risk AI systems.
* Approve defined exceptions.
* Accept risk within delegated limits.
* Require additional controls.
* Restrict AI functionality.
* Escalate material risk.
* Require remediation.
* Suspend an AI activity where authorized.

Decision rights should align with the organization's AI RACI and risk acceptance framework.

---

# 77. Approval Conditions

Governance approvals may be unconditional or conditional.

A conditional approval may state:

> The AI system may proceed provided that the identified security controls are implemented and independently verified before production use.

The charter should require conditions to be:

* Documented.
* Assigned.
* Time-bound.
* Verifiable.

---

# 78. Governance Exceptions

The charter should establish how exceptions to governance requirements are handled.

An exception process should normally include:

1. Requirement being excepted.
2. Business justification.
3. Risk assessment.
4. Compensating controls.
5. Exception owner.
6. Approval authority.
7. Expiration date.
8. Review requirements.

Exceptions should be treated as controlled deviations rather than informal agreements.

---

# 79. Exception Expiration

AI governance exceptions should normally have defined validity periods.

An exception should not remain indefinitely active simply because the organization has become accustomed to it.

Before expiration, the organization should determine whether to:

* Close the exception.
* Remediate the underlying issue.
* Renew the exception.
* Escalate the risk.
* Change the underlying requirement.

---

# 80. Governance Escalation Mechanism

The charter should define how matters outside the authority of the governance body are escalated.

For example:

```text
AI Governance Committee
        ↓
Matter Exceeds Authority
        ↓
Executive Management
        ↓
Decision
        ↓
Board-Level Reporting if Material
```

Escalation criteria should be documented rather than determined informally.

---

# 81. Risk Appetite Alignment

AI governance decisions should be aligned with enterprise risk appetite.

The charter should establish that decisions cannot knowingly exceed approved risk tolerance without appropriate escalation and authorization.

For example:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Within Appetite?
 ↙        ↘
Yes        No
 ↓          ↓
Manage    Escalate
            ↓
     Authorized Decision
```

Risk appetite provides an important boundary for governance decision-making.

---

# 82. Governance Reporting Hierarchy

The charter should establish the reporting relationship between AI governance and senior leadership.

A representative model may be:

```text
AI System Owners
       ↓
AI Governance Function
       ↓
AI Governance Committee
       ↓
Executive Management
       ↓
Board / Board Committee
```

The reporting structure should reflect organizational governance arrangements.

---

# 83. Reporting Frequency

The charter should define expected reporting frequency.

For example:

### Operational Reporting

Monthly or as required.

### Executive Reporting

Quarterly or more frequently for material matters.

### Board Reporting

Quarterly, semi-annually, or as appropriate to enterprise risk.

### Critical Matters

Immediate notification.

The exact frequency should be based on risk and organizational needs.

---

# 84. Immediate Notification

Certain matters should bypass normal reporting cycles.

Immediate notification may be required for:

* Critical AI security incidents.
* Material regulatory issues.
* Significant customer harm.
* Major AI system failure.
* Risk outside enterprise appetite.
* Significant unauthorized AI activity.

The charter should identify who must be notified.

---

# 85. Governance Metrics

The charter should establish responsibility for AI governance metrics.

Metrics may include:

* AI systems under governance.
* High-risk AI systems.
* Governance approvals.
* Exceptions.
* Risk acceptance.
* Open remediation.
* Escalations.
* Incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Decision cycle time.

Metrics should provide meaningful insight into governance effectiveness.

---

# 86. Governance Key Performance Indicators

Where appropriate, the organization may establish KPIs.

Examples include:

* Percentage of AI systems classified.
* Percentage of high-risk systems assessed.
* Percentage of required reviews completed.
* Percentage of governance actions completed on time.
* Percentage of exceptions reviewed before expiration.
* Percentage of material decisions properly documented.

KPIs should encourage effective governance rather than superficial compliance.

---

# 87. Governance Key Risk Indicators

Key Risk Indicators may provide early warning of increasing AI risk.

Examples include:

* Number of high-risk AI systems.
* Increasing unresolved AI risks.
* Growing exception volume.
* Repeated security incidents.
* Increasing overdue remediation.
* Increasing vendor risk.
* Model performance degradation.

KRIs should be monitored against defined thresholds.

---

# 88. Governance Effectiveness Review

The charter should require periodic evaluation of governance effectiveness.

The review should consider:

* Whether governance responsibilities remain appropriate.
* Whether decision rights remain clear.
* Whether escalation mechanisms work.
* Whether committees are effective.
* Whether reporting is useful.
* Whether AI risks are adequately covered.

The review may be performed annually or more frequently when circumstances require.

---

# 89. Charter Review

The charter itself should be reviewed periodically.

A formal review should consider changes in:

* AI strategy.
* Organizational structure.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Technology.
* AI risk profile.
* Governance maturity.
* Business operations.

A charter that no longer reflects the organization can create significant governance gaps.

---

# 90. Charter Change Authority

The charter should define who can approve amendments.

Depending on the organization's structure, amendments may require approval from:

* AI Governance Committee.
* Executive Management.
* Executive Sponsor.
* Board Committee.
* Board.

The authority should be proportionate to the significance of the charter.

---

# 91. Charter Version Control

The charter should be subject to formal document control.

It should contain information such as:

* Version.
* Effective date.
* Owner.
* Approver.
* Review date.
* Change history.
* Classification.

This ensures that governance participants are operating against the current approved version.

---

# 92. Charter Ownership

A named organizational role should own the charter.

The Charter Owner may be responsible for:

* Maintaining the document.
* Coordinating reviews.
* Proposing amendments.
* Ensuring approvals.
* Publishing the current version.
* Retaining historical versions.

Ownership should not remain ambiguous.

---

# 93. Charter Approval

The charter should receive formal approval from the appropriate authority.

Approval should demonstrate that:

* Governance authority is recognized.
* Responsibilities are accepted.
* Decision rights are authorized.
* Reporting relationships are established.

A draft charter should not be treated as an authoritative governance instrument until formally approved.

---

# 94. Effective Date

The charter should specify when it becomes effective.

The effective date establishes when:

* Governance authority begins.
* Responsibilities become applicable.
* Decision rights take effect.
* Reporting requirements become operational.

---

# 95. Review Date

The charter should include a scheduled review date.

A review date ensures that governance remains intentional rather than being maintained indefinitely without reassessment.

A review may also be triggered earlier by material organizational or regulatory change.

# 96. Trigger-Based Charter Review

In addition to periodic review, certain events should automatically trigger reconsideration.

Examples include:

* Major AI regulatory changes.
* Significant AI incidents.
* Major organizational restructuring.
* Introduction of autonomous AI.
* Major AI strategy changes.
* New jurisdictions.
* Significant changes to enterprise risk appetite.
* Major changes to governance responsibilities.

Trigger-based review makes the charter responsive to the operating environment.

# 97. Governance Maturity and Charter Evolution

The charter should evolve as the organization's AI governance capability matures.

An early-stage organization may require:

* Basic governance authority.
* Simple approval processes.
* Limited committee structures.

A mature organization may require:

* Specialized governance councils.
* Detailed decision rights.
* Automated workflows.
* Integrated risk management.
* Advanced metrics.
* Board-level reporting.
* Continuous monitoring.

The charter should support this evolution without creating unnecessary complexity.

Training may cover:

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
