# 03.16 — AI Decision Rights

## Introduction

AI Decision Rights define **who has the authority to make, approve, reject, modify, escalate, or reverse decisions relating to artificial intelligence across the enterprise**.

As AI becomes embedded into business processes, technology platforms, security operations, analytics, customer services, and autonomous workflows, organizations can no longer rely on informal assumptions about who is allowed to make AI-related decisions. Without clearly defined decision rights, organizations may experience delayed approvals, conflicting decisions, unauthorized risk acceptance, weak accountability, or excessive concentration of authority.

AI Decision Rights therefore form a critical component of the AI Governance Operating Model.

They establish the relationship between:

* **Decision authority** — who is authorized to decide.
* **Decision responsibility** — who is responsible for making the decision.
* **Decision accountability** — who remains accountable for the outcome.
* **Decision consultation** — who must provide advice or challenge.
* **Decision notification** — who must be informed.
* **Escalation authority** — who decides when the matter exceeds delegated authority.

A mature AI governance model does not attempt to centralize every AI decision. Instead, it establishes **clear boundaries for delegated authority**, allowing routine decisions to remain with operational teams while ensuring that high-impact, high-risk, strategic, or exceptional decisions receive appropriate governance oversight.

---

# 136. Definition of AI Decision Rights

AI Decision Rights are the formally established authorities assigned to individuals, roles, committees, or governance bodies to make specific AI-related decisions.

A decision right may include authority to:

* Approve.
* Reject.
* Modify.
* Suspend.
* Resume.
* Escalate.
* Accept risk.
* Approve an exception.
* Require remediation.
* Authorize deployment.
* Authorize material changes.
* Retire an AI system.

Decision rights should be explicitly defined rather than inferred from organizational hierarchy.

---

# 137. Purpose of AI Decision Rights

The primary purpose of AI Decision Rights is to ensure that AI decisions are made by the **appropriate authority at the appropriate organizational level**.

A well-designed decision-rights model helps the organization:

* Prevent unauthorized decisions.
* Reduce governance ambiguity.
* Accelerate legitimate AI adoption.
* Establish accountability.
* Prevent excessive centralization.
* Support risk-based governance.
* Improve escalation.
* Reduce decision conflicts.
* Strengthen auditability.
* Improve executive oversight.

Decision rights therefore connect governance structure with actual organizational behavior.

---

# 138. Decision Rights as a Governance Control

Decision rights should be treated as a governance control.

The basic control relationship is:

```text
AI Decision
    ↓
Defined Decision Type
    ↓
Risk / Impact Classification
    ↓
Authorized Decision Maker
    ↓
Required Evidence
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Recorded Accountability
```

The organization should be able to demonstrate that material decisions were made by individuals or bodies with appropriate authority.

---

# 139. Decision Rights and Accountability

Decision rights should always be connected to accountability.

An individual may have authority to approve an AI activity, while another individual remains responsible for operating the AI system.

For example:

```text
Governance Authority
       ↓
Approves AI Use Case
       ↓
Business Owner
       ↓
Owns Business Outcome
       ↓
AI System Owner
       ↓
Operates AI System
```

Approval authority does not automatically transfer operational accountability.

---

# 140. Decision Rights and Responsibility

Responsibility and authority should be aligned.

An individual should not normally be held accountable for a decision when they have no authority to influence or control that decision.

Conversely, individuals with significant authority should have clearly defined accountability.

This principle can be expressed as:

> **Authority should be sufficient to discharge responsibility, and accountability should be proportionate to decision authority.**

---

# 141. Decision Rights and Risk

AI decision rights should be aligned with risk.

A simple model may be:

```text
Low Risk
   ↓
Operational Authority

Moderate Risk
   ↓
Management / Functional Authority

High Risk
   ↓
AI Governance Authority

Critical / Material Risk
   ↓
Executive Authority
```

The exact thresholds should be established by the organization's AI risk framework.

---

# 142. Risk-Based Delegation

Decision authority should generally increase with the potential impact of the AI decision.

For example:

| AI Decision                      | Potential Authority                          |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Low-risk internal AI tool        | System / Business Owner                      |
| Moderate-risk AI deployment      | Functional Management                        |
| High-risk AI system              | AI Governance Committee                      |
| Significant risk exception       | Executive Management                         |
| Risk outside enterprise appetite | Executive / Board authority where applicable |

The objective is not to make every decision senior-management dependent.

---

# 143. Decision Rights Across the AI Lifecycle

Decision rights should exist across the entire AI lifecycle.

Key decision points may include:

1. AI use-case initiation.
2. Business justification.
3. AI classification.
4. Development authorization.
5. Data approval.
6. Security approval.
7. Validation.
8. Production deployment.
9. Material change.
10. Risk acceptance.
11. Incident response.
12. Suspension.
13. Reinstatement.
14. Retirement.

Each decision point should have an identified authority.

---

# 144. AI Use-Case Decision Rights

The organization should define who can authorize an AI use case to proceed beyond the initial concept stage.

The decision may depend on:

* Risk classification.
* Business impact.
* Data sensitivity.
* Regulatory requirements.
* AI autonomy.
* Customer impact.
* Security implications.

Low-risk experimentation may be delegated, while high-impact use cases may require formal governance approval.

---

# 145. AI Classification Decision Rights

AI classification determines the level of governance required.

The organization should establish who can:

* Classify an AI system.
* Validate the classification.
* Challenge the classification.
* Reclassify the system.
* Resolve classification disputes.

Classification should not be controlled solely by the business team seeking approval.

---

# 146. Development Authorization

The organization should define who can authorize development of AI systems.

For low-risk systems, authorization may remain within the business or technology function.

For higher-risk systems, development may require:

* AI governance review.
* Security review.
* Privacy review.
* Risk assessment.
* Legal review.

The authorization level should reflect potential impact.

---

# 147. Data Decision Rights

AI systems frequently depend on significant data decisions.

Decision rights should identify who can authorize:

* Use of specific datasets.
* Sensitive data processing.
* Training data.
* Production data.
* External data.
* Data sharing.
* Data retention.
* Data transfers.

Data ownership and AI system ownership should remain clearly distinguished.

---

# 148. Model Decision Rights

Organizations should establish authority over material model decisions.

These may include:

* Model selection.
* Model approval.
* Model validation.
* Model replacement.
* Model retraining.
* Model version changes.
* Model retirement.

Where model risk is material, independent validation may be required before authorization.

---

# 149. Security Decision Rights

AI security decision rights should identify who can:

* Approve AI security architecture.
* Approve security exceptions.
* Accept security risk within authority.
* Require additional security controls.
* Block deployment due to security concerns.
* Suspend an AI system during a security incident.

Security decision rights should be sufficiently strong to prevent material security concerns from being overridden without appropriate authority.

---

# 150. Privacy Decision Rights

Privacy-related AI decisions should have clearly defined authority.

Examples include:

* Approval of personal data use.
* Privacy impact assessment approval.
* Sensitive-data processing.
* Data subject considerations.
* Privacy exceptions.
* Privacy risk escalation.

Privacy authority should operate within applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

---

# 151. AI Deployment Decision Rights

Production deployment is one of the most important AI governance decision points.

The organization should establish who can authorize deployment based on:

* Risk classification.
* Security status.
* Privacy status.
* Testing results.
* Business readiness.
* Residual risk.
* Required approvals.

Deployment authority should not automatically belong to the development team.

---

# 152. Material Change Decision Rights

The organization should define who decides whether a proposed AI change is material.

Examples include:

* Major model replacement.
* Significant training-data change.
* New geographic deployment.
* New user population.
* New sensitive data.
* Increased autonomy.
* New external integration.

Material changes may require renewed governance approval.

---

# 153. AI Risk Acceptance Decision Rights

Risk acceptance authority should be explicitly delegated.

The organization should define:

* Which roles can accept low risk.
* Which roles can accept moderate risk.
* Which committees can accept high risk.
* Which risks require executive approval.
* Which risks cannot be accepted.

No individual should assume risk acceptance authority merely because they own the AI system.

---

# 154. AI Exception Decision Rights

The organization should distinguish between **risk acceptance** and **policy exception approval**.

Risk acceptance answers:

> Is the residual risk acceptable?

Exception approval answers:

> Is deviation from the established requirement authorized?

Both may require different authorities.

---

# 155. AI Suspension Decision Rights

The organization should identify who can suspend an AI system.

Suspension authority may be granted to:

* AI System Owners.
* Security Operations.
* Incident Response.
* AI Governance.
* Executive Management.

Emergency suspension authority may need to be broader than normal operating authority.

---

# 156. AI Reinstatement Decision Rights

The authority to resume an AI system should also be defined.

Reinstatement may require evidence that:

* The root cause has been addressed.
* Security controls are restored.
* Risk is acceptable.
* Required testing is complete.
* Governance conditions are satisfied.

The person who suspended the system should not necessarily be the sole authority for reinstatement.

---

# 157. AI Retirement Decision Rights

Retirement decisions should identify who can formally decommission an AI system.

The decision should consider:

* Business dependency.
* Data retention.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Security implications.
* Contractual obligations.
* Replacement systems.
* Residual risk.

Retirement should be recorded as a formal lifecycle decision.

---

# 158. Decision Rights for AI Agents

Autonomous AI agents introduce additional decision-right requirements.

The organization should establish boundaries for:

* Autonomous actions.
* Human approvals.
* Financial transactions.
* Privileged actions.
* External communications.
* System modifications.
* Data access.
* Security-sensitive actions.

For example:

```text
Low-Impact Action
       ↓
AI Agent Authorized

Material Action
       ↓
Human Approval

High-Risk Action
       ↓
Governance Authorization

Prohibited Action
       ↓
Technically Blocked
```

AI agents should not be granted authority beyond their explicitly approved operating boundaries.

---

# 159. Human Decision Rights

Human decision-makers should retain clearly defined authority where human oversight is required.

The organization should identify:

* Who reviews AI recommendations.
* Who can reject AI recommendations.
* Who can override AI outputs.
* Who can suspend automated decisions.
* Who is accountable for final decisions.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely procedural.

---

# 160. AI Decision Rights Matrix

A formal AI Decision Rights Matrix should document important decision authorities.

A simplified example:

| Decision              | Business Owner | System Owner | Security | Risk | AI Governance | Executive |
| --------------------- | -------------: | -----------: | -------: | ---: | ------------: | --------: |
| Low-risk use case     |              A |            R |        C |    C |             I |         I |
| High-risk use case    |              C |            R |        C |    C |             A |         I |
| Security exception    |              I |            R |        A |    C |             C |         I |
| Risk acceptance       |              C |            R |        C |   A* |             C |        A* |
| Production deployment |              A |            R |        C |    C |            A* |         I |
| Material change       |              A |            R |        C |    C |            A* |         I |
| Emergency suspension  |              I |            R |        A |    C |             A |        A* |

**R = Responsible**
**A = Accountable / Authorized**
**C = Consulted**
**I = Informed**

The actual matrix should be tailored to the organization's governance structure.

---

# 161. Single-Point Decision Accountability

Material decisions should have a clearly identifiable final decision authority.

Multiple people may participate in a decision, but the organization should avoid situations where:

> "Everyone was involved, but nobody was accountable."

A decision should therefore identify the final authority responsible for the outcome.

---

# 162. Collective Decision-Making

Some AI decisions should be made collectively.

This may be appropriate when decisions involve:

* Significant business impact.
* Multiple risk domains.
* Regulatory implications.
* Security concerns.
* Ethical considerations.
* Enterprise-wide consequences.

Collective decision-making should still identify the final authority or decision mechanism.

---

# 163. Veto Rights

Certain functions may require formally defined veto or blocking authority.

Examples may include:

* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Compliance.

Veto rights should be narrowly defined and documented.

The charter should explain:

* What can be blocked.
* Under what circumstances.
* How disagreements are resolved.
* Who can override the veto, if anyone.

---

# 164. Decision Escalation

When a decision exceeds delegated authority, the matter should be escalated rather than informally approved.

For example:

```text
Decision Requested
       ↓
Within Authority?
   ↙         ↘
 Yes          No
  ↓            ↓
Decide      Escalate
               ↓
       Higher Authority
```

This creates a controlled relationship between delegation and escalation.

---

# 165. Decision Rights and AI Governance RACI

The AI Decision Rights model should complement the AI Governance RACI.

The RACI identifies:

* Who performs work.
* Who is accountable.
* Who is consulted.
* Who is informed.

Decision rights go further by identifying:

> **Who has the actual authority to make the decision.**

A person can be Responsible for preparing an approval package without having authority to approve it.

---

# 166. Decision Rights and AI Governance Charter

The AI Governance Charter should establish the overarching authority for decision rights.

The charter should identify:

* Governance bodies.
* Delegated authorities.
* Decision boundaries.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Risk acceptance authority.
* Exception authority.

Detailed decision matrices can then operationalize these principles.

---

# 167. Decision Rights and AI Policy

AI policies establish mandatory requirements, while decision rights establish who can make governance decisions concerning those requirements.

For example:

**Policy:**

> High-risk AI systems must undergo formal governance review.

**Decision Right:**

> The AI Governance Committee has authority to approve high-risk AI systems within its delegated mandate.

This distinction prevents policy requirements from being confused with decision authority.

---

# 168. Decision Rights and AI Risk Appetite

Decision rights should be aligned with risk appetite.

A decision-maker should not be able to approve risk beyond their delegated authority simply because they own the business process.

For example:

```text
Risk Within Appetite
        ↓
Delegated Authority

Risk Outside Appetite
        ↓
Escalation

Risk Material to Enterprise
        ↓
Executive / Board Oversight
```

This creates an explicit boundary between operational authority and enterprise risk governance.

# Decision Authority, Delegation, Escalation, and Governance Boundaries

# 169. Decision Rights by Organizational Level

AI decision rights should be distributed across appropriate organizational levels.

A practical model may include:

### Operational Level

Responsible for routine, low-risk decisions.

Examples:

* Configuration changes.
* Routine monitoring.
* Approved AI use within established boundaries.
* Minor operational adjustments.

### Management Level

Responsible for decisions involving greater business or operational impact.

Examples:

* Moderate-risk deployments.
* Resource allocation.
* Significant process changes.
* Operational exceptions within delegated limits.

### Governance Level

Responsible for material AI governance decisions.

Examples:

* High-risk AI systems.
* Significant exceptions.
* Cross-functional risks.
* Material control deficiencies.

### Executive Level

Responsible for enterprise-level decisions.

Examples:

* Major AI investments.
* Material risks outside appetite.
* Significant strategic changes.
* Major regulatory exposure.

### Board Level

Responsible for matters requiring Board oversight.

Examples:

* Enterprise AI strategy.
* Material AI-related risk.
* Significant stakeholder impact.
* Major governance failures.

---

# 170. Principle of Subsidiarity

AI decisions should be made at the **lowest competent level of authority** capable of making the decision safely and effectively.

This principle prevents unnecessary centralization.

For example:

```text id="v5m2qa"
Routine Decision
      ↓
Operational Owner

Material Decision
      ↓
Management

High-Risk Decision
      ↓
Governance Body

Enterprise Decision
      ↓
Executive Management

Board-Level Matter
      ↓
Board
```

The lowest competent authority should not be confused with the lowest organizational position. The decision-maker must possess sufficient expertise, authority, and accountability.

---

# 171. Avoiding Excessive Centralization

Excessive centralization can create:

* Approval bottlenecks.
* Slow AI adoption.
* Committee overload.
* Excessive administrative work.
* Reduced business accountability.

AI governance should therefore avoid requiring senior-level approval for decisions that can safely be delegated.

---

# 172. Avoiding Excessive Delegation

The opposite problem is excessive delegation.

Delegating too much authority can result in:

* Inconsistent decisions.
* Uncontrolled risk acceptance.
* Policy exceptions without oversight.
* Fragmented AI governance.
* Regulatory exposure.

The organization should establish clear boundaries around delegated authority.

---

# 173. Delegation Principles

Delegation should follow several principles:

1. Authority should be explicit.
2. Authority should have defined limits.
3. Delegation should be documented.
4. Delegates should have appropriate competence.
5. Material matters should remain escalatable.
6. Delegation should be periodically reviewed.
7. Delegated authority should not override mandatory requirements.

---

# 174. Competence-Based Authority

Decision authority should consider the competence of the decision-maker.

Relevant competence may include:

* AI knowledge.
* Business knowledge.
* Risk management.
* Cybersecurity.
* Privacy.
* Legal requirements.
* Regulatory knowledge.
* Operational experience.

Authority without competence creates governance risk.

---

# 175. Role-Based Decision Rights

Decision rights should normally be assigned to **organizational roles**, not only named individuals.

For example:

> Chief Information Security Officer

rather than:

> John Smith.

This ensures that authority continues when personnel change.

---

# 176. Named Delegates

Where necessary, organizations may identify named delegates.

A named delegate may exercise authority when:

* The primary authority is unavailable.
* An emergency occurs.
* The authority is formally delegated.
* The delegate meets defined competency requirements.

Delegation should be recorded.

---

# 177. Temporary Decision Authority

Temporary authority may be granted for specific circumstances.

Examples include:

* Organizational restructuring.
* Emergency response.
* Major AI transformation programs.
* Absence of a key decision-maker.
* Temporary governance arrangements.

Temporary authority should have:

* Scope.
* Start date.
* End date.
* Conditions.
* Reporting requirements.

---

# 178. Expiration of Delegated Authority

Delegated decision rights should not remain active indefinitely without review.

The organization should periodically confirm:

* Whether delegation remains necessary.
* Whether the delegate remains qualified.
* Whether organizational responsibilities changed.
* Whether risk thresholds changed.

Expired delegations should be formally removed.

---

# 179. Decision Rights and Organizational Structure

Decision rights should reflect the organization's actual operating model.

A centralized AI organization may assign greater authority to a central AI Governance Committee.

A federated organization may delegate more authority to business units while retaining enterprise-level oversight.

The decision-rights model should therefore reflect how AI is actually managed.

---

# 180. Centralized AI Governance Model

In a centralized model:

```text id="w7x9ck"
Enterprise AI Governance
        ↓
Central Governance Authority
        ↓
Business Units
        ↓
AI Systems
```

Advantages may include:

* Consistency.
* Centralized expertise.
* Standardized controls.
* Easier enterprise reporting.

Potential disadvantages include:

* Slower decisions.
* Central bottlenecks.
* Reduced business autonomy.

---

# 181. Federated AI Governance Model

In a federated model:

```text id="f2r6yp"
Enterprise AI Governance
       ↓
 ┌─────┼─────┐
 ↓     ↓     ↓
BU A   BU B   BU C
 ↓     ↓     ↓
AI     AI     AI
```

Business units receive delegated authority while remaining subject to enterprise requirements.

This can improve agility but requires strong standards and oversight.

---

# 182. Hybrid Decision Rights Model

A hybrid model combines centralized oversight with delegated operational authority.

For example:

```text id="r9k3bx"
Enterprise Governance
        ↓
Defines Standards
Risk Thresholds
Decision Boundaries
        ↓
Business Units
        ↓
Make Delegated Decisions
        ↓
Escalate Material Matters
```

This is often appropriate for large enterprises with diverse AI use cases.

---

# 183. Decision Rights for Business Units

Business units should understand which AI decisions they can make independently.

Delegated authority may include:

* Low-risk AI adoption.
* Approved AI tools.
* Routine changes.
* Operational monitoring.
* Defined vendor selection.

Business-unit authority should remain subject to enterprise AI governance requirements.

---

# 184. Decision Rights for Corporate Functions

Corporate functions may possess specialized decision rights.

For example:

### Cybersecurity

Security control and security-risk decisions.

### Privacy

Privacy compliance decisions.

### Legal

Legal interpretation and contractual decisions.

### Risk

Enterprise risk assessment and challenge.

### Procurement

Supplier and commercial decisions.

Functional authority should not automatically override enterprise decision rights.

---

# 185. Cross-Functional Decisions

Many AI decisions cannot be assigned to a single function.

For example, deploying a customer-facing generative AI system may involve:

* Business.
* Technology.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Risk.
* Compliance.

The decision-rights framework should establish how cross-functional decisions are resolved.

---

# 186. Functional Review Versus Decision Authority

A function may be required to review an AI initiative without having final approval authority.

For example:

```text id="a2m8vf"
AI System
   ↓
Security Review
   ↓
Privacy Review
   ↓
Risk Review
   ↓
Governance Decision
```

Review participation should not automatically be interpreted as decision authority.

---

# 187. Mandatory Consultation

Certain decisions should require mandatory consultation.

Examples may include:

* High-risk AI.
* Sensitive data.
* Customer-impacting AI.
* Autonomous AI.
* Regulated activities.
* Significant security exposure.

The decision-rights framework should identify mandatory consultation requirements.

---

# 188. Mandatory Notification

Some stakeholders may not have decision authority but must still be informed.

For example:

* Executive management.
* Risk leadership.
* Security leadership.
* Privacy leadership.
* Compliance.
* Board committee.

Notification requirements should be based on materiality and risk.

---

# 189. Decision Preconditions

Certain decisions should not be permitted until defined prerequisites are satisfied.

For example:

```text id="h4z7qw"
Deployment Request
       ↓
Risk Assessment Complete?
       ↓
Security Review Complete?
       ↓
Privacy Review Complete?
       ↓
Required Testing Complete?
       ↓
Residual Risk Accepted?
       ↓
Authorized Decision
```

Decision rights should therefore be connected to governance gates.

---

# 190. Governance Gates

Governance gates are formal decision points where an AI system must meet defined conditions before progressing.

Typical gates may include:

* Concept approval.
* Development authorization.
* Validation approval.
* Production approval.
* Material change approval.
* Retirement approval.

Each gate should have a defined decision authority.

---

# 191. Stage-Gate Decision Rights

A stage-gate model may be represented as:

```text id="m8c2tp"
Idea
 ↓
Gate 1 — Business Approval
 ↓
Development
 ↓
Gate 2 — Risk / Security Review
 ↓
Validation
 ↓
Gate 3 — Governance Approval
 ↓
Production
 ↓
Gate 4 — Operational Review
 ↓
Continued Operation
```

The gates should be proportionate to AI risk.

---

# 192. Decision Rights for Low-Risk AI

Low-risk AI should generally have streamlined decision rights.

Examples may include:

* Internal productivity tools.
* Low-impact content assistance.
* Non-sensitive experimentation.
* Approved enterprise AI services.

Governance should establish predefined boundaries so that these decisions do not require unnecessary committee approval.

---

# 193. Decision Rights for Moderate-Risk AI

Moderate-risk AI may require additional management and functional review.

Examples may include:

* Internal decision-support systems.
* Business analytics.
* Customer-support applications.
* AI systems using moderately sensitive information.

The organization should define the approval authority and mandatory reviews.

---

# 194. Decision Rights for High-Risk AI

High-risk AI should generally require formal governance oversight.

The decision process may include:

* Detailed risk assessment.
* Security assessment.
* Privacy assessment.
* Legal or compliance review.
* Independent validation.
* Governance committee approval.
* Defined monitoring requirements.

The final decision authority should be explicitly documented.

---

# 195. Decision Rights for Prohibited AI

Some AI activities may be prohibited by:

* Law.
* Regulation.
* Corporate policy.
* Security requirements.
* Ethical restrictions.
* Contractual obligations.

Where an activity is prohibited, there should be no delegated authority to approve it.

The decision right is effectively:

> **No approval authority exists unless the underlying prohibition is formally removed by the appropriate authority.**

---

# 196. Risk Acceptance Versus Risk Transfer

Decision rights should distinguish between:

### Risk Acceptance

The organization knowingly accepts the residual risk.

### Risk Transfer

The organization attempts to transfer some consequences through mechanisms such as:

* Insurance.
* Contractual arrangements.
* Supplier agreements.

Risk transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying accountability.

---

# 197. Risk Acceptance Limits

Risk acceptance authority should have explicit limits.

For example:

```text id="u7p3mv"
Low Residual Risk
      ↓
System / Business Owner

Moderate Residual Risk
      ↓
Management

High Residual Risk
      ↓
Governance Committee

Risk Outside Appetite
      ↓
Executive Authority
```

The actual thresholds should be defined by the organization's risk framework.

---

# 198. Decision Rights During AI Incidents

AI incidents may require temporary changes to normal decision rights.

During an incident:

* Security may have emergency containment authority.
* System Owners may have shutdown authority.
* Incident Command may coordinate response.
* Executives may authorize major business decisions.

Emergency powers should be predefined.

---

# 199. Emergency Decision Override

Emergency authority should allow rapid intervention when delay could materially increase harm.

Examples include:

* Suspending an AI service.
* Revoking access.
* Blocking an AI integration.
* Disabling autonomous functionality.
* Isolating affected infrastructure.

Emergency actions should be reviewed after the incident.

---

# 200. Post-Incident Decision Review

After a material incident, the organization should review:

* Who made the decision.
* Whether the authority was appropriate.
* Whether escalation was timely.
* Whether decision rights were clear.
* Whether additional authority is required.
* Whether existing authority should be changed.

This allows decision rights to improve through operational experience.
