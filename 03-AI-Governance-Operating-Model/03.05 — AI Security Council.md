# 03.05 — AI Security Council

## Introduction

An AI Security Council is a specialized governance body responsible for providing enterprise-level oversight and strategic direction for the security of artificial intelligence systems, AI-enabled applications, AI infrastructure, AI data, models, agents, and supporting technologies.

As organizations increasingly integrate AI into business processes, traditional cybersecurity governance alone may not adequately address the unique security characteristics of AI.

AI introduces security considerations involving:

* Models.
* Training and inference data.
* Prompts and instructions.
* Model interfaces.
* AI agents.
* Tool integrations.
* Machine-to-machine interactions.
* Third-party AI services.
* Foundation models.
* AI supply chains.
* Autonomous actions.
* Model outputs.

The AI Security Council provides a mechanism for bringing together cybersecurity, AI engineering, architecture, risk, data, privacy, legal, and business stakeholders to establish a coordinated approach to AI security.

Its primary objective is to ensure that AI is **secure by design, securely deployed, continuously monitored, and appropriately governed throughout its lifecycle**.

---

## Purpose of the AI Security Council

The purpose of the AI Security Council is to establish and maintain enterprise-level oversight of AI security.

The council may be responsible for:

* Establishing AI security strategy.
* Defining AI security principles.
* Reviewing significant AI security risks.
* Establishing security requirements for AI systems.
* Coordinating AI security capabilities.
* Reviewing major AI security incidents.
* Addressing emerging AI-specific threats.
* Overseeing AI security architecture.
* Supporting secure AI adoption.
* Coordinating security requirements for third-party AI services.
* Escalating material AI security risks.

The council should provide strategic security oversight rather than replacing the operational responsibilities of cybersecurity teams.

---

## AI Security as a Specialized Governance Domain

AI security is related to cybersecurity but has characteristics that require specialized consideration.

Traditional cybersecurity primarily protects:

* Networks.
* Systems.
* Applications.
* Endpoints.
* Identity.
* Infrastructure.
* Data.

AI security must additionally consider:

* Model behavior.
* Prompt manipulation.
* Training-data integrity.
* Model integrity.
* Model extraction.
* Adversarial inputs.
* AI-specific supply-chain risks.
* Insecure tool use.
* Excessive AI permissions.
* Autonomous actions.
* Manipulated outputs.

Therefore:

**Cybersecurity Governance + AI-Specific Security Governance = Enterprise AI Security Governance**

The AI Security Council should help ensure that AI-specific security risks are integrated into the organization's broader cybersecurity framework.

# Strategic Role of the AI Security Council

The council should provide strategic direction for protecting AI capabilities throughout their lifecycle.

This includes security considerations during:

**Design → Development → Training → Testing → Deployment → Operation → Monitoring → Change → Retirement**

Security should therefore not be treated as a final approval step.

Instead, it should be incorporated throughout the AI lifecycle.

# AI Security Principles

The AI Security Council should establish principles that guide AI security across the organization.

Important principles may include:

### Secure by Design

Security should be incorporated into AI systems from the beginning.

### Least Privilege

AI systems and agents should receive only the permissions required to perform their intended functions.

### Defense in Depth

AI security should rely on multiple complementary controls.

### Zero Trust

AI components should not automatically be trusted based on network location or system identity alone.

### Data Protection

Sensitive data should be appropriately protected throughout the AI lifecycle.

### Model Integrity

Models and associated artifacts should be protected against unauthorized modification.

### Continuous Monitoring

AI systems should be monitored for security threats and abnormal behavior.

### Human Oversight

High-impact or high-risk AI activities should have appropriate human intervention mechanisms.

### Accountability

AI security responsibilities should be clearly assigned.

### Resilience

Critical AI capabilities should have appropriate recovery and fallback mechanisms.

# Risk-Based AI Security

Security requirements should be proportional to AI risk.

A simple model may be:

**Low Risk → Standard Security Controls**

**Moderate Risk → Enhanced Security Assessment**

**High Risk → Formal Security Review and Additional Controls**

**Critical Risk → Executive-Level Security Oversight**

This prevents organizations from applying identical security requirements to every AI system regardless of risk.

The AI Security Council provides a specialized mechanism for enterprise oversight of AI cybersecurity.

Its role is to ensure that AI systems are:

* Securely designed.
* Properly protected.
* Appropriately authorized.
* Continuously monitored.
* Resilient.
* Tested against relevant threats.

# AI Security Council — Part 2

## Governance Responsibilities and Security Oversight

The AI Security Council should translate the organization's AI security strategy into a structured governance and oversight mechanism. Its responsibilities should extend beyond reviewing individual security issues and should establish consistent expectations for how AI security is managed across the enterprise.

A mature council should provide visibility into AI security risks while ensuring that security responsibilities remain clearly assigned to operational cybersecurity, engineering, architecture, risk, and business functions.

## 1. Establish AI Security Governance Objectives

The council should establish clear objectives for enterprise AI security.

These may include:

* Protecting AI systems and supporting infrastructure.
* Protecting AI-related data.
* Preventing unauthorized model access or modification.
* Reducing AI-specific attack exposure.
* Ensuring secure AI development.
* Protecting AI supply chains.
* Managing AI agent privileges.
* Improving AI security monitoring.
* Supporting AI security incident response.
* Maintaining AI security resilience.

These objectives should align with the organization's broader cybersecurity strategy.

## 2. Define AI Security Governance Scope

The council should establish what technologies and activities fall within AI security governance.

The scope may include:

* Internally developed AI systems.
* Third-party AI services.
* Generative AI.
* Machine learning systems.
* Foundation models.
* AI APIs.
* AI agents.
* AI development environments.
* AI infrastructure.
* AI data pipelines.
* Model repositories.
* AI-enabled applications.
* AI-integrated enterprise systems.

The scope should be broad enough to prevent material AI systems from operating outside security oversight.

## 3. Establish AI Security Classification

AI systems should be classified according to security significance.

Classification factors may include:

* Data sensitivity.
* Business criticality.
* User population.
* External exposure.
* Degree of autonomy.
* Privilege level.
* Regulatory impact.
* Potential business impact.
* Customer impact.
* Dependency on third parties.

A highly privileged AI agent operating enterprise systems should generally receive more rigorous security oversight than an internal AI tool used for low-risk productivity tasks.

## 4. Establish AI Security Baselines

The council should define minimum security requirements that apply to AI systems.

A baseline may include:

* Identity and access management.
* Secure authentication.
* Least privilege.
* Encryption.
* Secure configuration.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Vulnerability management.
* Security testing.
* Secrets management.
* Incident response.
* Backup and recovery.

Additional requirements can be applied to higher-risk AI systems.

## 5. Establish Enhanced Controls for High-Risk AI

High-risk AI systems may require additional controls.

Examples include:

* Formal threat modeling.
* Adversarial testing.
* Penetration testing.
* Model security assessment.
* Enhanced monitoring.
* Human approval.
* Restricted network access.
* Stronger identity controls.
* Additional logging.
* Independent security review.

The council should establish criteria for determining when enhanced controls are mandatory.

## 6. AI Threat Modeling

AI threat modeling should identify threats specific to the system's architecture and intended use.

The assessment may consider:

* Attack surfaces.
* Data flows.
* Model interfaces.
* User inputs.
* External APIs.
* Tools.
* Plugins.
* Agents.
* Credentials.
* Model repositories.
* Third-party dependencies.

Threat modeling should be performed early enough to influence system design.

## 7. Prompt Injection Governance

Prompt injection is a significant consideration for systems that process untrusted instructions or content.

The council should establish security expectations for:

* Direct prompt injection.
* Indirect prompt injection.
* Malicious documents.
* Untrusted web content.
* Retrieved content.
* Tool instructions.

Controls may include:

* Input validation.
* Content isolation.
* Privilege separation.
* Tool restrictions.
* Output validation.
* Human approval.
* Monitoring.

Prompt injection should be treated as an architectural security concern rather than merely a user-input problem.

## 8. Model Integrity

The council should ensure that AI models and associated artifacts are protected from unauthorized modification.

Security considerations include:

* Model provenance.
* Repository access.
* Artifact integrity.
* Version control.
* Cryptographic verification.
* Controlled deployment.
* Change management.

An attacker who modifies a model may influence AI behavior without directly compromising the application infrastructure.

## 9. Training Data Security

Training and fine-tuning data should be protected against:

* Unauthorized access.
* Manipulation.
* Poisoning.
* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Improper modification.

Controls may include:

* Data access controls.
* Provenance tracking.
* Integrity verification.
* Dataset versioning.
* Data validation.
* Segregation.
* Monitoring.

Data security should be treated as an important component of model security.

## 10. Inference Data Security

Security should also protect data provided to AI systems during operation.

This may include:

* Customer information.
* Employee information.
* Business information.
* Confidential documents.
* Intellectual property.
* Credentials.
* Transaction data.

The council should establish requirements for minimizing, protecting, monitoring, and appropriately handling inference data.

## 11. AI Access Control

Access to AI systems should be based on business and security requirements.

The council should promote:

* Role-based access.
* Attribute-based access where appropriate.
* Least privilege.
* Privileged access management.
* Strong authentication.
* Service identity controls.
* Segregation of duties.

Access should be reviewed periodically.

## 12. AI Agent Authorization

AI agents require explicit authorization boundaries.

The organization should define:

* What systems the agent may access.
* What data it may retrieve.
* What tools it may use.
* What actions it may execute.
* What transactions it may perform.
* Which actions require human approval.

An AI agent should never receive unrestricted access merely because it is considered an internal application.

## 13. Human Approval Controls

Human approval may be required for actions with significant consequences.

Examples include:

* Financial transactions.
* Privileged system changes.
* Customer-impacting decisions.
* Security configuration changes.
* Destructive operations.
* Legal commitments.

Approval thresholds should be risk-based.

## 14. AI Security Testing

AI security testing should be incorporated into the AI lifecycle.

Testing may include:

* Vulnerability assessment.
* Penetration testing.
* Adversarial testing.
* Prompt injection testing.
* Model abuse testing.
* Access-control testing.
* Data leakage testing.
* Tool-use testing.
* Agent behavior testing.

Testing should reflect the actual threat environment rather than relying exclusively on conventional application security testing.

## 15. Red Teaming

High-risk AI systems may require dedicated AI security red teaming.

Red-team activities may attempt to identify:

* Prompt injection weaknesses.
* Data extraction.
* Privilege escalation.
* Model manipulation.
* Tool abuse.
* Unsafe autonomous behavior.
* Sensitive information disclosure.

Results should be documented and remediation tracked.

## 16. AI Vulnerability Management

The council should ensure that AI-specific vulnerabilities can be identified, assessed, prioritized, and remediated.

Potential sources include:

* AI platforms.
* Model frameworks.
* Libraries.
* APIs.
* Infrastructure.
* Applications.
* Third-party providers.

AI vulnerability management should integrate with the organization's existing vulnerability management process where practical.

## 19. AI Security Incident Response

The organization should define how AI security incidents are handled.

Incident response may include:

**Detect → Triage → Contain → Investigate → Eradicate → Recover → Learn**

AI-specific response considerations may include:

* Revoking credentials.
* Restricting model access.
* Blocking malicious prompts.

Potential escalation factors include:

* Compromised credentials.
* Model compromise.

Organizations may occasionally request deviations from established security requirements.

Exceptions should include:

Significant AI systems should undergo appropriate security architecture review.

## 26. AI Security by Design

Security should be incorporated from the earliest stages of AI development.

The council should promote:

**Security Requirements → Threat Modeling → Secure Design → Security Testing → Secure Deployment → Continuous Monitoring**

This is more effective than attempting to add security after an AI system has already been developed.

Where practical, secure configurations should be the default.

Examples include:

* Restricted access.
* Disabled unnecessary tools.
* Minimal permissions.

## 30. Security Governance Operating Principle

The AI Security Council should operate according to the principle:

**Identify → Protect → Detect → Respond → Recover → Improve**

The council's role is to ensure that AI security becomes an integrated enterprise capability rather than a collection of isolated technical controls.

A mature AI Security Council therefore provides the organizational mechanism for connecting **AI strategy, cybersecurity, architecture, engineering, data protection, threat management, incident response, third-party security, and enterprise risk** into a coherent AI security governance model.

# AI Security Council — Part 3

## AI Security Council Operating Model

The AI Security Council requires a formal operating model that converts AI security principles into repeatable governance, oversight, decision-making, and escalation practices.

The council should not function merely as a forum where cybersecurity teams provide periodic updates. It should operate as an active governance mechanism that identifies material AI security risks, establishes security direction, reviews significant security matters, coordinates cross-functional responses, and provides assurance that AI security requirements are being implemented effectively.

## 1. Establish the AI Security Council Charter

The council should operate under a formally approved charter.

The charter should define:

* Purpose.
* Scope.
* Mandate.
* Authority.
* Membership.
* Chair.
* Secretariat.
* Meeting frequency.
* Quorum.
* Decision rights.
* Escalation authority.
* Reporting requirements.
* Record-keeping.
* Review frequency.

The charter should clearly distinguish governance responsibilities from operational cybersecurity responsibilities.

## 2. Define the Council's Authority

The council should have sufficient authority to influence enterprise AI security.

Potential authorities include:

* Establishing AI security principles.
* Recommending security standards.
* Reviewing material AI security risks.
* Requiring security assessments for defined AI systems.
* Reviewing major security exceptions.
* Escalating unresolved security risks.
* Reviewing significant AI security incidents.
* Recommending suspension of unsafe AI capabilities.
* Recommending additional security investment.

The council should not assume authority that belongs to formally designated risk owners, executives, or operational teams.

## 3. Establish Council Membership

Membership should represent the organization's major AI security stakeholders.

Potential permanent members include:

* Chief Information Security Officer or delegate.
* AI security leader.
* Security architecture.
* AI engineering.
* Enterprise architecture.
* Data governance.
* AI governance.
* Enterprise risk.
* Privacy.
* Legal or compliance.
* Technology.
* Business representation.

Specialists may be invited for specific matters.

## 4. Establish the Council Chair

The chair should have sufficient organizational authority to coordinate AI security decisions.

Possible chairs include:

* CISO.
* Deputy CISO.
* Chief AI Security Officer.
* Chief Technology Officer.
* AI governance executive.

The appropriate structure depends on the organization's operating model.

The chair should ensure that security issues receive appropriate executive attention without unnecessarily taking over operational security functions.

## 5. Establish a Council Secretariat

A secretariat can support the council by managing:

* Meeting agendas.
* Decision papers.
* Minutes.
* Action registers.
* Risk registers.
* Security metrics.
* Documentation.
* Follow-up activities.

This creates administrative continuity and improves decision traceability.

Meeting frequency should reflect the organization's AI activity and risk.

Possible models include:

* Monthly standard meetings.
* Quarterly strategic reviews.
* Ad hoc meetings for major incidents.
* Special sessions for major AI deployments.

High-risk organizations may require more frequent oversight.

The council should avoid meetings that exist merely to satisfy a calendar requirement.

# AI Security Council — Part 4

As AI becomes increasingly embedded within enterprise applications, infrastructure, business processes, and autonomous workflows, the AI Security Council must evolve beyond traditional security review activities.

A mature council should provide strategic oversight of the organization's **AI attack surface, security architecture, AI supply chain, autonomous capabilities, resilience, security investments, emerging threats, and long-term security posture**.

The council should ensure that AI security remains aligned with enterprise strategy while continuously adapting to changes in technology and threat environments.

* Plugins.
* Tools.
* Data pipelines.
* Model repositories.
* AI development environments.
* Third-party AI services.
* Cloud AI platforms.

* Agent permissions.
* Available tools.
* Data access.
* Transaction limits.
* Autonomous decisions.
* Human approval.
* Execution boundaries.

**Greater AI Autonomy → Greater Security Control Requirements**
