# 17.14 — AI Incident Severity and Classification

## Part 1 — AI Incident Severity Foundations, Severity Concepts, Impact Dimensions, Classification and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Severity and Classification

AI incident severity and classification provide a structured method for determining the significance, impact, urgency, and response requirements of AI-related incidents.

Severity classification should distinguish:

- what happened;
- what was affected;
- how serious the impact is;
- how broadly the incident propagated;
- how reversible the consequences are;
- how quickly response is required.

Severity classification is an organizational governance mechanism unless a specific law, regulation, contractual requirement, or authoritative framework establishes a mandatory classification method.

### 2. Objective of AI Incident Severity and Classification

The objective is to establish consistent decision criteria for:

- incident prioritization;
- escalation;
- response activation;
- containment;
- executive notification;
- regulatory assessment;
- recovery;
- post-incident review.

Severity should support decisions rather than merely label incidents.

### 3. AI Incident Classification

AI incident classification identifies the nature and characteristics of an incident.

Classification may consider:

- security;
- safety;
- privacy;
- availability;
- integrity;
- fraud;
- abuse;
- operational impact;
- regulatory relevance.

Classification and severity are related but distinct.

### 4. AI Incident Severity

Severity represents the significance of an incident based on defined impact and risk dimensions.

A severity assessment may consider:

```text
IMPACT
  +
SCOPE
  +
DURATION
  +
CRITICALITY
  +
REVERSIBILITY
  +
CONTROL FAILURE
  +
BUSINESS CONSEQUENCE
       |
       v
INCIDENT SEVERITY
```

### 5. AI Incident Severity versus Incident Category

Incident category identifies **what type of incident occurred**.

Severity identifies **how significant the incident is**.

For example:

- a privacy incident may be low, medium, high, or critical;
- a security incident may be low, medium, high, or critical;
- a model failure may have different severity levels depending on its consequences.

### 6. AI Incident Severity versus Incident Impact

Impact is one input to severity.

Severity may additionally consider:

- urgency;
- criticality;
- duration;
- propagation;
- reversibility;
- affected populations;
- control failure;
- regulatory significance.

Therefore:

**Impact ≠ complete severity assessment.**

### 7. AI Incident Criticality

Criticality represents the importance of the affected:

- AI system;
- business service;
- transaction;
- decision;
- process;
- infrastructure;
- population.

A highly critical AI system can make an incident more significant even when the immediate technical impact appears limited.

### 8. AI Incident Materiality

Materiality considers whether an incident is sufficiently significant to require enhanced organizational attention.

Materiality may consider:

- financial consequences;
- operational disruption;
- customer impact;
- safety consequences;
- privacy consequences;
- regulatory implications;
- strategic importance.

Internal materiality thresholds should be documented.

### 9. AI Incident Impact Dimension

AI incident impact should be assessed across multiple dimensions rather than relying on a single severity indicator.

Potential dimensions include:

- confidentiality;
- integrity;
- availability;
- privacy;
- safety;
- financial;
- operational;
- regulatory;
- customer;
- reputational impact.

### 10. AI Confidentiality Impact

Confidentiality impact concerns unauthorized:

- disclosure;
- access;
- exposure;
- retrieval;
- inference;
- extraction

of protected information.

AI-specific examples may include:

- model secrets exposed through an AI interface;
- confidential documents returned to an unauthorized user;
- sensitive prompts exposed;
- protected information revealed through model output.

### 11. AI Integrity Impact

Integrity impact concerns unauthorized or incorrect alteration of:

- data;
- model artifacts;
- configurations;
- transactions;
- outputs;
- decisions;
- system state.

Integrity impact should distinguish accidental corruption from deliberate manipulation where evidence supports the distinction.

### 12. AI Availability Impact

Availability impact concerns disruption or loss of access to:

- AI services;
- models;
- inference infrastructure;
- datasets;
- APIs;
- supporting systems.

Availability severity should consider both duration and business criticality.

### 13. AI Privacy Impact

Privacy impact concerns consequences involving personal data or individuals' privacy interests.

Potential considerations include:

- number of affected individuals;
- type of information;
- sensitivity;
- exposure;
- unauthorized processing;
- potential harm.

Privacy impact should not automatically be treated as equivalent to a legally reportable personal-data breach.

### 14. AI Safety Impact

Safety impact concerns the possibility that an AI incident contributes to:

- physical harm;
- unsafe system behavior;
- dangerous instructions;
- unsafe decisions;
- loss of safety controls.

Safety impact can exist even where there is no cybersecurity compromise.

### 15. AI Financial Impact

Financial impact may include:

- direct loss;
- attempted loss;
- transaction exposure;
- recovery cost;
- investigation cost;
- operational cost;
- customer compensation;
- remediation cost.

Financial impact should be distinguished from financial risk that has not yet materialized.

### 16. AI Operational Impact

Operational impact concerns disruption to:

- business processes;
- customer services;
- internal operations;
- production systems;
- decision workflows.

Operational severity should consider both duration and criticality.

### 17. AI Regulatory Impact

Regulatory impact concerns potential implications for:

- regulated activities;
- regulatory obligations;
- supervisory attention;
- required assessments;
- possible notification duties.

Regulatory relevance does not automatically establish a regulatory violation.

### 18. AI Customer Impact

Customer impact may include:

- service disruption;
- financial loss;
- incorrect decisions;
- privacy consequences;
- account restrictions;
- exposure to fraudulent activity.

### 19. AI Reputational Impact

Reputational impact concerns potential effects on:

- customer trust;
- business relationships;
- public confidence;
- institutional credibility.

Reputational impact should be assessed using documented criteria rather than subjective assumptions.

### 20. AI Human Impact

AI incidents may affect individuals beyond direct financial or operational consequences.

Potential considerations include:

- physical harm;
- psychological or social consequences;
- loss of access;
- discrimination;
- privacy effects;
- decision-making consequences.

Assessment should remain evidence-based.

### 21. AI Incident Scope

Scope identifies the extent of the incident.

Scope may include:

- one user;
- one transaction;
- one account;
- one model;
- one application;
- one business unit;
- multiple systems;
- enterprise-wide exposure.

### 22. AI Incident Blast Radius

Blast radius represents the potential or actual extent to which an incident can propagate.

```text id="m7q3vx"
SINGLE USER
    |
    v
ACCOUNT
    |
    v
APPLICATION
    |
    v
BUSINESS PROCESS
    |
    v
BUSINESS UNIT
    |
    v
ENTERPRISE
    |
    v
EXTERNAL ECOSYSTEM
```

Blast radius may be larger than the impact observed at the time of detection.

### 23. AI Incident Duration

Duration measures how long the incident existed or remained capable of producing harmful effects.

Duration may be:

- known;
- estimated;
- unknown.

Unknown duration should be explicitly recorded rather than silently treated as zero.

### 24. AI Incident Persistence

Persistence concerns whether the incident continues to exist after initial detection.

Examples include:

- compromised credentials remaining active;
- malicious model modifications remaining deployed;
- poisoned datasets continuing to influence systems;
- fraudulent instructions remaining in knowledge repositories.

### 25. AI Incident Propagation

Propagation concerns movement or amplification of an incident across:

- systems;
- accounts;
- models;
- users;
- business processes;
- suppliers;
- external channels.

Propagation can increase severity even when the original incident was limited.

### 26. AI Incident Recurrence

Recurrence occurs when materially similar incidents happen again.

Recurrence may indicate:

- unresolved root cause;
- inadequate controls;
- ineffective remediation;
- repeated attacker activity;
- systemic weakness.

### 27. AI Incident Reversibility

Reversibility measures how easily the consequences of an incident can be undone.

Examples:

- reversible configuration change;
- reversible account restriction;
- reversible transaction;
- irreversible financial transfer;
- irreversible physical consequence.

Lower reversibility generally increases the significance of an incident.

### 28. AI Incident Detectability

Detectability describes how readily an incident can be identified using available controls.

An incident that is difficult to detect may remain active longer and therefore require different risk treatment.

### 29. AI Incident Containability

Containability measures the organization's ability to limit the incident.

Factors may include:

- isolation capability;
- account suspension;
- model rollback;
- transaction blocking;
- network segmentation;
- human intervention.

### 30. AI Incident Recoverability

Recoverability describes the organization's ability to restore affected systems or processes to an appropriately trusted state.

Recoverability depends on:

- backups;
- trusted configurations;
- alternative systems;
- recovery procedures;
- trained personnel;
- validated restoration.

### 31. AI Incident Urgency

Urgency describes how quickly action must be taken to prevent escalation or reduce harm.

Urgency may be increased by:

- active exploitation;
- ongoing financial loss;
- safety risk;
- rapid propagation;
- irreversible transactions;
- widespread exposure.

Urgency and severity should not automatically be treated as identical.

### 32. AI Incident Escalation Threshold

An escalation threshold defines when an incident requires transfer to a higher level of authority.

Possible escalation triggers include:

- critical business impact;
- material financial loss;
- safety impact;
- widespread customer impact;
- significant privacy implications;
- potential regulatory significance.

### 33. AI Incident Severity Level

Organizations may establish discrete severity levels.

For example:

```text id="b4m8qs"
SEV-1  Critical
SEV-2  High
SEV-3  Medium
SEV-4  Low
```

The terminology and number of levels should be defined by the organization's incident-management framework.

These labels are internal governance constructs unless an external authority specifies a classification scheme.

### 34. AI Critical Incident

A critical incident is an incident meeting the organization's highest severity criteria.

Potential characteristics include:

- significant safety consequences;
- major financial impact;
- widespread service disruption;
- extensive data exposure;
- systemic compromise;
- severe impact on critical operations.

### 35. AI High-Severity Incident

A high-severity incident may materially affect:

- important business processes;
- customers;
- financial transactions;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety.

It generally requires accelerated response and management attention.

### 36. AI Medium-Severity Incident

A medium-severity incident may have meaningful but contained consequences.

Examples may include:

- limited business disruption;
- localized unauthorized activity;
- contained model failure;
- limited customer impact.

### 37. AI Low-Severity Incident

A low-severity incident generally has:

- limited scope;
- limited impact;
- strong containment;
- high reversibility.

Low severity does not mean the incident should necessarily be ignored.

### 38. AI Incident Severity Override

Certain impact dimensions may justify overriding a calculated severity.

For example, an otherwise limited incident may receive elevated treatment when it involves:

- safety;
- critical infrastructure;
- highly sensitive information;
- irreversible financial activity;
- privileged systems.

### 39. AI Safety Severity Override

Organizations may establish safety-specific escalation criteria where an incident creates potential physical or safety-related harm.

A safety override is an internal governance mechanism unless a specific legal or regulatory framework establishes mandatory thresholds.

### 40. AI Privacy Severity Override

Privacy-related incidents may require enhanced assessment where they involve:

- highly sensitive information;
- large populations;
- vulnerable individuals;
- significant exposure;
- potentially serious consequences.

The internal severity level should not be represented as equivalent to a statutory breach or notification threshold.

### 41. AI Security Severity Override

Security severity may be elevated where an incident involves:

- privileged access;
- active exploitation;
- credential compromise;
- persistent access;
- model compromise;
- large-scale data exposure.

### 42. AI Fraud Severity Override

Fraud severity may require enhanced treatment where an incident involves:

- high-value transactions;
- multiple accounts;
- systemic payment exposure;
- executive impersonation;
- widespread customer targeting;
- irreversible transactions.

### 43. AI Incident Multi-Dimensional Severity

A single severity score can hide important differences between impact dimensions.

Organizations should therefore retain the underlying dimensions.

### 44. AI Incident Severity Confidence

Severity should include confidence where information is incomplete.

Examples:

- confirmed high severity;
- probable high severity;
- provisional high severity;
- severity under investigation.

Confidence and severity are separate attributes.

### 45. AI Incident Provisional Classification

Early incident classification may be provisional because facts are still developing.

The classification should be updated as evidence becomes available.

### 46. AI Incident Reclassification

Reclassification may occur when:

- impact increases;
- scope expands;
- new evidence appears;
- containment fails;
- regulatory relevance changes;
- affected populations increase.

Changes should be documented.

### 47. AI Incident Classification Evidence

Severity decisions should be supported by evidence such as:

- logs;
- transaction records;
- model telemetry;
- authentication records;
- investigation results;
- customer reports;
- system observations.

### 48. AI Incident Severity Decision Record

Material severity decisions should record:

- classification;
- severity;
- evidence;
- assumptions;
- decision authority;
- timestamp;
- rationale;
- subsequent changes.

### 49. AI Incident Severity Governance Principle

AI incident severity should be:

- evidence-based;
- consistent;
- explainable;
- risk-sensitive;
- reviewable;
- adaptable as facts change.

The classification framework should distinguish internal governance criteria from external legal or regulatory thresholds.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Severity and Classification

**AI incident severity should represent the significance of an incident using defined, evidence-based impact and risk dimensions while preserving the distinction between internal severity classification and external legal, regulatory, contractual, or supervisory requirements.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- incident category ≠ incident severity;
- severity ≠ impact alone;
- impact ≠ root cause;
- criticality ≠ severity;
- urgency ≠ severity;
- scope ≠ blast radius;
- detectability ≠ severity;
- containment ≠ recoverability;
- recoverability ≠ recovery;
- provisional classification ≠ final classification;
- internal severity ≠ statutory severity;
- regulatory relevance ≠ regulatory violation;
- regulatory assessment ≠ automatic notification obligation;
- contractual classification ≠ legal classification;
- internal governance control ≠ statutory requirement.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
