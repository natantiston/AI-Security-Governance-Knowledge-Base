# 17.01 — AI Incident Management Foundations

## Part 1 — AI Incident Management Foundations, Incident Concepts, AI Failure, Response Boundaries and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Management

AI Incident Management is the organizational capability for identifying, assessing, responding to, containing, recovering from, documenting, and learning from incidents involving artificial intelligence systems.

AI incidents may involve security compromise, privacy violations, unsafe behavior, model failure, data corruption, malicious manipulation, operational disruption, fraud, abuse, or other events that create actual or potential harm.

AI incident management extends conventional incident response because AI systems can exhibit probabilistic, adaptive, opaque, and data-dependent behavior.

---

### 2. Objective of AI Incident Management

The objective of AI Incident Management is to ensure that AI-related incidents are:

* Detected promptly.
* Classified consistently.
* Assessed according to impact and severity.
* Escalated to appropriate stakeholders.
* Contained effectively.
* Eradicated where applicable.
* Recovered safely.
* Investigated using reliable evidence.
* Reported when required.
* Used to improve controls and resilience.

The objective is not merely to restore technical availability.

An AI incident may require behavioral validation before the system is considered safe to return to service.

---

### 3. AI Incident

An AI incident is an event or series of events involving an AI system that results in, or creates a credible potential for, adverse effects on security, privacy, safety, integrity, availability, reliability, compliance, operations, or affected individuals or organizations.

Examples include:

* Compromised model artifacts.
* Unauthorized model changes.
* Prompt-injection attacks.
* Data poisoning.
* Sensitive-data exposure.
* Harmful model behavior.
* Material hallucination.
* AI-enabled fraud.
* Deepfake misuse.
* Unauthorized AI system use.
* Model service disruption.
* Failure of AI-dependent business processes.

---

### 4. AI Incident versus AI Event

An **AI event** is an observable occurrence associated with an AI system.

An **AI incident** is an event or combination of events that meets defined organizational criteria for incident treatment.

For example:

```text
AI Event
   |
   v
Observable anomaly
   |
   v
Initial assessment
   |
   +---- No material impact
   |          |
   |          v
   |      Event record
   |
   +---- Credible impact/risk
              |
              v
         AI Incident
```

Organizations should define objective criteria for determining when an event becomes an incident.

---

### 5. AI Security Incident

An AI security incident is an AI-related incident involving compromise or attempted compromise of confidentiality, integrity, availability, authenticity, accountability, or security controls.

Examples include:

* Model theft.
* Model tampering.
* Prompt injection.
* Credential compromise.
* Unauthorized API use.
* Malicious model modification.
* Supply-chain compromise.
* Data exfiltration.
* Unauthorized tool execution.

AI security incidents should be integrated with the organization's broader cybersecurity incident-response capability.

---

### 6. AI Safety Incident

An AI safety incident involves AI behavior or system conditions that create actual or potential harm to people, property, operations, or other protected interests.

Examples may include:

* Unsafe automated recommendations.
* Unsafe autonomous actions.
* Incorrect high-impact decisions.
* Failure of safety controls.
* Dangerous model behavior.
* Unsafe agent execution.

AI safety incidents should not automatically be treated as conventional cybersecurity incidents.

They may require participation from:

* AI governance.
* Product owners.
* Safety specialists.
* Legal.
* Compliance.
* Risk management.
* Business leadership.

---

### 7. AI Privacy Incident

An AI privacy incident involves unauthorized or inappropriate access to, disclosure of, processing of, retention of, or other handling of personal data through an AI system.

Examples include:

* Prompt disclosure of personal information.
* Unauthorized training-data use.
* Data leakage through model output.
* Exposure through logs.
* Improper data retention.
* Unauthorized third-party processing.
* Sensitive information retrieval.

Privacy incident handling should connect with the organization's privacy and data-breach processes.

---

### 8. AI Model Failure

AI model failure occurs when an AI model does not perform its intended function within defined operational, security, safety, quality, or reliability boundaries.

Examples include:

* Significant accuracy degradation.
* Unexpected output behavior.
* Model drift.
* Broken classification.
* Invalid predictions.
* Unsafe recommendations.
* Failure after model updates.
* Failure caused by corrupted dependencies.

Model failure does not necessarily mean that the model was maliciously compromised.

---

### 9. AI Operational Incident

An AI operational incident is an incident affecting the availability, performance, reliability, configuration, integration, or operation of an AI service.

Examples include:

* AI API outage.
* Inference service failure.
* GPU infrastructure failure.
* Model-serving failure.
* Retrieval system outage.
* Vector database failure.
* Dependency outage.
* Configuration error.
* Capacity exhaustion.

Operational incidents may become security, safety, privacy, or business-continuity incidents depending on their consequences.

### 13. AI Incident Identification

Incident identification determines whether an observed event is sufficiently relevant to enter the organization's incident-management process.

Identification should establish:

* Affected AI system.
* Affected model.
* Affected data.
* Affected users.
* Event time.
* Detection time.
* Initial impact.
* Potential impact.
* Known attack vector.
* Known failure mode.
* Initial severity.
* Relevant dependencies.

Early identification may be incomplete.

Incident records should therefore distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions and hypotheses.

### 14. AI Incident Triage

AI incident triage is the rapid assessment of an incident to determine its significance, scope, urgency, and appropriate response.

Triage should consider:

* Security impact.
* Privacy impact.
* Safety impact.
* Operational impact.
* Financial impact.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Customer impact.
* Reputational impact.
* Number of affected systems.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Model criticality.
* Business criticality.
* Potential for continued harm.

Triage should prioritize containment of material harm while preserving evidence.

### 27. AI Incident Containment

Containment limits the ongoing impact of an incident.

Potential containment actions include:

* Isolating an AI service.
* Blocking an API.
* Revoking credentials.
* Disabling tools.
* Restricting model access.
* Disabling autonomous actions.
* Blocking affected data flows.
* Quarantining model artifacts.
* Removing compromised dependencies.
* Switching to a fallback model.
* Restricting affected users.

Containment should be proportionate to the incident and should consider evidence preservation.

---

### 28. AI Incident Eradication

Eradication removes the underlying cause or malicious condition responsible for an incident.

Examples include:

* Removing malicious software.
* Replacing compromised model artifacts.
* Removing poisoned datasets.
* Revoking compromised credentials.
* Correcting vulnerable configurations.
* Patching affected components.
* Removing unauthorized integrations.
* Eliminating persistence mechanisms.

Eradication should not be assumed merely because the immediate symptom disappears.

---

### 29. AI Incident Recovery

Recovery restores affected AI capabilities to an acceptable operational state.

Recovery may involve:

* Restoring infrastructure.
* Restoring trusted model artifacts.
* Restoring datasets.
* Rebuilding environments.
* Reissuing credentials.
* Reconfiguring integrations.
* Restoring service dependencies.
* Switching from fallback systems.
* Revalidating model behavior.

AI recovery should include technical and behavioral validation where relevant.

---

### 30. AI Incident Recovery Validation

Recovery validation determines whether the recovered AI system is sufficiently trustworthy to return to service.

Validation may include:

* Integrity verification.
* Configuration validation.
* Security testing.
* Model evaluation.
* Regression testing.
* Safety testing.
* Privacy validation.
* Dependency verification.
* Access-control verification.
* Monitoring verification.

A restored system should not automatically be considered safe merely because it is operational.

---

### 31. AI Incident Closure

Incident closure occurs when defined closure criteria have been satisfied.

Closure criteria may include:

* Containment completed.
* Root cause understood sufficiently.
* Required remediation completed.
* Recovery validated.
* Residual risk assessed.
* Required notifications addressed.
* Evidence preserved.
* Documentation completed.
* Corrective actions assigned.

Closure should not mean that every underlying risk has disappeared.

Residual risk may remain and should be formally managed.

### 49. AI Incident Management Governance Principle

AI incident management should be based on the principle that **AI incidents require lifecycle-based management across security, safety, privacy, operations, resilience, and governance domains**.

The organization should be capable of moving from:

```text
Detection
   ↓
Assessment
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Containment
   ↓
Recovery
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Learning
   ↓
Improvement
```

Incident management should remain risk-based and proportionate to the nature and consequences of the incident.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Management Foundations

The foundational principle is:

**An AI system is not considered resilient merely because it can detect or recover from a technical failure; resilience requires the ability to identify material AI incidents, contain harmful behavior, preserve evidence, recover trusted capabilities, validate restored behavior, communicate appropriately, and improve controls based on lessons learned.**

Key distinctions:

* AI event ≠ AI incident.
* AI incident ≠ cybersecurity incident in every case.
* Security incident ≠ safety incident.
* Model failure ≠ model compromise.
* Operational outage ≠ security breach.
* Detection ≠ response.
* Containment ≠ eradication.
* Recovery ≠ validated recovery.
* System restoration ≠ behavioral assurance.
* Backup ≠ recovery capability.
* Rollback ≠ complete remediation.
* Kill capability ≠ resilience by itself.
* Incident procedure ≠ demonstrated incident capability.
* Incident count ≠ incident-management effectiveness.
* Low incident frequency ≠ low incident risk.
* Monitoring ≠ complete incident detection.
* Supplier notification ≠ fulfillment of the organization's own notification obligations.
* Regulatory assessment ≠ automatic regulatory notification.
* Internal incident classification ≠ legal classification.
* Internal governance control ≠ statutory or regulatory requirement.

**Taxonomy note:** `AI-IMR-*` identifiers used throughout this chapter are internal knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal requirements unless separately mapped to an applicable authoritative source.

### 125. AI Incident Response Control Principle

The core control principle for AI incident response is:

**Incident management should be evidence-driven, risk-based, and lifecycle-oriented, with detection, triage, containment, eradication, recovery, validation, communication, and corrective action treated as connected control activities rather than isolated technical tasks.**

Key distinctions:

* Alert ≠ Incident.
* Detection ≠ Identification.
* Identification ≠ Root Cause.
* Classification ≠ Legal Determination.
* Severity ≠ Probability alone.
* Containment ≠ Eradication.
* Eradication ≠ Recovery.
* Recovery ≠ Validated Recovery.
* Model Rollback ≠ Complete Remediation.
* Shutdown ≠ Root-Cause Resolution.
* Fallback ≠ Equivalent Replacement.
* Backup ≠ Tested Recovery.
* Playbook ≠ Demonstrated Capability.
* Vendor Notification ≠ Organizational Incident Assessment.
* Incident Assessment ≠ Automatic Regulatory Notification.
* Internal Severity ≠ Statutory Severity.
* Governance Control ≠ Legal Requirement.

**Governance note:** Detection controls, incident registers, severity matrices, playbooks, recovery testing, tabletop exercises, kill-switch testing, post-incident reviews, and corrective-action tracking are generally governance and implementation practices. They should not be described as statutory or regulatory obligations unless a specific applicable law, regulation, regulatory decision, contract, or authoritative requirement makes the particular activity mandatory.

**Taxonomy note:** `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are internal knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal requirements.

```text id="3m8q2d"
       v
Recovery Controls
       |
       v

       |

```

No single control should be assumed to prevent or contain every AI incident.

### 210. AI Continuous Incident-Management Assurance Principle

AI incident management should be continuously evaluated through:

* Incident evidence.
* Monitoring.
* Testing.
* Exercises.
* Recovery validation.
* Control assessments.
* Trend analysis.
* Corrective actions.
* Management review.

The objective is to establish whether the organization can **detect, contain, recover from, and learn from AI incidents under realistic conditions**.

Key distinctions:

* Monitoring ≠ Complete Detection.
* Alert Correlation ≠ Confirmed Incident.
* Behavioral Anomaly ≠ Malicious Compromise.
* Vulnerability ≠ Exploitation.
* Exploitation ≠ Material Business Impact.
* Supplier Incident ≠ Automatic Customer Incident.
* Alternative Provider ≠ Tested Resilience.
* Alternative Model ≠ Behavioral Equivalence.
* Recovery Plan ≠ Recovery Capability.
* Recovery Capability ≠ Tested Recovery.
* RTO ≠ Guaranteed Recovery.
* RPO ≠ Zero Data Loss.
* Kill Switch ≠ Complete Incident Response.
* Human Override ≠ Guaranteed Safe Outcome.
* Tabletop Exercise ≠ Technical Recovery Test.
* Technical Recovery Test ≠ Complete Resilience.
* Incident Metric ≠ Complete Risk Measurement.
* Low Incident Count ≠ Low Incident Risk.
* Corrective Action ≠ Verified Control Improvement.
* Continuous Monitoring ≠ Continuous Assurance.

**Governance note:** Continuous monitoring, behavioral baselines, recovery exercises, concentration analysis, substitution testing, resilience exercises, incident dashboards, KRIs, KPIs, and continuous assurance are generally governance and implementation practices. They should not be represented as universally mandatory legal requirements unless a specific applicable law, regulation, contract, or authoritative requirement establishes such an obligation.

**Taxonomy note:** `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are internal knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal requirements unless separately mapped to an authoritative source.
