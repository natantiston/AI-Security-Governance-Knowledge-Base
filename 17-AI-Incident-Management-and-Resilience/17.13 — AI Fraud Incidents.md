# 17.13 — AI Fraud Incidents

## Part 1 — AI Fraud Incident Foundations, Fraud Concepts, Deception, Transaction Integrity and Governance

### 1. AI Fraud Incident

An **AI Fraud Incident** is an event in which AI is used, manipulated, impersonated, or involved in deceptive activity that may result in unauthorized benefit, financial loss, property loss, service abuse, or other material harm.

AI fraud may involve:

- AI-generated deception;
- synthetic identity;
- deepfake impersonation;
- automated fraud;
- fraudulent transactions;
- manipulated documents;
- account takeover;
- social engineering;
- misuse of AI systems.

Whether conduct constitutes **fraud as a legal matter** depends on the applicable law and facts. An organization's internal fraud classification should therefore remain distinct from legal classification.

### 2. Objective of AI Fraud Incident Management

The objective is to establish a structured capability to:

- identify suspected AI-enabled fraud;
- distinguish legitimate activity from fraudulent activity;
- assess transaction and identity risk;
- detect suspicious behavior;
- contain ongoing loss;
- preserve evidence;
- investigate the fraud path;
- recover affected operations or assets;
- reduce recurrence.

The objective is not to classify every unusual transaction or AI-generated communication as fraud.

### 3. AI Fraud

**AI Fraud** refers to fraudulent activity in which AI is used as:

- an attack capability;
- a deception mechanism;
- an automation mechanism;
- an identity-manipulation mechanism;
- a content-generation mechanism;
- an analytical or targeting capability.

AI may be central to the fraud or merely one component of a broader fraud scheme.

### 4. AI Fraud Event

An **AI Fraud Event** is an observed or reported activity that may involve AI-enabled fraudulent behavior.

Examples include:

- suspicious synthetic voice;
- abnormal automated transaction;
- unusual account activity;
- manipulated identity evidence;
- AI-generated fraudulent communication.

A fraud event becomes an incident when it meets defined organizational incident criteria.

### 5. AI Fraud Incident versus Fraud Alert

A **Fraud Alert** is a detection signal requiring investigation.

An **AI Fraud Incident** is a confirmed or sufficiently supported event that meets the organization's incident criteria.

```text id="q6m4vx"
ACTIVITY
   |
   v
FRAUD SIGNAL
   |
   v
FRAUD ALERT
   |
   v
INVESTIGATION
   |
   v
INCIDENT CLASSIFICATION
```

An alert should not automatically be treated as confirmed fraud.

### 6. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Abuse and Misuse

AI abuse and misuse are broader categories than fraud.

For example:

- unauthorized use of an AI service may be misuse;
- excessive API consumption may be abuse;
- deceptive use of AI to obtain unauthorized financial benefit may involve fraud.

Fraud therefore represents one possible outcome or category of AI misuse.

### 7. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Deepfake Incident

Deepfakes may facilitate fraud, but not every deepfake is fraudulent.

Examples of non-fraudulent synthetic media may include:

- entertainment;
- research;
- education;
- authorized security testing.

A deepfake becomes relevant to fraud management when it is connected to deceptive activity intended to produce an unauthorized benefit or loss.

### 8. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Security Incident

AI fraud can occur without technical compromise of an organization's infrastructure.

For example, an attacker may use a synthetic voice to persuade an employee to authorize a payment without compromising the organization's systems.

Conversely, fraud may occur as a consequence of:

- account compromise;
- malware;
- credential theft;
- application compromise.

Therefore:

- fraud ≠ cybersecurity incident;
- cybersecurity incident ≠ fraud;
- both can occur simultaneously.

### 9. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Privacy Incident

Fraud may involve personal information without necessarily constituting a privacy incident.

A privacy assessment should separately consider:

- personal-data processing;
- unauthorized access;
- disclosure;
- purpose;
- applicable privacy requirements.

### 10. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Security Control Failure

A fraud incident may expose a security-control weakness, but the fraud itself and the control failure should be separately recorded.

For example:

```text id="m8r2ks"
FRAUD
  |
  v
UNAUTHORIZED TRANSACTION
  |
  v
CONTROL FAILURE
  |
  v
INSUFFICIENT VERIFICATION
```

This distinction supports effective root-cause analysis.

### 11. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Model Failure

Model failure concerns unintended technical or behavioral failure.

Fraud concerns deceptive or unauthorized activity.

A model may operate correctly while an attacker uses it for fraud.

### 12. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Model Compromise

A fraud actor may use an uncompromised AI system.

Alternatively, a compromised AI system may be used to facilitate fraud.

Model compromise and fraud should therefore be classified independently.

### 13. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Prompt Injection

Prompt injection is an attack technique that manipulates model instructions or context.

It can facilitate fraud but is not itself necessarily fraudulent activity.

### 14. AI Fraud Incident versus AI Data Poisoning

Data poisoning involves manipulation of data used by an AI system.

Poisoned data may facilitate fraudulent outcomes, but data poisoning and fraud are distinct classifications.

### 15. AI Fraud Incident versus Traditional Fraud

Traditional fraud may use:

- human impersonation;
- forged documents;
- compromised credentials;
- deceptive communications.

AI-enabled fraud may add:

- synthetic identity;
- deepfake media;
- automated social engineering;
- AI-generated documents;
- scalable targeting.

The underlying fraud concept does not necessarily change merely because AI is involved.

### 16. AI Fraud Attack Surface

The AI fraud attack surface includes the points through which fraud actors can influence:

- identities;
- AI systems;
- users;
- transactions;
- data;
- communication channels;
- automated workflows.

Examples include:

- customer portals;
- AI agents;
- APIs;
- payment systems;
- identity-verification systems;
- support channels.

### 17. AI Fraud Trust Boundary

A trust boundary exists where the organization relies on an external or internal assertion concerning:

- identity;
- authorization;
- transaction legitimacy;
- source authenticity;
- data accuracy.

Fraud frequently exploits weaknesses in these trust assumptions.

### 18. AI Fraud Authorization Boundary

Authorization determines which actions a user, agent, application, or service is permitted to perform.

```text id="v4q7nx"
IDENTITY
   |
   v
AUTHENTICATION
   |
   v
AUTHORIZATION
   |
   v
TRANSACTION
```

Authentication does not establish transaction authorization by itself.

### 19. AI Fraud Transaction

A transaction is an action that creates, modifies, transfers, or commits value.

Examples include:

- payments;
- account changes;
- purchases;
- transfers;
- refunds;
- contractual commitments.

AI systems capable of initiating transactions create additional governance requirements.

### 20. AI Fraud Transaction Integrity

Transaction integrity concerns whether a transaction:

- originated from an authorized source;
- was not improperly modified;
- contains accurate material information;
- received appropriate authorization.

### 21. AI Fraud Transaction Authenticity

Transaction authenticity concerns whether the purported originator actually initiated or authorized the transaction.

This is particularly important when AI-generated:

- voice;
- video;
- email;
- messages

are used to create false authority.

### 22. AI Fraud Transaction Authorization

Transaction authorization determines whether the relevant person or system had authority to perform the transaction.

A request can appear authentic while still being unauthorized.

### 23. AI Fraud Transaction Verification

Transaction verification provides an independent mechanism for confirming a material transaction.

Examples include:

- secondary approval;
- out-of-band confirmation;
- transaction signing;
- known-contact verification.

### 24. AI Fraud Out-of-Band Verification

Out-of-band verification confirms a request through a channel independent from the potentially compromised or manipulated communication.

```text id="r9m5wk"
SUSPICIOUS REQUEST
       |
       X
       |
TRUSTED CHANNEL
       |
       v
INDEPENDENT CONFIRMATION
       |
       v
AUTHORIZED ACTION
```

### 25. AI Fraud Transaction Reversibility

Reversibility concerns whether a fraudulent transaction can be:

- cancelled;
- recalled;
- reversed;
- recovered.

Irreversible or difficult-to-recover transactions generally require stronger preventive controls.

### 26. AI Fraud Transaction Velocity

Transaction velocity represents the frequency or speed of transactions.

Abnormal velocity may be a fraud signal, but legitimate high-volume activity can also produce high velocity.

### 27. AI Fraud Transaction Anomaly

A transaction anomaly is a deviation from expected transaction behavior.

Potential dimensions include:

- amount;
- timing;
- location;
- beneficiary;
- device;
- user;
- frequency.

An anomaly is a detection signal, not proof of fraud.

### 28. AI Fraud Behavioral Baseline

A behavioral baseline describes expected activity for a:

- customer;
- employee;
- supplier;
- service account;
- AI agent.

Baselines should account for legitimate variation.

### 29. AI Fraud Behavioral Deviation

Behavioral deviation occurs when activity differs materially from an established baseline.

Examples include:

- unusual payment destinations;
- unexpected account changes;
- abnormal AI-generated communications;
- unusual transaction volume.

### 30. AI Fraud Synthetic Identity

A synthetic identity combines real and fabricated identity attributes to create an identity representation that may not correspond to a genuine individual.

AI can facilitate synthetic-identity activity through:

- generated images;
- generated documents;
- synthetic voices;
- automated profile creation.

### 31. AI Fraud Identity Impersonation

Identity impersonation occurs when an actor represents themselves as another individual or organization.

AI may facilitate impersonation through:

- voice cloning;
- facial synthesis;
- generated documents;
- automated communications.

### 32. AI Fraud Account Takeover

Account takeover occurs when an unauthorized actor obtains control of an account.

AI may facilitate:

- phishing;
- social engineering;
- credential attacks;
- synthetic identity verification.

### 33. AI Fraud Credential Abuse

Credential abuse occurs when credentials are used in an unauthorized manner.

AI may be involved in:

- credential harvesting;
- automated phishing;
- social engineering;
- credential testing.

### 34. AI Fraud Social Engineering

AI-enabled social engineering uses AI-generated or AI-assisted content to influence a person into:

- disclosing information;
- transferring funds;
- granting access;
- changing account information;
- performing another unauthorized action.

### 35. AI Fraud Business Email Compromise Enhancement

AI may enhance business-email-compromise activity by generating:

- convincing messages;
- contextual responses;
- multilingual communications;
- impersonation content.

AI does not fundamentally redefine business-email compromise; it can increase its scalability or sophistication.

### 36. AI Fraud Executive Impersonation

Executive impersonation uses the apparent identity of an executive to influence employees or business partners.

Potential objectives include:

- payment;
- credential disclosure;
- confidential-data disclosure;
- emergency access.

### 37. AI Fraud Customer Impersonation

Customer impersonation may target:

- account recovery;
- refunds;
- payments;
- identity changes;
- sensitive customer information.

### 38. AI Fraud Supplier Impersonation

Supplier impersonation may target:

- payment instructions;
- account details;
- contracts;
- invoices;
- system access.

### 39. AI Fraud Document Manipulation

AI may be used to generate or modify:

- invoices;
- identity documents;
- contracts;
- receipts;
- financial records.

Document authenticity should therefore be assessed separately from document appearance.

### 40. AI Fraud Evidence Fabrication

Evidence fabrication involves creating or manipulating information to create a false representation of events or facts.

AI-generated media can be one mechanism for fabrication.

### 41. AI Fraud Automated Fraud

Automated fraud uses software, AI agents, or other automation to perform fraudulent activity at scale.

Potential characteristics include:

- high transaction volume;
- automated account creation;
- automated interaction;
- adaptive behavior;
- rapid targeting.

### 42. AI Fraud Agentic Fraud

Agentic fraud involves AI-enabled agents that can plan or execute multiple actions in support of fraudulent activity.

Governance should distinguish:

- human intent;
- AI-generated plan;
- authorized capability;
- actual execution.

### 43. AI Fraud Fraud Automation Scale

AI can increase the scale of fraud by automating:

- target selection;
- content generation;
- communication;
- identity manipulation;
- transaction attempts.

Scale is a risk factor, not itself proof of fraud.

### 44. AI Fraud Fraud-as-a-Service

Fraud-as-a-service refers to organized offerings that provide fraud capabilities to other actors.

AI may be incorporated into such services through:

- synthetic media;
- automated targeting;
- generated communications;
- identity manipulation.

### 45. AI Fraud Targeting

AI can support targeting by analyzing information to identify potentially susceptible:

- individuals;
- organizations;
- accounts;
- business processes.

Targeting capability can increase social-engineering risk.

### 46. AI Fraud Personalization

AI can personalize fraudulent communications based on available contextual information.

Examples include:

- role;
- organization;
- known relationships;
- recent activity.

Personalization can increase credibility but does not establish fraud by itself.

### 47. AI Fraud Evidence

Evidence may include:

- transaction records;
- authentication logs;
- communications;
- prompts;
- AI outputs;
- tool calls;
- account activity;
- device information;
- network telemetry.

Evidence should be preserved according to applicable legal, privacy, security, and retention requirements.

### 48. AI Fraud Timeline

A fraud timeline should establish:

TARGETING

   v

   |
   v

   |

Known facts should be separated from assumptions and unresolved questions.

### 49. AI Fraud Incident Governance Principle

**AI fraud management should distinguish fraud signals, suspicious activity, confirmed fraudulent conduct, technical compromise, policy violations, and legitimate activity before assigning incident classification.**

Assessment should consider:

- identity;
- authorization;
- transaction integrity;
- intent;
- impact;
- evidence;
- reversibility;
- applicable legal and contractual context.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Fraud Incidents

**AI fraud governance should protect the integrity of identity, authorization, transactions, communications, and evidence while recognizing that AI can amplify existing fraud mechanisms without changing the underlying need for evidence-based fraud assessment.**

The following distinctions are foundational:

- fraud alert ≠ fraud incident;
- anomaly ≠ fraud;
- suspicious activity ≠ confirmed fraud;
- deepfake ≠ fraud;
- AI misuse ≠ fraud;
- fraud ≠ cybersecurity incident;
- fraud ≠ privacy incident;
- fraud ≠ model failure;
- fraud ≠ model compromise;
- impersonation ≠ confirmed identity fraud;
- authentication ≠ authorization;
- transaction authenticity ≠ transaction authorization;
- detection ≠ prevention;
- containment ≠ recovery;
- recovery ≠ recovered assets;
- technical evidence ≠ legal determination;
- internal fraud classification ≠ legal classification;
- internal governance control ≠ statutory requirement.

Fraud monitoring, transaction verification, behavioral analytics, anomaly detection, identity controls, out-of-band verification, investigation procedures, evidence preservation, containment, recovery, and fraud-risk governance are generally **governance or implementation practices** unless a specific applicable law, regulation, contractual obligation, or authoritative requirement mandates a particular activity.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
