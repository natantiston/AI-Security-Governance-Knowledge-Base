# 17.19 — AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management

## Part 1 — AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management Foundations, Continuity Concepts, Critical Services, Crisis Governance and Resilience

### 1. AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management

AI business continuity and crisis management is the organizational capability to maintain or restore critical AI-enabled business services during significant disruption while coordinating decisions, resources, communications, and recovery activities.

It connects AI-specific operational resilience with broader business continuity and crisis-management structures.

### 2. Objective of AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management

The objective is to ensure that critical AI-dependent business services can continue, degrade safely, transition to alternatives, or recover following disruptive events.

The capability should address:

- service continuity;
- operational resilience;
- human oversight;
- fallback capability;
- crisis decision-making;
- communications;
- recovery coordination.

### 3. AI Business Continuity

AI business continuity is the capability to maintain critical business services when an AI system, supporting technology, supplier, or infrastructure component becomes unavailable or materially impaired.

Business continuity focuses on the **business service**, not merely the technology.

### 4. AI Business Continuity versus AI Disaster Recovery

Business continuity addresses how a business service continues during disruption.

Disaster recovery addresses restoration of technology and supporting capabilities.

An organization may maintain business continuity through:

- manual processing;
- reduced functionality;
- alternate AI;
- alternate providers;
- temporary service restrictions;

while disaster recovery of the primary AI platform is still underway.

### 5. AI Business Continuity versus AI Incident Management

Incident management addresses the identification, response, containment, escalation, and management of incidents.

Business continuity addresses maintaining critical services when disruption affects normal operations.

A major incident may activate business-continuity procedures, but not every incident requires formal business-continuity activation.

### 6. AI Crisis Management

AI crisis management is the coordinated organizational response to a disruption whose scale, uncertainty, impact, or duration exceeds normal operational management capability.

Crisis management may involve:

- executive leadership;
- legal;
- communications;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- business continuity;
- technology;
- supplier management.

### 7. AI Crisis

An AI crisis is a significant disruption involving an AI system or AI-dependent business service that requires coordinated executive or enterprise-level management.

The term should be defined by organizational governance rather than assumed from the technical nature of an incident.

### 8. AI Crisis Trigger

A crisis trigger is a predefined or assessed condition that may justify activation of crisis-management structures.

Potential triggers include:

- prolonged critical-service disruption;
- major safety consequences;
- widespread customer impact;
- significant data compromise;
- inability to contain an incident;
- systemic supplier failure.

An internal crisis trigger is not automatically a statutory threshold.

### 9. AI Crisis Activation

Crisis activation is the formal transition from normal operational management to an established crisis-management structure.

Activation should identify:

- authority;
- scope;
- affected services;
- decision structure;
- communication channels;
- initial priorities.

### 10. AI Crisis Management Authority

Crisis authority defines who can make material decisions during an AI-related crisis.

Authority may cover:

- service suspension;
- emergency spending;
- customer communications;
- supplier escalation;
- regulatory engagement;
- recovery prioritization;
- risk acceptance.

### 11. AI Crisis Management Team

The crisis-management team coordinates organizational response to a material AI disruption.

Depending on the organization, participants may include:

- executive leadership;
- business owners;
- AI governance;
- cybersecurity;
- privacy;
- legal;
- safety;
- communications;
- infrastructure;
- supplier management.

### 12. AI Crisis Commander

The crisis commander coordinates the overall crisis response and maintains a common decision structure.

The role should remain distinct from technical incident-command responsibilities where organizational scale requires separate functions.

### 13. AI Crisis Decision Authority

Decision authority should define which decisions remain with:

- technical teams;
- business owners;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- executives;
- crisis leadership.

### 14. AI Crisis Decision Log

Material crisis decisions should be recorded with:

- decision;
- timestamp;
- decision authority;
- rationale;
- available evidence;
- affected service;
- expected consequence.

### 15. AI Crisis Situation Awareness

Situation awareness is the organization's understanding of:

- what happened;
- what services are affected;
- what remains operational;
- what dependencies are failing;
- what actions have been taken;
- what uncertainty remains.

Situation awareness should distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions and hypotheses.

### 16. AI Crisis Information Flow

Crisis information should flow between operational, technical, business, legal, regulatory, and executive functions.

Information should be:

- timely;
- relevant;
- appropriately protected;
- traceable;
- sufficiently reliable.

### 18. AI Critical Business Service

A critical business service is a service whose prolonged disruption could create unacceptable consequences according to organizational criteria.

AI may be:

- the primary technology enabling the service;
- one component of the service;
- an augmentation to a human process;
- a supporting capability.

### 19. AI Business-Service Dependency

An AI business-service dependency exists when a business service materially relies on:

- an AI model;
- AI application;
- AI infrastructure;
- AI provider;
- AI data;
- AI-enabled decision process.

### 20. AI Business-Service Mapping

Organizations should map AI systems to the business services they support.

A basic relationship may be represented as:

```text id="k5r8pn"

   |
   v
AI APPLICATION
   |
   v
BUSINESS PROCESS
   |
   v
BUSINESS SERVICE
   |
   v
CUSTOMER / BUSINESS OUTCOME
```

### 21. AI Business-Service Criticality

AI service criticality should reflect the consequences of disruption rather than technical complexity alone.

Relevant considerations may include:

- safety;
- financial impact;
- customer dependence;
- operational dependency;
- regulatory significance;
- recovery difficulty.

### 22. AI Business Impact Analysis

A Business Impact Analysis should determine how disruption to AI-supported services affects the organization.

It may assess:

- operational impact;
- financial impact;
- customer impact;
- safety impact;
- privacy impact;
- legal or regulatory implications.

### 23. AI Maximum Tolerable Downtime

Maximum tolerable downtime is the longest period a business service can remain unavailable before consequences become unacceptable according to organizational criteria.

It should be established for the **business service**, not merely the AI component.

### 24. AI Recovery Time Objective

The Recovery Time Objective (RTO) defines the target period within which an affected service should be restored following disruption.

RTO should be consistent with business-service requirements and realistic recovery capability.

### 25. AI Recovery Point Objective

The Recovery Point Objective (RPO) defines the maximum acceptable amount of data or state loss for a recovery scenario.

For AI-enabled services, RPO may apply to:

- datasets;
- model artifacts;
- configurations;
- transactions;
- agent state;
- audit records.

### 26. AI Business Continuity Strategy

A business-continuity strategy defines how a critical service will continue during disruption.

Strategies may include:

- manual processing;
- reduced functionality;
- alternate AI;
- alternate provider;
- alternate infrastructure;
- service prioritization.

### 27. AI Continuity Operating State

A continuity operating state is a controlled mode in which a business service operates during disruption.

Examples include:

- full operation;
- degraded operation;
- manual operation;
- restricted operation;
- alternate-provider operation.

### 28. AI Graceful Degradation

Graceful degradation reduces AI functionality while preserving selected critical business capabilities.

Examples include:

- disabling autonomous actions;
- requiring human approval;
- restricting high-risk tools;
- reducing automation;
- limiting service scope.

Graceful degradation is a continuity mechanism, not necessarily a complete recovery mechanism.

### 29. AI Manual Fallback

Manual fallback allows critical business operations to continue without the affected AI capability.

Manual fallback should identify:

- responsible personnel;
- procedures;
- workload capacity;
- decision authority;
- duration limits;
- quality controls.

### 30. AI Alternative Service

An alternative service provides another mechanism for maintaining a critical business capability when the primary AI service is unavailable.

It may involve:

- another AI system;
- another provider;
- deterministic software;
- manual operations.

### 31. AI Alternative Model

An alternative model may support continuity when the primary model becomes unavailable.

The organization should assess whether the alternative model differs materially in:

- behavior;
- performance;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- decision outcomes.

### 32. AI Alternative Provider

An alternative provider may support continuity when the primary provider becomes unavailable.

Provider substitution should consider:

- data handling;
- contractual constraints;
- technical compatibility;
- security;
- capacity;
- operational readiness.

### 33. AI Service Substitution

Service substitution is the controlled replacement of one AI capability with another mechanism.

Substitution should not be assumed to preserve equivalent:

- functionality;
- performance;
- behavior;
- risk;
- regulatory characteristics.

### 34. AI Continuity Capacity

Continuity capacity is the organization's ability to maintain required service levels during disruption.

Capacity may include:

- personnel;
- infrastructure;
- manual processing;
- alternate AI;
- supplier capacity;
- communication resources.

### 35. AI Continuity Surge Capacity

Surge capacity is additional operational capacity required when disruption creates increased workload or recovery demand.

Examples include:

- additional human operators;
- additional infrastructure;
- additional support personnel;
- additional supplier capacity.

### 36. AI Continuity Priority

Continuity priority determines which services receive limited resources during disruption.

Prioritization may consider:

- business criticality;
- safety;
- customer impact;
- legal obligations;
- operational dependency;
- recovery complexity.

### 37. AI Continuity Dependency

Continuity dependencies are components required to maintain a service during disruption.

They may include:

- identity;
- network;
- cloud infrastructure;
- data;
- models;
- APIs;
- suppliers;
- human personnel.

### 39. AI Continuity Single Point of Failure

A single point of failure is a dependency whose failure can interrupt continuity despite otherwise available fallback mechanisms.

Examples include:

- one identity provider;
- one cloud region;
- one AI provider;
- one specialist team;
- one critical API.

### 40. AI Continuity Common-Mode Failure

Common-mode failure occurs when primary and continuity mechanisms depend on the same underlying component or condition.

For example, primary and alternate AI services may both depend on the same:

- cloud provider;
- identity service;
- network;
- data source;
- supplier.

### 41. AI Continuity Resilience

AI continuity resilience is the ability to maintain or restore critical business services despite disruptive events.

Resilience depends on more than redundancy.

It may require:

- independence;
- tested fallback;
- recovery capacity;
- dependency management;
- human capability.

### 42. AI Crisis Communications

Crisis communications provide coordinated information to internal and external stakeholders during a material disruption.

Communication should distinguish:

- confirmed facts;
- preliminary findings;
- uncertainty;
- actions underway;
- expected service effects.

### 43. AI Crisis Communication Authority

The organization should define who may communicate externally during an AI crisis.

Potential functions include:

- executive leadership;
- communications;
- legal;
- customer support;
- regulatory affairs.

### 44. AI Crisis Communication Evidence

Material external communications should be traceable to the information available at the time.

This supports:

- consistency;
- accountability;
- later review;
- correction of inaccurate information.

### 45. AI Crisis Stakeholder Coordination

Crisis coordination may involve:

- employees;
- customers;
- suppliers;
- regulators where applicable;
- business partners;
- executives.

Stakeholder coordination should reflect the nature and scope of the disruption.

### 46. AI Crisis Uncertainty Management

Crisis management should explicitly track uncertainty.

Examples include uncertainty concerning:

- root cause;
- affected population;
- duration;
- recovery time;
- system behavior;
- supplier status.

Uncertainty should not automatically be interpreted as low or high severity.

### 47. AI Crisis Escalation

Escalation should occur when predefined or assessed conditions exceed normal operational-management capability.

Escalation criteria may consider:

- severity;
- business impact;
- duration;
- uncertainty;
- safety;
- customer impact;
- containment failure.

Internal escalation thresholds should be distinguished from statutory or regulatory thresholds.

### 48. AI Crisis Governance Principle

**AI business continuity and crisis management should maintain critical business services through controlled continuity states while providing coordinated decision-making, clear authority, reliable information, appropriate fallback capability, and structured escalation during material disruption.**

### 49. AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management Evidence

Evidence may include:

- Business Impact Analyses;
- continuity plans;
- crisis-management plans;
- exercise records;
- decision logs;
- recovery records;
- communications;
- dependency maps;
- validation results.

Documentation alone does not demonstrate operational continuity capability.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Business Continuity and Crisis Management

**AI business continuity should focus on maintaining critical business services rather than merely keeping AI technology available, while crisis management should provide coordinated organizational decision-making when disruption exceeds normal operational capability.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- business continuity ≠ disaster recovery;
- business continuity ≠ incident response;
- crisis management ≠ incident management;
- crisis activation ≠ regulatory notification;
- continuity ≠ availability;
- resilience ≠ redundancy;
- redundancy ≠ independence;
- fallback ≠ recovery;
- manual fallback ≠ full service equivalence;
- alternate model ≠ behavioral equivalent;
- alternate provider ≠ independent provider;
- graceful degradation ≠ full recovery;
- RTO ≠ maximum tolerable downtime;
- RPO ≠ recovery capability;
- recovery plan ≠ demonstrated continuity capability;
- documentation ≠ operational capability;
- internal crisis trigger ≠ statutory threshold;
- internal continuity control ≠ legal obligation;
- contractual continuity requirement ≠ statutory requirement;
- supplier continuity commitment ≠ demonstrated resilience;
- risk acceptance ≠ risk elimination;
- automation ≠ accountability.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers remain knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
