# 17.02 — AI Incident Taxonomy

## Part 1 — AI Incident Taxonomy Foundations, Classification Dimensions, Incident Types and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Taxonomy

An AI Incident Taxonomy is a structured classification system used to identify, categorize, describe, and analyze incidents involving artificial intelligence systems.

A taxonomy provides a common language for:

* Incident identification.
* Incident classification.
* Severity assessment.
* Escalation.
* Reporting.
* Trend analysis.
* Root-cause analysis.
* Regulatory assessment.
* Management reporting.
* Lessons learned.

A well-designed taxonomy should distinguish between **what happened**, **how it happened**, **what was affected**, and **what impact resulted**.

---

### 2. Purpose of AI Incident Taxonomy

The primary purpose of an AI incident taxonomy is to establish consistent classification across different AI systems and business units.

Without a common taxonomy, similar incidents may be recorded differently:

```text id="j8p3kd"
Same Incident
     |
     +---- Security Team → Security Event
     |
     +---- AI Team ------> Model Failure
     |
     +---- Privacy ------> Data Incident
     |
     +---- Business ------> Service Outage
```

A multidimensional taxonomy can represent these perspectives without forcing the incident into only one category.

---

### 3. AI Incident Classification

AI incident classification assigns defined categories and attributes to an incident.

Classification may include:

* Incident type.
* Attack or failure mechanism.
* Affected AI component.
* Impact dimension.
* Severity.
* Scope.
* Root cause.
* Detection source.
* Response status.
* Regulatory relevance.

Classification should be based on available evidence and should be updated when the investigation produces new information.

---

### 4. AI Incident Category

An incident category is a broad classification used to group incidents with similar characteristics.

Common AI incident categories include:

* Security.
* Safety.
* Privacy.
* Model.
* Data.
* Operational.
* Availability.
* Integrity.
* Fraud.
* Abuse.
* Supply chain.
* Compliance.

An incident may belong to multiple categories.

---

### 5. AI Security Incident Category

The security category covers incidents involving unauthorized access, compromise, manipulation, exploitation, or disruption of AI systems and their supporting infrastructure.

Examples include:

* Model compromise.
* Prompt injection.
* Credential compromise.
* Unauthorized access.
* Data exfiltration.
* Supply-chain compromise.
* Malicious model modification.

Security classification should not prevent an incident from also receiving safety, privacy, or operational classifications.

---

### 6. AI Safety Incident Category

The safety category covers incidents involving AI behavior or AI-enabled actions that create actual or potential harm.

Examples include:

* Unsafe recommendations.
* Unsafe autonomous actions.
* Dangerous output.
* Failure of safety controls.
* High-impact decision failure.

Safety incidents may occur without a cybersecurity compromise.

---

### 7. AI Privacy Incident Category

The privacy category covers incidents involving inappropriate access to, disclosure of, loss of control over, or other unauthorized processing of personal data through an AI system.

Examples include:

* Personal-data disclosure through model output.
* Unauthorized training use.
* Exposure through logs.
* Prompt-based data leakage.
* Unauthorized data retrieval.

Privacy classification should be coordinated with applicable privacy incident processes.

---

### 8. AI Model Incident Category

The model category covers incidents primarily involving model behavior, model integrity, model performance, model configuration, or model lifecycle.

Examples include:

* Model failure.
* Model drift.
* Model corruption.
* Model compromise.
* Unexpected model behavior.
* Model-version regression.
* Unsafe model behavior.

Model incidents may also create security, privacy, safety, or operational impacts.

---

### 9. AI Data Incident Category

The data category covers incidents involving data used, processed, stored, transmitted, retrieved, or generated by AI systems.

Examples include:

* Data poisoning.
* Data corruption.
* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Dataset integrity failure.
* Data-loss incident.
* Unauthorized data modification.
* Data-quality failure.

Data incidents should distinguish between data integrity, confidentiality, availability, quality, and privacy characteristics.

---

### 10. AI Operational Incident Category

An operational AI incident affects the functioning or reliability of an AI system without necessarily involving malicious activity.

Examples include:

* AI service outage.
* Inference failure.
* Capacity exhaustion.
* Configuration error.
* Integration failure.
* Dependency outage.
* Deployment failure.

Operational incidents may escalate into security, safety, privacy, or business-continuity incidents.

---

### 11. AI Availability Incident Category

An availability incident affects the ability of authorized users or systems to access an AI capability.

Examples include:

* Model-service outage.
* API outage.
* Cloud infrastructure failure.
* Denial-of-service condition.
* Network failure.
* Resource exhaustion.

Availability classification should consider both technical downtime and business consequences.

---

### 12. AI Integrity Incident Category

An integrity incident involves unauthorized or unintended alteration of AI-related assets or behavior.

Affected assets may include:

* Model weights.
* Model configurations.
* Datasets.
* Prompts.
* Software.
* APIs.
* Policies.
* Deployment artifacts.

Examples include:

* Model tampering.
* Dataset poisoning.
* Configuration manipulation.
* Unauthorized deployment.

---

### 13. AI Authenticity Incident Category

An authenticity incident concerns uncertainty or compromise involving the identity or origin of an AI artifact, source, provider, or communication.

Examples include:

* Fake model artifact.
* Impersonated provider.
* Fraudulent AI-generated communication.
* Unauthorized model substitution.
* Tampered distribution channel.

Authenticity should be distinguished from integrity.

An artifact can be unchanged from a source but still originate from an untrusted source.

---

### 14. AI Fraud Incident Category

An AI fraud incident involves AI being used to facilitate, enable, conceal, or materially contribute to fraudulent activity.

Examples include:

* Synthetic identity generation.
* AI-generated impersonation.
* Automated fraud.
* Deepfake-based deception.
* Manipulated AI-generated documents.
* Fraudulent automated communications.

Fraud classification describes the incident context; it does not by itself determine criminal or legal liability.

---

### 15. AI Abuse Incident Category

An AI abuse incident involves inappropriate or malicious use of an AI capability.

Examples include:

* Unauthorized use.
* Malicious prompting.
* Automated abuse.
* Circumvention of safeguards.
* Harassment through AI systems.
* Malicious content generation.

Abuse may be perpetrated by external actors, insiders, customers, or compromised accounts.

---

### 16. AI Misuse Incident Category

AI misuse involves use of an AI capability in a manner inconsistent with its intended purpose, authorization, policy, or operating constraints.

Misuse may be:

* Intentional.
* Accidental.
* Negligent.
* Caused by misunderstanding.
* Caused by inadequate controls.

Misuse does not automatically imply malicious intent.

---

### 17. AI Supply-Chain Incident Category

A supply-chain incident originates from or propagates through an external or internal dependency associated with the AI system.

Examples include:

* Compromised model.
* Malicious software package.
* Poisoned dataset.
* Compromised model provider.
* Repository compromise.
* Signing-key compromise.
* Third-party API compromise.

Supply-chain classification should consider both direct and transitive dependencies.

---

### 18. AI Third-Party Incident Category

A third-party incident involves an AI vendor, provider, supplier, or external service that affects the organization's AI environment.

Examples include:

* Provider outage.
* Vendor breach.
* Model compromise.
* Data incident.
* Supplier vulnerability.
* Provider-side model change.

A supplier incident does not automatically mean that the organization has suffered the same incident.

The organization's own impact must be assessed.

---

### 19. AI Compliance Incident Category

A compliance incident involves a failure to meet an applicable internal, contractual, regulatory, or legal requirement.

Examples may include:

* Failure to follow an approved control.
* Contractual control failure.
* Required record not maintained.
* Applicable notification requirement not fulfilled.
* Required AI governance process not followed.

The specific obligation should always be identified rather than assuming that an internal policy requirement is a legal obligation.

---

### 20. AI Incident Vector

The incident vector describes the mechanism through which an incident occurred or was initiated.

Potential vectors include:

* Prompt.
* API.
* Credential.
* Model artifact.
* Dataset.
* Software dependency.
* Network.
* User account.
* Tool.
* Agent.
* Configuration.
* Supplier.
* Physical infrastructure.

Vector classification helps identify attack and failure patterns.

---

### 21. AI Attack Vector

An attack vector is the pathway through which a malicious actor attempts to compromise an AI system.

Examples include:

* Prompt injection.
* Credential theft.
* Malicious package.
* Poisoned dataset.
* Compromised model.
* Vulnerable API.
* Exposed management interface.
* Malicious tool invocation.

Attack-vector classification should be supported by evidence where possible.

---

### 22. AI Failure Vector

A failure vector describes the mechanism through which an unintended AI failure occurred.

Examples include:

* Configuration error.
* Software defect.
* Model regression.
* Data-quality problem.
* Capacity exhaustion.
* Dependency outage.
* Infrastructure failure.
* Human error.

Failure vectors should be distinguished from malicious attack vectors.

---

### 23. AI Incident Trigger

An incident trigger is the condition that initiates or precipitates an incident.

Triggers may include:

* Model update.
* Software update.
* Dataset change.
* Configuration change.
* Credential compromise.
* Provider outage.
* User action.
* Malicious input.
* Security vulnerability.
* Infrastructure failure.

A trigger is not necessarily the root cause.

---

### 24. AI Incident Root Cause

Root cause represents an underlying condition that materially contributed to an incident.

Potential root causes include:

* Inadequate access control.
* Poor configuration management.
* Vulnerable software.
* Insufficient model testing.
* Weak data governance.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Supplier weakness.
* Process failure.
* Human error.

Root-cause classification should be based on investigation evidence.

---

### 25. AI Incident Contributing Factor

A contributing factor is a condition that increased the likelihood, severity, duration, or impact of an incident without necessarily being the primary root cause.

Examples include:

* Missing monitoring.
* Poor documentation.
* Staff shortage.
* Excessive privilege.
* Provider dependency.
* Insufficient capacity.
* Delayed escalation.

Multiple contributing factors may exist for one incident.

---

### 26. AI Incident Impact Dimension

Impact dimensions describe the types of consequences produced by an AI incident.

Common dimensions include:

```text id="y5c7na"
Confidentiality
Integrity
Availability
Privacy
Safety
Financial
Operational
Regulatory
Customer
Reputational
```

An incident may affect multiple dimensions simultaneously.

---

### 27. AI Confidentiality Incident

A confidentiality incident involves unauthorized access to or disclosure of information.

In AI environments, this may involve:

* Prompt data.
* Model outputs.
* Training data.
* Retrieval data.
* Credentials.
* Customer information.
* Proprietary information.

Confidentiality impact should be assessed independently from integrity and availability.

---

### 28. AI Integrity Impact

Integrity impact concerns unauthorized or unintended alteration of information, models, configurations, or AI behavior.

Examples include:

* Modified model weights.
* Poisoned datasets.
* Altered configurations.
* Manipulated outputs.
* Unauthorized policy changes.

Integrity impact can occur without loss of confidentiality.

---

### 29. AI Availability Impact

Availability impact concerns interruption or degradation of AI capability.

It may involve:

* Complete outage.
* Partial outage.
* Increased latency.
* Reduced capacity.
* Restricted functionality.
* Provider unavailability.

Availability should be assessed in relation to business requirements.

---

### 30. AI Privacy Impact

Privacy impact concerns consequences for individuals or organizations arising from inappropriate processing or exposure of personal information.

Potential factors include:

* Data sensitivity.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Duration.
* Exposure scope.
* Potential consequences.
* Re-identification risk.

Privacy impact should be assessed using the applicable privacy framework and organizational process.

---

### 31. AI Safety Impact

Safety impact concerns actual or potential harm resulting from AI behavior or AI-enabled actions.

Potential factors include:

* Severity of potential harm.
* Number of affected persons.
* Duration.
* Ability to intervene.
* Degree of autonomy.
* Consequence of incorrect output.

Safety impact may exist even without security compromise.

---

### 32. AI Financial Impact

Financial impact represents direct or indirect financial consequences associated with an AI incident.

Examples include:

* Revenue loss.
* Fraud loss.
* Recovery cost.
* Customer compensation.
* Regulatory penalties where applicable.
* Contractual costs.
* Additional infrastructure costs.

Financial impact should be based on defined organizational assessment methods.

---

### 33. AI Operational Impact

Operational impact measures disruption to business processes, employees, systems, or services.

Examples include:

* Service interruption.
* Manual-workload increase.
* Processing delays.
* Loss of productivity.
* Customer-service disruption.

Operational impact should be assessed in relation to business criticality.

---

### 34. AI Regulatory Impact

Regulatory impact concerns the potential consequences of an incident under applicable laws, regulations, supervisory requirements, or regulatory commitments.

Assessment may involve:

* Jurisdiction.
* Sector.
* Data involved.
* Affected population.
* AI system classification.
* Incident characteristics.
* Applicable obligations.

Regulatory impact should be assessed by appropriate legal or compliance functions where required.

---

### 35. AI Customer Impact

Customer impact represents consequences experienced by customers or users.

Examples include:

* Service interruption.
* Incorrect outputs.
* Privacy exposure.
* Fraud.
* Unsafe recommendations.
* Loss of access.

Customer impact should be assessed separately from internal operational impact.

---

### 36. AI Reputational Impact

Reputational impact concerns potential effects on trust, public perception, customer confidence, or stakeholder relationships.

Reputational impact can result from:

* Publicly visible incidents.
* Customer harm.
* Data exposure.
* AI misuse.
* Fraud.
* Unsafe behavior.

Reputational impact is an assessment dimension and should not replace factual analysis of the underlying incident.

---

### 37. AI Incident Scope Dimension

Incident scope identifies the breadth of affected assets, users, processes, and jurisdictions.

Scope may include:

* Single model.
* Single application.
* Multiple applications.
* Business unit.
* Enterprise.
* Multiple regions.
* External customers.
* Third-party ecosystem.

Scope may expand as investigation progresses.

---

### 38. AI Incident Duration

Incident duration measures how long the relevant incident condition persisted.

Possible measurements include:

* Time from occurrence to detection.
* Time from detection to containment.
* Time from containment to recovery.
* Total service impact.
* Duration of data exposure.

Duration should be recorded using consistent timestamps.

---

### 39. AI Incident Persistence

Persistence describes whether an incident condition remains active after initial detection or containment.

Examples include:

* Persistent unauthorized access.
* Malicious model persistence.
* Repeated prompt attacks.
* Persistent credential compromise.
* Recurring data leakage.

Persistence can materially affect severity and response priority.

---

### 40. AI Incident Propagation

Propagation describes the movement or expansion of an incident from one component to another.

Potential paths include:

```text id="p8v4kq"
Initial Component
      |
      v
Model / Service
      |
      v
Application
      |
      v
Tool / API
      |
      v
Business Process
      |
      v
External Impact
```

Propagation analysis supports containment and blast-radius assessment.

---

### 41. AI Incident Blast Radius

Blast radius describes the maximum or observed extent of an incident across connected systems and dependencies.

Factors include:

* Shared models.
* Shared APIs.
* Shared credentials.
* Shared infrastructure.
* Shared datasets.
* Shared suppliers.
* Shared identity systems.

Blast radius may be broader than the initially observed impact.

---

### 42. AI Incident Recurrence

Recurrence occurs when substantially similar incidents occur again after a previous incident or corrective action.

Recurrence may indicate:

* Incomplete remediation.
* Weak corrective controls.
* Insufficient root-cause analysis.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Repeated configuration errors.

Recurrence should be tracked as a governance signal.

---

### 43. AI Incident Novelty

Incident novelty describes whether the organization has previously observed or modeled the incident type.

An incident may be:

* Known.
* Previously observed.
* Expected.
* New.
* Unanticipated.

Novelty does not automatically determine severity.

---

### 44. AI Incident Detectability

Detectability represents how readily an incident can be identified through available monitoring and reporting mechanisms.

High-detectability incidents may generate clear technical indicators.

Low-detectability incidents may involve:

* Subtle behavioral changes.
* Slow data leakage.
* Model manipulation.
* Gradual degradation.
* Supplier-side activity.

Detectability can influence incident-response design.

---

### 45. AI Incident Containability

Containability represents the organization's ability to limit incident impact after detection.

Containability may depend on:

* Isolation.
* Kill capability.
* Access controls.
* Model rollback.
* Provider substitution.
* Network segmentation.
* Human intervention.

Low containability can increase potential blast radius.

---

### 46. AI Incident Recoverability

Recoverability represents the organization's ability to restore an affected AI capability to an acceptable trusted state.

Factors include:

* Backups.
* Model artifacts.
* Dataset recovery.
* Infrastructure.
* Alternative providers.
* Recovery procedures.
* Recovery testing.
* Staff capability.

Recoverability should be assessed before incidents occur for critical systems.

---

### 47. AI Incident Evidence Classification

Incident evidence can be classified according to its reliability and status.

A useful internal distinction is:

```text id="f9x6sw"
Confirmed Fact
     ↓
Strongly Supported Finding
     ↓
Probable Finding
     ↓
Working Hypothesis
     ↓
Unknown
```

This prevents assumptions from being represented as established facts.

---

### 48. AI Incident Status Classification

Incident status describes the current state of incident handling.

Possible states include:

* Detected.
* Under assessment.
* Confirmed.
* Contained.
* Eradication in progress.
* Recovering.
* Recovery validated.
* Monitoring.
* Closed.

Status should be updated as response activities progress.

---

### 49. AI Incident Taxonomy Governance Principle

An AI incident taxonomy should provide a consistent and multidimensional classification structure capable of representing:

* Incident type.
* Vector.
* Affected asset.
* Impact.
* Severity.
* Scope.
* Root cause.
* Status.
* Regulatory relevance.
* Response state.

The taxonomy should support operational response without confusing internal categories with legal classifications.

---

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Taxonomy

The foundational principle is:

**An AI incident should be classified across multiple dimensions rather than forced into a single category, because the same incident may simultaneously involve security, safety, privacy, model, data, operational, financial, and regulatory characteristics.**

Key distinctions:

* Event ≠ Incident.
* Incident category ≠ Incident cause.
* Attack vector ≠ Root cause.
* Trigger ≠ Root cause.
* Contributing factor ≠ Root cause.
* Security incident ≠ Every AI incident.
* Safety incident ≠ Security compromise.
* Model failure ≠ Model compromise.
* Data incident ≠ Privacy incident in every case.
* Operational incident ≠ Non-security incident in every case.
* Supplier incident ≠ Automatically an organizational incident.
* Severity ≠ Impact alone.
* Impact ≠ Root cause.
* Scope ≠ Blast radius.
* Detection ≠ Containment.
* Containment ≠ Eradication.
* Recoverability ≠ Recovery.
* Internal taxonomy ≠ Legal classification.
* Regulatory relevance ≠ Regulatory breach.
* Compliance incident ≠ Automatically a statutory violation.

**Governance note:** Incident categories, taxonomies, severity matrices, impact dimensions, root-cause classifications, and internal incident-status models are generally organizational governance mechanisms. They should not be presented as statutory or regulatory classifications unless a specific applicable authority establishes the relevant classification.

**Taxonomy note:** `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are internal knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal requirements unless separately mapped to an authoritative source.

## Part 2 — AI Incident Classification Controls, Taxonomy Design, Severity Assessment, Impact Analysis and Governance

### 51. AI Incident Taxonomy Control

An AI Incident Taxonomy Control establishes the organizational mechanism used to classify AI incidents consistently across security, safety, privacy, operational, model, data, fraud, abuse, supply-chain, third-party, and compliance domains.

The taxonomy should define standardized categories, classification dimensions, severity attributes, impact dimensions, incident vectors, root-cause classifications, and status states.

The control should ensure that materially similar incidents are classified consistently across different AI systems, business units, vendors, and operating environments.

A taxonomy should be sufficiently detailed to support investigation and reporting without becoming so complex that responders cannot apply it during an active incident.

```text
AI Incident
     |
     +-- Category
     |     +-- Security
     |     +-- Safety
     |     +-- Privacy
     |     +-- Model
     |     +-- Data
     |     +-- Operational
     |     +-- Supply Chain
     |     +-- Third Party
     |     +-- Compliance
     |
     +-- Vector
     +-- Root Cause
     +-- Impact
     +-- Scope
     +-- Severity
     +-- Status
```

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-051`

### 52. AI Incident Taxonomy Ownership

The organization should assign ownership for maintaining the AI incident taxonomy.

Ownership should define who approves taxonomy categories, who may create new categories, who resolves classification disputes, and who periodically reviews whether the taxonomy remains aligned with the organization's AI environment.

Possible ownership may include:

* AI governance;
* cybersecurity;
* operational risk;
* privacy;
* AI safety;
* legal and compliance;
* enterprise risk management;
* business continuity;
* internal audit as an independent assurance function.

Taxonomy ownership should not mean that every incident must be managed by the taxonomy owner. Operational incident ownership should remain with the designated incident-management structure.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-052`

### 53. AI Incident Classification Authority

The organization should define who has authority to establish or approve an incident's classification.

Classification authority should be proportional to incident severity.

For example:

* operational teams may classify routine incidents;
* security teams may classify cybersecurity incidents;
* privacy teams may classify privacy impacts;
* safety specialists may assess safety impacts;
* incident commanders may establish the operational classification;
* senior governance authorities may approve classification of critical incidents.

Where multiple domains are affected, the organization should permit multi-disciplinary classification rather than forcing an incident into a single category.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-053`

### 54. Multi-Category AI Incident Classification

An AI incident may belong to multiple categories simultaneously.

For example, a compromised AI application could involve:

* a security incident because credentials were compromised;
* a model incident because model behavior was altered;
* a privacy incident because personal data was exposed;
* an operational incident because service availability was affected;
* a regulatory concern because applicable notification or compliance obligations may arise.

The taxonomy should therefore support primary and secondary classifications.

```text
                    AI Incident
                         |
          +--------------+--------------+
          |              |              |
       Primary        Secondary      Impact
       Category       Categories     Dimensions
          |              |              |
       Security     Privacy/Safety   Financial
                     Model/Data      Regulatory
                    Operational      Customer
```

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-054`

### 55. Primary Incident Category

The primary incident category identifies the dominant characteristic used to organize the incident operationally.

The primary category should not prevent the organization from recording additional affected domains.

For example, an incident may initially be classified as a security incident because unauthorized access was detected. During investigation, the same incident may acquire secondary privacy, model-integrity, and regulatory classifications.

The taxonomy should permit classification changes as evidence develops.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-055`

### 56. Secondary Incident Categories

Secondary categories identify additional incident dimensions that materially affect investigation, response, notification, recovery, or governance.

Secondary categories are particularly important for AI incidents because a single technical event may create different consequences across multiple risk domains.

A secondary classification should be evidence-based rather than added merely because a category is potentially relevant.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-056`

### 57. AI Incident Classification Confidence

Incident classification should include a confidence level where classification is uncertain.

Possible internal confidence states include:

* Confirmed;
* Highly supported;
* Probable;
* Suspected;
* Unknown.

Confidence should be distinguished from severity.

An incident can be:

* highly severe but poorly understood; or
* well understood but low severity.

```text
Classification Confidence
          |
          +-- Confirmed
          +-- Supported
          +-- Probable
          +-- Suspected
          +-- Unknown

Severity
          |
          +-- Low
          +-- Moderate
          +-- High
          +-- Critical
```

These dimensions should not be merged into a single score because they represent different properties.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-057`

### 58. AI Incident Classification Evidence

Incident classification should be supported by evidence appropriate to the classification.

Evidence may include:

* security logs;
* model telemetry;
* model version information;
* access records;
* application logs;
* prompts and responses;
* dataset lineage;
* configuration records;
* vulnerability information;
* user reports;
* supplier notifications;
* forensic artifacts;
* privacy assessments;
* safety assessments;
* business-impact information.

The evidence supporting classification should be recorded in the incident record.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-058`

### 59. AI Incident Reclassification

AI incidents should be capable of being reclassified as investigation findings change.

Initial classifications are frequently based on incomplete information.

For example:

```text
Initial Detection
      |
      v
Suspected Security Incident
      |
      v
Investigation
      |
      +----> Privacy Impact Identified
      |
      +----> Model Integrity Impact Identified
      |
      +----> Supplier Dependency Identified
      |
      v
Updated Multi-Category Classification
```

Reclassification should preserve the original classification and the evidence supporting the change.

This provides an auditable history of how the organization's understanding evolved.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-059`

### 60. AI Incident Taxonomy Version Control

The incident taxonomy itself should be version controlled.

Changes may include:

* new categories;
* removed categories;
* renamed categories;
* revised definitions;
* new severity dimensions;
* new impact dimensions;
* changed classification rules;
* new regulatory mapping;
* revised reporting requirements.

Historical incidents should normally retain the taxonomy version under which they were originally classified.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-060`

### 61. AI Incident Taxonomy Change Management

Changes to the taxonomy should follow controlled change-management procedures.

The organization should evaluate whether taxonomy changes affect:

* incident records;
* dashboards;
* metrics;
* regulatory reporting;
* operational procedures;
* automated classification;
* incident-management platforms;
* historical trend analysis;
* audit evidence.

A taxonomy change should not unintentionally invalidate historical reporting.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-061`

### 62. AI Incident Taxonomy Mapping

Organizations may maintain mappings between internal incident categories and external frameworks, standards, contractual requirements, or regulatory concepts.

The mapping should clearly identify whether the external source is:

* legally binding;
* regulatory guidance;
* contractual;
* standards-based;
* voluntary;
* or internally defined.

An internal category should not be presented as though it were an official legal classification merely because a mapping exists.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-062`

### 63. AI Incident Severity Classification

Severity classification determines the relative urgency and organizational significance of an incident.

Severity may consider:

* affected users;
* affected systems;
* safety consequences;
* privacy consequences;
* security consequences;
* financial consequences;
* operational disruption;
* regulatory exposure;
* duration;
* geographic scope;
* reversibility;
* likelihood of propagation;
* strategic importance of the affected AI capability.

Severity should be determined using documented criteria rather than individual intuition.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-063`

### 64. AI Incident Severity Dimensions

Severity should be based on multiple dimensions rather than a single impact measure.

A structured model may include:

```text
Severity
   |
   +-- Impact
   +-- Scope
   +-- Duration
   +-- Propagation
   +-- Criticality
   +-- Recoverability
   +-- Regulatory Relevance
   +-- Safety Relevance
   +-- Security Relevance
```

The organization may weight dimensions differently depending on the AI use case.

A high-impact safety event, for example, may require immediate escalation even if the number of affected users is relatively small.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-064`

### 65. AI Incident Severity Matrix

An organization may use a severity matrix to translate incident characteristics into operational severity levels.

Example:

| Dimension       | Low          | Moderate       | High                | Critical            |
| --------------- | ------------ | -------------- | ------------------- | ------------------- |
| Scope           | Limited      | Multiple users | Major business unit | Enterprise/systemic |
| Duration        | Short        | Moderate       | Extended            | Sustained           |
| Safety          | None/minimal | Potential      | Material            | Severe              |
| Privacy         | Limited      | Material       | Significant         | Major               |
| Security        | Limited      | Contained      | Significant         | Extensive           |

### 125. AI Incident Classification Control Principle

An effective AI incident taxonomy should provide a consistent language for describing **what happened, how it happened, why it happened, what was affected, how severe it was, how confident the organization is in its assessment, and what operational or governance state the incident occupies**.

The taxonomy should maintain clear separation between:

* event and incident;
* category and cause;
* trigger and root cause;
* attack vector and failure vector;
* impact and severity;
* scope and blast radius;
* detection and containment;
* containment and recovery;
* confirmed evidence and hypothesis;
* regulatory relevance and legal violation;
* contractual requirement and statutory requirement;
* internal governance classification and external legal classification.

The taxonomy itself is an **internal governance mechanism** unless a specific law, regulation, regulator, contract, or other authoritative requirement establishes a particular classification.

Internal identifiers such as `AI-IMR-TAX-*` are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal obligations.

## Part 3 — Continuous AI Incident Taxonomy Monitoring, Advanced Classification, Correlation, Response Intelligence and Assurance

### 126. AI Incident Taxonomy Monitoring

AI incident taxonomy monitoring is the continuous observation of incident classifications, classification quality, emerging patterns, and changes in incident characteristics across the AI environment.

Monitoring should examine whether incidents are:

* consistently categorized;
* correctly assigned to severity levels;
* repeatedly associated with the same vectors;
* concentrated around particular models or suppliers;
* propagating across multiple systems;
* increasingly difficult to detect;
* recurring after corrective action;
* producing changing impact patterns.

Taxonomy monitoring should distinguish operational incident trends from changes in the taxonomy itself.

### 127. AI Incident Classification Drift

Classification drift occurs when the way incidents are classified changes over time without a corresponding intentional change in taxonomy definitions.

Possible causes include:

* responder interpretation differences;
* organizational restructuring;
* changes in incident-management personnel;
* new AI architectures;
* changing incident patterns;
* inconsistent training;
* automation changes.

Classification drift can make historical trend analysis unreliable.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-TAX-127`

### 128. AI Incident Taxonomy Drift

Taxonomy drift occurs when the existing taxonomy progressively becomes less representative of the organization's AI environment.

Indicators may include:

* increasing use of “other” categories;
* frequent manual overrides;
* repeated requests for new categories;
* high classification uncertainty;
* large numbers of incidents mapped to generic categories;
* new AI capabilities not represented in the taxonomy.

Taxonomy drift should be assessed separately from classification drift.

### 399. AI Incident Taxonomy Governance Optimization Principle

An effective enterprise taxonomy should balance **precision, usability, consistency, evidence quality, analytical value, operational speed, and governance assurance**.

* incident and event;
* category and cause;
* cause and trigger;
* vector and impact;
* severity and materiality;
* scope and blast radius;
* internal classification and external legal classification;
* regulatory relevance and confirmed legal obligation;
* supplier classification and organizational determination;
* automated recommendation and accountable decision.

### 400. AI Incident Taxonomy Enterprise Governance Principle

AI incident taxonomy is most effective when treated as an **enterprise governance, operational intelligence, and assurance capability**, rather than merely as a list of incident labels.

A robust taxonomy should enable the organization to:

* identify what happened;
* classify how it happened;
* distinguish cause from trigger;
* measure actual and potential impact;
* assess severity and materiality;
* represent uncertainty;
* correlate incidents across models, systems, suppliers, and dependencies;
* identify recurring and systemic conditions;
* support appropriate response and escalation;
* preserve evidence and historical integrity;
* inform risk, control, architecture, supplier, privacy, safety, and resilience decisions;
* support applicable legal and regulatory assessments;
* provide reliable management and assurance reporting;
* continuously improve as AI technologies and incident patterns evolve.

The final governance distinction remains fundamental:

**Internal taxonomy is not law. Internal severity is not automatically regulatory severity. Regulatory relevance is not automatically a legal violation. A governance control is not automatically a statutory requirement. A recommended practice is not automatically a regulatory obligation.**

Internal identifiers such as `AI-IMR-TAX-*` are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal obligations unless explicitly mapped to an authoritative source.
