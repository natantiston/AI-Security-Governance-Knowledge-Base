# 17.16 — AI Incident Containment and Eradication

## Part 1 — AI Incident Containment and Eradication Foundations, Containment Concepts, Isolation, Removal and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Containment and Eradication

AI incident containment and eradication is the controlled process of limiting the impact of an AI incident, preventing further propagation, removing or neutralizing the underlying cause, and restoring the affected AI environment to an appropriately trusted state.

Containment focuses on limiting ongoing impact.

Eradication focuses on removing or neutralizing the cause or persistence mechanism.

They are related but distinct activities.

### 2. Objective of AI Incident Containment and Eradication

The objectives are to:

- prevent additional harm;
- limit incident propagation;
- protect unaffected systems;
- preserve evidence;
- remove malicious or defective conditions;
- eliminate persistence;
- prevent recurrence;
- establish a trusted recovery state.

Containment should not unnecessarily destroy evidence required for investigation.

### 3. AI Incident Containment

Containment is the set of actions taken to limit the scope, propagation, or consequences of an incident.

Examples include:

- isolating an AI service;
- disabling a compromised tool;
- restricting credentials;
- quarantining a dataset;
- blocking malicious inputs;
- suspending an agent.

Containment does not necessarily remove the underlying cause.

### 4. AI Incident Eradication

Eradication is the process of removing or neutralizing the underlying cause, malicious artifact, unauthorized modification, persistence mechanism, or defective configuration responsible for an incident.

Examples include:

- removing malicious code;
- replacing compromised credentials;
- removing poisoned data;
- restoring a trusted model artifact;
- correcting an insecure configuration.

### 5. AI Incident Containment versus Eradication

Containment limits the incident.

Eradication removes or neutralizes its cause.

Successful containment does not demonstrate successful eradication.

### 14. AI Incident Isolation

Examples include:

- network isolation;
- application isolation;
- model-serving isolation;
- account isolation;
- agent isolation.

Isolation should be validated after implementation.

### 15. AI Incident Quarantine

Quarantine places an affected asset into a controlled state where it cannot normally interact with trusted production resources.

Assets that may be quarantined include:

- model artifacts;
- datasets;
- files;
- containers;
- agents;
- endpoints.

### 16. AI Incident Model Quarantine

A potentially compromised or unsafe model may be removed from normal production use and placed into a controlled environment for investigation.

Model quarantine should preserve:

- model identity;
- version;
- provenance;
- relevant configuration;
- evidence.

### 17. AI Incident Dataset Quarantine

Potentially poisoned, contaminated, or compromised datasets may be isolated to prevent further training or inference dependency.

Quarantine should preserve sufficient information to support investigation.

### 18. AI Incident Agent Quarantine

An AI agent suspected of unauthorized behavior may be:

- suspended;
- isolated;
- prevented from invoking tools;
- restricted from accessing sensitive resources.

### 19. AI Incident Tool Isolation

Tools accessible to an affected AI system may be disabled or isolated where continued access could increase impact.

- email;

- payment systems;
- file systems;

Compromised or suspected credentials may require:

- suspension;

### 21. AI Incident Privilege Containment

Where compromise involves excessive privileges, containment may reduce access to the minimum required for safe operation.

### 22. AI Incident Session Containment

Active sessions associated with suspicious activity may be:

- terminated;

### 23. AI Incident Network Containment

### 24. AI Incident API Containment

- endpoint restriction;

### 25. AI Incident Data-Flow Containment

Data flows associated with an incident may be restricted to prevent:

- exfiltration;
- contamination;
- unauthorized processing;

### 26. AI Incident Model-Serving Containment

Affected model-serving infrastructure may be:

- isolated;
- removed from traffic;
- placed behind additional controls;
- switched to a trusted model version.

### 27. AI Incident Deployment Containment

Where an incident originates from a deployment, additional deployment or promotion should be suspended until the condition is understood.

### 28. AI Incident Change Freeze

A controlled change freeze may prevent additional changes from obscuring evidence or introducing further risk.

The freeze should be scoped so that emergency remediation remains possible.

### 29. AI Incident Emergency Containment

Emergency containment may require rapid action before complete investigation is available.

Examples include:

- disabling an exposed endpoint;
- revoking compromised credentials;
- suspending an AI agent;
- isolating a compromised workload.

### 30. AI Incident Containment Trade-Off

Containment can itself create business or operational consequences.

Organizations should consider:

- service availability;
- customer impact;
- safety;
- business continuity;
- evidence preservation;
- reversibility.

### 31. AI Incident Containment Reversibility

Containment actions should be evaluated according to whether they can be reversed safely.

Irreversible actions should generally require stronger authorization where circumstances permit.

### 32. AI Incident Containment Validation

After containment, the organization should verify that the intended condition was achieved.

Validation may include:

- confirming access was removed;
- verifying network isolation;
- checking that traffic stopped;
- confirming model removal;
- validating credential invalidation.

### 33. AI Incident Containment Effectiveness

Containment effectiveness should consider whether:

- propagation stopped;
- additional impact was prevented;
- affected assets remained isolated;
- attackers or defective processes retained access.

### 34. AI Incident Containment Failure

Containment failure may occur when:

- the incident continues;
- propagation continues;
- alternative paths remain available;
- compromised credentials remain active;
- affected assets retain unauthorized access.

Containment failure may require escalation and broader containment.

### 39. AI Incident Eradication Scope

Eradication should address the known or reasonably suspected source and persistence mechanisms of the incident.

Potential scope includes:

- malicious code;
- compromised accounts;
- malicious prompts;
- poisoned datasets;
- compromised model artifacts;
- vulnerable dependencies.

### 40. AI Incident Root-Cause Eradication

Where technically possible, eradication should address root causes rather than only visible symptoms.

For example, replacing a compromised credential without correcting the access-control weakness may leave the underlying condition unresolved.

### 41. AI Incident Persistence Removal

Eradication should identify and remove mechanisms that allow an incident to persist.

Potential persistence mechanisms include:

- unauthorized accounts;
- scheduled tasks;
- malicious configuration;
- compromised agents;
- injected instructions;
- malicious dependencies.

### 42. AI Incident Malware and Malicious Artifact Removal

Where malicious software or artifacts are involved, eradication should remove the malicious components from affected environments.

Removal should be validated rather than assumed.

### 43. AI Incident Unauthorized Access Removal

Eradication should eliminate unauthorized access paths, including:

- compromised credentials;
- unauthorized accounts;
- excessive permissions;
- exposed interfaces.

### 44. AI Incident Model Artifact Eradication

Where a model artifact is compromised, eradication may require:

- removing the compromised artifact;
- identifying the trusted version;
- validating provenance;
- verifying integrity;
- controlling redeployment.

### 45. AI Incident Dataset Eradication

Where poisoning or unauthorized modification is confirmed, eradication may require:

- removing contaminated records;
- restoring a trusted dataset;
- identifying affected training data;
- retraining affected models.

### 46. AI Incident Configuration Eradication

Eradication may require correcting unauthorized or unsafe configurations.

Configuration remediation should be validated against the approved trusted state.

### 47. AI Incident Dependency Eradication

Where a vulnerable or compromised dependency causes the incident, eradication may require:

- patching;
- replacement;
- removal;
- version rollback;
- provider substitution.

### 48. AI Incident Eradication Evidence

Eradication should maintain evidence demonstrating:

- what was removed;
- what was changed;
- why the change was necessary;
- who authorized it;
- how effectiveness was validated.

### 49. AI Incident Containment and Eradication Governance Principle

**AI incident containment and eradication should limit ongoing impact while preserving evidence and progressively removing the underlying cause or persistence mechanism, with actions proportionate to incident risk and subject to appropriate authority, validation, and accountability.**

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Containment and Eradication

**Containment is not eradication, eradication is not recovery, and technical remediation is not equivalent to trusted recovery.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- containment ≠ eradication;
- eradication ≠ recovery;
- containment ≠ incident closure;
- isolation ≠ eradication;
- quarantine ≠ remediation;
- credential revocation ≠ root-cause removal;
- model replacement ≠ behavioral assurance;
- dataset restoration ≠ proof that contamination has been eliminated;
- patching ≠ complete eradication;
- rollback ≠ complete remediation;
- service restoration ≠ trusted recovery;
- containment success ≠ incident resolution;
- evidence preservation ≠ incident eradication;
- internal containment threshold ≠ statutory requirement;
- internal eradication procedure ≠ legal obligation;
- supplier remediation ≠ fulfillment of the organization's own legal obligations;
- internal governance control ≠ statutory requirement.
