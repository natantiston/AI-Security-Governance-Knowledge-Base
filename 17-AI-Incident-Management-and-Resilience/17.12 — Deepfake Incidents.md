# 17.12 — Deepfake Incidents

## Part 1 — AI Deepfake Incident Foundations, Synthetic Media Concepts, Authenticity, Deception, Impact and Governance

### 1. AI Deepfake Incident

An **AI Deepfake Incident** is an event involving synthetic or manipulated audio, video, images, or other media that creates a material security, fraud, privacy, safety, operational, reputational, or governance impact.

Deepfake incidents may involve:

- fabricated identity;
- manipulated identity;
- synthetic voice;
- synthetic video;
- synthetic images;
- impersonation;
- fraudulent communications;
- manipulated evidence;
- unauthorized synthetic representations.

A synthetic-media event does not automatically constitute an incident. Incident classification should consider materiality, impact, intent, authorization, and applicable organizational criteria.

### 2. Objective of AI Deepfake Incident Management

The objective is to establish a structured capability to:

- identify suspected synthetic media;
- distinguish manipulation from legitimate media;
- assess authenticity;
- determine potential impact;
- contain fraudulent or harmful activity;
- preserve evidence;
- investigate origin and distribution;
- coordinate response;
- support recovery;
- reduce recurrence.

The objective is not to assume that every unusual or technically imperfect media artifact is a deepfake.

### 3. AI Deepfake

An **AI Deepfake** is synthetic or manipulated media generated or altered using AI techniques in a manner that can create a misleading representation of a person, event, statement, action, or environment.

Deepfakes can involve:

- face synthesis;
- face replacement;
- voice synthesis;
- lip synchronization;
- image generation;
- video generation;
- identity manipulation.

The term should be applied carefully because conventional editing and non-AI manipulation can produce similar effects.

### 4. AI Deepfake Event

A **Deepfake Event** is an observed or reported instance of suspected synthetic or manipulated media.

Examples include:

- suspicious video;
- synthetic voice call;
- manipulated photograph;
- fabricated executive message;
- altered recording.

A deepfake event becomes an incident when defined organizational impact or risk thresholds are met.

### 5. AI Deepfake Incident versus Synthetic-Media Event

A synthetic-media event may be benign or authorized.

Examples include:

- entertainment;
- advertising;
- research;
- authorized testing;
- accessibility applications;
- legitimate creative production.

The existence of synthetic media alone does not establish abuse or incident status.

### 6. AI Deepfake Incident versus Media Manipulation

Media manipulation is broader than deepfakes.

It can include:

- conventional editing;
- cropping;
- splicing;
- selective presentation;
- AI-generated content;
- AI-assisted modification.

Deepfake governance should therefore avoid assuming that all manipulated media was generated by AI.

### 7. AI Deepfake Incident versus Misinformation

**Misinformation** generally concerns false or inaccurate information that is communicated without necessarily establishing an intent to deceive.

A deepfake can be used to distribute misinformation, but the concepts are not equivalent.

### 8. AI Deepfake Incident versus Disinformation

**Disinformation** generally refers to false or misleading information deliberately created or distributed to deceive or manipulate.

A deepfake can be an instrument of disinformation, but a synthetic media artifact does not by itself establish the actor's intent.

### 9. AI Deepfake Incident versus Impersonation

Impersonation involves representing oneself as another person or creating the appearance that another person performed an action or made a statement.

Deepfake technology can facilitate impersonation through:

- synthetic voice;
- face replacement;
- fabricated video;
- generated images.

Impersonation can also occur without deepfake technology.

### 10. AI Deepfake Incident versus Identity Fraud

Identity fraud involves deceptive use of identity information or identity representation.

A deepfake may facilitate identity fraud, but deepfake detection and identity-fraud detection address different dimensions of the problem.

### 11. AI Deepfake Incident versus Social Engineering

Social engineering manipulates people into taking actions or disclosing information.

Deepfakes may strengthen social-engineering attacks by making communications appear to originate from:

- executives;
- customers;
- family members;
- suppliers;
- government officials.

Deepfake technology is therefore a potential social-engineering mechanism rather than a synonym for social engineering.

### 12. AI Deepfake Incident versus Fraud

Fraud involves deceptive conduct intended to obtain an unauthorized benefit or cause loss.

Deepfakes may be used as:

- evidence fabrication;
- impersonation mechanisms;
- payment-fraud mechanisms;
- account-recovery mechanisms;
- investment-fraud mechanisms.

Whether conduct constitutes fraud is a legal and factual determination.

### 13. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Security Incident

A deepfake incident can have security consequences without necessarily involving compromise of the organization's technical infrastructure.

For example:

- an attacker may impersonate an executive using synthetic audio;
- the organization may authorize a fraudulent payment;
- no internal system may have been technically compromised.

Conversely, a compromised system may generate deepfake content as part of a broader security incident.

### 14. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Privacy Incident

A deepfake incident can create privacy implications where a person's likeness, voice, image, biometric characteristics, or personal information is used without appropriate authorization.

However, not every deepfake incident is a privacy incident.

Privacy assessment should separately consider:

- whether personal data is involved;
- the applicable processing context;
- authorization;
- disclosure;
- purpose;
- applicable legal requirements.

### 15. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Safety Incident

A deepfake may create safety consequences when synthetic media causes a person or system to take an unsafe action.

Examples may include:

- fabricated emergency instructions;
- false safety warnings;
- manipulated operational communications.

Safety impact should be assessed independently from media authenticity.

### 16. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Model Compromise

A deepfake can be generated by an uncompromised AI model.

Conversely, a compromised model could be used to generate deepfakes.

Therefore:

- deepfake ≠ model compromise;
- model compromise ≠ deepfake;
- the two can occur together.

### 17. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Abuse and Misuse

Deepfake technology may be misused, but authorized synthetic media production is not automatically misuse.

Examples of potentially legitimate use include:

- film production;
- education;
- research;
- accessibility;
- authorized security exercises.

### 18. AI Deepfake Incident versus AI Hallucination

Hallucination concerns model-generated information that is inaccurate, unsupported, or fabricated.

A deepfake concerns synthetic or manipulated media.

A hallucinating AI system can generate a false textual statement without producing a deepfake.

### 19. AI Deepfake Incident versus Fabricated Evidence

Fabricated evidence is information created or altered to falsely represent facts or events.

Deepfakes can be used to fabricate evidence, but evidence fabrication can also occur without synthetic media.

### 20. AI Deepfake Incident versus Authentic Media

Authentic media is media whose relevant provenance and integrity can be appropriately established for the intended purpose.

Authenticity should not be inferred merely because:

- the media looks realistic;
- the file is high quality;
- the source is familiar;
- metadata appears normal.

### 21. AI Deepfake Authenticity

**Authenticity** concerns whether media genuinely represents what it purports to represent.

Authenticity assessment may consider:

- source;
- provenance;
- integrity;
- acquisition method;
- chain of custody;
- corroborating evidence.

Authenticity is different from whether the media file itself has remained unchanged since acquisition.

### 22. AI Deepfake Integrity

Media integrity concerns whether the relevant artifact has been altered after a defined point of trust.

Cryptographic integrity can establish whether a file has changed relative to a reference value.

It does not necessarily establish that the original file was authentic.

### 23. AI Deepfake Provenance

Provenance describes the history and origin of media.

Relevant information may include:

- creator;
- capture device;
- acquisition time;
- processing history;
- editing history;
- transmission path;
- storage history.

Provenance claims require appropriate evidence.

### 24. AI Deepfake Source Authenticity

Source authenticity concerns whether the purported source of media can be appropriately established.

A trusted communication channel does not automatically prove that the media content itself is authentic.

### 25. AI Deepfake Capture Authenticity

Capture authenticity concerns whether media originated from the claimed capture event or device.

Potential evidence may include:

- device records;
- secure capture mechanisms;
- timestamps;
- provenance metadata;
- independent corroboration.

### 26. AI Deepfake Content Authenticity

Content authenticity concerns whether the content accurately represents the underlying person, event, environment, or statement it claims to depict.

A file can be authentic as an artifact while its represented event remains misleading.

### 27. AI Deepfake Identity Representation

Identity representation concerns whether a media artifact genuinely represents the person it appears to depict or the person claimed by the source.

Deepfake incidents often exploit weaknesses in identity representation.

### 28. AI Deepfake Facial Manipulation

Facial manipulation modifies or synthesizes visual representations of faces.

Techniques may include:

- face replacement;
- facial reenactment;
- expression synthesis;
- identity transfer;
- generated faces.

Detection should not rely on one visual characteristic alone.

### 29. AI Deepfake Voice Synthesis

Voice synthesis creates or modifies speech to resemble a target speaker.

Potential risks include:

- executive impersonation;
- account recovery fraud;
- payment fraud;
- social engineering;
- fabricated statements.

Voice similarity alone does not establish speaker identity.

### 30. AI Deepfake Video Synthesis

Video synthesis may generate or modify:

- facial appearance;
- speech;
- gestures;
- expressions;
- scenes;
- identities.

Video realism should not be treated as proof of authenticity.

### 31. AI Deepfake Image Synthesis

AI-generated or manipulated images can create false representations of:

- people;
- documents;
- locations;
- events;
- products;
- evidence.

### 32. AI Deepfake Audio Manipulation

Audio manipulation may involve:

- voice conversion;
- speech synthesis;
- editing;
- cloning;
- splicing.

Detection should consider both technical and contextual evidence.

### 33. AI Deepfake Lip-Synchronization Manipulation

Lip-synchronization manipulation modifies visual mouth movements to correspond to generated or altered speech.

This can make synthetic speech appear visually consistent with an authentic speaker.

### 34. AI Deepfake Face-Swap Manipulation

Face swapping replaces the facial representation of one person with another.

It can be used for:

- entertainment;
- authorized testing;
- impersonation;
- fraud;
- deceptive communications.

### 35. AI Deepfake Identity Cloning

Identity cloning attempts to reproduce recognizable characteristics of a person, potentially including:

- face;
- voice;
- mannerisms;
- communication style.

Identity cloning can increase social-engineering risk.

### 36. AI Deepfake Executive Impersonation

Executive impersonation uses synthetic or manipulated media to create the appearance that an executive has issued an instruction or communication.

Potential consequences include:

- unauthorized payments;
- credential disclosure;
- data disclosure;
- emergency actions;
- reputational impact.

### 37. AI Deepfake Customer Impersonation

Customer impersonation may use synthetic media to influence:

- support operations;
- account recovery;
- identity verification;
- financial transactions.

### 38. AI Deepfake Supplier Impersonation

Supplier impersonation can use synthetic voice, video, or imagery to create the appearance that a trusted supplier issued a request.

This may target:

- payment instructions;
- credentials;
- contracts;
- system access.

### 39. AI Deepfake Government or Authority Impersonation

Synthetic media may be used to imitate government officials, regulators, law-enforcement personnel, or other authority figures.

Such claims should be independently verified when consequential actions are requested.

### 40. AI Deepfake Authentication Risk

Deepfakes can undermine authentication mechanisms that rely heavily on:

- facial appearance;
- voice;
- video;
- behavioral characteristics.

A biometric or audiovisual signal should therefore be evaluated according to the security architecture in which it is used.

### 41. AI Deepfake Identity Verification Risk

Identity verification systems may be targeted by:

- synthetic faces;
- replay attacks;
- synthetic voices;
- manipulated documents;
- generated video.

The relevant assurance level depends on the identity-verification method and threat model.

### 42. AI Deepfake Liveness Risk

Liveness controls attempt to distinguish a live person from:

- recordings;
- generated images;
- synthetic video;
- replayed media.

Liveness is a control mechanism, not proof that the person is authorized.

### 43. AI Deepfake Social-Engineering Risk

Deepfakes can increase the credibility of social-engineering attempts by providing audiovisual cues that appear familiar.

Risk may increase when combined with:

- urgency;
- authority;
- secrecy;
- financial pressure;
- contextual information.

### 44. AI Deepfake Fraud Risk

Deepfake fraud risk concerns the possibility that synthetic media facilitates deceptive financial or commercial activity.

Assessment should consider:

- transaction authority;
- verification controls;
- reversibility;
- financial exposure;
- independent confirmation.

### 45. AI Deepfake Reputational Risk

Deepfake incidents may create reputational harm when false media is attributed to an individual or organization.

Impact can depend on:

- audience;
- distribution;
- perceived authenticity;
- subject matter;
- duration;
- speed of correction.

### 46. AI Deepfake Propagation

Propagation describes how synthetic or manipulated media spreads across:

- social platforms;
- messaging systems;
- email;
- websites;
- news channels;
- internal systems.

Propagation may increase impact even after the original source has been contained.

### 47. AI Deepfake Blast Radius

Blast radius represents the population, systems, organizations, or processes potentially affected by a deepfake incident.

Factors may include:

- distribution scale;
- audience;
- identity involved;
- downstream automation;
- media persistence.

### 48. AI Deepfake Evidence

Relevant evidence may include:

- original media;
- copies;
- metadata;
- provenance records;
- communication records;
- platform information;
- authentication logs;
- transaction records;
- independent corroboration.

Evidence preservation should account for applicable privacy, legal, security, and retention requirements.

### 49. AI Deepfake Incident Governance Principle

**Deepfake incident management should treat media authenticity as an evidence-assessment problem rather than relying solely on visual or auditory realism or on a single detection technology.**

Assessment should consider:

- provenance;
- integrity;
- source;
- context;
- corroboration;
- identity;
- technical analysis;
- downstream impact.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Deepfake Incidents

**The presence of realistic synthetic media does not by itself establish deception, malicious intent, fraud, or legal violation. Deepfake incident management should establish authenticity, authorization, context, impact, and evidence before assigning the appropriate incident classification.**

The following distinctions are foundational:

- synthetic media ≠ automatically a deepfake incident;
- deepfake ≠ automatically malicious;
- deepfake ≠ automatically illegal;
- deepfake ≠ misinformation;
- deepfake ≠ disinformation;
- deepfake ≠ impersonation;
- deepfake ≠ fraud;
- deepfake ≠ social engineering;
- deepfake ≠ privacy incident;
- deepfake ≠ security incident;
- deepfake ≠ model compromise;
- media integrity ≠ media authenticity;
- provenance ≠ proof of authenticity;
- source authenticity ≠ content authenticity;
- detection signal ≠ confirmed deepfake;
- technical anomaly ≠ malicious intent;
- internal incident classification ≠ legal classification;
- internal governance control ≠ statutory requirement.

Deepfake detection, provenance controls, media-authenticity assessment, identity-verification controls, monitoring, incident classification, evidence preservation, and response procedures are generally **governance or implementation practices** unless a specific applicable law, regulation, contractual obligation, or authoritative requirement mandates a particular activity.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
