# 17.07 — Hallucination Incidents

## Part 1 — AI Hallucination Incident Foundations, Hallucination Concepts, Failure Boundaries, Impact and Governance

### 1. AI Hallucination Incident

An AI Hallucination Incident is an event in which an AI system generates, presents, retrieves, or propagates information that is materially unsupported, fabricated, misleading, or incorrect in a manner that creates an incident-level impact within its intended operating context.

A hallucination does not automatically constitute an incident.

The organization should evaluate:

- materiality;
- use case;
- user reliance;
- affected population;
- severity;
- frequency;
- detectability;
- reversibility;
- downstream consequences; and
- applicable requirements.

A hallucination in a low-risk creative application may be an ordinary model-quality issue, while the same behavior in a critical decision-support process may constitute a material AI incident.

### 2. Objective of AI Hallucination Incident Management

The objective of AI Hallucination Incident Management is to identify, assess, contain, investigate, remediate, and learn from hallucination events that create material operational, security, safety, privacy, financial, compliance, or customer impact.

The capability should enable the organization to:

- detect hallucination patterns;
- distinguish isolated model error from material incident;
- identify affected outputs and users;
- determine whether the information was unsupported or incorrect;
- assess downstream reliance;
- contain further propagation;
- preserve relevant evidence;
- identify contributing factors;
- validate remediation;
- assess recurrence risk; and
- improve model and governance controls.

### 3. AI Hallucination

AI Hallucination is a model-generated output that contains information that is unsupported by available evidence, fabricated, materially inaccurate, or presented as factual without adequate basis.

Hallucination may include:

- fabricated facts;
- nonexistent citations;
- invented sources;
- incorrect calculations;
- fabricated quotations;
- false claims about events;
- nonexistent entities;
- inaccurate summaries;
- unsupported conclusions; or
- invented technical details.

The term is commonly used in AI governance and engineering, but its exact operational definition should be established by the organization for each AI system.

### 4. AI Hallucination Event

An AI Hallucination Event is an occurrence in which an AI system produces a potentially hallucinated output.

An event does not automatically become an incident.

The distinction may be represented as:

```text id="a7k4pd"
AI OUTPUT
    |
    v
Potentially Unsupported Information
    |
    v
Validation
    |
    +------> Supported / Acceptable
    |
    v
Unsupported / Incorrect
    |
    v
Material Impact?
    |
  NO -----------------> Model Quality Issue
    |
   YES
    |
    v
AI Hallucination Incident
```

### 5. AI Hallucination Incident versus AI Model Failure

A hallucination incident is a specific type of model-behavior problem involving unsupported or incorrect information.

Model failure is broader.

A model may fail through:

- availability loss;
- incorrect classification;
- unsafe behavior;
- security-control failure;
- excessive latency;
- integration failure; or
- configuration failure.

Therefore:

**Hallucination incident ⊂ potential model-failure conditions.**

Not every model failure involves hallucination.

### 6. AI Hallucination Incident versus AI Security Incident

A hallucination incident is not automatically a cybersecurity incident.

For example, a language model may fabricate a citation because of ordinary generative behavior without being compromised.

However, hallucination can occur as a consequence of security events such as:

- prompt injection;
- retrieval manipulation;
- poisoned data;
- compromised model artifacts;
- malicious context;
- unauthorized configuration changes; or
- compromised external tools.

Security assessment should therefore be performed where evidence indicates a potential security cause.

### 7. AI Hallucination Incident versus AI Privacy Incident

A hallucination can create a privacy incident when it:

- fabricates personal information;
- exposes actual personal information;
- incorrectly attributes information to an individual;
- produces sensitive information about an identifiable person; or
- causes unauthorized disclosure through downstream use.

However, hallucination itself does not automatically establish a privacy incident.

Privacy classification requires assessment of the actual information, processing activity, affected individuals, applicable law, and circumstances.

### 8. AI Hallucination Incident versus AI Safety Incident

Hallucination can create safety consequences where users rely on incorrect information in a safety-sensitive context.

Examples may include:

- incorrect operational instructions;
- incorrect safety procedures;
- fabricated emergency information;
- unsafe recommendations; or
- incorrect information used in safety-critical workflows.

Whether a particular event constitutes a legally relevant safety incident depends on the applicable sector, jurisdiction, system, and facts.

### 9. AI Hallucination Incident versus Misinformation

Misinformation is information that is false or inaccurate.

Hallucination specifically concerns AI-generated or AI-mediated production of unsupported or fabricated information.

An organization should therefore avoid using the terms interchangeably without defining them.

A hallucination may become misinformation when the output is distributed to other people or systems, but not every hallucination necessarily reaches another recipient.

### 10. AI Hallucination versus Disinformation

Disinformation generally involves intentionally deceptive or misleading information.

AI hallucination does not inherently involve intent.

A model may generate false information without any intention to deceive.

Therefore:

**Hallucination ≠ disinformation.**

Intentional misuse of hallucinated outputs may create a separate abuse, fraud, manipulation, or security concern.

### 11. AI Hallucination versus Fabrication

Fabrication refers to the creation of information that does not correspond to an actual underlying fact, source, event, person, citation, or artifact.

In generative AI, fabrication can be a manifestation of hallucination.

Examples include:

- nonexistent references;
- invented legal cases;
- fabricated statistics;
- invented quotations;
- fictional organizations presented as real; or
- nonexistent technical features.

### 12. AI Hallucination versus Inaccuracy

Inaccuracy is information that is incorrect relative to a reliable reference.

Hallucination is generally associated with unsupported or fabricated generation.

A model can therefore produce an inaccurate answer without the answer necessarily representing a hallucination in the narrow sense.

Organizations should define their classification criteria to avoid inconsistent incident reporting.

### 13. AI Hallucination versus Unsupported Claim

An Unsupported Claim is a claim for which sufficient evidence has not been established.

An unsupported claim may be:

- true but unverified;
- false;
- incomplete;
- outdated; or
- contextually misleading.

Therefore, evidence status should be assessed before concluding that an unsupported statement is definitively fabricated.

### 14. AI Hallucination versus Factual Error

A Factual Error is an objectively incorrect statement.

A hallucination may contain factual errors, but the concepts are not necessarily identical.

For governance purposes, the organization may classify hallucinations according to:

- factual correctness;
- evidentiary support;
- materiality;
- source availability;
- confidence;
- downstream reliance.

### 15. AI Hallucination versus Ambiguity

Ambiguity occurs when available information permits multiple reasonable interpretations.

An ambiguous answer is not necessarily a hallucination.

For example, where a question legitimately has several interpretations, selecting one interpretation may be an ordinary model behavior.

A hallucination occurs when the model introduces unsupported information or represents an unsupported interpretation as established fact.

### 16. AI Hallucination versus Model Uncertainty

Model Uncertainty concerns the model's lack of confidence or inability to determine the correct answer reliably.

Hallucination can occur when the model produces a confident answer despite insufficient knowledge or evidence.

Therefore:

**Uncertainty ≠ hallucination.**

However, inadequate uncertainty representation can increase hallucination risk because users may incorrectly interpret generated information as reliable.

### 17. AI Hallucination Confidence Failure

Confidence Failure occurs when the AI system presents unreliable or unsupported information with an inappropriate level of confidence.

Examples include:

- asserting uncertain information as fact;
- presenting invented citations confidently;
- failing to identify knowledge limitations;
- suppressing uncertainty indicators; or
- providing highly specific unsupported details.

Confidence failure can increase the probability of user reliance.

### 18. AI Hallucination Source Failure

Source Failure occurs when an AI system relies on nonexistent, inappropriate, outdated, inaccessible, or unreliable sources.

Examples include:

- fabricated citations;
- incorrect URLs;
- nonexistent publications;
- obsolete documentation;
- irrelevant sources; or
- sources that do not support the generated claim.

Source verification should therefore be separated from the model's own confidence.

### 19. AI Hallucination Citation Failure

Citation Failure occurs when an AI system provides citations that:

- do not exist;
- do not support the claim;
- point to the wrong source;
- contain incorrect metadata;
- refer to an unrelated passage; or
- are otherwise misleading.

Citation presence alone does not establish evidentiary reliability.

A citation should be evaluated for both existence and substantive support.

### 20. AI Hallucination Retrieval Failure

Retrieval Failure occurs when an AI system retrieves incorrect, irrelevant, incomplete, outdated, or unauthorized information and uses it to generate an answer.

Possible causes include:

- poor retrieval ranking;
- stale documents;
- incorrect indexing;
- inadequate access controls;
- semantic mismatch;
- incomplete corpus; or
- corrupted retrieval data.

Retrieval failure can create hallucination-like outputs even when the underlying language model is functioning normally.

### 21. AI Hallucination Grounding Failure

Grounding Failure occurs when an AI system does not adequately constrain its output to reliable information available from designated sources.

Grounding mechanisms may include:

- retrieval-augmented generation;
- approved knowledge bases;
- structured databases;
- verified documentation;
- source citations;
- tool-based verification; and
- deterministic checks.

Grounding reduces some hallucination risks but does not guarantee factual correctness.

### 22. AI Hallucination Verification Failure

Verification Failure occurs when an organization fails to identify or correct a materially hallucinated output before it causes consequential impact.

Verification may involve:

- source checking;
- rule validation;
- automated consistency checks;
- human review;
- retrieval comparison;
- external reference comparison; or
- downstream validation.

Verification requirements should reflect the risk of the use case.

### 23. AI Hallucination Detection

AI Hallucination Detection identifies outputs that may contain unsupported, fabricated, or materially inaccurate information.

Detection methods may include:

- retrieval comparison;
- fact checking;
- reference matching;
- consistency analysis;
- confidence analysis;
- anomaly detection;
- user feedback; and
- human review.

No single detection mechanism should be assumed to identify all hallucinations.

### 24. AI Hallucination Detection Failure

Detection Failure occurs when a hallucinated output remains undetected despite controls that should reasonably have identified it.

Possible causes include:

- inadequate validation;
- poor monitoring;
- insufficient source coverage;
- detection-model errors;
- excessive confidence;
- incomplete sampling; or
- novel hallucination patterns.

Detection failure should be distinguished from hallucination itself.

### 25. AI Hallucination Propagation

Hallucination Propagation occurs when an incorrect or unsupported AI-generated output is transferred into another system, document, decision, model, dataset, or communication.

Potential propagation paths include:

```text id="n6r8qt"
AI MODEL
   |
   v
GENERATED OUTPUT
   |
   v
HUMAN / APPLICATION
   |
   v
DOCUMENT / DATABASE
   |
   v
DOWNSTREAM AI / DECISION
   |
   v
CUSTOMER / BUSINESS PROCESS
```

Propagation can increase the difficulty and cost of remediation.

### 26. AI Hallucination Contamination

Hallucination Contamination occurs when incorrect AI-generated information becomes embedded into a dataset, knowledge base, documentation repository, workflow, or subsequent AI system.

Examples include:

- hallucinated text added to a knowledge base;
- fabricated records stored in a database;
- incorrect summaries used as source material;
- synthetic data containing unsupported facts; or
- generated content used for subsequent model training.

Contamination can cause repeated or amplified errors.

### 27. AI Hallucination Feedback Loop

A Hallucination Feedback Loop occurs when AI-generated misinformation becomes part of the information environment subsequently used by the same or another AI system.

For example:

```text id="q4t8mc"
MODEL A
   |
   v
HALLUCINATED OUTPUT
   |
   v
KNOWLEDGE BASE
   |
   v
RETRIEVAL
   |
   v
MODEL B
   |
   v
REPEATED / AMPLIFIED CLAIM
```

Such loops can make false information appear increasingly credible through repetition.

### 28. AI Hallucination Ground-Truth Reference

A Ground-Truth Reference is an authoritative or sufficiently reliable reference used to evaluate whether an AI output is factually or evidentially correct.

Potential references include:

- authoritative databases;
- approved corporate records;
- primary-source documents;
- verified datasets;
- official technical documentation; and
- validated expert-reviewed information.

Ground truth should be appropriate to the specific domain and question.

### 29. AI Hallucination Ground-Truth Availability

Ground-Truth Availability describes whether an appropriate reference exists against which an AI output can be evaluated.

Some tasks have:

- objective ground truth;
- incomplete ground truth;
- disputed information;
- evolving information; or
- no readily available authoritative reference.

Hallucination detection becomes more difficult when reliable ground truth is unavailable.

### 30. AI Hallucination Factuality Assessment

Factuality Assessment evaluates whether an AI-generated statement corresponds to available reliable evidence.

Assessment may consider:

- factual correctness;
- source quality;
- source relevance;
- temporal validity;
- context;
- completeness; and
- uncertainty.

Factuality assessment should distinguish objectively verifiable claims from opinions, predictions, or inherently uncertain statements.

### 31. AI Hallucination Evidence Assessment

Evidence Assessment determines how strongly available evidence supports an AI-generated claim.

A useful evidence hierarchy may distinguish:

```text id="u2x7mv"
PRIMARY / AUTHORITATIVE SOURCE
          |
          v
VERIFIED SECONDARY SOURCE
          |
          v
RELIABLE REFERENCE
          |
          v
UNVERIFIED SOURCE
          |
          v
NO SUPPORTING EVIDENCE
```

The hierarchy should be adapted to the domain.

### 32. AI Hallucination Source Reliability

Source Reliability evaluates whether a source is sufficiently trustworthy for the specific claim being assessed.

Factors may include:

- authority;
- provenance;
- recency;
- independence;
- methodology;
- consistency; and
- relevance.

A source being accessible or popular does not automatically make it reliable.

### 33. AI Hallucination Temporal Validity

Temporal Validity assesses whether information was correct for the relevant time period.

An AI output may appear factual but be outdated because:

- laws changed;
- prices changed;
- personnel changed;
- products changed;
- technical documentation changed;
- events occurred after the source was created.

Time-sensitive claims therefore require appropriate temporal validation.

### 34. AI Hallucination Contextual Accuracy

Contextual Accuracy evaluates whether an otherwise factual statement is appropriate for the question and operating context.

A statement may be factually true but misleading when:

- important qualifications are omitted;
- the context differs;
- the information applies to another jurisdiction;
- the date differs;
- the population differs; or
- the use case differs.

### 35. AI Hallucination Completeness Failure

Completeness Failure occurs when an AI output omits material information necessary for a correct understanding of the issue.

An answer can therefore be composed entirely of individually true statements while still producing a materially misleading result through omission.

Completeness should be assessed according to the intended use.

### 36. AI Hallucination Misleading Output

A Misleading Output is an AI-generated result that causes a reasonable user to form an incorrect understanding, even if individual statements are not necessarily fabricated.

Possible causes include:

- omission;
- framing;
- unsupported certainty;
- selective information;
- incorrect context; or
- aggregation errors.

Misleading output should be distinguished from intentional deception.

### 37. AI Hallucination User Reliance

User Reliance is the extent to which a person or system uses an AI-generated output as a basis for:

- decision-making;
- communication;
- action;
- analysis;
- documentation; or
- further processing.

Reliance is an important determinant of incident severity.

A hallucination that is immediately rejected may have limited impact, while the same hallucination incorporated into a consequential decision may become materially significant.

### 38. AI Hallucination Downstream Reliance

Downstream Reliance occurs when another system, process, person, or model relies on the hallucinated output.

Examples include:

- automated workflows;
- reports;
- customer communications;
- risk assessments;
- knowledge bases;
- databases;
- subsequent AI prompts; and
- business decisions.

Downstream reliance can substantially increase incident scope.

### 39. AI Hallucination Decision Impact

Decision Impact measures the consequences of a hallucinated output when it influences a decision.

Potential impacts include:

- financial;
- operational;
- legal;
- safety;
- privacy;
- security;
- customer; and
- reputational consequences.

Decision impact should be assessed based on actual or reasonably foreseeable consequences.

### 40. AI Hallucination Materiality

Hallucination Materiality determines whether a hallucination is significant enough to require incident-level management.

Materiality may consider:

- severity;
- reliance;
- affected population;
- frequency;
- persistence;
- reversibility;
- downstream propagation;
- business criticality; and
- potential harm.

Materiality thresholds are organizational unless an applicable authority establishes a specific mandatory threshold.

### 41. AI Hallucination Severity

Hallucination Severity reflects the seriousness of the consequences associated with the hallucination.

Severity may consider:

- correctness;
- confidence;
- user reliance;
- decision impact;
- affected population;
- persistence;
- detectability;
- reversibility;
- safety;
- privacy; and
- security.

Severity should not be determined solely by the number of hallucinated statements.

### 42. AI Hallucination Scope

Hallucination Scope identifies the systems, outputs, users, transactions, time periods, and processes potentially affected.

Scope may include:

- one response;
- one user;
- one model version;
- one business unit;
- multiple applications;
- multiple customers; or
- an enterprise-wide AI service.

Scope should be updated as investigation evidence develops.

### 43. AI Hallucination Blast Radius

Blast Radius describes the maximum or observed extent to which hallucinated information can affect connected systems, processes, users, or decisions.

Factors include:

- automation;
- integration;
- downstream systems;
- shared knowledge bases;
- model reuse;
- user population; and
- propagation mechanisms.

A highly connected AI system can have a larger blast radius than an isolated model even when its initial hallucination rate is similar.

### 44. AI Hallucination Persistence

Persistence describes how long hallucinated information remains available or capable of causing impact.

Persistence may occur when information is:

- stored;
- indexed;
- cached;
- copied;
- published;
- embedded into datasets; or
- incorporated into subsequent models.

Persistent hallucinations may require broader remediation than transient outputs.

### 45. AI Hallucination Recurrence

Recurrence occurs when substantially similar hallucinations repeatedly occur after detection or remediation.

Recurrence may indicate:

- unresolved model limitations;
- inadequate grounding;
- weak validation;
- insufficient monitoring;
- poor remediation; or
- systemic architecture problems.

Recurrence should be assessed separately from overall hallucination frequency.

### 46. AI Hallucination Detectability

Detectability measures how readily a hallucination can be identified using available controls.

Detectability may be high when:

- ground truth is available;
- outputs are deterministic;
- source validation is possible;
- automated checks exist.

Detectability may be lower when:

- information is ambiguous;
- ground truth is unavailable;
- sources are incomplete;
- outputs are highly contextual.

Low detectability can increase residual risk.

### 47. AI Hallucination Reversibility

Reversibility measures whether the consequences of hallucination can be corrected or undone.

Highly reversible examples may include:

- an incorrect draft rejected before use;
- an internal answer corrected immediately.

Less reversible examples may include:

- incorrect customer communications;
- irreversible transactions;
- contaminated datasets;
- decisions that cannot easily be reversed.

Reversibility should influence severity and containment requirements.

### 48. AI Hallucination Governance Evidence

Governance Evidence demonstrates how the organization identifies, assesses, manages, and learns from hallucination incidents.

Evidence may include:

- monitoring records;
- validation results;
- incident records;
- output samples;
- source verification;
- investigation results;
- containment actions;
- corrective actions; and
- recovery validation.

Evidence should be proportionate to risk and subject to appropriate privacy and access controls.

### 49. AI Hallucination Incident Governance Principle

AI Hallucination Incident Governance should ensure that hallucinations are evaluated according to their actual context and consequences rather than treated uniformly as either harmless model errors or security incidents.

The governance approach should:

- establish a clear hallucination definition;
- distinguish events from incidents;
- assess factuality and evidence;
- determine user and downstream reliance;
- evaluate materiality;
- identify propagation;
- investigate technical and non-technical causes;
- contain material impact;
- validate remediation; and
- monitor recurrence.

Internal hallucination categories and thresholds should be clearly distinguished from legal or regulatory classifications.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Hallucination Incident Foundations

**An AI hallucination becomes an incident when unsupported, fabricated, or materially incorrect AI-generated information creates a sufficiently significant impact within the organization's defined risk context.**

The central governance distinctions are:

```text id="k8v3ps"
HALLUCINATION
     ≠
AUTOMATIC INCIDENT

HALLUCINATION
     ≠
CYBERSECURITY COMPROMISE

HALLUCINATION
     ≠
PRIVACY BREACH

HALLUCINATION
     ≠
SAFETY VIOLATION

HALLUCINATION
     ≠
REGULATORY VIOLATION
```

Each condition requires separate assessment against the applicable technical, operational, security, safety, privacy, contractual, and legal requirements.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations. Where hallucination-management requirements are legally mandatory, they should be separately mapped to the authoritative source, applicable jurisdiction, organizational role, affected activity, and effective date.

## Part 2 — AI Hallucination Incident Controls, Detection, Validation, Containment, Investigation and Remediation

### 51. AI Hallucination Incident Management Policy

An AI Hallucination Incident Management Policy establishes the organizational requirements for identifying, assessing, containing, investigating, remediating, and learning from material hallucination incidents.

The policy should define:

- hallucination terminology;
- incident thresholds;
- model and application scope;
- ownership;
- detection requirements;
- validation requirements;
- escalation;
- containment;
- evidence preservation;
- remediation;
- recovery;
- reporting; and
- exception management.

The policy should distinguish internal governance requirements from applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations.

### 52. AI Hallucination Incident Scope

AI Hallucination Incident Scope establishes which AI systems and use cases are subject to hallucination controls.

Scope may include:

- generative AI;
- conversational AI;
- retrieval-augmented generation;
- AI agents;
- recommendation systems;
- decision-support systems;
- AI APIs;
- embedded AI;
- internally developed models; and
- third-party models.

The scope should consider both internally hosted models and externally provided AI capabilities.

### 53. AI Hallucination Incident Register

An AI Hallucination Incident Register records material hallucination incidents and their associated investigation and remediation information.

Typical fields include:

- incident identifier;
- model;
- model version;
- application;
- date and time;
- hallucination type;
- affected output;
- source or evidence;
- severity;
- affected population;
- downstream impact;
- containment;
- root cause;
- corrective action;
- owner; and
- closure status.

The register provides historical evidence for recurrence and trend analysis.

### 54. AI Hallucination Incident Intake

AI Hallucination Incident Intake provides controlled mechanisms for reporting suspected hallucinations.

Potential sources include:

- users;
- customers;
- employees;
- model monitoring;
- application monitoring;
- security teams;
- privacy teams;
- quality teams;
- business operations;
- automated validation;
- suppliers; and
- internal audit.

The intake process should allow uncertain reports to be submitted without requiring the reporter to prove hallucination before investigation begins.

### 55. AI Hallucination Detection Control

An AI Hallucination Detection Control identifies outputs that may contain unsupported, fabricated, or materially inaccurate information.

Detection mechanisms may include:

- source verification;
- retrieval comparison;
- reference matching;
- factuality models;
- rule-based validation;
- consistency checks;
- user feedback;
- human review; and
- downstream validation.

Detection should be proportionate to the consequences of incorrect information.

### 56. AI Hallucination Sampling Control

Sampling Control evaluates a defined subset of model outputs for potential hallucinations.

Sampling may be:

- random;
- risk-based;
- event-triggered;
- population-based;
- model-version-based; or
- focused on high-risk use cases.

Sampling provides evidence about model behavior but cannot establish that unobserved outputs are free from hallucination.

### 57. AI Hallucination High-Risk Output Monitoring

High-Risk Output Monitoring focuses validation on outputs where hallucination could create significant consequences.

Examples may include:

- legal information;
- financial information;
- security instructions;
- medical information;
- safety instructions;
- customer decisions;
- regulatory information; and
- operational commands.

The organization should define high-risk categories according to its actual use cases.

### 58. AI Hallucination Source Verification

Source Verification determines whether the information presented by the AI is supported by an appropriate source.

Verification should consider:

- source existence;
- source authority;
- source relevance;
- source date;
- source context;
- source accessibility; and
- whether the source actually supports the claim.

A citation should not be treated as valid merely because a URL or document reference exists.

### 59. AI Hallucination Citation Validation

Citation Validation determines whether generated citations accurately correspond to the claims they are presented as supporting.

Validation may verify:

- citation existence;
- document identity;
- cited passage;
- publication date;
- source authority;
- claim-to-source relationship.

Fabricated or mismatched citations should be separately recorded because they may increase user reliance.

### 60. AI Hallucination Grounding Control

A Grounding Control constrains model responses using approved or verified information sources.

Grounding mechanisms may include:

- retrieval-augmented generation;
- approved knowledge repositories;
- structured databases;
- verified APIs;
- enterprise documents;
- authoritative external sources.

Grounding reduces some hallucination risks but does not guarantee correctness.

### 61. AI Hallucination Retrieval Validation

Retrieval Validation evaluates whether the information retrieved for generation is:

- relevant;
- current;
- authorized;
- complete;
- sufficiently reliable; and
- appropriate to the query.

Poor retrieval can produce hallucinated or misleading responses even where the model itself has not materially changed.

### 62. AI Hallucination Retrieval-Source Integrity

Retrieval-Source Integrity verifies that documents and information used by retrieval systems have not been improperly altered, replaced, corrupted, or contaminated.

Controls may include:

- document integrity checks;
- access control;
- provenance;
- versioning;
- source validation; and
- repository governance.

### 63. AI Hallucination Ground-Truth Control

Ground-Truth Control establishes reliable reference information for evaluating model outputs where objective verification is possible.

Ground truth may come from:

- authoritative records;
- approved databases;
- primary sources;
- verified technical documentation;
- validated datasets.

The organization should document cases where authoritative ground truth is unavailable.

### 64. AI Hallucination Fact-Checking Control

Fact-Checking Control evaluates claims against appropriate evidence before they are relied upon in defined high-risk contexts.

Fact checking may be:

- automated;
- human;
- hybrid;
- rule-based; or
- source-based.

The appropriate method depends on the consequence and complexity of the claim.

### 65. AI Hallucination Claim Validation

Claim Validation breaks generated responses into individual factual or substantive claims and evaluates them against available evidence.

A response may contain:

- supported claims;
- unsupported claims;
- incorrect claims;
- ambiguous claims;
- outdated claims.

This prevents an entire response from being treated as either completely correct or completely incorrect.

### 66. AI Hallucination Evidence-Confidence Assessment

Evidence-Confidence Assessment evaluates how strongly available evidence supports a generated claim.

Confidence should consider:

- source authority;
- source consistency;
- source recency;
- evidence completeness;
- corroboration;
- uncertainty.

High confidence should require stronger evidence where consequences are significant.

### 67. AI Hallucination Uncertainty Indicator

An Uncertainty Indicator communicates when an AI system lacks sufficient evidence or confidence to provide a reliable answer.

Possible mechanisms include:

- confidence scores;
- abstention;
- source availability;
- uncertainty statements;
- escalation;
- human review.

Uncertainty indicators should be validated because poorly calibrated confidence can itself create risk.

### 68. AI Hallucination Abstention Control

An Abstention Control allows an AI system to decline to provide a substantive answer when available evidence is insufficient.

Abstention may be appropriate when:

- evidence is missing;
- sources conflict;
- confidence is below threshold;
- the request exceeds system capability;
- the consequence of error is high.

Abstention is a control mechanism, not proof that hallucination has been eliminated.

### 69. AI Hallucination Human-Review Control

Human Review Control requires a qualified reviewer to validate selected AI outputs before they are relied upon.

Human review may be triggered by:

- high-risk use case;
- low confidence;
- unsupported claims;
- conflicting sources;
- unusual output;
- high-impact decision;
- detected hallucination.

Human reviewers should have sufficient expertise and authority to reject or correct the output.

### 70. AI Hallucination Dual-Validation Control

Dual Validation requires two independent validation mechanisms for selected high-risk outputs.

For example:

```text id="e8r5tm"
AI OUTPUT
    |
    +------> AUTOMATED SOURCE CHECK
    |
    +------> HUMAN REVIEW
    |
    v
VALIDATED OUTPUT
```

Dual validation can reduce dependence on a single detection mechanism.

### 71. AI Hallucination Output Filtering

Output Filtering evaluates generated content before delivery or downstream use.

Filters may identify:

- prohibited content;
- unsupported claims;
- sensitive information;
- malformed output;
- suspicious citations;
- policy violations.

Filtering should be evaluated for both false positives and false negatives.

### 72. AI Hallucination Response Blocking

Response Blocking prevents a potentially harmful or insufficiently verified output from reaching the user or downstream system.

Blocking may be appropriate where:

- confidence is below threshold;
- source validation fails;
- safety risk is high;
- privacy exposure is suspected;
- required human review is unavailable.
