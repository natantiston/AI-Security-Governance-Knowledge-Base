# 17.06 — AI Model Failure

## Part 1 — AI Model Failure Foundations, Failure Modes, Behavioral Impact, Detection Boundaries and Governance

### 1. AI Model Failure

AI Model Failure is a condition in which an AI model does not perform its intended function, produces materially incorrect or unreliable outputs, violates defined operational or security expectations, or behaves outside an approved operating envelope.

Model failure can arise without malicious activity. It may result from model defects, unsuitable data, distribution shift, model drift, configuration errors, integration problems, dependency failures, insufficient evaluation, unexpected inputs, inadequate guardrails, or interactions between the model and its operating environment.

An AI model failure should therefore be investigated independently from the assumption of cyberattack or compromise.

The organization should establish what constitutes a model failure for each AI system based on its intended purpose, expected behavior, criticality, acceptable error tolerance, safety constraints, security requirements, and business impact.

### 2. Objective of AI Model Failure Management

The objective of AI Model Failure Management is to identify, classify, contain, investigate, recover from, and learn from failures that affect the reliability, security, safety, privacy, compliance, or availability of an AI-enabled service.

A mature model-failure capability should enable the organization to:

- detect material deviations from expected model behavior;
- distinguish ordinary model error from material failure;
- determine whether a failure is isolated or systemic;
- identify affected models, versions, datasets, users, and services;
- contain harmful or unreliable model behavior;
- preserve evidence required for investigation;
- determine whether the failure resulted from the model or its surrounding system;
- restore an appropriately trusted operating state;
- assess residual risk after recovery;
- identify corrective and preventive actions; and
- incorporate lessons learned into model lifecycle governance.

Model-failure management is broader than model monitoring because a failure may only become apparent when model behavior interacts with a business process, human user, downstream system, or external dependency.

### 3. AI Model

An AI model is a computational representation used to generate predictions, classifications, recommendations, content, decisions, or other outputs from inputs.

For governance purposes, the model should be considered together with the relevant operational context, including:

- model version;
- training or fine-tuning data;
- prompts or input specifications;
- system instructions;
- retrieval components;
- tools and external services;
- inference infrastructure;
- configuration;
- safety controls;
- access controls;
- monitoring;
- human oversight; and
- downstream processes.

A model cannot always be assessed independently from this surrounding environment.

### 4. AI Model Behavior

AI Model Behavior is the observable manner in which a model responds to defined inputs and operating conditions.

Behavior may include:

- prediction accuracy;
- classification accuracy;
- generation quality;
- refusal behavior;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- response consistency;
- latency;
- tool selection;
- agent actions;
- data retrieval;
- policy adherence;
- safety behavior;
- security behavior; and
- interaction with human operators.

Expected behavior should be defined sufficiently to allow meaningful detection of material deviations.

### 5. Expected Model Behavior

Expected Model Behavior is the behavior that an organization has established as acceptable for a model within a defined operating context.

Expected behavior may be defined through:

- technical specifications;
- model evaluation criteria;
- performance thresholds;
- security requirements;
- safety requirements;
- service-level requirements;
- approved use cases;
- model cards or equivalent documentation;
- validation results;
- business requirements;
- contractual requirements; and
- applicable legal or regulatory requirements.

Expected behavior should not be defined solely by historical average performance when the model operates in environments where rare but severe failures are possible.

### 6. Model Failure Condition

A Model Failure Condition exists when model behavior crosses a defined failure threshold or violates an important operating requirement.

Examples include:

- accuracy falling below an approved threshold;
- unsafe outputs occurring above an accepted tolerance;
- systematic hallucination in a critical workflow;
- unauthorized disclosure through model outputs;
- failure to apply required restrictions;
- materially incorrect classifications;
- inability to perform an essential function;
- unexpected tool execution;
- repeated failure to follow required constraints; or
- materially degraded performance following a model or configuration change.

The threshold for failure should be risk-based rather than identical across all AI systems.

### 7. Model Error

Model Error is an incorrect model output relative to an appropriate reference, expected result, or defined objective.

Not every model error constitutes an incident.

For example, an isolated incorrect recommendation in a low-impact application may represent ordinary model error, while systematic incorrect decisions in a high-impact process may constitute a material AI incident.

Organizations should therefore distinguish:

```text
MODEL ERROR
     |
     v
Is the error within accepted tolerance?
     |
   YES ------------------> Normal model performance variation
     |
    NO
     |
     v
Does it create material impact or violate a critical requirement?
     |
   NO -------------------> Model performance issue
     |
   YES
     |
     v
Potential AI Model Failure / AI Incident
```

### 8. Model Failure versus Model Error

Model error describes incorrect output.

Model failure describes a condition in which model behavior becomes unacceptable relative to defined requirements, tolerances, or operating conditions.

A model can therefore produce errors without failing, while a model failure may involve a pattern, severity, persistence, or systemic consequence that goes beyond ordinary error.

This distinction is important for incident thresholds, monitoring, escalation, and management reporting.

### 9. Model Failure versus Model Compromise

Model Failure does not necessarily imply Model Compromise.

A model may fail because of:

- distribution shift;
- defective training data;
- incorrect configuration;
- implementation defects;
- unsuitable deployment;
- model drift;
- integration failure; or
- inadequate evaluation.

Model compromise, by contrast, generally involves an unauthorized alteration, manipulation, control, or other security compromise of the model or its associated environment.

A model that produces abnormal results should therefore be investigated for both benign failure causes and potential malicious causes.

### 10. Model Failure versus Model Drift

Model Drift is a change in model behavior or performance over time.

Drift can be:

- expected;
- gradual;
- statistically detectable;
- operationally acceptable; or
- materially harmful.

Model drift becomes a model-failure concern when it causes the system to operate outside approved performance or risk boundaries.

Therefore:

**Model drift ≠ model failure.**

Drift is a possible cause or precursor of failure rather than automatically being a failure.

### 11. Model Performance Failure

Model Performance Failure occurs when the model's measurable performance falls below an established requirement.

Depending on the AI system, performance measures may include:

- accuracy;
- precision;
- recall;
- F1 score;
- false-positive rate;
- false-negative rate;
- calibration;
- ranking quality;
- generation quality;
- latency;
- throughput; or
- task completion rate.

The appropriate metric depends on the model's purpose.

A single aggregate metric should not be treated as sufficient when failure can occur differently across populations, use cases, environments, or critical workflows.

### 12. Model Reliability Failure

Model Reliability Failure occurs when the model does not consistently perform an intended function under expected operating conditions.

Examples include:

- intermittent incorrect outputs;
- inconsistent responses to equivalent inputs;
- unexpected failures under load;
- unstable tool selection;
- unpredictable refusal behavior;
- repeated inference errors; or
- degradation after configuration changes.

Reliability should be assessed over appropriate time periods and operating conditions rather than from a single successful test.

### 13. Model Availability Failure

Model Availability Failure occurs when the model or required model-serving capability cannot provide the expected service.

Causes may include:

- infrastructure failure;
- provider outage;
- capacity exhaustion;
- network failure;
- dependency failure;
- authentication failure;
- service misconfiguration;
- rate limiting; or
- model-serving errors.

Availability failure is distinct from behavioral failure because the model may be unavailable without producing incorrect outputs.

### 14. Model Integrity Failure

Model Integrity Failure occurs when the organization cannot rely on the model artifact, configuration, deployment state, or associated components remaining in an approved and trustworthy state.

Potential indicators include:

- unexpected model hash changes;
- unauthorized model replacement;
- unexplained configuration modification;
- unexpected deployment artifact;
- altered model dependencies;
- unauthorized fine-tuning; or
- unexplained changes in model behavior following deployment.

Model integrity concerns may overlap with cybersecurity incidents and supply-chain compromise.

### 15. Model Behavioral Failure

Model Behavioral Failure occurs when a model continues to operate technically but behaves materially outside its approved behavioral expectations.

Examples include:

- producing prohibited content;
- systematically ignoring required instructions;
- generating materially misleading outputs;
- repeatedly bypassing safety controls;
- selecting unauthorized tools;
- revealing restricted information; or
- producing unacceptable decisions.

Behavioral failure is particularly important for generative and agentic AI because conventional infrastructure availability metrics may remain normal while the system is materially failing.

### 16. Model Safety Failure

Model Safety Failure occurs when model behavior creates or materially increases a safety risk beyond the organization's accepted operating boundaries.

Examples may include:

- unsafe recommendations;
- failure of safety constraints;
- unsafe autonomous actions;
- incorrect safety classifications;
- failure to refuse dangerous requests; or
- unsafe interaction with physical or operational systems.

Whether a particular failure creates a legally relevant safety obligation depends on the applicable jurisdiction, sector, AI system, use case, and regulatory framework.

An internal safety-failure classification should not automatically be treated as a statutory classification.

### 17. Model Security Failure

Model Security Failure occurs when model behavior or model operation fails to satisfy defined security requirements.

Examples include:

- unauthorized information disclosure;
- failure of access restrictions;
- insecure tool invocation;
- successful prompt-injection exploitation;
- unauthorized model access;
- security-control bypass; or
- exploitable model-serving behavior.

A security failure may exist even when the model continues to meet ordinary performance metrics.

### 18. Model Privacy Failure

Model Privacy Failure occurs when model behavior, configuration, or supporting processes create an unacceptable privacy condition.

Examples include:

- inappropriate disclosure of personal data;
- failure to respect deletion requirements;
- excessive retention;
- inappropriate retrieval of personal information;
- leakage of training information; or
- unauthorized processing through model interactions.

A privacy failure does not automatically establish that a legally defined personal-data breach has occurred.

The applicable legal classification must be assessed separately against the relevant jurisdiction and facts.

### 19. Model Compliance Failure

Model Compliance Failure occurs when an AI system does not operate in accordance with an applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internally approved requirement.

The organization should distinguish:

- legal requirements;
- regulatory requirements;
- contractual obligations;
- internal policies;
- technical standards; and
- recommended practices.

Failure against an internal policy is not automatically a legal violation.

Similarly, the existence of an AI model failure does not by itself establish regulatory non-compliance.

### 20. Model Functional Failure

Model Functional Failure occurs when the model cannot perform an intended function at an acceptable level.

Examples include:

- classification no longer functioning correctly;
- retrieval producing unusable results;
- recommendation generation becoming unreliable;
- summarization omitting critical information;
- translation becoming materially inaccurate; or
- an agent repeatedly failing to complete an authorized workflow.

Functional failure should be evaluated against the model's intended purpose rather than against generic expectations of AI performance.

### 21. Model Context Failure

Model Context Failure occurs when a model produces inappropriate behavior because it does not correctly interpret or operate within the relevant context.

Examples include:

- misunderstanding domain-specific terminology;
- applying general knowledge to a specialized environment;
- ignoring important business context;
- interpreting an instruction outside its intended scope; or
- generating outputs that are technically plausible but operationally inappropriate.

Context failure can occur even when the underlying model performs well on general benchmarks.

### 22. Model Generalization Failure

Model Generalization Failure occurs when a model performs adequately on known or evaluated conditions but performs inadequately on materially different inputs or environments.

Potential causes include:

- insufficient training diversity;
- distribution shift;
- unfamiliar data;
- new user populations;
- new operational conditions; or
- changes in the surrounding environment.

Generalization failure is particularly important when deployment conditions differ substantially from evaluation conditions.

### 23. Model Distribution-Shift Failure

Distribution Shift occurs when the characteristics of operational inputs differ materially from those represented in the model's training, validation, or evaluation environment.

Examples include:

```text
TRAINING DATA
      |
      v
VALIDATION DATA
      |
      v
DEPLOYMENT ENVIRONMENT
      |
      v
INPUT DISTRIBUTION CHANGES
      |
      v
MODEL PERFORMANCE DEGRADES
      |
      v
POTENTIAL MODEL FAILURE
```

Distribution shift is not itself evidence of failure.

The organization should determine whether the shift materially affects model behavior or risk.

### 24. Model Input Failure

Model Input Failure occurs when incorrect, malformed, incomplete, adversarial, unexpected, or otherwise unsuitable input causes materially incorrect model behavior.

Potential causes include:

- corrupted data;
- missing fields;
- unexpected formats;
- adversarial inputs;
- invalid user instructions;
- sensor errors;
- retrieval errors; or
- upstream system failures.

The investigation should determine whether the model itself failed or whether an upstream input-control failure caused the observed result.

### 25. Model Output Failure

Model Output Failure occurs when the model produces an output that materially violates defined quality, security, safety, privacy, or functional expectations.

Examples include:

- materially incorrect classification;
- fabricated information;
- unauthorized disclosure;
- unsafe recommendation;
- invalid structured output;
- prohibited content; or
- incorrect action instruction.

Output failure is particularly important in generative AI because fluent output can appear credible despite being incorrect.

### 26. Hallucination-Related Model Failure

Hallucination-Related Model Failure occurs when a model generates information that is unsupported, fabricated, materially inaccurate, or presented with inappropriate confidence.

Not every hallucination constitutes an incident.

The materiality of hallucination depends on:

- use case;
- user reliance;
- domain;
- severity of consequences;
- frequency;
- detectability;
- available human review; and
- applicable controls.

A hallucination in a low-risk creative application may have very different consequences from a hallucination in a critical professional workflow.

### 27. Model Consistency Failure

Model Consistency Failure occurs when materially equivalent inputs produce unexpectedly inconsistent outputs without an acceptable explanation.

Consistency may be relevant for:

- repeated inference;
- equivalent prompts;
- equivalent transactions;
- repeated classification;
- policy enforcement; or
- workflow execution.

However, some probabilistic AI systems are intentionally nondeterministic.

Therefore, consistency requirements should be defined according to the technical architecture and intended use rather than assuming that every AI system must produce identical outputs.

### 28. Model Calibration Failure

Model Calibration Failure occurs when the model's confidence estimates do not appropriately correspond to the actual likelihood of correctness.

For example, a model that repeatedly produces highly confident but incorrect predictions may present a greater operational risk than a model that expresses appropriate uncertainty.

Calibration failure can therefore affect:

- human reliance;
- automated decision thresholds;
- escalation;
- triage;
- risk scoring; and
- downstream automation.

### 29. Model Uncertainty Failure

Model Uncertainty Failure occurs when uncertainty is inadequately represented, communicated, or managed relative to the decision context.

Potential conditions include:

- excessive confidence;
- failure to indicate insufficient information;
- failure to escalate ambiguous cases;
- inappropriate automatic decisions; or
- suppression of uncertainty information by downstream systems.

Uncertainty management should be proportionate to the consequences of incorrect decisions.

### 30. Model Threshold Failure

Model Threshold Failure occurs when an operational decision threshold produces unacceptable outcomes.

Examples include:

- classification thresholds that create excessive false negatives;
- fraud-detection thresholds that generate unacceptable false positives;
- safety thresholds that permit unsafe conditions;
- confidence thresholds that permit unreliable outputs to enter automated workflows.

Thresholds should be governed as configuration elements rather than treated as permanent properties of the model.

### 31. Model Configuration Failure

Model Configuration Failure occurs when configuration settings materially affect model behavior outside approved parameters.

Relevant configurations may include:

- system prompts;
- inference parameters;
- temperature;
- context limits;
- retrieval settings;
- tool permissions;
- filtering policies;
- confidence thresholds;
- routing rules;
- model-selection logic; and
- access permissions.

A model may therefore appear to fail even though the underlying model artifact has not changed.

### 32. Model Dependency Failure

Model Dependency Failure occurs when a dependency required for acceptable model operation fails or behaves unexpectedly.

Dependencies may include:

- APIs;
- databases;
- vector stores;
- identity providers;
- cloud infrastructure;
- external model providers;
- retrieval systems;
- security services;
- monitoring platforms; and
- downstream applications.

The investigation should distinguish a model failure from a dependency failure that manifests as model failure.

### 33. Model Integration Failure

Model Integration Failure occurs when the interaction between the model and another system causes materially incorrect, unsafe, insecure, or unavailable behavior.

Examples include:

- incorrect API parameter mapping;
- malformed structured outputs;
- incompatible model versions;
- incorrect data transformation;
- broken authentication;
- unexpected downstream interpretation; or
- failure of an orchestration layer.

This distinction is important because remediation may belong to the integration layer rather than the model itself.

### 34. Model Version Failure

Model Version Failure occurs when a particular model version introduces materially unacceptable behavior compared with an approved baseline or predecessor.

Potential indicators include:

- increased error rates;
- new safety failures;
- changed refusal behavior;
- degraded accuracy;
- increased latency;
- changed tool behavior;
- unexpected output-format changes; or
- newly observed security weaknesses.

Model version changes should therefore be traceable to deployments and evaluation evidence.

### 35. Model Update Failure

Model Update Failure occurs when a model update, fine-tuning operation, configuration change, provider update, or automated update produces unacceptable behavior.

Potential causes include:

- insufficient pre-deployment testing;
- incompatible dependencies;
- changed model behavior;
- changed provider configuration;
- altered training data;
- changed safety controls; or
- unexpected interaction with the production environment.

Automated updates can increase this risk because changes may occur without traditional release timing.

### 36. Model Regression Failure

Model Regression Failure occurs when a change causes a previously acceptable capability to degrade.

Regression may affect:

- accuracy;
- security;
- safety;
- privacy;
- latency;
- availability;
- fairness;
- tool use; or
- instruction adherence.

Regression testing should therefore consider multiple risk dimensions rather than only the primary performance metric.

### 37. Model Evaluation Failure

Model Evaluation Failure occurs when evaluation processes fail to identify a material model weakness before or during deployment.

Potential causes include:

- insufficient test coverage;
- unrealistic test data;
- inadequate adversarial testing;
- inappropriate metrics;
- insufficient edge-case testing;
- evaluation leakage;
- outdated benchmarks; or
- failure to test deployment-specific conditions.

An evaluation failure may therefore be a contributing factor to a subsequent model failure.

### 38. Model Monitoring Failure

Model Monitoring Failure occurs when the organization fails to detect or appropriately respond to material model degradation or abnormal behavior that should reasonably have been observable through established monitoring.

Monitoring failure may result from:

- insufficient telemetry;
- incorrect thresholds;
- monitoring gaps;
- disabled controls;
- excessive alert noise;
- missing behavioral indicators;
- incomplete coverage; or
- inadequate escalation.

Monitoring failure should be assessed separately from model failure because the model and the monitoring system may have different root causes.

### 39. Model Detection Failure

Model Detection Failure occurs when a material model failure remains undetected for an unacceptable period.

Detection effectiveness depends on:

- observability;
- monitoring coverage;
- detection thresholds;
- logging;
- testing;
- user reporting;
- incident intake;
- anomaly detection; and
- human oversight.

A model can therefore fail while the incident-management capability simultaneously fails to detect it.

### 40. Model Recovery Failure

Model Recovery Failure occurs when an organization cannot restore the AI system to an acceptable and sufficiently trusted operating state following model failure.

Potential causes include:

- unavailable rollback artifacts;
- corrupted backups;
- unavailable alternative models;
- dependency failures;
- incomplete configuration records;
- inadequate recovery procedures;
- untested fallback mechanisms; or
- inability to validate restored behavior.

Recovery should not be considered complete merely because the service becomes technically available again.

### 41. Trusted Model Recovery State

A Trusted Model Recovery State is a condition in which the organization has sufficient evidence that the recovered model and its supporting environment are operating within approved requirements.

Validation may include:

- model-version verification;
- artifact integrity verification;
- configuration verification;
- security testing;
- functional testing;
- behavioral testing;
- dependency validation;
- access-control validation; and
- monitoring confirmation.

```text
FAILURE
   |
   v
CONTAINMENT
   |
   v
RECOVERY
   |
   v
TECHNICAL RESTORATION
   |
   v
BEHAVIORAL VALIDATION
   |
   v
SECURITY / SAFETY / PRIVACY VALIDATION
   |
   v
TRUSTED OPERATING STATE
```

### 42. Model Rollback

Model Rollback is the controlled reversion from a problematic model version or configuration to a previously approved state.

Rollback can reduce exposure when the previous state is known to be acceptable.

However:

**Rollback ≠ complete remediation.**

The organization should determine why the failure occurred and verify that the restored version does not contain the same or another unresolved risk.

### 43. Model Fallback

Model Fallback is the use of an alternative model, processing method, provider, or human procedure when the primary model cannot safely or reliably perform its intended function.

Fallback options may include:

- an earlier approved model;
- another validated model;
- deterministic rules;
- manual processing;
- reduced-functionality mode; or
- suspension of the affected function.

Fallback should be tested before it is relied upon during a material incident.

### 44. Model Shutdown

Model Shutdown is the controlled disabling of a model or AI capability when continued operation creates unacceptable risk.

Shutdown may be appropriate where:

- harmful behavior cannot be contained;
- model integrity is uncertain;
- security compromise is suspected;
- safety controls have failed;
- unauthorized data exposure is occurring; or
- recovery cannot be safely performed while the system remains active.

Shutdown authority should be clearly defined for systems where rapid intervention is necessary.

### 45. Model Kill Capability

A Model Kill Capability is a technical or operational mechanism that enables authorized personnel or systems to stop model execution or disable a defined AI capability.

A kill capability may be implemented through:

- service shutdown;
- API disablement;
- access revocation;
- traffic blocking;
- model-routing changes;
- tool disablement; or
- infrastructure isolation.

A kill capability is a control mechanism, not proof that the organization has effective incident resilience.

### 46. Model Failure Evidence

Model Failure Evidence is information used to establish what happened, when it happened, which model or version was involved, what behavior occurred, and what impact resulted.

Relevant evidence may include:

- model version;
- model hash;
- deployment records;
- input samples;
- output samples;
- prompts;
- system instructions;
- configuration;
- logs;
- telemetry;
- evaluation results;
- user reports;
- tool-execution records;
- retrieval records; and
- dependency information.

Evidence collection should respect applicable privacy, security, retention, access-control, and legal requirements.

A Model Failure Timeline records the sequence of relevant events surrounding a model failure.

It may include:

### 48. Model Failure Root Cause

Model Failure Root Cause is the underlying condition or combination of conditions that explains why the failure occurred.

Potential root causes include:

- defective model design;
- inadequate training data;
- data poisoning;
- distribution shift;
- configuration error;
- deployment error;
- integration failure;
- dependency failure;
- insufficient testing;
- inadequate monitoring;
- unauthorized modification;
- supplier failure; or
- human process failure.

Root cause should not be assigned solely from the first visible symptom.

### 49. AI Model Failure Governance Principle

AI Model Failure Governance should ensure that model failures are:

- detected through appropriate monitoring and reporting mechanisms;
- classified according to materiality and impact;
- investigated using sufficient evidence;
- distinguished from compromise and other incident types;
- contained proportionately to risk;
- recovered to a sufficiently trusted state;
- reviewed for root cause and contributing factors;
- linked to corrective and preventive actions; and
- incorporated into the AI system lifecycle and governance processes.

Where a specific law, regulation, regulator, contract, or authoritative requirement establishes mandatory incident-management, notification, safety, security, privacy, or resilience obligations, those obligations should be mapped separately to their authoritative source.

Internal model-failure classifications and controls should not be represented as statutory requirements merely because they support regulatory compliance.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Model Failure

**AI model failure is a failure of intended model behavior or model capability; it is not automatically a cybersecurity compromise, safety violation, privacy breach, or regulatory violation.**

Effective AI model-failure management requires the organization to establish expected behavior, define material failure thresholds, monitor meaningful indicators, preserve evidence, distinguish model causes from environmental causes, contain unacceptable behavior, validate recovery, and use failure evidence to improve the AI lifecycle.

The central governance distinction is:

```text
MODEL ERROR
     ≠
MODEL FAILURE
     ≠
MODEL COMPROMISE
     ≠
REGULATORY VIOLATION

Each condition requires separate assessment
against its applicable technical, operational,
security, safety, privacy, contractual, and
legal requirements.
```

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers used throughout this chapter are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
