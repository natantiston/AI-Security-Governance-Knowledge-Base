# 17.10 — AI Model Compromise

## Part 1 — AI Model Compromise Foundations, Compromise Concepts, Attack Surface, Integrity and Governance

### 1. AI Model Compromise

**AI Model Compromise** occurs when an unauthorized party gains the ability to modify, replace, manipulate, control, or otherwise undermine the security or trustworthy operation of an AI model or its associated model assets.

A compromise may involve:

- model artifacts;
- model weights;
- configuration;
- deployment;
- inference infrastructure;
- model access;
- model-serving components;
- model repositories;
- model supply chains.

Model compromise should be distinguished from ordinary model failure, model drift, or poor model performance.

### 2. Objective of AI Model Compromise Management

The objective is to prevent, detect, contain, investigate, remediate, and recover from unauthorized changes or control affecting AI models.

The capability should address:

- model integrity;
- model authenticity;
- model access;
- model provenance;
- deployment integrity;
- supply-chain security;
- credential security;
- behavioral validation;
- evidence;
- recovery.

### 3. AI Model

An **AI Model** is a computational artifact that has been developed, trained, configured, or otherwise prepared to perform AI-related inference, prediction, generation, classification, reasoning, or decision-support functions.

Depending on the architecture, the model environment may include:

- weights;
- architecture;
- tokenizer;
- configuration;
- adapters;
- prompts;
- retrieval components;
- inference runtime.

### 4. AI Model Asset

An AI Model Asset is any component required to create, deploy, operate, reproduce, or validate an AI model.

Examples include:

- model weights;
- checkpoints;
- configuration;
- tokenizer;
- adapters;
- training artifacts;
- model container;
- inference runtime;
- model metadata.

Protecting only the model weights may therefore be insufficient.

### 5. AI Model Compromise Event

A Model Compromise Event is an observed condition indicating that a model or model-related asset may have been unauthorizedly modified, replaced, accessed, or controlled.

Examples include:

- unexpected model hash change;
- unauthorized model upload;
- unexplained model-version change;
- suspicious repository activity;
- unauthorized deployment.

An event is not automatically a confirmed compromise.

### 6. AI Model Compromise Incident

A Model Compromise Incident is a confirmed or sufficiently credible security condition involving unauthorized modification, replacement, access, control, or manipulation of a model or its trusted model environment that meets organizational incident criteria.

The distinction between event and incident should be defined by organizational incident-management procedures.

### 7. AI Model Compromise versus AI Model Failure

A **model failure** concerns unacceptable or unexpected behavior.

A **model compromise** concerns unauthorized influence, modification, replacement, access, or control.

A compromised model may continue to perform normally under ordinary tests while containing malicious behavior.

Conversely, a model can fail without being compromised.

### 8. AI Model Compromise versus AI Model Drift

Model drift generally concerns changes in model performance or behavior over time.

Compromise concerns unauthorized security-relevant changes or control.

Drift can therefore occur without compromise, while compromise can exist without immediately observable drift.

### 9. AI Model Compromise versus Model Artifact Corruption

Artifact corruption may result from:

- accidental damage;
- storage failure;
- transmission errors;
- unauthorized modification.

Corruption alone does not establish malicious compromise.

Investigation should determine whether the integrity failure resulted from an attack, operational failure, or another cause.

### 10. AI Model Compromise versus Model Theft

Model theft concerns unauthorized acquisition or copying of a model or model-related intellectual property.

Model compromise concerns unauthorized modification, control, or manipulation.

They can occur independently or together.

### 11. AI Model Compromise versus Model Extraction

Model extraction attempts to reconstruct information about a model through repeated interaction.

Model compromise generally involves unauthorized influence over the model or its trusted operating environment.

An attacker may perform extraction without compromising the model itself.

### 12. AI Model Compromise versus Data Poisoning

Data poisoning manipulates data used to influence model training, fine-tuning, evaluation, or operation.

Model compromise directly targets the model or its trusted artifacts or environment.

The two can be connected:

```text id="d5q8mv"
POISONED DATA
      |
      v
TRAINING / FINE-TUNING
      |
      v
MODEL BEHAVIOR
      |
      v
MODEL ARTIFACT
```

A poisoned training process does not automatically prove that the final model artifact was maliciously modified.

### 13. AI Model Compromise versus Supply-Chain Compromise

Supply-chain compromise occurs when an upstream component, supplier, repository, dependency, or delivery mechanism is compromised.

Model compromise may result from such an attack.

For example:

```text id="n7r3kx"
UPSTREAM SUPPLIER
       |
       v
MODEL REPOSITORY
       |
       v
MODEL ARTIFACT
       |
       v
DEPLOYMENT
       |
       v
PRODUCTION AI
```

The organization's model may therefore be compromised without a direct attack against its production environment.

### 14. AI Model Compromise Attack Surface

The model-compromise attack surface includes all components through which an attacker could alter or control a model.

Potential surfaces include:

- model repositories;
- artifact registries;
- CI/CD pipelines;
- training infrastructure;
- fine-tuning infrastructure;
- model-serving platforms;
- cloud storage;
- developer environments;
- administrative interfaces;
- model APIs;
- deployment systems.

### 15. AI Model Compromise Trust Boundary

A Trust Boundary identifies where model artifacts or model-control information move between environments with different trust assumptions.

Examples include:

- external repository to enterprise repository;
- development to production;
- supplier to organization;
- training environment to serving environment.

Each boundary should have appropriate:

- authentication;
- authorization;
- integrity verification;
- provenance;
- monitoring.

### 16. AI Model Compromise Model Artifact

A Model Artifact is a deployable or reproducible representation of a model.

It may contain:

- weights;
- configuration;
- architecture;
- tokenizer;
- metadata;
- adapters;
- dependencies.

Artifact governance should establish which components are considered security-critical.

### 17. AI Model Compromise Model Weights

Model weights contain learned parameters used during model inference.

Unauthorized modification of weights can potentially alter model behavior without changing the surrounding application.

Weight integrity should therefore be protected and verified.

### 18. AI Model Compromise Model Configuration

Model configuration defines parameters governing how a model is loaded or operated.

Examples may include:

- architecture settings;
- inference parameters;
- context limits;
- adapters;
- safety configurations;
- runtime settings.

A secure model requires protection of configuration as well as weights.

### 19. AI Model Compromise Model Adapter

Adapters, including parameter-efficient fine-tuning components, can materially change model behavior.

Unauthorized adapter modification may therefore create compromise even when the underlying base model remains unchanged.

### 20. AI Model Compromise Model Checkpoint

A checkpoint represents a stored model state.

Checkpoints may be:

- training checkpoints;
- fine-tuning checkpoints;
- evaluation checkpoints;
- production checkpoints.

Unauthorized checkpoint substitution or modification can introduce security risk.

### 21. AI Model Compromise Model Repository

A Model Repository stores or distributes model artifacts.

Security controls should address:

- access;
- authentication;
- authorization;
- integrity;
- versioning;
- provenance;
- change management.

### 22. AI Model Compromise Artifact Registry

An Artifact Registry stores approved model artifacts for controlled deployment.

It should distinguish:

- uploaded artifacts;
- validated artifacts;
- approved artifacts;
- production artifacts.

### 23. AI Model Compromise Model Provenance

Model Provenance records where a model originated and how it was developed, modified, transferred, or approved.

Provenance may include:

- source;
- publisher;
- version;
- build;
- training lineage;
- fine-tuning;
- transfer history.

Provenance does not by itself prove that a model is secure.

### 24. AI Model Compromise Model Authenticity

Model Authenticity concerns whether an artifact is genuinely associated with its claimed source or publisher.

Authentication mechanisms may include:

- digital signatures;
- trusted repositories;
- certificates;
- verified publisher identity.

Authenticity does not establish that the model is safe or free from vulnerabilities.

### 25. AI Model Compromise Model Integrity

Model Integrity concerns whether a model artifact remains unchanged from its approved state, or whether changes are authorized and controlled.

Integrity mechanisms may include:

- cryptographic hashes;
- signatures;
- immutable storage;
- version control;
- access logging.

Integrity verification does not establish behavioral correctness.

### 26. AI Model Compromise Behavioral Integrity

Behavioral Integrity concerns whether the model behaves consistently with its approved security and functional expectations.

A model may pass a hash check while exhibiting malicious or unexpected behavior because:

- the approved artifact was already compromised;
- the wrong artifact was approved;
- malicious behavior is conditional;
- the compromise affects runtime dependencies.

### 27. AI Model Compromise Cryptographic Integrity

Cryptographic Integrity uses cryptographic mechanisms to detect unauthorized artifact modification.

Examples include:

- SHA-family hashes;
- digital signatures;
- signed manifests.

Cryptographic integrity establishes evidence about artifact changes, not overall trustworthiness.

### 28. AI Model Compromise Signature Verification

Signature Verification confirms whether an artifact has a valid digital signature from a trusted signing authority.

Verification should establish:

- signer identity;
- trust chain;
- signature validity;
- artifact association;
- key status.

A valid signature does not prove that the signed artifact is free from malicious functionality.

### 29. AI Model Compromise Hash Verification

Hash Verification compares an artifact's cryptographic digest against an approved reference.

A mismatch may indicate:

- unauthorized modification;
- corruption;
- version change;
- replacement.

A matching hash does not prove that the artifact is safe.

### 30. AI Model Compromise Trusted Model State

A Trusted Model State is a model state for which the organization has sufficient evidence of:

- provenance;
- integrity;
- authorization;
- configuration;
- validation;
- deployment status.

Trusted state should be explicitly defined for critical models.

### 31. AI Model Compromise Model Identity

Model Identity uniquely identifies a model artifact or approved model state.

Identity may include:

- model name;
- version;
- digest;
- publisher;
- artifact identifier;
- environment.

Model identity should be sufficiently precise to prevent ambiguity between similar artifacts.

### 32. AI Model Compromise Model Version

A Model Version identifies a specific release or state of a model.

Security governance should distinguish:

- development;
- testing;
- approved;
- production;
- retired

versions.

### 33. AI Model Compromise Model Version Substitution

Version Substitution occurs when an attacker or unauthorized process replaces an expected model with another artifact.

Potential causes include:

- compromised registry;
- stolen credentials;
- malicious deployment;
- pipeline compromise.

### 34. AI Model Compromise Unauthorized Model Modification

Unauthorized Model Modification occurs when model weights, configuration, adapters, or other model components are changed without approved authorization.

### 35. AI Model Compromise Unauthorized Model Replacement

Unauthorized Model Replacement occurs when an approved model is replaced by another model without appropriate authorization.

This can be more difficult to detect when:

- model names remain similar;
- version identifiers are manipulated;
- repositories are trusted incorrectly.

### 36. AI Model Compromise Unauthorized Model Access

Unauthorized Model Access occurs when an actor gains access to model artifacts or model-management capabilities without appropriate authorization.

Access should be distinguished from successful modification.

### 37. AI Model Compromise Unauthorized Model Control

Unauthorized Model Control occurs when an actor gains sufficient privileges to alter model deployment, configuration, version, or behavior.

### 38. AI Model Compromise Credential Compromise

Credential Compromise occurs when credentials used to access or manage model assets are exposed or misused.

Relevant credentials may include:

- repository credentials;
- cloud identities;
- CI/CD credentials;
- deployment keys;
- API tokens.

### 39. AI Model Compromise Privilege Escalation

Privilege Escalation occurs when an attacker gains permissions beyond those originally authorized.

Potential consequences include:

- model modification;
- deployment;
- artifact deletion;
- configuration changes;
- access to sensitive model assets.

### 40. AI Model Compromise Deployment Integrity

Deployment Integrity concerns whether the model deployed into production is the authorized artifact and configuration.

A secure deployment process should establish:

```text id="w8m4qt"
APPROVED MODEL
      |
      v
INTEGRITY VERIFICATION
      |
      v
AUTHORIZED DEPLOYMENT
      |
      v
RUNTIME VALIDATION
      |
      v
PRODUCTION
```

### 41. AI Model Compromise Runtime Integrity

Runtime Integrity concerns whether the model and its execution environment remain in an approved state while operating.

Relevant components may include:

- model files;
- containers;
- runtime libraries;
- configuration;
- infrastructure;
- model-serving process.

### 42. AI Model Compromise Model-Serving Integrity

Model-Serving Integrity protects the infrastructure responsible for loading and serving the model.

Potential controls include:

- hardened runtime;
- access control;
- image integrity;
- process monitoring;
- configuration management.

### 43. AI Model Compromise Training-Environment Integrity

Training-Environment Integrity protects the infrastructure and artifacts used to create models.

It should address:

- training code;
- datasets;
- checkpoints;
- credentials;
- dependencies;
- compute infrastructure.

### 44. AI Model Compromise Fine-Tuning Integrity

Fine-Tuning Integrity protects the process used to modify an existing model.

Relevant components include:

- base model;
- fine-tuning data;
- adapters;
- training configuration;
- output artifacts.

### 45. AI Model Compromise CI/CD Integrity

CI/CD Integrity ensures that model build and deployment pipelines execute authorized code and promote authorized artifacts.

Potential controls include:

- protected branches;
- signed commits;
- controlled runners;
- artifact verification;
- deployment approval.

### 46. AI Model Compromise Supply-Chain Integrity

Supply-Chain Integrity ensures that model components obtained from external sources are appropriately verified before organizational use.

Verification may consider:

- provenance;
- publisher;
- integrity;
- dependencies;
- vulnerabilities;
- security testing.

### 47. AI Model Compromise Evidence

Relevant evidence may include:

- model hashes;
- signatures;
- repository logs;
- access logs;
- deployment records;
- CI/CD logs;
- configuration history;
- credentials;
- model behavior;
- network telemetry.

Evidence should be protected against unauthorized alteration.

### 49. AI Model Compromise Governance Principle

**AI model compromise should be governed as an integrity, authorization, supply-chain, and runtime-security problem rather than merely as a model-performance problem.**

Effective governance should address:

- model identity;
- provenance;
- authenticity;
- artifact integrity;
- access control;
- deployment integrity;
- runtime integrity;
- supply-chain security;
- behavioral validation;
- incident response.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Model Compromise

**An AI model should not be considered trustworthy merely because it is available, correctly named, cryptographically intact, or obtained from a recognized source. Trust should be established through appropriate evidence covering provenance, integrity, authorization, deployment, runtime state, and behavioral expectations.**

Organizations should maintain clear separation between:

- model failure and model compromise;
- model drift and unauthorized modification;
- model theft and model compromise;
- data poisoning and model compromise;
- provenance and trustworthiness;
- authenticity and security;
- cryptographic integrity and behavioral integrity;
- model identity and model safety;
- artifact integrity and runtime integrity;
- supplier assurance and independent assurance;
- model availability and model trust;
- internal governance controls and legal obligations.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations. Where a model-compromise-related requirement is legally mandatory, the knowledge base should separately identify the authoritative source, applicable jurisdiction, organizational role, affected activity, effective date, and specific requirement.

## Part 2 — AI Model Compromise Controls, Detection, Assessment, Containment, Investigation and Remediation

### 51. AI Model Compromise Management Policy

An **AI Model Compromise Management Policy** establishes organizational requirements for preventing, detecting, investigating, containing, remediating, and recovering from unauthorized model modification or control.

The policy should define:

- scope;
- ownership;
- model criticality;
- approved repositories;
- integrity requirements;
- access requirements;
- incident escalation;
- evidence requirements;
- recovery requirements.

The policy is an internal governance control unless a specific legal, regulatory, contractual, or authoritative requirement establishes a corresponding obligation.

### 52. AI Model Compromise Scope

The scope should cover the model lifecycle and supporting environments, including:

- training;
- fine-tuning;
- evaluation;
- model registration;
- artifact storage;
- deployment;
- serving;
- monitoring;
- retirement.

The scope should also identify relevant third-party model services and dependencies.

### 53. AI Model Compromise Model Register

A **Model Register** provides an authoritative inventory of models used by the organization.

Typical fields include:

- model identifier;
- version;
- owner;
- source;
- repository;
- environment;
- business purpose;
- criticality;
- deployment status;
- integrity reference;
- supplier;
- dependencies.

An incomplete model inventory creates a governance blind spot.

### 54. AI Model Compromise Approved Model Baseline

An **Approved Model Baseline** establishes the known-good state against which deployed or stored models can be compared.

The baseline may include:

- artifact digest;
- signature;
- version;
- configuration;
- dependencies;
- runtime image;
- approved tools;
- expected behavior.

### 55. AI Model Compromise Artifact Hash Control

Artifact Hash Control records cryptographic hashes for approved model artifacts.

A hash mismatch should generate investigation rather than automatically being treated as proof of malicious compromise.

Possible explanations include:

- authorized update;
- corruption;
- version change;
- packaging difference;
- unauthorized modification.

### 56. AI Model Compromise Digital-Signature Control

Digital-Signature Control verifies that model artifacts are signed by an appropriately trusted signing authority.

Verification should include:

- signer identity;
- certificate or key status;
- signature validity;
- artifact association;
- trust chain.

### 57. AI Model Compromise Trusted-Repository Control

A **Trusted-Repository Control** restricts model acquisition and deployment to approved repositories or explicitly authorized sources.

Repository trust should consider:

- publisher identity;
- access controls;
- integrity;
- provenance;
- security history;
- organizational approval.

### 58. AI Model Compromise Repository Access Control

Repository Access Control limits who can:

- upload;
- modify;
- delete;
- approve;
- promote;
- retrieve

model artifacts.

Access should follow least-privilege principles.

### 59. AI Model Compromise Model Approval Control

Model Approval Control establishes authorization before a model enters an approved or production state.

Approval may consider:

- provenance;
- integrity;
- security testing;
- model evaluation;
- licensing;
- dependencies;
- intended use.

### 60. AI Model Compromise Model Promotion Control

Model Promotion Control governs movement between environments.

```text id="a7m4qx"
DEVELOPMENT
     |
     v
SECURITY / EVALUATION
     |
     v
APPROVAL
     |
     v
STAGING
     |
     v
PRODUCTION
```

Promotion should preserve model identity and integrity evidence.

### 61. AI Model Compromise Deployment Authorization

Deployment Authorization ensures that only approved personnel, pipelines, or automation can deploy a model.

Authorization should consider:

- identity;
- role;
- environment;
- model criticality;
- deployment context.

### 62. AI Model Compromise Least-Privilege Control

Least Privilege limits access to model artifacts and model-management functions to the minimum necessary permissions.

Separate permissions may be appropriate for:

- reading;
- uploading;
- modifying;
- approving;
- deploying;
- deleting.

### 63. AI Model Compromise Segregation of Duties

Segregation of Duties reduces the risk that one identity can independently:

1. modify a model;
2. approve the model;
3. deploy the model.

For critical models, these activities should generally be separated where practical.

### 64. AI Model Compromise Privileged-Access Management

Privileged-access management should protect highly sensitive model-management accounts.

Controls may include:

- just-in-time access;
- strong authentication;
- session monitoring;
- privileged approval;
- credential rotation.

### 65. AI Model Compromise Credential Protection

Credentials used to access model repositories, pipelines, and deployment systems should be protected against:

- theft;
- reuse;
- unauthorized sharing;
- long-term exposure.

### 66. AI Model Compromise Secret Management

Secrets associated with model infrastructure should be managed through controlled secret-management mechanisms.

Examples include:

- API keys;
- signing keys;
- repository credentials;
- deployment credentials;
- cloud service identities.

### 67. AI Model Compromise Signing-Key Protection

Signing keys used to establish model authenticity should receive enhanced protection.

Compromise of a trusted signing key can undermine confidence in otherwise valid signatures.

### 68. AI Model Compromise Immutable Artifact Storage

Immutable storage can prevent unauthorized modification of approved model artifacts.

It may support:

- version preservation;
- rollback;
- forensic investigation;
- trusted recovery.

### 69. AI Model Compromise Model-Version Control

Version Control ensures that every approved model state has a unique and traceable identity.

A version record should associate the model with:

- digest;
- source;
- approval;
- deployment;
- change history.

### 70. AI Model Compromise Model Change Control

Model Change Control requires authorization and evidence for changes to:

- weights;
- adapters;
- configuration;
- architecture;
- dependencies;
- deployment parameters.

### 71. AI Model Compromise Model Integrity Monitoring

Integrity Monitoring continuously or periodically checks whether model artifacts differ from approved baselines.

Monitoring frequency should be proportional to:

- criticality;
- exposure;
- change frequency;
- threat environment.

### 72. AI Model Compromise Configuration Integrity Monitoring

Configuration monitoring should detect unauthorized changes to:

- model parameters;
- serving configuration;
- tool permissions;
- environment variables;
- security settings.

### 73. AI Model Compromise Deployment Integrity Monitoring

Deployment monitoring verifies that the model running in production corresponds to the approved artifact.

### 74. AI Model Compromise Runtime Integrity Monitoring

Runtime monitoring assesses whether the serving environment remains consistent with the approved state.

Potential indicators include:

- unexpected processes;
- unauthorized file changes;
- unexpected network connections;
- runtime configuration changes.

### 75. AI Model Compromise Repository Activity Monitoring

Repository monitoring can identify:

- unusual uploads;
- unexpected downloads;
- permission changes;
- deletion activity;
- unusual access locations;
- suspicious administrative actions.

### 76. AI Model Compromise CI/CD Monitoring

CI/CD monitoring should detect unauthorized changes involving:

- source code;
- pipeline definitions;
- build runners;
- model artifacts;
- deployment stages;
- credentials.

### 77. AI Model Compromise Model Deployment Alert

A deployment alert should be generated when model deployment activity violates defined expectations.

Potential triggers include:

- unapproved version;
- unexpected deployment time;
- unauthorized identity;
- unexpected environment;
- failed signature verification.

### 78. AI Model Compromise Integrity Alert Validation

An integrity alert should be validated before incident classification.

Validation may compare:

- change records;
- deployment records;
- approved versions;
- repository history;
- administrative activity.

### 79. AI Model Compromise Behavioral Monitoring

Behavioral Monitoring evaluates whether model behavior deviates materially from approved expectations.

Possible signals include:

- unexpected outputs;
- abnormal refusal behavior;
- unusual tool interactions;
- unexplained security-policy deviations.

Behavioral deviation does not by itself prove model compromise.

### 80. AI Model Compromise Behavioral Baseline

A Behavioral Baseline establishes expected model behavior for relevant use cases.

The baseline may include:

- expected output characteristics;
- refusal behavior;
- tool-use patterns;
- security constraints;
- performance ranges.

### 81. AI Model Compromise Model-Behavior Differential Testing

Differential Testing compares the behavior of:

- current production model;
- approved baseline;
- previous version;
- independently validated reference.

Significant unexplained differences should trigger investigation.

### 82. AI Model Compromise Canary Testing

Canary Testing introduces a model to a limited environment before broad deployment.

This can provide an opportunity to identify:

- behavioral anomalies;
- security-control bypasses;
- unexpected tool behavior;
- deployment issues.

### 83. AI Model Compromise Pre-Deployment Security Testing

Security testing should assess the model and its deployment environment before production use.

Potential testing includes:

- artifact integrity;
- dependency security;
- adversarial evaluation;
- prompt-injection testing;
- behavioral testing;
- access-control validation.

### 84. AI Model Compromise Model Quarantine

Model Quarantine isolates a suspected compromised model from production use while preserving relevant evidence.

Quarantine may include:

- disabling inference;
- preventing further deployment;
- restricting repository access;
- isolating the artifact.

### 85. AI Model Compromise Artifact Quarantine

Artifact Quarantine prevents a suspected model artifact from being promoted or redistributed.

The original artifact should be preserved where required for investigation.

### 86. AI Model Compromise Deployment Quarantine

Deployment Quarantine prevents a potentially compromised model from being deployed to additional environments.

### 87. AI Model Compromise Repository Quarantine

Repository Quarantine may restrict access to affected artifacts or repositories when compromise of the repository itself is suspected.

### 88. AI Model Compromise Credential Revocation

Credentials suspected of enabling unauthorized model access should be revoked or rotated according to incident procedures.

Affected credentials may include:

- repository tokens;
- cloud credentials;
- deployment keys;
- CI/CD identities.

### 89. AI Model Compromise Session Revocation

Active sessions associated with suspected compromise may be terminated.

This can limit continued unauthorized access.

### 90. AI Model Compromise Network Isolation

Network Isolation may restrict communication between compromised model infrastructure and other systems.

Isolation should consider evidence preservation and business continuity.

### 91. AI Model Compromise Pipeline Suspension

A compromised model-development or deployment pipeline may need to be suspended to prevent further propagation.

### 92. AI Model Compromise Promotion Suspension

Model promotion should be suspended when:

- integrity cannot be established;
- provenance is uncertain;
- signing infrastructure is compromised;
- unauthorized changes are suspected.

### 93. AI Model Compromise Tool Disablement

Where a compromised model has access to external tools, selected tools may be disabled to reduce potential impact.

### 94. AI Model Compromise Containment Validation

Containment should be validated rather than assumed.

Validation may confirm that:

- unauthorized access has stopped;
- affected artifacts are isolated;
- deployment has ceased;
- credentials are revoked;
- propagation paths are controlled.

### 95. AI Model Compromise Evidence Preservation

Evidence should be preserved before destructive remediation where practical.

Potential evidence includes:

- model artifacts;
- hashes;
- signatures;
- repository records;
- access logs;
- deployment logs;
- CI/CD records;
- network telemetry.

### 96. AI Model Compromise Artifact Preservation

The suspected artifact should be preserved in a controlled manner so investigators can determine:

- what changed;
- when it changed;
- how it changed;
- whether the change was authorized.

### 97. AI Model Compromise Model-Memory Preservation

Where applicable, relevant model-serving memory, runtime state, or temporary artifacts should be preserved when they may provide evidence of compromise.

### 98. AI Model Compromise Configuration Preservation

Configuration at the time of suspected compromise should be preserved before remediation changes overwrite relevant evidence.

### 99. AI Model Compromise Deployment-State Preservation

The deployed state should be recorded, including:

- model version;
- digest;
- runtime image;
- configuration;
- environment;
- deployment identity.

### 100. AI Model Compromise Evidence Integrity

Evidence should itself be protected against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

- cryptographic hashing;
- restricted access;
- immutable storage;
- chain-of-custody records.

### 102. AI Model Compromise Root-Cause Analysis

Root-Cause Analysis determines why the compromise was possible.

Potential causes include:

- excessive privilege;
- stolen credentials;
- vulnerable pipeline;
- compromised supplier;
- weak repository security;
- inadequate change control.

### 103. AI Model Compromise Contributing-Factor Analysis

Contributing factors may include:

- insufficient monitoring;
- undocumented dependencies;
- weak segregation of duties;
- outdated infrastructure;
- inadequate testing;
- poor evidence retention.

Contributing factors should not automatically be treated as root causes.

### 399. AI Model Compromise Enterprise Resilience Principle

**Enterprise model-compromise resilience requires the organization to preserve trustworthy AI capability even when a model, provider, repository, pipeline, identity, or supporting dependency can no longer be trusted.**

Resilience therefore depends on more than backups or redundant infrastructure. It requires:

- trustworthy artifacts;
- independent recovery paths;
- tested alternatives;
- controlled deployment;
- validated restoration;
- appropriate human decision authority.

### 400. AI Model Compromise Enterprise Assurance Principle

**Enterprise assurance should provide evidence that model identity, provenance, authenticity, integrity, authorization, deployment, runtime behavior, incident response, recovery, and strategic dependencies are governed as an integrated trust chain.**

The principal distinctions established across this topic are:

- model failure ≠ model compromise;
- model drift ≠ unauthorized modification;
- artifact corruption ≠ confirmed malicious compromise;
- model theft ≠ model compromise;
- model extraction ≠ model compromise;
- provenance ≠ trustworthiness;
- authenticity ≠ security;
- cryptographic integrity ≠ behavioral integrity;
- model identity ≠ model safety;
- artifact integrity ≠ runtime integrity;
- model version ≠ artifact identity;
- detection ≠ investigation;
- investigation ≠ attribution;
- containment ≠ eradication;
- eradication ≠ trusted recovery;
- rollback ≠ complete remediation;
- fallback capability ≠ tested resilience;
- provider redundancy ≠ effective diversification;
- supplier assurance ≠ independent assurance;
- documentation ≠ demonstrated capability;
- monitoring ≠ continuous assurance;
- regulatory relevance ≠ regulatory violation;
- contractual requirement ≠ statutory requirement;
- internal governance control ≠ legal obligation;
- risk acceptance ≠ risk elimination;
- automation ≠ accountability.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They are not statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations. Where a model-compromise requirement is legally mandatory, it should be separately mapped to the authoritative source, applicable jurisdiction, organizational role, affected activity, effective date, and specific requirement.
