# 17.08 — Data Poisoning Incidents

## Part 1 — AI Data Poisoning Incident Foundations, Poisoning Concepts, Attack Boundaries, Impact and Governance

### 1. AI Data Poisoning Incident

An **AI Data Poisoning Incident** occurs when manipulated, malicious, corrupted, misleading, or otherwise unauthorized data influences an AI system in a manner that creates a material security, integrity, privacy, safety, operational, or other adverse impact.

Data poisoning can affect different stages of the AI lifecycle, including:

- training;
- fine-tuning;
- evaluation;
- retrieval;
- knowledge bases;
- embeddings;
- feedback systems;
- reinforcement processes; and
- runtime data pipelines.

A suspected poisoned dataset is not automatically a confirmed incident. Confirmation requires appropriate evidence and assessment.

### 2. Objective of AI Data Poisoning Incident Management

The objective is to identify, assess, contain, investigate, remediate, and learn from incidents in which compromised or inappropriate data influences AI behavior.

The capability should address:

- data integrity;
- provenance;
- ingestion;
- model behavior;
- affected datasets;
- affected model versions;
- downstream systems;
- containment;
- recovery; and
- residual risk.

### 3. AI Data Poisoning

**AI Data Poisoning** is the deliberate or accidental introduction of data that causes an AI system to learn, retrieve, infer, or produce undesirable behavior.

Poisoning may involve:

- incorrect labels;
- manipulated examples;
- malicious instructions;
- fabricated records;
- altered metadata;
- contaminated documents;
- malicious feedback;
- compromised external feeds.

Poisoning can be intentional or unintentional.

### 4. AI Data Poisoning Event

An AI Data Poisoning Event is an observed condition suggesting that data may have been manipulated or contaminated but has not yet been established as a material incident.

Examples include:

- unexpected dataset changes;
- unexplained records;
- suspicious labels;
- anomalous source behavior;
- unexpected retrieval results.

An event becomes an incident when its assessed circumstances meet the organization's incident criteria.

### 5. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Data Quality Issue

A data-quality issue concerns inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent, outdated, or otherwise defective data.

A poisoning incident specifically concerns data whose presence or manipulation creates an AI security or integrity concern.

The distinction may depend on intent, mechanism, impact, and evidence.

### 6. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Data Breach

A data breach generally concerns unauthorized access to, disclosure of, alteration of, or loss of protected information under an applicable legal or organizational definition.

Data poisoning concerns manipulation or contamination of information used by an AI system.

The two can overlap. For example, an attacker may obtain unauthorized access to a dataset and then modify it to influence model behavior.

Neither condition should automatically be classified as the other.

### 7. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus AI Security Incident

A poisoning incident may constitute an AI security incident when an adversary intentionally compromises data integrity or uses unauthorized access to influence an AI system.

However, not every poisoning incident is necessarily caused by a cyberattack.

Examples of non-malicious causes may include:

- defective data pipelines;
- incorrect labeling;
- accidental ingestion;
- faulty transformation;
- uncontrolled synthetic data.

### 8. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Model Compromise

**Model compromise** concerns unauthorized modification, manipulation, replacement, or control of the model or model artifact.

**Data poisoning** concerns manipulation or contamination of data that influences the model or AI system.

The two can occur together:

```text id="d4p7kx"
COMPROMISED DATA
       |
       v
TRAINING / RETRIEVAL PIPELINE
       |
       v
AI SYSTEM
       |
       v
ALTERED BEHAVIOR
```

A compromised model artifact and poisoned training data require different investigative paths.

### 9. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Model Failure

Model failure describes unacceptable or unexpected model behavior.

Data poisoning describes a potential causal mechanism involving compromised or contaminated data.

A model can fail without being poisoned, and poisoned data can exist without producing an immediately observable model failure.

### 10. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Data Drift

**Data drift** is a change in the statistical or semantic characteristics of data over time.

**Poisoning** involves harmful manipulation or contamination of data.

A drift condition is not automatically evidence of poisoning.

Conversely, carefully designed poisoning may be difficult to distinguish from normal distribution changes.

### 11. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Data Contamination

Data contamination is a broader concept describing unwanted or inappropriate information entering a dataset or processing environment.

Poisoning is a specific form of contamination where the data negatively influences AI behavior or creates a deliberate or material integrity concern.

### 12. AI Data Poisoning Incident versus Data Corruption

Data corruption refers to unintended alteration or damage to data.

Poisoning may involve intentional manipulation, although accidental poisoning conditions can also occur.

Investigation should determine:

- what changed;
- how it changed;
- when it changed;
- who or what caused the change;
- whether AI behavior was affected.

### 13. AI Data Poisoning Attack

An AI Data Poisoning Attack is an intentional attempt to manipulate data so that an AI system produces attacker-beneficial or otherwise undesirable behavior.

Potential objectives include:

- degrading model performance;
- creating targeted misclassification;
- influencing recommendations;
- causing unsafe behavior;
- creating persistent model weaknesses;
- bypassing detection;
- influencing specific users or contexts.

### 14. AI Data Poisoning Vector

A poisoning vector is the pathway through which manipulated information enters an AI data environment.

Potential vectors include:

- training datasets;
- fine-tuning datasets;
- public data collection;
- user feedback;
- web scraping;
- third-party feeds;
- APIs;
- knowledge repositories;
- annotation systems;
- synthetic data;
- retrieval repositories.

### 15. AI Data Poisoning Entry Point

The entry point is the specific interface, process, system, or repository through which poisoned information enters the AI lifecycle.

Examples include:

- data-ingestion API;
- upload interface;
- annotation platform;
- database;
- model-training pipeline;
- retrieval index.

Identifying the entry point is important for containment.

### 16. AI Data Poisoning Attack Surface

The poisoning attack surface includes all components through which an attacker or accidental process could influence AI-relevant data.

It may include:

- data sources;
- collection mechanisms;
- transformation pipelines;
- storage;
- annotation;
- training;
- evaluation;
- retrieval;
- feedback;
- deployment.

The attack surface extends beyond the model itself.

### 17. AI Data Poisoning Trust Boundary

A trust boundary exists where data moves between environments with different trust assumptions.

Examples include:

- public data entering an enterprise repository;
- supplier data entering a training pipeline;
- user feedback entering model improvement;
- external documents entering a retrieval system.

Each trust boundary should have appropriate validation and integrity controls.

### 18. AI Data Poisoning Data Source

A data source is the origin of information used by an AI system.

Sources may be:

- internal;
- external;
- public;
- proprietary;
- user-generated;
- supplier-provided;
- synthetic;
- automatically collected.

Source identity alone does not establish source trustworthiness.

### 19. AI Data Poisoning Source Trust

Source Trust represents the organization's confidence that a data source is authentic, appropriate, reliable, and sufficiently controlled.

Trust may depend on:

- provenance;
- ownership;
- integrity;
- access controls;
- historical reliability;
- validation;
- contractual assurances.

A trusted source can still become compromised.

### 20. AI Data Poisoning Data Provenance

Data Provenance identifies where data originated and how it moved or changed through the AI lifecycle.

Provenance may include:

- source;
- collection time;
- transformation;
- processing;
- version;
- owner;
- destination.

Provenance supports investigation but does not by itself prove that data is trustworthy.

### 21. AI Data Poisoning Data Lineage

Data Lineage records the movement and transformation of data between systems.

A lineage model may show:

```text id="h8v3qs"
SOURCE
  |
  v
COLLECTION
  |
  v
TRANSFORMATION
  |
  v
DATASET
  |
  v
TRAINING / RETRIEVAL
  |
  v
AI SYSTEM
```

Lineage is particularly important for identifying all models and systems potentially affected by poisoned information.

### 22. AI Data Poisoning Dataset

A dataset is a structured or unstructured collection of information used by an AI system.

Relevant dataset types include:

- training;
- validation;
- testing;
- fine-tuning;
- retrieval;
- evaluation;
- feedback.

The same poisoning technique can have different consequences depending on dataset purpose.

### 23. AI Data Poisoning Training Dataset

A training dataset contains information used to develop or modify model behavior.

Poisoning may cause:

- altered decision boundaries;
- reduced performance;
- targeted behavior;
- backdoors;
- incorrect associations.

The presence of suspicious data does not establish that the model learned from it.

### 24. AI Data Poisoning Fine-Tuning Dataset

A fine-tuning dataset modifies an existing model's behavior for a specific task, domain, or style.

Poisoning may therefore introduce:

- targeted behaviors;
- incorrect responses;
- domain-specific weaknesses;
- persistent undesirable associations.

Fine-tuning data should be governed with appropriate provenance and integrity controls.

### 25. AI Data Poisoning Evaluation Dataset

An evaluation dataset is used to measure model behavior.

Poisoning an evaluation dataset may create a false impression of model quality by:

- removing difficult examples;
- introducing favorable examples;
- manipulating labels;
- hiding known weaknesses.

This can produce an assurance failure even when the production model itself has not been directly poisoned.

### 26. AI Data Poisoning Validation Dataset

A validation dataset is used during model development to support model selection, tuning, or validation.

Compromise of validation data can distort:

- parameter selection;
- model comparison;
- threshold selection;
- performance estimates.

### 27. AI Data Poisoning Retrieval Dataset

A retrieval dataset contains information available to an AI system during runtime retrieval.

Poisoning may cause the system to retrieve:

- false information;
- malicious instructions;
- misleading content;
- outdated information.

Retrieval poisoning can therefore affect model outputs without modifying model weights.

### 28. AI Data Poisoning Knowledge Base

An AI knowledge base contains information used to provide context or grounding to AI systems.

Poisoning may involve:

- document manipulation;
- fabricated information;
- malicious instructions;
- incorrect metadata;
- altered records.

A knowledge-base poisoning incident may affect many users simultaneously.

### 29. AI Data Poisoning Feedback Data

Feedback Data includes information generated by users, reviewers, or systems to influence future AI behavior.

Potential sources include:

- ratings;
- corrections;
- preference data;
- annotations;
- conversation feedback.

Because feedback may influence future model behavior, it should be treated as an AI data supply-chain component.

### 30. AI Data Poisoning Synthetic Data

Synthetic Data is artificially generated information used in AI development, evaluation, retrieval, or training.

Synthetic data can create poisoning risk when:

- it contains fabricated information;
- provenance is unclear;
- generated content is repeatedly reused;
- quality controls are weak.

Synthetic data should not automatically be considered reliable simply because it was generated by another AI system.

### 31. AI Data Poisoning Label Manipulation

Label Manipulation occurs when classification or annotation labels are altered to influence model learning.

Potential consequences include:

- incorrect classifications;
- degraded model performance;
- targeted misclassification;
- biased behavior.

Label integrity should therefore be treated as part of the AI data integrity boundary.

### 32. AI Data Poisoning Feature Manipulation

Feature Manipulation changes input attributes used by a model in order to influence learned behavior.

Potential effects include:

- incorrect predictions;
- degraded detection;
- targeted model behavior.

Feature manipulation may occur during data preparation rather than within the model itself.

### 33. AI Data Poisoning Backdoor

A poisoning backdoor is a condition in which manipulated training or fine-tuning data causes a model to behave normally under ordinary conditions but produce attacker-desired behavior under specific triggers.

A conceptual pattern is:

```text id="n6q2wr"
NORMAL INPUT
     |
     v
NORMAL BEHAVIOR

TRIGGERED INPUT
     |
     v
POISONED BEHAVIOR
```

Detection can be difficult because ordinary testing may not activate the trigger.

### 34. AI Data Poisoning Targeted Poisoning

Targeted Poisoning attempts to influence a specific class of inputs, users, decisions, or behaviors.

Examples may include:

- specific classifications;
- particular entities;
- defined phrases;
- selected images;
- specific operational conditions.

Targeted poisoning may have limited overall statistical impact while creating significant localized risk.

### 35. AI Data Poisoning Availability Attack

Availability-oriented poisoning attempts to degrade the usefulness or reliability of an AI system.

Potential effects include:

- widespread incorrect outputs;
- model degradation;
- excessive false positives;
- excessive false negatives;
- operational disruption.

### 36. AI Data Poisoning Integrity Attack

Integrity-oriented poisoning attempts to cause the AI system to produce outputs that differ from the intended behavior.

The objective may be:

- altered classification;
- incorrect recommendations;
- manipulated rankings;
- changed responses.

### 37. AI Data Poisoning Confidentiality Relationship

Poisoning primarily concerns data integrity, but confidentiality may also be involved.

For example, an attacker may first obtain unauthorized access to confidential training data and then modify it.

The confidentiality and integrity impacts should therefore be assessed separately.

### 38. AI Data Poisoning Privacy Relationship

Poisoning can create privacy implications when manipulated data:

- contains personal data;
- causes disclosure;
- affects profiling;
- changes decisions involving individuals;
- introduces fabricated information about identifiable persons.

Privacy impact should be separately assessed rather than inferred solely from the presence of poisoning.

### 39. AI Data Poisoning Safety Relationship

Poisoned data can create safety implications when it causes AI systems to generate unsafe outputs or recommendations.

Potentially affected systems may include:

- industrial systems;
- healthcare applications;
- transportation systems;
- safety monitoring;
- critical operational processes.

The existence of poisoned data does not itself establish a safety incident.

### 40. AI Data Poisoning Regulatory Relationship

A poisoning incident may have regulatory implications depending on:

- jurisdiction;
- AI system;
- organizational role;
- sector;
- affected population;
- use case;
- applicable legal instrument;
- actual impact.

An internal designation such as “critical poisoning incident” should not automatically be treated as a statutory classification.

### 41. AI Data Poisoning Business Impact

Business Impact assesses consequences for organizational operations.

Potential impacts include:

- incorrect decisions;
- service disruption;
- financial loss;
- customer impact;
- remediation cost;
- loss of productivity;
- operational instability.

### 42. AI Data Poisoning Blast Radius

Blast Radius represents the scope of AI systems, users, processes, or decisions potentially affected by poisoned data.

Factors include:

- shared datasets;
- shared models;
- shared knowledge bases;
- deployment scope;
- downstream dependencies.

A single poisoned source can have a large blast radius when reused widely.

### 43. AI Data Poisoning Persistence

Persistence describes how long the effects of poisoned information may remain after the original data is removed.

Persistence may result from:

- trained model weights;
- cached outputs;
- copied documents;
- embeddings;
- indexes;
- downstream datasets;
- derivative models.

Removing the original poisoned record does not necessarily remove its learned or propagated effects.

### 44. AI Data Poisoning Detectability

Detectability represents how difficult it is to identify poisoning.

Detection may be difficult when:

- poisoning affects only a small percentage of records;
- changes resemble legitimate data;
- triggers are rare;
- ground truth is unavailable;
- provenance is incomplete.

Low detectability increases the importance of preventive and detective controls.

### 45. AI Data Poisoning Recoverability

Recoverability represents the organization's ability to restore trustworthy AI behavior after poisoning.

Recovery may require:

- trusted datasets;
- clean model versions;
- reproducible pipelines;
- known-good configurations;
- alternative models;
- validation.

Restoring the model service does not automatically establish that poisoning has been eliminated.

### 46. AI Data Poisoning Evidence

Evidence may include:

- original dataset;
- modified dataset;
- dataset hashes;
- provenance;
- access logs;
- pipeline logs;
- model versions;
- training records;
- evaluation results;
- affected outputs.

Evidence should be preserved in accordance with applicable security, privacy, retention, and investigation requirements.

### 47. AI Data Poisoning Timeline

A poisoning timeline establishes the sequence of:

```text id="s7m4vp"

    |
    v

    |
    v
PROCESSING
    |
    v

    |
    v

    |

    |
    v
CONTAINMENT
    |
    v

    |
    v

```

The timeline should distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions and investigative hypotheses.

### 48. AI Data Poisoning Root Cause

Root Cause identifies the underlying condition that allowed poisoned data to enter or influence the AI lifecycle.

Potential root causes include:

- inadequate access control;
- weak source validation;
- compromised supplier;
- insecure ingestion;
- poor provenance;
- insufficient dataset review;
- pipeline compromise;
- inadequate monitoring.

Root cause should be established through evidence rather than inferred solely from observed model behavior.

### 49. AI Data Poisoning Incident Governance Principle

**AI data poisoning incident management should treat data integrity as a lifecycle security concern extending from source acquisition through ingestion, transformation, storage, training, retrieval, evaluation, deployment, and downstream use.**

Effective management requires the organization to distinguish:

- data quality from malicious poisoning;
- contamination from confirmed compromise;
- data manipulation from model compromise;
- correlation from causation;
- suspicious data from confirmed root cause;
- technical restoration from trustworthy recovery.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Data Poisoning Incidents

**AI data poisoning risk must be governed as an end-to-end AI supply-chain and data-integrity problem rather than solely as a model-security problem.**

A mature incident capability should be able to establish:

- where the affected data originated;
- how the data entered the AI lifecycle;
- what transformations occurred;
- which datasets and models were affected;
- whether model behavior changed;
- what systems and users were exposed;
- how the poisoning was contained;
- whether downstream contamination occurred;
- whether recovery restored a trustworthy state.

Data poisoning classifications, dataset registers, provenance controls, integrity checks, poisoning detection, incident severity models, containment procedures, and recovery mechanisms are generally **organizational governance and implementation practices** unless a specific law, regulation, contract, or authoritative requirement makes a particular activity mandatory.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.

## Part 2 — AI Data Poisoning Incident Controls, Detection, Assessment, Containment, Investigation and Remediation

### 51. AI Data Poisoning Incident Management Policy

An AI Data Poisoning Incident Management Policy establishes organizational requirements for detecting, assessing, containing, investigating, remediating, and learning from suspected or confirmed data poisoning.

The policy should define:

- scope;
- affected AI lifecycle stages;
- ownership;
- incident thresholds;
- evidence requirements;
- escalation;
- containment;
- recovery;
- notification assessment;
- remediation; and
- exception management.

The policy should distinguish internal governance requirements from applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations.

### 52. AI Data Poisoning Incident Scope

Incident scope should cover data sources and processing stages that can influence AI behavior.

Potential scope includes:

- training datasets;
- fine-tuning datasets;
- evaluation datasets;
- retrieval repositories;
- knowledge bases;
- embeddings;
- feedback systems;
- synthetic datasets;
- external data feeds;
- data-transformation pipelines.

### 53. AI Data Poisoning Incident Register

An incident register records suspected and confirmed poisoning events.

Typical fields include:

- incident identifier;
- affected dataset;
- dataset version;
- source;
- model;
- model version;
- detection date;
- suspected entry point;
- poisoning type;
- affected systems;
- severity;
- containment;
- investigation status;
- root cause;
- remediation;
- recovery status.

### 54. AI Data Poisoning Intake

Poisoning Intake provides mechanisms for reporting suspicious data or AI behavior.

Potential sources include:

- data engineers;
- security teams;
- model-validation teams;
- AI users;
- suppliers;
- monitoring systems;
- internal audit;
- automated integrity controls.

Reports should be accepted even when poisoning has not yet been confirmed.

### 55. AI Data Poisoning Detection Control

A Data Poisoning Detection Control identifies suspicious manipulation or contamination of AI-relevant data.

Detection mechanisms may include:

- integrity checks;
- anomaly detection;
- provenance analysis;
- statistical analysis;
- duplicate detection;
- label consistency checks;
- source validation;
- behavioral testing.

No single detection mechanism should be assumed to detect all poisoning techniques.

### 56. AI Data Integrity Monitoring

Data Integrity Monitoring continuously evaluates whether AI-relevant datasets remain consistent with approved integrity expectations.

Monitoring may examine:

- hashes;
- record counts;
- schema;
- labels;
- metadata;
- timestamps;
- source identifiers;
- unauthorized modifications.

### 57. AI Dataset Version Monitoring

Dataset Version Monitoring tracks changes between approved and operational dataset versions.

Important information includes:

- version identifier;
- creation time;
- change description;
- owner;
- source;
- approval;
- deployment status.

Unexpected versions should trigger investigation according to defined thresholds.

### 58. AI Dataset Hash Verification

Hash Verification can detect changes to datasets or artifacts by comparing calculated hashes with trusted reference values.

However:

**Hash integrity ≠ data trustworthiness.**

A poisoned dataset can have a valid hash if the poisoned version was legitimately hashed after manipulation.

### 59. AI Dataset Digital-Signature Verification

Digital-signature verification can provide evidence that an artifact originated from an identified signing entity and has not been modified after signing.

However:

**Signature validity ≠ dataset correctness.**

The organization must still assess whether the signer, dataset, and intended use are trustworthy.

### 60. AI Dataset Provenance Verification

Provenance Verification evaluates whether dataset origin and processing history can be supported by evidence.

Evidence may include:

- source records;
- collection logs;
- transformation records;
- ownership;
- timestamps;
- version history.

### 61. AI Data Source Validation

Source Validation evaluates whether information originates from an approved and appropriate source.

Validation may consider:

- identity;
- authorization;
- reputation;
- provenance;
- integrity;
- relevance;
- expected data characteristics.

Source approval does not permanently establish trust.

### 62. AI Data Ingestion Control

Data Ingestion Control regulates how information enters AI processing environments.

Controls may include:

- authentication;
- authorization;
- schema validation;
- malware scanning;
- provenance capture;
- integrity verification;
- quarantine;
- approval.

### 63. AI Data Ingestion Quarantine

Ingestion Quarantine temporarily isolates new or suspicious data before it becomes available to AI systems.

Potential quarantine triggers include:

- unknown source;
- integrity failure;
- anomalous volume;
- suspicious content;
- missing provenance;
- validation failure.

### 64. AI Dataset Integrity Baseline

An Integrity Baseline defines expected characteristics for an approved dataset.

The baseline may include:

- size;
- schema;
- distributions;
- labels;
- source composition;
- metadata;
- expected update frequency.

Unexpected deviations should be investigated rather than automatically classified as poisoning.

### 65. AI Dataset Statistical Anomaly Detection

Statistical Anomaly Detection identifies unusual characteristics in datasets.

Indicators may include:

- abnormal class distributions;
- unusual feature values;
- unexpected duplication;
- sudden source concentration;
- unusual temporal patterns.

Statistical anomalies are indicators, not proof of malicious poisoning.

### 66. AI Dataset Distribution Monitoring

Distribution Monitoring evaluates changes in dataset characteristics over time.

The organization should distinguish:

- legitimate distribution shift;
- natural population change;
- data-quality failure;
- malicious manipulation.

### 67. AI Label Integrity Control

Label Integrity Control protects classification and annotation information from unauthorized or inappropriate modification.

Controls may include:

- reviewer separation;
- approval;
- versioning;
- sampling;
- consistency checks.

### 68. AI Annotation Integrity Control

Annotation Integrity Control protects human or automated labeling processes used to create training and evaluation data.

Potential controls include:

- annotator authentication;
- quality review;
- disagreement analysis;
- audit trails;
- role separation.

### 69. AI Synthetic-Data Integrity Control

Synthetic-Data Integrity Control verifies that generated datasets meet defined quality, provenance, and integrity requirements before being used.

Controls may include:

- generation traceability;
- source labeling;
- human review;
- duplicate detection;
- validation against trusted references.

### 70. AI Feedback-Data Integrity Control

Feedback-Data Integrity Control protects user and system feedback used to modify or evaluate AI behavior.

Potential controls include:

- authenticated feedback;
- anti-manipulation controls;
- anomaly detection;
- reviewer validation;
- rate limiting.

### 71. AI Retrieval-Data Integrity Control

Retrieval-Data Integrity Control protects information used by runtime retrieval systems.

It may include:

- repository access control;
- document integrity;
- versioning;
- provenance;
- content validation;
- indexing controls.

### 72. AI Knowledge-Base Integrity Control

Knowledge-Base Integrity Control prevents unauthorized or unverified information from becoming trusted AI context.

Controls may include:

- approved-source lists;
- change approval;
- content validation;
- provenance;
- access control;
- integrity monitoring.

### 73. AI Embedding Integrity Control

Embedding Integrity Control protects vector representations used by retrieval systems.

Potential controls include:

- versioning;
- source traceability;
- controlled generation;
- access control;
- integrity validation.

An embedding can remain technically intact while representing contaminated source information.

### 74. AI Data Transformation Integrity

Transformation Integrity ensures that preprocessing and transformation steps do not unintentionally or maliciously alter data.

Controls may include:

- version-controlled code;
- reproducible pipelines;
- checksums;
- automated tests;
- change approval;
- output validation.

Pipeline Access Control restricts who or what can:

Segregation of Duties separates sensitive activities where practical.

- one person prepares data;
- another approves it;
- another promotes it into production.

### 125. AI Data Poisoning Incident Control Principle

**AI data poisoning controls should provide an evidence-based chain from data ingestion and integrity monitoring through detection, quarantine, forensic investigation, remediation, recovery, and behavioral validation.**

Organizations should avoid assuming that:

- an unchanged model artifact is necessarily trustworthy;
- a valid dataset hash proves data correctness;
- a signed dataset is free from poisoning;
- an anomalous record is necessarily malicious;
- removing poisoned records automatically removes learned effects;
- retraining automatically restores trustworthy behavior.

Dataset integrity controls, provenance, ingestion validation, anomaly detection, quarantine, forensic analysis, model comparison, behavioral testing, recovery validation, and supplier investigation are generally **governance and implementation practices** unless a specific law, regulation, contract, or authoritative requirement makes a particular activity mandatory.

### 400. AI Data Poisoning Enterprise Assurance Principle

**Enterprise assurance should demonstrate, through appropriate evidence, that AI data remains sufficiently trustworthy throughout its lifecycle and that poisoning can be detected, contained, investigated, remediated, and recovered from without relying on a single control or dependency.**

The organization should maintain clear separation between:

- data quality and data poisoning;
- data contamination and confirmed compromise;
- anomaly and confirmed attack;
- correlation and causation;
- dataset integrity and dataset correctness;
- provenance and trustworthiness;
- model integrity and behavioral integrity;
- technical recovery and trustworthy recovery;
- supplier assurance and independent assurance;
- contractual requirements and statutory requirements;
- internal controls and legal obligations;
- monitoring and continuous assurance;
- automation and accountability.
