# 17.04 — AI Safety Incidents

## Part 1 — AI Safety Incident Foundations, Safety Failure, Harm, Governance and Incident Boundaries

### 1. AI Safety Incident

An AI safety incident is an event in which an AI system causes, contributes to, or creates a material risk of physical, psychological, societal, environmental, or other safety-related harm.

An AI safety incident may arise from:

* unsafe model behavior;
* incorrect or unreliable outputs;
* unsafe recommendations;
* uncontrolled autonomous actions;
* failure of safety controls;
* inadequate human oversight;
* unsafe tool execution;
* unsafe interaction with external systems;
* unexpected model behavior;
* misuse that creates safety consequences.

An AI safety incident does not necessarily involve malicious activity or cybersecurity compromise.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-001`

### 2. Objective of AI Safety Incident Management

The objective of AI safety incident management is to identify, assess, contain, investigate, recover from, and learn from AI-related safety events.

The capability should protect:

* individuals;
* customers;
* employees;
* communities;
* physical systems;
* critical operations;
* organizational assets;
* the environment where relevant.

The objective is not simply to restore system availability. A system may be technically available while continuing to produce unsafe outcomes.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-002`

### 3. AI Safety Event versus AI Safety Incident

An AI safety event is an observable occurrence involving an AI system that may have safety significance.

An AI safety incident is an event that meets defined organizational criteria for incident treatment because actual or potential safety consequences are sufficiently material.

For example:

```text id="a7k3md"
AI Output Anomaly
       |
       v
Safety-Relevant?
       |
   +---+---+
   |       |
  No      Yes
   |       |
Monitor   Assess Impact
           |
           v
      Incident Criteria?
         /       \
       No         Yes
       |           |
     Record      Incident
```

Not every unusual AI output should automatically be classified as a safety incident.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-003`

### 4. AI Safety Incident versus AI Security Incident

An AI safety incident concerns harmful or potentially harmful system behavior.

An AI security incident concerns compromise, unauthorized activity, or violation of security properties.

The two can overlap.

For example, an attacker may compromise an AI agent and cause it to execute an unsafe physical action. The event may therefore have both:

* a security dimension; and
* a safety dimension.

Classification should preserve both dimensions when applicable.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-004`

### 5. AI Safety Incident versus AI Model Failure

An AI model failure occurs when a model does not perform as intended.

A safety incident occurs when the failure has safety significance.

For example:

* a model producing an incorrect answer in a low-risk internal application may be a model-quality problem;
* the same type of failure in a safety-sensitive application may become a safety incident.

Model failure and safety incident should therefore not be treated as interchangeable concepts.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-005`

### 6. AI Safety Harm

AI safety harm is a negative consequence resulting from, or materially associated with, AI-system behavior.

Potential harm may involve:

* physical injury;
* unsafe exposure;
* psychological harm;
* financial consequences;
* loss of access to essential services;
* environmental impact;
* damage to property;
* broader societal effects.

The organization should distinguish confirmed harm from potential harm.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-006`

### 7. AI Safety Hazard

An AI safety hazard is a condition or capability that could contribute to harm if not adequately controlled.

Examples include:

* unsafe autonomous action;
* uncontrolled tool access;
* inadequate fail-safe mechanisms;
* unreliable perception;
* unsafe recommendations;
* excessive system authority.

A hazard is not itself necessarily an incident.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-007`

### 8. AI Safety Risk

AI safety risk represents the possibility that an AI-related hazard could produce harmful consequences under specified circumstances.

Risk analysis should consider:

* hazard;
* initiating conditions;
* exposure;
* likelihood;
* severity;
* affected population;
* controls;
* uncertainty.

Safety risk should be evaluated throughout the AI lifecycle.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-008`

### 9. AI Safety Control

An AI safety control is a measure designed to prevent, detect, limit, or recover from unsafe AI behavior.

Controls may include:

* safety constraints;
* human oversight;
* access restrictions;
* validation;
* testing;
* rate limits;
* safe-state mechanisms;
* tool restrictions;
* emergency shutdown;
* monitoring.

A documented safety control should not automatically be considered effective without appropriate evidence.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-009`

### 10. AI Safety Boundary

An AI safety boundary defines the conditions within which an AI system is permitted to operate.

A boundary may specify:

* permitted actions;
* prohibited actions;
* authorized users;
* allowable data;
* approved environments;
* acceptable output conditions;
* human approval requirements.

A safety incident may occur when the system crosses a defined safety boundary.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-010`

### 11. AI Safety Constraint

A safety constraint restricts system behavior to reduce the possibility or severity of harm.

Examples include:

* prohibited actions;
* maximum operating limits;
* restricted tools;
* approval requirements;
* output restrictions;
* transaction limits.

Constraints should be validated against realistic failure and misuse scenarios.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-011`

### 12. AI Safety Guardrail

An AI safety guardrail is a preventive or detective mechanism intended to reduce unsafe behavior.

Guardrails may operate at:

* input;
* model;
* output;
* application;
* tool;
* workflow;
* infrastructure layers.

Guardrails should not be treated as infallible. Their failure modes should be evaluated.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-012`

### 13. AI Safety Failure

An AI safety failure occurs when a safety mechanism, process, assumption, or system behavior does not provide the expected level of protection.

Examples include:

* a safety constraint being bypassed;
* human approval being ineffective;
* an unsafe output not being detected;
* a shutdown mechanism failing;
* a safety test failing to identify a known issue.

A safety failure may exist without resulting in actual harm.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-013`

### 14. AI Safety Control Failure

A safety control failure occurs when an established safety control fails to operate as designed or fails to achieve its intended outcome.

Control failure may result from:

* design weakness;
* implementation error;
* configuration;
* inadequate monitoring;
* unexpected system behavior;
* human error;
* environmental conditions.

Control failure should be investigated separately from the resulting impact.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-014`

### 15. AI Safety Near Miss

An AI safety near miss is an event where unsafe behavior could reasonably have resulted in harm but harm did not occur.

Near misses may provide valuable evidence about:

* control weaknesses;
* latent hazards;
* detection gaps;
* unsafe assumptions;
* human-interaction problems.

Near misses should not be ignored merely because no harm occurred.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-015`

### 16. AI Safety Potential Harm

Potential harm represents a plausible harmful consequence that has not been confirmed as having occurred.

Incident records should distinguish:

* actual harm;
* credible potential harm;
* theoretical possibility.

This distinction prevents exaggerated incident classification while ensuring meaningful risks are not ignored.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-016`

### 17. AI Safety Actual Harm

Actual harm represents a confirmed negative consequence associated with an AI system.

Evidence may include:

* incident records;
* operational evidence;
* affected-person reports;
* technical evidence;
* investigation findings.

Actual harm should be documented separately from estimated or hypothetical impact.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-017`

### 18. AI Safety Severity

Safety severity represents the significance of an AI safety event or incident based on its consequences and relevant risk factors.

Severity may consider:

* harm magnitude;
* number of affected individuals;
* duration;
* reversibility;
* likelihood of recurrence;
* exposure;
* criticality;
* uncertainty.

An internal severity classification should not automatically be treated as a regulatory classification.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-018`

### 19. AI Safety Impact

Safety impact describes the consequences associated with an AI safety event.

Potential dimensions include:

* physical;
* psychological;
* operational;
* financial;
* environmental;
* societal.

Impact should be assessed using available evidence and clearly identified assumptions.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-019`

### 20. AI Safety Exposure

Safety exposure represents the population, system, environment, or process potentially exposed to an unsafe AI behavior.

Exposure analysis may consider:

* number of users;
* geographic scope;
* duration;
* affected system;
* affected process;
* interaction frequency.

Exposure does not necessarily mean harm occurred.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-020`

### 21. AI Safety Blast Radius

Safety blast radius represents the extent to which an AI safety incident could propagate across systems, users, processes, or environments.

Potential factors include:

* shared models;
* shared tools;
* common infrastructure;
* common decision processes;
* shared datasets;
* common providers.

A single unsafe behavior may therefore have a much larger potential impact than the initially observed incident.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-021`

### 22. AI Safety Propagation

Safety propagation occurs when an unsafe condition spreads from one AI component, workflow, or environment to another.

Propagation may occur through:

* shared models;
* automated workflows;
* shared data;
* APIs;
* agents;
* downstream decision systems.

Propagation should be investigated when multiple systems exhibit related safety failures.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-022`

### 23. AI Safety Containment

Safety containment limits further exposure to unsafe behavior.

Containment may include:

* disabling a feature;
* restricting users;
* disabling tools;
* reducing system authority;
* switching to human approval;
* moving to a safe state;
* shutting down the affected capability.

Containment aims to reduce ongoing exposure and does not necessarily identify the underlying cause.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-023`

### 24. AI Safety Eradication

Safety eradication removes or corrects the underlying condition that caused or enabled unsafe behavior.

Examples include:

* correcting a defect;
* replacing a model;
* modifying a safety control;
* removing an unsafe tool;
* correcting training data;
* changing system architecture.

Stopping the immediate unsafe behavior does not necessarily mean that its underlying cause has been eradicated.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-024`

### 25. AI Safety Recovery

Safety recovery restores the AI system to an acceptable operating condition after an incident.

Recovery may require:

* corrected model;
* validated configuration;
* restored controls;
* tested fallback;
* human oversight;
* behavioral validation.

Technical service restoration is not necessarily safety recovery.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-025`

### 26. AI Safety Recovery Validation

Recovery validation determines whether the AI system can safely resume the intended operation.

Validation may examine:

* safety controls;
* model behavior;
* tool permissions;
* human oversight;
* known failure modes;
* monitoring.

Recovery should not be considered complete solely because the system is operational.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-026`

### 27. AI Safety Safe State

A safe state is a predefined operating condition intended to reduce unacceptable safety risk.

Examples include:

* stopping autonomous operation;
* disabling external actions;
* requiring human approval;
* limiting functionality;
* restricting access;
* moving to manual operation.

A safe state should be appropriate to the system's actual hazards.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-027`

### 28. AI Safety Fail-Safe

A fail-safe mechanism causes a system to transition toward a safer condition when specified failure conditions occur.

Examples include:

* automatic shutdown;
* tool disablement;
* transaction blocking;
* human escalation;
* restricted operation.

Fail-safe behavior should itself be tested because a failed fail-safe can increase incident severity.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-028`

### 29. AI Safety Fail-Operational Behavior

Fail-operational behavior allows an AI system to continue operating during certain failures while maintaining defined safety constraints.

This approach may be appropriate where immediate shutdown could itself create unacceptable consequences.

The organization should explicitly determine when:

* fail-safe;
* fail-operational;
* degraded operation

is appropriate.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-029`

### 30. AI Safety Human Oversight

Human oversight provides a mechanism for humans to supervise, intervene in, or stop AI behavior.

Effective oversight should consider:

* authority;
* competence;
* response time;
* visibility;
* intervention capability;
* workload.

Human presence alone does not demonstrate effective human oversight.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-030`

### 31. AI Safety Human Intervention

Human intervention allows an authorized person to alter or stop AI behavior when predefined conditions occur.

Intervention may include:

* rejecting an AI recommendation;
* stopping an action;
* disabling a tool;
* changing system mode;
* initiating shutdown.

Intervention mechanisms should be usable under realistic operating conditions.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-031`

### 32. AI Safety Human Override

Human override provides authorized personnel with the ability to supersede an AI system's action or decision.

An override should define:

* who may exercise it;
* under what conditions;
* how it is authenticated;
* what action it produces;
* how the action is logged.

An override that exists only procedurally but cannot be executed reliably is not an effective control.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-032`

### 33. AI Safety Kill Capability

A safety kill capability allows authorized personnel or mechanisms to rapidly disable an AI capability when continued operation presents unacceptable risk.

It may disable:

* model serving;
* agent execution;
* external tools;
* automated workflows;
* specific actions.

Kill capability should be tested under controlled conditions.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-033`

### 34. AI Safety Tool Restriction

AI systems capable of invoking external tools should have controls limiting which actions they can perform.

Restrictions may include:

* allowlists;
* privilege limits;
* transaction thresholds;
* human approval;
* network segmentation;
* sandboxing.

Tool restrictions are particularly important where AI outputs can cause external effects.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-034`

### 35. AI Safety Action Authorization

Actions with safety significance should be subject to appropriate authorization.

Authorization may depend on:

* action type;
* consequence;
* confidence;
* user authority;
* system state;
* human approval.

The AI system's ability to generate an action should not automatically imply authorization to execute it.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-035`

### 36. AI Safety Autonomy Boundary

An autonomy boundary defines which decisions or actions an AI system may perform without human intervention.

The boundary should identify:

* autonomous actions;
* human-approved actions;
* prohibited actions;
* escalation conditions.

Autonomy should be proportional to the system's safety risk.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-036`

### 37. AI Safety Decision Boundary

A decision boundary specifies when an AI system should:

* act;
* request human review;
* abstain;
* escalate;
* stop.

Decision boundaries should account for uncertainty and consequences.

A confidence score alone does not necessarily establish safety.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-037`

### 38. AI Safety Abstention

Abstention occurs when an AI system declines to provide an output or execute an action because defined conditions are not satisfied.

Abstention may be appropriate when:

* confidence is insufficient;
* required information is missing;
* a safety constraint is triggered;
* authorization is absent;
* the requested action exceeds system authority.

Abstention mechanisms should be tested for both excessive and insufficient abstention.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-038`

### 39. AI Safety Uncertainty

AI safety uncertainty represents uncertainty about whether an AI system's behavior, output, or action is safe under the relevant conditions.

Sources may include:

* incomplete data;
* model uncertainty;
* unknown environment;
* novel inputs;
* distribution shift;
* incomplete testing.

Safety processes should make material uncertainty visible to decision-makers.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-039`

### 40. AI Safety Distribution Shift

Distribution shift occurs when operational inputs differ materially from the conditions represented in development or evaluation.

Potential effects include:

* reduced accuracy;
* unexpected behavior;
* failure of safety assumptions;
* increased false positives;
* increased false negatives.

Material distribution shifts should be considered in safety monitoring.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-040`

### 41. AI Safety Out-of-Distribution Condition

An out-of-distribution condition occurs when an AI system encounters inputs or circumstances materially different from those represented in its expected operating environment.

Potential responses include:

* abstention;
* human review;
* restricted operation;
* additional validation.

Out-of-distribution detection is not necessarily reliable and should itself be evaluated.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-041`

### 42. AI Safety Unexpected Behavior

Unexpected behavior is AI-system behavior that differs materially from documented or reasonably expected behavior.

Unexpected behavior may be:

* benign;
* operationally problematic;
* security-relevant;
* safety-relevant.

Unexpected behavior should be investigated according to its potential impact rather than automatically treated as an incident.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-042`

### 43. AI Safety Emergent Behavior

Emergent behavior refers to behavior that becomes apparent through system interactions, scale, deployment conditions, or combinations of capabilities and was not adequately anticipated during development.

Examples may involve:

* unexpected tool interactions;
* unexpected agent behavior;
* novel failure modes;
* unexpected coordination.

Emergent behavior should be treated as an investigation signal when it creates material safety uncertainty.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-043`

### 44. AI Safety Cascading Failure

A cascading safety failure occurs when one failure causes additional failures across dependent components or processes.

Example:

```text id="t6n9rx"
Model Error
    ↓
Unsafe Recommendation
    ↓
Automated Action
    ↓
External System Change
    ↓
Secondary Failure
    ↓
Additional Impact
```

Cascade analysis should identify where intervention could have interrupted the chain.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-044`

### 45. AI Safety Common-Mode Failure

A common-mode safety failure occurs when multiple AI systems fail because they share a common dependency, design assumption, model, dataset, infrastructure component, or control.

Examples include:

* shared model defect;
* common safety library;
* shared data problem;
* common provider failure.

Common-mode failures can defeat apparent redundancy.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-045`

### 46. AI Safety Single Point of Failure

A single point of failure is a component whose failure could materially compromise the safety of one or more AI systems without an adequate alternative.

Potential examples include:

* centralized safety gateway;
* single model provider;
* common identity system;
* shared safety service;
* single human approval function.

Single points of failure should be identified and evaluated.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-046`

### 47. AI Safety Residual Risk

Residual safety risk is the risk remaining after safety controls have been implemented.

Residual risk may remain because:

* controls are imperfect;
* uncertainty remains;
* hazards cannot be eliminated;
* operational conditions change;
* human intervention is limited.

Residual risk should have an accountable owner where material.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-047`

### 48. AI Safety Incident Evidence

Safety incident evidence may include:

* model inputs;
* model outputs;
* system state;
* tool actions;
* human interventions;
* configuration;
* logs;
* timestamps;
* affected outcomes;
* environmental conditions.

Evidence should be preserved with appropriate security, privacy, and integrity controls.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-048`

### 49. AI Safety Incident Governance Principle

AI safety incident governance should ensure that safety-relevant events are identified, assessed, contained, investigated, and used to improve the AI system and its surrounding controls.

Governance should distinguish:

* hazard from incident;
* event from incident;
* near miss from actual harm;
* potential harm from confirmed harm;
* model failure from safety incident;
* security incident from safety incident;
* control failure from resulting impact;
* containment from eradication;
* recovery from validated recovery;
* human presence from effective human oversight;
* documented shutdown capability from tested shutdown capability;
* AI autonomy from authorization;
* model confidence from safety assurance;
* unexpected behavior from confirmed unsafe behavior.

Internal governance classifications should not be represented as legal or regulatory classifications unless an applicable authority explicitly establishes them.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-049`

### 50. Core Principle of AI Safety Incident Foundations

AI safety incident management should focus on **preventing harm, detecting unsafe conditions, limiting exposure, protecting affected people and systems, restoring safe operation, and learning from failures and near misses**.

A mature safety incident capability should recognize that an AI system can remain technically operational while being unsafe. Conversely, an AI system can experience an operational or technical failure without creating a safety incident.

The central governance principle is therefore:

```text id="p9w4lc"
AI System Behavior
       ↓
Safety-Relevant Condition
       ↓
Detection / Reporting
       ↓
Safety Assessment
       ↓
Containment
       ↓
Investigation
       ↓
Eradication / Correction
       ↓
Safety Validation
       ↓
Controlled Recovery
       ↓
Lessons Learned
       ↓
Improved Safety Controls
```

AI safety incident management should integrate with AI governance, cybersecurity, privacy, business continuity, risk management, engineering, legal and compliance functions where relevant, while maintaining clear distinctions between their respective responsibilities.

Controls represented by `AI-IMR-SIR-*` are internal knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers. They do not themselves constitute statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, or legal obligations. Where a specific safety requirement is legally or regulatorily mandated, the authoritative source and applicable jurisdiction should be mapped separately.

## Part 2 — AI Safety Incident Controls, Detection, Assessment, Containment, Investigation and Recovery

### 51. AI Safety Incident Management Policy

The organization should establish an AI safety incident-management policy defining how safety-relevant events are identified, assessed, escalated, contained, investigated, recovered, and reviewed.

The policy should define:

* scope;
* roles;
* incident categories;
* severity;
* escalation;
* evidence;
* containment;
* recovery;
* communication;
* corrective action;
* governance.

The policy should distinguish organizational governance requirements from applicable legal or regulatory obligations.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-051`

### 52. AI Safety Incident Scope Control

The organization should define which AI systems and lifecycle stages are covered by safety incident management.

Scope may include:

* development;
* testing;
* validation;
* production;
* monitoring;
* retirement;
* third-party AI services;
* embedded AI components.

Safety incident scope should reflect the organization's AI risk profile.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-052`

### 53. AI Safety Incident Register

A centralized or appropriately distributed incident register should record material AI safety incidents and relevant near misses.

Records may include:

* incident identifier;
* date;
* affected system;
* hazard;
* impact;
* severity;
* affected population;
* containment;
* root cause;
* recovery;
* corrective actions.

The register should preserve appropriate historical records.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-053`

### 54. AI Safety Incident Intake

The organization should provide mechanisms through which safety concerns can be reported.

Sources may include:

* employees;
* customers;
* operators;
* developers;
* safety monitoring;
* security teams;
* suppliers;
* automated systems;
* internal audit.

Reporting channels should be accessible to relevant stakeholders.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-054`

### 55. AI Safety Incident Detection Control

Detection controls should identify unsafe AI behavior or conditions that may lead to harm.

Detection mechanisms may include:

* output monitoring;
* safety-rule violations;
* anomaly detection;
* human reports;
* operational telemetry;
* automated testing;
* tool monitoring;
* behavioral monitoring.

Detection coverage should be assessed against relevant hazards.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-055`

### 56. AI Safety Event Reporting

Safety-relevant events should be reportable even when they do not initially meet the organization's incident threshold.

This allows the organization to identify:

* near misses;
* emerging patterns;
* repeated failures;
* weak safety controls;
* new hazards.

Event reporting should not require the reporter to establish root cause.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-056`

### 57. AI Safety Monitoring

Safety monitoring should provide visibility into behavior relevant to identified hazards.

Monitoring may include:

* output quality;
* prohibited behavior;
* abnormal actions;
* tool execution;
* decision outcomes;
* human interventions;
* safety-control activation.

Monitoring requirements should be proportional to risk.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-057`

### 58. AI Safety Behavioral Baseline

A behavioral baseline describes expected AI behavior under defined operating conditions.

The baseline may include:

* normal outputs;
* expected action patterns;
* tool usage;
* response ranges;
* escalation frequency;
* human-intervention rates.

Baseline changes should be governed when they affect safety expectations.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-058`

### 59. AI Safety Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection identifies behavior that differs from expected safety-related patterns.

Potential anomalies include:

* unusual actions;
* unexpected tool use;
* abnormal output distributions;
* unexpected escalation;
* unusual failure rates.

An anomaly is a signal for investigation and does not automatically constitute a safety incident.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-059`

### 60. AI Safety Rule-Violation Detection

Where explicit safety rules exist, monitoring should identify violations.

Examples include:

* prohibited action;
* unauthorized tool invocation;
* unsafe transaction;
* restricted output;
* exceeded operational limit.

Rule violations should be assessed for actual or potential safety impact.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-060`

### 61. AI Safety Threshold Monitoring

Safety thresholds may define boundaries for acceptable operation.

Examples include:

* maximum transaction value;
* maximum autonomous duration;
* maximum operating range;
* confidence threshold;
* escalation threshold;
* human-approval threshold.

Thresholds should be periodically reviewed against actual operating conditions.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-061`

### 62. AI Safety Alert Validation

Safety alerts should be validated before being classified as confirmed incidents where practical.

Validation may determine whether the alert represents:

* expected behavior;
* false positive;
* safety event;
* near miss;
* confirmed safety incident.

Validation should be proportionate to potential harm.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-062`

### 63. AI Safety Incident Triage

Safety incident triage should rapidly determine:

* what happened;
* which system is affected;
* whether harm occurred;
* whether harm is continuing;
* who may be affected;
* whether immediate containment is required.

Triage should prioritize protection from continuing harm.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-063`

### 64. AI Safety Severity Matrix

The organization should establish criteria for classifying safety incident severity.

Example:

| Dimension       | Lower             | Moderate       | High              | Critical                 |
| --------------- | ----------------- | -------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------ |
| Harm            | None/minimal      | Limited        | Significant       | Severe                   |
| Exposure        | Limited           | Multiple users | Broad             | Systemic                 |
| Duration        | Short             | Moderate       | Extended          | Persistent               |
| Reversibility   | Easily reversible | Recoverable    | Difficult         | Potentially irreversible |
| Control failure | Limited           | Material       | Multiple controls | Systemic                 |

This matrix is an internal governance mechanism unless an applicable authority establishes a different mandatory classification.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-064`

### 65. AI Safety Impact Assessment

Safety impact assessment should identify the actual and potential consequences of an incident.

Assessment may consider:

* affected persons;
* severity;
* duration;
* physical consequences;
* psychological consequences;
* financial consequences;
* operational consequences;
* environmental consequences.

Actual and potential impacts should remain separately identified.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-065`

### 66. AI Safety Population Impact

The organization should determine the population potentially affected by an AI safety incident.

Analysis may include:

* number of individuals;
* user categories;
* geographic distribution;
* vulnerable populations where relevant;
* duration of exposure.

Population estimates should be supported by available evidence.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-066`

### 67. AI Safety Vulnerability Consideration

Where an AI safety incident may disproportionately affect vulnerable individuals or groups, the investigation should consider that possibility.

The analysis should be evidence-based and should not assume vulnerability without a relevant basis.

Potential factors may include:

* dependence on the affected service;
* inability to override the AI system;
* heightened consequences from incorrect decisions;
* limited access to alternatives.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-067`

### 68. AI Safety Duration Assessment

Incident duration should be measured from the earliest relevant occurrence through containment or another defined endpoint.

The timeline should distinguish:

* first occurrence;
* detection;
* reporting;
* containment;
* recovery.

Delayed detection can materially increase exposure even where the underlying unsafe behavior is limited.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-068`

### 69. AI Safety Exposure Assessment

Exposure assessment should determine how extensively the unsafe behavior was available or delivered.

Factors may include:

* system availability;
* number of interactions;
* affected users;
* operating environment;
* geographic scope;
* duration.

Exposure should not be equated automatically with harm.

Control ID: `AI-IMR-SIR-069`

### 70. AI Safety Reversibility Assessment

The organization should assess whether the consequences of unsafe AI behavior can be reversed.

Examples:

* reversible transaction;
* reversible configuration;
* recoverable data;
* recoverable decision;
* irreversible physical or societal consequence.

Lower reversibility may justify stronger preventive and containment controls.
