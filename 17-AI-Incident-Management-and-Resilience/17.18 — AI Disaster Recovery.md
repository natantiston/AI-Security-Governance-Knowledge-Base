# 17.18 — AI Disaster Recovery

## Part 1 — AI Disaster Recovery Foundations, Recovery Concepts, Trusted Recovery, Dependencies and Governance

### 1. AI Disaster Recovery

AI disaster recovery is the organizational capability to restore AI systems, supporting infrastructure, data, models, configurations, and critical dependencies after a major disruptive event.

AI disaster recovery extends beyond restoring servers or applications. Recovery must establish whether the restored AI capability is sufficiently trustworthy to return to operation.

### 2. Objective of AI Disaster Recovery

The objective of AI disaster recovery is to restore critical AI-enabled services within defined recovery objectives while maintaining appropriate:

- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- integrity;
- availability;
- human oversight;
- regulatory and contractual compliance.

### 3. AI Disaster

An AI disaster is a disruptive event that materially impairs the organization's ability to operate one or more AI systems or AI-dependent business services.

Examples may include:

- major infrastructure failure;
- cloud-region outage;
- destructive cyberattack;
- ransomware;
- model repository loss;
- critical dataset corruption;
- identity-platform failure;
- supplier-wide outage.

### 4. AI Disaster Recovery Event

An AI disaster recovery event is an occurrence that requires recovery activities beyond normal operational restoration procedures.

Not every AI incident is a disaster-recovery event.

### 5. AI Disaster Recovery versus AI Incident Response

Incident response focuses primarily on detecting, containing, investigating, and managing an incident.

Disaster recovery focuses primarily on restoring affected technology and business capabilities following a significant disruption.

The two capabilities should operate together but should not be treated as identical.

### 6. AI Disaster Recovery versus Business Continuity

Business continuity addresses how critical business services continue during disruption.

Disaster recovery addresses restoration of technology and supporting capabilities.

An AI business service may continue through manual or alternative processes while the primary AI platform remains unavailable.

### 7. AI Disaster Recovery versus AI Recovery

AI recovery is a broader concept that may include recovery from individual incidents, failures, configuration problems, or operational disruptions.

Disaster recovery generally addresses larger-scale disruption requiring formally planned recovery capabilities.

### 8. AI Disaster Recovery Scope

The recovery scope should identify all components necessary to restore the AI-enabled service.

This may include:

- models;
- model-serving infrastructure;
- applications;
- datasets;
- vector databases;
- configurations;
- identities;
- secrets;
- networks;
- APIs;
- monitoring;
- security controls.

### 9. AI Disaster Recovery Asset

An AI disaster-recovery asset is any technology, information, configuration, service, or capability required to recover an AI system or AI-dependent business service.

### 10. AI Disaster Recovery Criticality

Recovery criticality represents the importance of restoring an AI capability relative to organizational objectives.

Criticality may consider:

- business-service dependency;
- safety impact;
- customer impact;
- financial impact;
- regulatory significance;
- operational dependency.

### 11. AI Disaster Recovery Business Service

The business service is the organizational capability that depends on AI rather than merely the underlying AI technology.

Recovery planning should therefore establish relationships between:

### 12. AI Disaster Recovery Dependency

A recovery dependency is a component that must be available or restored before an AI service can successfully return to operation.

Dependencies may include:

- identity;
- networking;
- compute;
- storage;
- databases;
- model registries;
- datasets;
- cloud services;
- external APIs;
- suppliers.

### 13. AI Disaster Recovery Dependency Mapping

Dependency mapping identifies relationships between AI systems and the components required for recovery.

A recovery dependency map should include both technical and organizational dependencies.

### 14. AI Disaster Recovery Dependency Chain

Recovery may require a sequence of dependent components.

Restoring a downstream component before its critical dependencies may not produce a functioning service.

### 15. AI Disaster Recovery Single Point of Failure

A single point of failure is a dependency whose failure can prevent recovery of a material AI capability.

Examples may include:

- one model registry;
- one cloud region;
- one identity service;
- one specialized recovery team;
- one proprietary deployment platform.

### 16. AI Disaster Recovery Common-Mode Failure

Common-mode failure occurs when multiple supposedly independent recovery mechanisms depend on the same underlying component or condition.

Examples include:

- primary and backup systems using the same cloud provider;
- primary and backup identity systems sharing the same failure domain;
- multiple recovery environments depending on the same network;
- separate model copies stored under the same compromised account.

### 17. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Strategy

A recovery strategy defines how the organization will restore AI capabilities following a major disruption.

Strategies may include:

- restoration;
- rollback;
- failover;
- alternate infrastructure;
- alternate model;
- alternate provider;
- manual fallback;
- reduced-function operation.

### 18. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Plan

A recovery plan documents the procedures required to execute the selected recovery strategy.

It should identify:

- roles;
- dependencies;
- sequence;
- recovery targets;
- procedures;
- validation;
- communication;
- authorization.

### 19. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Procedure

A recovery procedure provides operational instructions for restoring a specific component or service.

Procedures should be sufficiently detailed for authorized personnel to execute them during stressful conditions.

### 20. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Runbook

A recovery runbook provides structured operational steps for recovery.

A runbook may include:

- prerequisites;
- commands or actions;
- dependencies;
- decision points;
- validation criteria;
- escalation conditions;
- rollback procedures.

### 21. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Authority

Recovery authority defines who may authorize restoration of an AI capability.

Authority may differ between:

- technical restoration;
- business-service restoration;
- security recovery;
- safety recovery;
- final production re-enablement.

### 22. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Sequence

Recovery should follow an appropriately defined sequence.

A generic sequence may be:

### 23. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Target

A recovery target is the defined state to which an AI component or service should be restored.

A recovery target should specify sufficient detail to distinguish:

- version;
- configuration;
- data state;
- dependencies;
- security state;
- operational state.

### 24. AI Disaster Recovery Trusted Recovery State

A trusted recovery state is a recovery state that has been sufficiently validated against defined security, integrity, safety, privacy, and operational criteria.

Restoration to a functioning state does not automatically establish a trusted recovery state.

### 25. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Environment

A recovery environment is the infrastructure or platform used to restore and validate an AI capability.

It may be:

- a secondary cloud region;
- an alternate data center;
- a disaster-recovery platform;
- a separate tenant;
- an isolated recovery environment.

### 26. AI Disaster Recovery Isolated Recovery Environment

An isolated recovery environment separates recovery activities from potentially compromised or unstable production environments.

Isolation may reduce the risk of:

- reinfection;
- contamination;
- unauthorized access;
- propagation;
- recovery-state corruption.

### 27. AI Disaster Recovery Clean Recovery Environment

A clean recovery environment is an environment sufficiently separated and validated to support recovery without relying on potentially compromised components.

The definition of “clean” should be supported by evidence rather than assumed from physical or logical separation alone.

### 28. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Artifact

Recovery artifacts may include:

- model files;
- container images;
- infrastructure definitions;
- application packages;
- configurations;
- policies;
- datasets;
- backup metadata.

### 29. AI Disaster Recovery Model Recovery

Model recovery restores the model artifact and associated components required to serve the model.

Model recovery should consider:

- artifact integrity;
- provenance;
- version;
- dependencies;
- configuration;
- behavioral validation.

### 30. AI Disaster Recovery Dataset Recovery

Dataset recovery restores required data from an approved recovery point.

The organization should assess whether recovered datasets are:

- complete;
- usable;
- appropriately authorized;
- sufficiently intact;
- free from known contamination.

### 31. AI Disaster Recovery Configuration Recovery

Configuration recovery restores the configuration necessary for correct operation.

This may include:

- model parameters;
- application configuration;
- security policies;
- access controls;
- routing;
- tool permissions.

### 32. AI Disaster Recovery Identity Recovery

Identity recovery restores the authentication and authorization capabilities required to operate the AI service securely.

Identity recovery should include appropriate controls for:

- privileged accounts;
- service identities;
- credentials;
- secrets;
- access policies.

### 33. AI Disaster Recovery Dependency Recovery

Critical dependencies should be recovered in an order that supports successful restoration of the AI service.

### 34. AI Disaster Recovery Backup

A backup is a preserved copy of information or system state that may support recovery.

A backup does not by itself demonstrate:

- recoverability;
- integrity;
- compatibility;
- security;
- behavioral correctness.

### 35. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Point

A recovery point represents the state to which data or system components can be restored.

Recovery-point selection should consider:

- data loss tolerance;
- model changes;
- configuration changes;
- transactional state;
- regulatory requirements where applicable.

### 36. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Point Objective

The Recovery Point Objective (RPO) defines the maximum tolerable amount of data or state loss expressed as a recovery-point requirement.

For AI systems, RPO may apply to:

- datasets;
- model artifacts;
- configurations;
- prompts;
- agent state;
- transaction records;
- audit logs.

### 37. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Time Objective

The Recovery Time Objective (RTO) defines the target period within which an affected service or capability should be restored following a disruption.

RTO should be defined according to business-service criticality and recovery feasibility.

### 38. AI Disaster Recovery Maximum Tolerable Downtime

Maximum tolerable downtime represents the longest period an organization can tolerate disruption to a business service before consequences become unacceptable according to its governance criteria.

It should not automatically be equated with the RTO.

### 39. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Capacity

Recovery capacity represents the organization's ability to restore required services under expected disruption conditions.

Capacity may include:

- infrastructure;
- personnel;
- bandwidth;
- storage;
- model-serving resources;
- supplier capacity.

### 40. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Priority

Recovery priority establishes the order in which AI systems or business services should be restored when resources are constrained.

Prioritization may consider:

- business criticality;
- safety;
- customer impact;
- dependency centrality;
- regulatory significance;
- operational consequences.

### 41. AI Disaster Recovery Failover

Failover transfers service operation from a failed primary environment to an alternative environment.

Failover does not automatically establish that the alternate environment is secure, current, or behaviorally equivalent.

### 42. AI Disaster Recovery Failback

Failback returns service operation from the recovery environment to the designated primary environment.

Failback should be separately planned and validated.

### 43. AI Disaster Recovery Alternate Model

An alternate model may provide continued service when the primary model is unavailable.

An alternate model should not automatically be assumed to have equivalent:

- behavior;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- performance.

### 44. AI Disaster Recovery Manual Fallback

Manual fallback provides a non-AI or reduced-automation process for maintaining critical business operations.

Manual fallback capacity should be assessed against realistic workload requirements.

### 45. AI Disaster Recovery Graceful Degradation

Graceful degradation reduces AI functionality while preserving selected critical services.

Examples include:

- disabling autonomous actions;
- reducing automation;
- restricting tool access;
- switching to human approval;
- limiting functionality.

### 46. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Validation

Recovery validation establishes whether the restored system is sufficiently operational and trustworthy for its intended use.

Validation may include:

- functional testing;
- security testing;
- integrity verification;
- privacy checks;
- safety checks;
- performance testing;
- dependency validation.

### 47. AI Disaster Recovery Reconnection

Reconnection returns a recovered AI component to broader production or enterprise environments.

Reconnection should occur only after appropriate validation and authorization.

### 48. AI Disaster Recovery Re-Enablement

Re-enablement restores normal AI functionality after recovery.

Re-enablement should be distinguished from technical restoration because a restored system may require additional monitoring or restrictions before full operation.

### 49. AI Disaster Recovery Governance Principle

**AI disaster recovery should restore critical AI-enabled services through controlled, dependency-aware, evidence-based recovery processes that distinguish technical restoration from validated and sufficiently trusted operational recovery.**

### 50. Core Principle of AI Disaster Recovery

**An AI system is not considered reliably recovered merely because it is running again; disaster recovery should establish that the required model, data, configuration, infrastructure, dependencies, security controls, and operational state have been restored and appropriately validated before normal operation is resumed.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- disaster recovery ≠ incident response;
- disaster recovery ≠ business continuity;
- recovery ≠ restoration;
- restoration ≠ trusted recovery;
- backup ≠ recoverability;
- backup ≠ validated recovery;
- recovery point ≠ recovery capability;
- RTO ≠ maximum tolerable downtime;
- failover ≠ resilience by itself;
- failback ≠ automatic restoration of trust;
- alternate model ≠ behavioral equivalent;
- manual fallback ≠ full service equivalence;
- shutdown ≠ disaster recovery;
- rollback ≠ disaster recovery;
- recovery ≠ eradication;
- technical validation ≠ complete assurance;
- documentation ≠ demonstrated recovery capability;
- recovery procedure ≠ tested recovery capability;
- internal recovery target ≠ statutory requirement;
- internal RTO/RPO ≠ automatically a legal requirement;
- contractual recovery requirement ≠ statutory requirement;
- internal governance control ≠ legal obligation.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers remain knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.

## Part 2 — AI Disaster Recovery Controls, Recovery Planning, Restoration, Validation and Governance

### 51. AI Disaster Recovery Policy

The organization should maintain an AI disaster recovery policy defining:

- scope;
- recovery objectives;
- criticality;
- recovery responsibilities;
- recovery authority;
- testing requirements;
- evidence requirements;
- exception management.

The policy should distinguish disaster recovery from incident response, business continuity, and ordinary operational recovery.

### 52. AI Disaster Recovery Scope Control

The recovery scope should identify AI systems and supporting components that require disaster-recovery capability.

The scope should include material dependencies rather than only the primary AI application.

### 53. AI Disaster Recovery Inventory

The organization should maintain an inventory of recoverable AI assets, including:

- models;
- datasets;
- applications;
- infrastructure;
- configurations;
- identities;
- APIs;
- tools;
- monitoring;
- suppliers.

### 54. AI Disaster Recovery Criticality Assessment

Each material AI capability should have an established criticality classification based on its potential effect on:

- business services;
- customers;
- safety;
- security;
- privacy;
- financial operations.

### 55. AI Disaster Recovery Business Impact Assessment

A Business Impact Analysis (BIA) should identify the consequences of prolonged disruption to AI-dependent business services.

The assessment may determine:

- critical services;
- dependencies;
- acceptable downtime;
- recovery priorities;
- recovery objectives.

### 56. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Objective Control

Recovery objectives should be formally defined for material AI services.

Relevant objectives may include:

- RTO;
- RPO;
- maximum tolerable downtime;
- recovery capacity;
- recovery priority.

### 57. AI Disaster Recovery RTO Control

RTO values should be established according to business-service criticality and realistic recovery capability.

An RTO should not be selected without considering:

- infrastructure capacity;
- personnel;
- dependencies;
- supplier availability;
- recovery complexity.

### 58. AI Disaster Recovery RPO Control

RPO requirements should identify how much data or state loss is acceptable for the affected service.

For AI systems, this may include:

- datasets;
- model versions;
- configurations;
- agent state;
- transactions;
- audit records.

### 59. AI Disaster Recovery Maximum-Downtime Control

Maximum tolerable downtime should be established separately from RTO.

The relationship may be represented as:

```text id="f3n8qy"
          |
          v
       RTO TARGET
          |
          v

```

An RTO should normally provide sufficient margin within the organization's established tolerance.

### 60. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Strategy Control

Each material AI service should have an approved recovery strategy.

Potential strategies include:

- restore;
- failover;
- rollback;
- alternate infrastructure;
- alternate provider;
- alternate model;
- manual fallback.

### 61. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Plan Control

Recovery plans should define:

- activation criteria;
- recovery sequence;
- responsibilities;
- dependencies;
- recovery targets;
- validation;
- communications;
- authorization.

### 62. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Procedure Control

Procedures should provide actionable instructions for recovering individual components.

Procedures should identify prerequisites and expected outcomes.

### 63. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Runbook Control

Recovery runbooks should provide operational steps that can be executed under incident or disaster conditions.

A runbook may include:

1. assessment;
2. authorization;
3. dependency restoration;
4. component restoration;
5. validation;
6. reconnection;
7. monitoring.

### 64. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Authority Control

Recovery authority should be formally assigned.

Different authorities may apply to:

- technical restoration;
- security restoration;
- business-service restoration;
- safety-related restoration;
- final re-enablement.

### 65. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Escalation Control

Recovery plans should define escalation when:

- recovery exceeds RTO;
- dependencies fail;
- recovery targets are unavailable;
- validation fails;
- residual risk exceeds authority.

### 66. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Dependency Control

Recovery dependencies should be identified before a disaster occurs.

Dependencies may include:

- identity;
- networking;
- storage;
- compute;
- databases;
- model repositories;
- security services;
- external providers.

### 69. AI Disaster Recovery Common-Mode Dependency Control

Recovery environments should be appropriately maintained and protected.

- where the environment exists;

### 71. AI Disaster Recovery Isolated Recovery Environment Control

Where compromise is possible, recovery should occur in an environment sufficiently isolated from affected production systems.

- compromise indicators;
- configuration integrity;

- identified;
- version controlled;

- unauthorized modification;

### 75. AI Disaster Recovery Model Provenance Control

The provenance of recovery models should be sufficiently documented to establish their origin and approved status.

### 76. AI Disaster Recovery Model Integrity Control

Model integrity should be validated before a recovered model is placed into production.

### 77. AI Disaster Recovery Dataset Backup Control

Required datasets should have recovery copies appropriate to their criticality.

- retention;

### 78. AI Disaster Recovery Dataset Integrity Control

Recovered datasets should be validated for integrity before use.

Where relevant, validation should assess whether the dataset may contain:

- corruption;

### 79. AI Disaster Recovery Dataset Recovery-Point Control

Dataset recovery points should be sufficiently identified to establish:

- creation time;

- application configuration;

### 81. AI Disaster Recovery Infrastructure-as-Code Control

### 82. AI Disaster Recovery Secrets Recovery Control

### 85. AI Disaster Recovery Monitoring Recovery Control

### 86. AI Disaster Recovery Security-Control Recovery

Security controls should be restored before normal production operation where their absence would create unacceptable exposure.

Relevant controls may include:

- access control;

### 87. AI Disaster Recovery Backup Integrity Control

Backups should be protected against unauthorized modification and deletion.

Potential mechanisms include:

- immutable storage;
- access restrictions;

### 88. AI Disaster Recovery Backup Isolation Control

Critical backups should be sufficiently isolated from production environments to reduce the risk of common compromise.

### 89. AI Disaster Recovery Backup Testing

Backup recovery should be tested rather than relying solely on successful backup jobs.

Testing should establish whether backups can actually support recovery.

### 90. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Testing

Recovery testing should assess the complete recovery process, including:

- dependencies;

- model;

- configuration;

### 91. AI Disaster Recovery Component Recovery Testing

### 92. AI Disaster Recovery End-to-End Recovery Testing

### 93. AI Disaster Recovery Failover Testing

Failover testing should verify that an alternate environment can provide the required service under defined conditions.

### 94. AI Disaster Recovery Failback Testing

Failback should be tested separately from failover.

- synchronization;
- data integrity;
- configuration;

- final validation.

### 95. AI Disaster Recovery Alternate-Model Testing

Alternate models should be tested for:

- functional compatibility;
- performance;
- security;
- privacy;
- safety;
- behavioral differences.

### 96. AI Disaster Recovery Manual-Fallback Testing

Manual fallback should be tested under realistic workload conditions.

### 97. AI Disaster Recovery Graceful-Degradation Testing

Graceful-degradation mechanisms should be tested to determine whether reduced functionality can be maintained safely and predictably.

### 98. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery-Time Measurement

Actual recovery time should be measured during exercises and real recovery events.

Measured performance should be compared with:

- RTO;
- service criticality;
- recovery capacity.

### 99. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery-Point Measurement

### 100. AI Disaster Recovery Recovery Validation Control

Recovery validation should confirm that the recovered environment satisfies defined requirements before production re-enablement.

### 101. AI Disaster Recovery Functional Validation

### 102. AI Disaster Recovery Security Validation

### 104. AI Disaster Recovery Safety Validation

### 107. AI Disaster Recovery Configuration Validation

### 108. AI Disaster Recovery Identity Validation

### 109. AI Disaster Recovery Monitoring Validation

### 110. AI Disaster Recovery Trusted-State Validation

- security validation;

### 125. AI Disaster Recovery Control Principle

**AI disaster recovery controls should establish repeatable, tested, dependency-aware mechanisms for restoring AI systems and AI-dependent business services while validating model, data, configuration, infrastructure, security, privacy, safety, and operational integrity before normal service is resumed.**

- alternate model ≠ behavioral equivalent;
- alternate provider ≠ independent provider;
- redundancy ≠ independence;
- manual fallback ≠ full service equivalence;
- RTO ≠ actual recovery time;
- RPO ≠ guaranteed recoverable state;
- technical validation ≠ complete assurance;
