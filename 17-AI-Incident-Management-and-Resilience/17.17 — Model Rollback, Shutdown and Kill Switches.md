# 17.17 — AI Incident Rollback, Shutdown and Kill Switches

## Part 1 — AI Incident Rollback, Shutdown and Kill Switch Foundations, Emergency Control, Safe-State Transition and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Rollback, Shutdown and Kill Switches

AI incident rollback, shutdown, and kill-switch capability is the controlled ability to reverse an affected AI deployment, stop an AI system or capability, or immediately terminate defined AI operations when continued operation could create unacceptable risk.

These mechanisms support incident containment and recovery but do not replace investigation, eradication, or root-cause analysis.

### 2. Objective of AI Incident Rollback, Shutdown and Kill Switches

The objectives are to:

- stop harmful or compromised AI behavior;
- limit incident propagation;
- restore a previously trusted state where appropriate;
- prevent unauthorized continued operation;
- provide emergency intervention capability;
- preserve human control over high-impact AI systems.

### 3. AI Incident Rollback

Rollback is the controlled return of an AI system, model, configuration, or deployment to a previously approved state.

Rollback may involve:

- model version;
- application version;
- configuration;
- policy;
- prompt configuration;
- deployment package;
- dependency version.

Rollback does not automatically establish that the previous state is safe or appropriate.

### 4. AI Incident Shutdown

Shutdown is the controlled cessation of an AI system or defined AI capability.

Shutdown may be:

- planned;
- emergency;
- partial;
- full;
- temporary;
- permanent.

The scope should be explicitly defined.

### 5. AI Incident Kill Switch

A kill switch is an emergency mechanism capable of rapidly disabling a defined AI capability or operation.

A kill switch should have a clearly defined:

- activation condition;
- authority;
- scope;
- technical mechanism;
- fallback;
- validation process.

### 6. AI Incident Rollback versus Shutdown

Rollback attempts to return the system to a previous state.

Shutdown stops the system or capability.

```text id="r8m4qx"
INCIDENT
   |
   +--> ROLLBACK
   |       |
   |       v
   |   PREVIOUS STATE
   |
   +--> SHUTDOWN
   |       |
   |       v
   |   NO ACTIVE OPERATION
   |
   +--> KILL SWITCH
           |
           v
      EMERGENCY DISABLEMENT
```

The appropriate mechanism depends on incident characteristics and risk.

### 7. AI Incident Kill Capability

Kill capability is the broader organizational and technical ability to stop an AI system or defined capability when required.

A kill switch may be one implementation mechanism for that capability.

### 8. AI Incident Emergency Shutdown

Emergency shutdown is used when continued operation presents material risk and immediate cessation is considered necessary.

Examples may include:

- active compromise;
- uncontrolled autonomous behavior;
- dangerous tool execution;
- severe data exposure;
- uncontrolled propagation.

### 9. AI Incident Partial Shutdown

Partial shutdown disables only a defined component or capability.

Examples include:

- disabling external tools;
- disabling autonomous actions;
- disabling write access;
- disabling a specific model;
- disabling a high-risk workflow.

Partial shutdown can preserve lower-risk functionality where appropriate.

### 10. AI Incident Full Shutdown

Full shutdown stops the AI service or system.

It may be required where the incident cannot be adequately contained through narrower controls.

Full shutdown should consider business continuity and downstream dependencies.

### 11. AI Incident Functional Shutdown

Functional shutdown disables a specific capability while allowing other functions to continue.

Examples include:

- disabling automated transactions;
- disabling autonomous execution;
- disabling external communications;
- disabling high-impact decisions.

### 12. AI Incident Model Shutdown

Model shutdown removes a model from active service.

Possible mechanisms include:

- traffic removal;
- serving termination;
- model endpoint disablement;
- model registry blocking.

### 13. AI Incident Agent Shutdown

An AI agent may be suspended or terminated when it exhibits:

- unauthorized actions;
- uncontrolled tool use;
- compromised state;
- unsafe behavior;
- excessive privilege use.

### 14. AI Incident Tool Shutdown

Individual tools may be disabled without shutting down the entire AI system.

This can reduce blast radius where the tool provides the path to:

- external actions;
- sensitive data;
- financial transactions;
- administrative operations.

### 15. AI Incident API Shutdown

An API endpoint may be disabled when it represents an active attack or failure path.

API shutdown may be:

- endpoint-specific;
- tenant-specific;
- service-wide.

### 16. AI Incident Autonomous-Action Shutdown

For agentic AI, autonomous action capability may be disabled while retaining conversational or analytical functionality.

This creates a distinction between:

- AI inference;
- AI recommendation;
- AI action.

### 17. AI Incident Human-Control Transition

Where an AI system is shut down or restricted, control may transition to an authorized human operator.

The transition should define:

- decision authority;
- manual procedure;
- required information;
- operating limits.

### 18. AI Incident Safe-State Transition

A safe-state transition moves the AI system into a predefined condition intended to reduce risk.

Examples include:

- read-only operation;
- human approval required;
- no external tool execution;
- no autonomous transactions;
- restricted data access.

### 19. AI Incident Fail-Safe State

A fail-safe state is a condition designed to reduce harmful consequences when normal AI operation cannot safely continue.

The appropriate fail-safe state depends on the system's purpose and risk.

### 20. AI Incident Fail-Secure State

A fail-secure state prioritizes protection against unauthorized access or unsafe security conditions when normal operation cannot continue.

The fail-secure condition may differ from the operationally safest state.

### 21. AI Incident Graceful Degradation

Graceful degradation reduces AI functionality while maintaining lower-risk services.

Examples include:

- lower automation;
- restricted tool access;
- reduced model capability;
- human review;
- manual processing.

### 22. AI Incident Fallback

Fallback provides an alternative method for continuing a business or technical function when the primary AI capability is unavailable.

Fallback may be:

- another model;
- another provider;
- deterministic software;
- manual processing;
- a reduced-function service.

### 23. AI Incident Manual Fallback

Manual fallback transfers a function from AI automation to human execution.

Manual fallback should define:

- authorized personnel;
- procedures;
- capacity;
- data requirements;
- decision authority.

### 24. AI Incident Alternative Model

An alternative model may be used when the primary model is:

- compromised;
- defective;
- unavailable;
- under investigation.

Alternative-model use should not be assumed to provide behavioral equivalence.

### 25. AI Incident Rollback Target

A rollback target is the specific previously approved state to which the AI system may be returned.

The target should have identifiable:

- version;
- configuration;
- provenance;
- approval;
- deployment status.

### 26. AI Incident Trusted Rollback State

A trusted rollback state is a previous state for which sufficient evidence exists to support its use.

A historical state is not automatically a trusted state.

### 27. AI Incident Rollback Eligibility

A rollback candidate should be evaluated for:

- integrity;
- security;
- known vulnerabilities;
- compatibility;
- behavioral performance;
- regulatory or contractual implications.

### 28. AI Incident Rollback Validation

Rollback should be validated before or immediately after restoration, depending on incident urgency and technical architecture.

Validation may include:

- artifact integrity;
- configuration verification;
- security testing;
- behavioral testing;
- dependency validation.

### 29. AI Incident Rollback Failure

Rollback failure may occur when:

- the previous version is unavailable;
- dependencies have changed;
- configuration is incompatible;
- the previous state was itself compromised;
- restoration does not correct the incident.

Rollback failure should trigger reassessment rather than repeated blind rollback.

### 30. AI Incident Rollback versus Eradication

Rollback can remove a recently introduced change, but it does not necessarily eliminate the underlying cause.

For example:

```text id="k6p2vd"
COMPROMISED DEPLOYMENT
        |
        v
ROLLBACK
        |
        v
PREVIOUS VERSION
        |
        v
UNDERLYING CREDENTIAL COMPROMISE?
        |
       YES
        |
        v
ERADICATION STILL REQUIRED
```

### 31. AI Incident Shutdown Trigger

Shutdown triggers should be defined according to the system's risk.

Potential triggers include:

- uncontrolled behavior;
- confirmed compromise;
- inability to contain an incident;
- critical safety condition;
- unacceptable data exposure;
- loss of trusted system integrity.

### 32. AI Incident Kill-Switch Trigger

Kill-switch activation criteria should be explicit enough to support rapid action without requiring unnecessary interpretation during a crisis.

Triggers may be:

- technical;
- security-based;
- safety-based;
- operational;
- manually authorized.

### 33. AI Incident Kill-Switch Authority

The organization should define who can activate the kill switch.

For critical systems, authority should remain available when normal management structures are unavailable.

### 34. AI Incident Kill-Switch Scope

The scope of a kill switch should be clearly defined.

It may affect:

- one model;
- one agent;
- one tenant;
- one application;
- one workflow;
- one business service;
- an entire AI platform.

### 35. AI Incident Kill-Switch Independence

Where justified by risk, the kill mechanism should have sufficient independence from the AI system being controlled.

An AI system should not be the sole authority capable of disabling itself when compromise could affect its own control path.

### 36. AI Incident Kill-Switch Availability

Critical kill capability should remain available during:

- network disruption;
- application failure;
- identity-system failure;
- AI platform compromise;
- supplier outage.

### 37. AI Incident Kill-Switch Authentication

Activation of a kill mechanism should use appropriate authentication and authorization controls while ensuring that emergency access remains practical.

### 38. AI Incident Kill-Switch Authorization

Authorization should distinguish:

- who may activate;
- who may deactivate;
- who may override;
- who reviews activation.

High-impact activation should have appropriate governance.

### 39. AI Incident Kill-Switch Protection

Kill mechanisms should be protected against:

- unauthorized activation;
- unauthorized deactivation;
- tampering;
- suppression;
- configuration manipulation.

### 40. AI Incident Kill-Switch Testing

Kill-switch capability should be periodically tested to determine whether it actually produces the intended system state.

Testing may include:

- technical tests;
- controlled exercises;
- failover tests;
- recovery tests.

Testing should be designed to minimize unnecessary production impact.

### 41. AI Incident Shutdown Validation

After shutdown, the organization should verify that the intended AI capability has actually stopped.

Validation may include:

- endpoint testing;
- process verification;
- traffic monitoring;
- tool-access verification;
- transaction monitoring.

### 42. AI Incident Kill-Switch Validation

Kill-switch validation should establish that:

- activation occurred;
- intended components stopped;
- prohibited actions ceased;
- alternate execution paths were addressed.

### 43. AI Incident Shutdown Bypass

Shutdown controls should account for alternative paths through which an AI system may continue operating.

Examples include:

- duplicate deployments;
- alternate APIs;
- cached services;
- secondary agents;
- parallel providers.

### 44. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Evidence

Material rollback and shutdown actions should generate evidence including:

- activation time;
- actor;
- authorization;
- affected asset;
- target state;
- validation result;
- subsequent actions.

### 45. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Auditability

The organization should retain sufficient records to reconstruct:

- why rollback occurred;
- why shutdown occurred;
- who authorized it;
- what state was restored;
- whether the action was successful.

### 46. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Governance

Rollback and shutdown mechanisms should be integrated with:

- incident response;
- change management;
- business continuity;
- disaster recovery;
- AI governance;
- security governance.

Emergency procedures may require expedited change processes while retaining appropriate evidence and accountability.

### 47. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Recovery Boundary

Rollback or shutdown should define the conditions required before normal AI operation can resume.

These may include:

- containment;
- eradication;
- validation;
- risk assessment;
- recovery authorization.

### 48. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Residual Risk

Residual risk should be assessed after rollback or shutdown.

Important questions include:

- Is the underlying cause removed?
- Is the restored state trusted?
- Are dependencies secure?
- Has the blast radius been addressed?
- Is continued manual fallback required?

### 49. AI Incident Rollback and Shutdown Governance Principle

**AI rollback, shutdown, and kill-switch mechanisms should provide controlled and accountable means of reversing, restricting, or terminating AI operations when continued operation could create unacceptable risk, while preserving evidence and maintaining a defined path toward trusted recovery.**

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Rollback, Shutdown and Kill Switches

**Rollback is not eradication, shutdown is not recovery, and a kill switch is not resilience by itself.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- rollback ≠ eradication;
- rollback ≠ recovery;
- rollback ≠ proof of trust;
- shutdown ≠ eradication;
- shutdown ≠ recovery;
- kill switch ≠ complete incident response;
- kill capability ≠ resilience by itself;
- partial shutdown ≠ full shutdown;
- functional shutdown ≠ complete system shutdown;
- graceful degradation ≠ shutdown;
- fallback ≠ recovery;
- alternative model ≠ behavioral equivalent;
- historical version ≠ trusted rollback state;
- rollback success ≠ root-cause removal;
- shutdown success ≠ incident closure;
- kill-switch activation ≠ proof of compromise;
- kill-switch availability ≠ tested kill capability;
- documentation ≠ demonstrated capability;
- monitoring ≠ assurance;
- internal shutdown threshold ≠ statutory threshold;
- internal kill-switch control ≠ legal requirement;
- contractual requirement ≠ statutory requirement;
- risk acceptance ≠ risk elimination.

Approved rollback targets should be maintained in a controlled registry containing:

Rollback targets should be protected from unauthorized modification.

Integrity controls may include:

- access controls;
- immutable storage.

### 60. AI Incident Rollback Target Provenance

Rollback targets should have sufficient provenance information to establish where the artifact originated and how it entered the approved environment.

### 61. AI Incident Rollback Target Validation

Rollback targets should be validated according to their risk before being relied upon during an incident.

Rollback should assess compatibility with:

- current infrastructure;
- data schemas;
- APIs;
- dependencies;
- security controls;
- configurations.

### 63. AI Incident Rollback Dependency Assessment

Dependencies should be evaluated because rollback of the primary AI artifact may not restore the previous dependency state.

### 64. AI Incident Rollback Configuration Restoration

Where configuration contributed to the incident, rollback should address relevant configuration state rather than reverting only the model or application.

### 65. AI Incident Rollback Database Compatibility

Rollback should consider database or state changes that may have occurred after the target version was deployed.

- memory;
- learned state;
- external transactions;
- stored embeddings;
- configuration changes.

### 67. AI Incident Rollback Data Compatibility

Rollback should assess whether current data remains compatible with the rollback target.

### 68. AI Incident Rollback Behavioral Validation

Validation may include:

- functional testing;
- security testing;
- safety testing;
- performance testing;
- regression testing.

### 69. AI Incident Rollback Security Validation

Security validation should determine whether rollback restores a state with acceptable security characteristics.

### 70. AI Incident Rollback Safety Validation

### 71. AI Incident Rollback Privacy Validation

### 72. AI Incident Rollback Functional Validation

### 73. AI Incident Rollback Regression Validation
