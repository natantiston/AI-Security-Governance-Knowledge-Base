
# 17.15 — AI Incident Escalation

## Part 1 — AI Incident Escalation Foundations, Escalation Concepts, Authority, Thresholds and Governance

### 1. AI Incident Escalation

AI incident escalation is the controlled process of transferring an AI incident to a higher level of authority, expertise, response capability, or governance when defined conditions are met.

Escalation may occur because of:

- increasing severity;
- expanding scope;
- safety implications;
- privacy implications;
- security compromise;
- financial impact;
- regulatory significance;
- inability to contain the incident;
- uncertainty requiring specialized expertise.

### 2. Objective of AI Incident Escalation

The objective is to ensure that incidents receive an appropriate level of:

- authority;
- expertise;
- resources;
- decision-making;
- oversight;
- communication;
- response coordination.

Escalation should occur early enough to reduce avoidable impact while avoiding unnecessary escalation of routine incidents.

### 3. AI Incident Escalation versus AI Incident Response

Incident response describes the activities performed to manage an incident.

Escalation describes when responsibility, authority, expertise, or governance is elevated.

An incident may require response without escalation.

Conversely, an incident may require immediate escalation while technical investigation is still underway.

### 4. AI Incident Escalation Event

An escalation event occurs when a defined condition causes an incident to be transferred or elevated to another:

- team;
- authority;
- management level;
- specialist;
- governance function;
- external stakeholder.

### 5. AI Incident Escalation Trigger

An escalation trigger is a condition that requires or recommends escalation.

Examples include:

- severity threshold exceeded;
- safety impact identified;
- material customer impact;
- active compromise;
- rapid propagation;
- containment failure;
- significant uncertainty.

Escalation triggers should be documented.

### 6. AI Incident Escalation Threshold

An escalation threshold defines the point at which an incident must be elevated.

Thresholds may be based on:

- severity;
- financial impact;
- number of affected users;
- duration;
- criticality;
- safety;
- privacy;
- regulatory relevance;
- operational disruption.

Internal thresholds are governance mechanisms unless an external requirement establishes a mandatory threshold.

### 7. AI Incident Escalation Authority

Escalation authority defines who may initiate or approve escalation.

Potential authorities include:

- incident commander;
- security manager;
- AI system owner;
- risk owner;
- privacy officer;
- compliance function;
- executive management.

### 8. AI Incident Escalation Owner

An escalation owner is responsible for ensuring that an incident reaches the appropriate authority when escalation criteria are met.

The owner should ensure:

- criteria are applied;
- escalation is documented;
- receiving parties are informed;
- decisions are tracked.

### 10. AI Incident Escalation Matrix

An escalation matrix maps incident conditions to required actions.

| Condition | Initial Response | Potential Escalation |
|---|---|---|
| Limited operational impact | Operational team | Incident manager |
| High security impact | Security response | Security leadership |
| Material privacy impact | Privacy response | Privacy leadership / legal |
| Safety concern | Safety response | Executive / safety governance |
| Material financial impact | Fraud / finance | Executive management |
| Major business disruption | Incident response | Crisis management |

The matrix should be tailored to the organization's operating model.

### 12. AI Incident Functional Escalation

Functional escalation transfers an incident to a specialist function.

Examples include:

- cybersecurity;
- privacy;
- legal;
- fraud;
- safety;
- AI governance;
- business continuity.

### 13. AI Incident Hierarchical Escalation

Hierarchical escalation transfers an incident to a higher management level.

Examples include:

- team lead;
- department manager;
- executive;
- crisis-management authority.

### 14. AI Incident Technical Escalation

Technical escalation occurs when specialized expertise is required.

Examples include:

- model security;
- AI infrastructure;
- identity;
- cloud security;
- data engineering;
- forensic investigation.

### 15. AI Incident Business Escalation

Business escalation occurs when an incident materially affects a business service or process.

Potential considerations include:

- service criticality;
- customer impact;
- revenue exposure;
- operational disruption;
- business continuity.

### 16. AI Incident Security Escalation

Security escalation should occur when defined criteria indicate potential or confirmed:

- compromise;
- unauthorized access;
- credential abuse;
- data exfiltration;
- malicious manipulation;
- supply-chain compromise.

### 17. AI Incident Privacy Escalation

Privacy escalation should occur when an incident may materially involve:

- personal data;
- unauthorized disclosure;
- unauthorized access;
- privacy risk;
- affected individuals.

The internal escalation decision should remain separate from any formal legal determination of whether a personal-data breach occurred.

### 18. AI Incident Safety Escalation

Safety escalation should address incidents that may affect:

- physical safety;
- safety-critical systems;
- safety controls;
- dangerous AI outputs;
- safety-related decisions.

Safety escalation may require specialized technical and operational expertise.

### 19. AI Incident Fraud Escalation

Fraud escalation may be required where incidents involve:

- high-value transactions;
- account takeover;
- identity fraud;
- executive impersonation;
- widespread targeting;
- material financial exposure.

### 20. AI Incident Regulatory Escalation

Regulatory escalation involves transferring an incident for legal or compliance assessment where relevant.

Potential triggers include:

- potentially applicable regulatory obligations;
- regulated activities;
- material customer impact;
- significant privacy implications;
- possible reporting requirements.

Regulatory relevance does not automatically establish a regulatory violation.

### 21. AI Incident Legal Escalation

Legal escalation may be appropriate where incidents raise questions involving:

- legal obligations;
- contracts;
- liability;
- evidence preservation;
- regulatory requirements;
- law-enforcement interaction.

### 22. AI Incident Executive Escalation

Executive escalation may be appropriate when an incident exceeds operational response authority or could materially affect:

- customers;
- finances;
- critical operations;
- reputation;
- regulatory relationships;
- strategic objectives.

### 23. AI Incident Crisis Escalation

Crisis escalation occurs when an incident exceeds normal incident-management capability and requires coordinated enterprise-level response.

Potential indicators include:

- widespread disruption;
- multiple simultaneous incidents;
- major safety impact;
- significant financial exposure;
- severe customer impact.

### 24. AI Incident External Escalation

External escalation involves notifying or coordinating with an external organization where appropriate.

Potential parties include:

- suppliers;
- customers;
- regulators;
- law enforcement;
- insurers;
- specialized response providers.

External escalation should follow applicable legal, contractual, and organizational requirements.

### 25. AI Incident Supplier Escalation

Supplier escalation occurs when a third party materially affects incident response.

Examples include:

- cloud provider;
- AI provider;
- model provider;
- payment provider;
- identity provider.

Supplier escalation does not automatically transfer the organization's own responsibilities.

### 26. AI Incident Fourth-Party Escalation

Fourth-party escalation may be necessary where an upstream provider materially affects:

- incident containment;
- evidence;
- service restoration;
- customer impact.

### 27. AI Incident Cross-Functional Escalation

AI incidents frequently cross organizational boundaries.

A single incident may require coordination between:

### 28. AI Incident Escalation by Severity

Severity may determine escalation level.

For example:

- low severity → operational handling;
- medium severity → management visibility;
- high severity → specialist and executive involvement;
- critical severity → crisis-management consideration.

The actual thresholds should be defined by the organization.

### 29. AI Incident Escalation by Impact

Escalation may be triggered by a particular impact dimension even where overall severity has not yet been finalized.

Examples include:

- safety impact;
- privacy exposure;
- financial loss;
- critical service disruption.

### 30. AI Incident Escalation by Scope

Escalation may be triggered when the number of affected:

- systems;
- users;
- transactions;
- business units;
- geographic regions

increases beyond defined thresholds.

### 31. AI Incident Escalation by Blast Radius

Potential propagation can justify escalation even before extensive impact has occurred.

A high blast radius may indicate that:

- containment is uncertain;
- multiple systems may become affected;
- the incident requires additional authority.

### 32. AI Incident Escalation by Duration

Long-running incidents may require escalation where they exceed defined:

- detection-to-response thresholds;
- containment thresholds;
- recovery thresholds.

### 33. AI Incident Escalation by Persistence

Persistent incidents may require enhanced response because they remain capable of producing additional impact.

Examples include:

- active credentials;
- persistent model compromise;
- malicious configurations;
- poisoned datasets.

### 34. AI Incident Escalation by Uncertainty

High uncertainty may justify escalation where:

- the cause is unknown;
- scope is unknown;
- affected populations are uncertain;
- potential impact is material.

Uncertainty does not itself establish high severity.

### 35. AI Incident Escalation by Irreversibility

Potentially irreversible consequences may justify rapid escalation.

Examples include:

- irreversible financial transactions;
- permanent data exposure;
- physical harm;
- irreversible customer decisions.

### 36. AI Incident Escalation by Criticality

Incidents affecting highly critical systems should receive escalation appropriate to their business importance.

Criticality may involve:

- essential business services;
- safety-critical systems;
- financial systems;
- identity infrastructure;
- enterprise AI platforms.

### 37. AI Incident Escalation by Control Failure

Escalation may be triggered when critical controls:

- fail;
- are bypassed;
- are unavailable;
- prove insufficient.

### 38. AI Incident Escalation by Containment Failure

Failure to contain an incident within the defined response period should trigger reassessment and, where appropriate, escalation.

### 39. AI Incident Escalation by Recovery Failure

Escalation may be required when recovery:

- fails;
- exceeds defined objectives;
- produces additional impact;
- cannot restore a trusted state.

### 40. AI Incident Escalation by Recurrence

Repeated materially similar incidents may trigger escalation because recurrence can indicate:

- ineffective remediation;
- systemic control weakness;
- unresolved root cause.

### 41. AI Incident Escalation by Novelty

Novel incidents may require specialist escalation when existing playbooks do not adequately address:

- the attack technique;
- model behavior;
- system interaction;
- impact mechanism.

Novelty alone does not determine severity.

### 42. AI Incident Escalation by Regulatory Significance

An incident may require legal or compliance escalation where facts suggest potential relevance to:

- regulated activity;
- reporting requirements;
- supervisory expectations;
- contractual regulatory commitments.

This is an assessment trigger, not automatically a notification requirement.

### 43. AI Incident Escalation by Customer Impact

Customer-impact thresholds should define when incidents require:

- customer-service leadership;
- executive visibility;
- legal review;
- communications coordination.

### 44. AI Incident Escalation by Financial Impact

Financial escalation criteria may include:

- actual loss;
- attempted loss;
- potential exposure;
- recovery cost;
- material business interruption.

### 45. AI Incident Escalation by Safety Impact

Safety escalation should use defined safety thresholds and should prioritize immediate risk reduction where appropriate.

### 46. AI Incident Escalation by Privacy Impact

Privacy escalation should consider:

- type of information;
- number of affected individuals;
- sensitivity;
- exposure;
- potential consequences.

Internal escalation criteria should not be represented as statutory notification thresholds.

### 47. AI Incident Escalation by Security Impact

Security escalation may be triggered by:

- active compromise;
- privileged access;
- credential compromise;
- persistent access;
- significant data exposure.

### 48. AI Incident Escalation Governance Principle

AI incident escalation should be:

- timely;
- evidence-based;
- proportional;
- clearly authorized;
- traceable;
- coordinated.

Escalation should increase response capability rather than merely increase the number of people informed.

### 49. AI Incident Escalation Evidence

Escalation decisions should retain evidence showing:

- trigger;
- threshold;
- incident status;
- severity;
- impact;
- decision authority;
- escalation time;
- receiving authority.

### 50. Core Principle of AI Incident Escalation Foundations

**AI incident escalation ensures that incidents receive the appropriate level of technical expertise, operational authority, management oversight, and governance attention when defined thresholds relating to severity, impact, uncertainty, criticality, propagation, or response failure are reached.**

The following distinctions are fundamental:

- escalation ≠ incident response;
- escalation ≠ notification;
- escalation ≠ regulatory reporting;
- functional escalation ≠ hierarchical escalation;
- technical escalation ≠ executive escalation;
- supplier escalation ≠ transfer of organizational responsibility;
- severity ≠ escalation by itself;
- impact ≠ severity;
- uncertainty ≠ confirmed severity;
- criticality ≠ incident severity;
- containment failure ≠ automatic regulatory breach;
- regulatory relevance ≠ notification obligation;
- internal escalation threshold ≠ statutory threshold;
- internal governance control ≠ legal requirement.

Internal `AI-IMR-*` identifiers are knowledge-base taxonomy identifiers and do not represent statutory provisions, regulatory article numbers, certification requirements, or legal obligations.
