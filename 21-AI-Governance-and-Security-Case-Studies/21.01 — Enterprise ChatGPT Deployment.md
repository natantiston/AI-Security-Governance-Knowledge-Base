# 21.01 — Enterprise ChatGPT Deployment
## Part 1: Business Context, Enterprise Architecture, and Deployment Model

## 1. Introduction to the Case Study

Enterprise adoption of ChatGPT introduces a combination of business opportunities, information security challenges, data governance requirements, third-party risks, and regulatory considerations. Organizations may deploy ChatGPT to improve employee productivity, accelerate research, support software development, summarize corporate documents, assist customer-facing teams, and improve internal knowledge discovery.

However, deploying an AI assistant across an enterprise is not simply a matter of providing employees with access to a chatbot. The organization must determine what information employees can submit, how enterprise data is protected, which capabilities are enabled, how the service integrates with corporate systems, and who is accountable for the associated risks.

From an AI Security Governance perspective, the central question is:

**How can an organization deploy ChatGPT to improve productivity while maintaining appropriate control over corporate information, access permissions, AI-generated outputs, and operational risk?**

This case study examines a hypothetical multinational enterprise deploying ChatGPT to support employees across business, technology, finance, human resources, legal, customer service, and cybersecurity functions.

The case study focuses on governance and security decisions rather than assuming that a particular product configuration automatically provides sufficient protection.

### 1.1 Case Study Objectives

By examining this scenario, an AI governance or GRC practitioner should be able to:

- Define the business scope and objectives of an enterprise ChatGPT deployment.
- Identify stakeholders and assign governance responsibilities.
- Distinguish consumer AI usage from organization-managed enterprise usage.
- Understand the major components of a typical enterprise AI deployment.
- Identify sensitive information and critical trust boundaries.
- Assess the principal security, privacy, compliance, and operational concerns.
- Establish the foundations for an AI system inventory, risk assessment, and control framework.
- Determine the governance decisions required before broad organizational rollout.

### 1.2 Hypothetical Organization

For this case study, assume the organization has the following characteristics.

| Attribute | Case Study Assumption |
|---|---|
| Organization | Global telecommunications and digital services company |
| Employees | Approximately 25,000 |
| Operating regions | Europe, the Middle East, Asia-Pacific, and North America |
| Industry | Telecommunications, digital services, and enterprise technology |
| Existing IT environment | Microsoft 365, enterprise identity management, cloud platforms, on-premises applications, and enterprise SaaS |
| Information classification | Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted |
| AI deployment objective | Provide employees with a managed AI assistant for approved business activities |
| Initial deployment scope | Selected business units and controlled pilot groups |
| Potential expansion | Wider employee access after risk assessment and pilot evaluation |
| Governance functions | IT, cybersecurity, GRC, privacy, legal, procurement, HR, and business leadership |
| Deployment approach | Organization-managed service with centrally defined policies and access controls |

These assumptions are illustrative. They do not describe an actual company or establish the capabilities of any specific ChatGPT subscription or configuration.

### 1.3 Business Problem

Before the enterprise deployment, employees have started using publicly available AI tools independently.

Examples include:

- Copying lengthy reports into AI assistants to generate executive summaries.
- Asking AI to review contracts and supplier proposals.
- Uploading spreadsheets containing business performance information.
- Using AI to draft customer communications.
- Submitting configuration files and troubleshooting logs for technical analysis.
- Asking AI to explain security alerts or vulnerability reports.
- Using AI to generate scripts, queries, and infrastructure configuration examples.
- Providing internal procedures and project documents to obtain recommendations.

These activities can produce productivity benefits, but unmanaged usage creates governance challenges.

The organization may not know which AI services employees use, what information they disclose, whether the service is approved for the relevant data, which contractual protections apply, or whether the information can be accessed or processed in ways inconsistent with corporate requirements.

This creates a potential **shadow AI problem**: employees adopt AI tools outside the organization's approved procurement, security, privacy, and governance processes.

The organization therefore decides to establish a centrally governed ChatGPT deployment.

### 1.4 Business Objectives and Success Criteria

The organization identifies five primary objectives.

| Objective | Intended Benefit | Example Success Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Improve productivity | Reduce time spent on repetitive knowledge work | Measured reduction in task completion time |
| Improve knowledge work | Support summarization, drafting, analysis, and research | Employee evaluation of output usefulness |
| Improve technical efficiency | Assist with approved troubleshooting and documentation tasks | Reduced time spent preparing technical documentation |
| Strengthen governance | Reduce unmanaged AI usage and establish accountability | Percentage of identified AI use cases registered and assessed |
| Protect information | Reduce inappropriate disclosure and unauthorized access | Number and severity of AI-related data incidents |

These indicators require careful interpretation. Increased usage does not necessarily mean improved productivity, and a low incident count does not prove that information security controls are effective.

The organization should establish baselines, measurement methods, reporting owners, and review periods before evaluating the deployment.

## 2. Defining the Enterprise ChatGPT Use Cases

An enterprise AI deployment should not be treated as one homogeneous business use case. Different activities involve different information types, levels of human oversight, business impacts, and risk profiles.

The organization must define which use cases are permitted, which require additional approval, and which are prohibited.

### 2.1 Initial Use-Case Catalogue

| Use-Case ID | Business Function | Example Activity | Potential Data | Initial Governance Consideration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-UC-001 | Corporate Communications | Draft internal announcements | Internal business information | Validate factual accuracy and disclosure restrictions |
| GPT-UC-002 | Finance | Summarize approved financial reports | Confidential financial information | Restrict access to authorized information and approved workflows |
| GPT-UC-003 | Human Resources | Draft generic job descriptions | Internal HR documentation | Avoid unnecessary employee personal data |
| GPT-UC-004 | Legal | Summarize contract clauses | Confidential contracts | Require legal review and assess confidentiality restrictions |
| GPT-UC-005 | Cybersecurity | Explain security alerts | Logs, indicators, vulnerability details | Remove secrets and assess whether data can be shared with the service |
| GPT-UC-006 | IT Operations | Assist with troubleshooting | System logs and configuration details | Prevent disclosure of credentials, tokens, and sensitive infrastructure details |
| GPT-UC-007 | Software Engineering | Explain code and generate examples | Source code and technical documentation | Assess intellectual property, secrets, licensing, and secure coding requirements |
| GPT-UC-008 | Customer Service | Draft responses for agent review | Customer queries and case information | Protect personal data and retain appropriate human review |
| GPT-UC-009 | Procurement | Summarize supplier proposals | Supplier and commercial information | Respect confidentiality obligations and procurement controls |
| GPT-UC-010 | Enterprise Knowledge | Explain internal policies | Internal knowledge documents | Verify authorization, source quality, and output accuracy |

The table is an initial register, not blanket approval for every activity in each function.

For example, summarizing a publicly available contract template differs materially from analyzing a confidential acquisition agreement. Similarly, explaining a generic security concept differs from submitting production logs containing authentication tokens or customer identifiers.

### 2.2 Use-Case Risk Classification

The organization should assess each use case using defined risk criteria.

Relevant factors include:

1. **Information sensitivity:** What data will be submitted, retrieved, or generated?
2. **Business impact:** Could an incorrect output cause financial loss, service disruption, legal exposure, or reputational damage?
3. **Individual impact:** Could the activity affect employees, customers, applicants, or other individuals?
4. **Decision authority:** Is the AI assisting a person, recommending a decision, or executing an action?
5. **Integration level:** Does the AI service interact with internal applications or external systems?
6. **Autonomy:** Can the system initiate actions without human approval?
7. **Regulatory exposure:** Are specific legal, regulatory, contractual, or sector requirements relevant?
8. **Reversibility:** Can an incorrect output or action be detected and corrected before material harm occurs?
9. **Monitoring capability:** Can the organization detect misuse, unexpected outputs, or policy violations?
10. **Third-party dependency:** What risks arise from the service provider and its supporting infrastructure?

A simple initial classification may use three operational categories.

| Category | Description | Typical Governance Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Limited-impact tasks using public or approved non-sensitive information | Standard acceptable-use requirements and general output verification |
| Moderate | Business activities involving internal information or consequential professional work | Defined use-case approval, additional safeguards, and documented review |
| High | Activities involving sensitive information, material decisions, significant individual impact, privileged system access, or substantial operational consequences | Formal risk assessment, specialist review, explicit approval, enhanced controls, and ongoing monitoring |

This three-level model is illustrative. The organization should establish its own risk methodology and determine whether additional categories are needed.

The classification should also consider applicable legal definitions. An internal operational risk rating does not determine whether a system is legally classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act or another regulatory regime.

### 2.3 Human Oversight Requirements

Human oversight must be proportionate to the consequences of the use case.

Consider three examples:

**Example A — Internal email drafting**

An employee asks ChatGPT to improve the wording of a routine internal announcement. The employee reviews the draft and decides whether to use it.

The human retains responsibility for the final communication.

**Example B — Cybersecurity analysis**

An analyst submits an approved, sanitized alert summary and asks ChatGPT to explain possible causes. The analyst validates the explanation against security telemetry and established incident-response procedures.

The AI provides analytical assistance but does not establish the final incident determination.

**Example C — Customer account action**

An AI workflow proposes suspending a customer's account based on a summarized case. If the system can execute that action, the potential impact is substantially greater than that of a drafting assistant.

The organization must establish appropriate decision authority, validation requirements, authorization controls, and safeguards against erroneous or unauthorized execution.

These examples illustrate an important governance distinction:

**Human review of generated text is not equivalent to human control over an AI system that can execute consequential actions.**

## 3. Enterprise Deployment Models and Security Boundaries

The organization must determine how employees will access ChatGPT and what level of organizational control is required.

Different access arrangements may have materially different identity, administration, data-handling, retention, and security characteristics. The organization must verify the actual features, contractual terms, and configuration of the service it selects.

### 3.1 Consumer Access Versus Organization-Managed Access

A personal account used independently by an employee is not necessarily subject to the organization's identity controls, approved procurement arrangements, administrative policies, or information governance procedures.

An organization-managed deployment can provide a basis for more consistent administration, but it does not automatically make every use case safe or compliant.

| Governance Dimension | Unmanaged Personal Use | Organization-Managed Deployment |
|---|---|---|
| Account administration | May be outside corporate control | Can be centrally administered, depending on the selected arrangement |
| Identity integration | May not use corporate identity controls | May support organization-managed identity and access policies |
| User lifecycle | Corporate joiner/mover/leaver processes may not apply | Can be aligned with enterprise identity processes where supported |
| Data-handling terms | May differ from corporate requirements | Can be evaluated through enterprise procurement and contractual review |
| Usage policies | May be difficult to enforce consistently | Can be supported by organizational policies and technical controls |
| Logging and oversight | Visibility may be limited | Available oversight depends on product capabilities, configuration, and applicable terms |
| Incident response | May be difficult to investigate or contain | Can be integrated into corporate processes where sufficient telemetry and controls exist |
| Compliance evidence | May be incomplete | Can be documented through approved processes, configuration records, and evidence |
| Residual risk | Depends on actual usage and service terms | Remains and must be assessed, treated, and monitored |

The GRC practitioner must avoid treating the phrase *enterprise deployment* as proof that the required controls are present.

The appropriate question is not simply, “Is the company using an enterprise plan?”

It is:

**Which required governance and security outcomes are supported by the selected service, which depend on customer configuration, and which require additional organizational controls?**

### 3.3 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary marks a point where information or control passes between environments with different security assumptions, ownership, or authorization rules.

For enterprise ChatGPT, relevant boundaries include:

1. **Employee-to-device boundary:** The user interacts with a managed or unmanaged endpoint.
2. **Device-to-enterprise-identity boundary:** The organization authenticates the user and evaluates applicable access policies.
3. **Enterprise-to-AI-service boundary:** Information leaves the organization's directly controlled application environment and is submitted to an external service.
4. **User-to-AI-output boundary:** The user receives generated information that may be inaccurate, incomplete, misleading, or inappropriate for the intended task.
5. **AI-output-to-business-process boundary:** Generated content is copied into reports, communications, code, decisions, or other business activities.
6. **Administrative boundary:** Organization-managed configuration and access privileges are exercised by authorized administrators.
7. **Third-party service boundary:** The organization relies on the provider's security, operational resilience, and contractual commitments.

Each boundary should be examined for confidentiality, integrity, availability, authorization, accountability, and failure handling.

The organization must verify the provider's actual data-handling arrangements, including applicable retention, deletion, processing, access, and service-improvement terms. These conditions must not be assumed to be identical across all products, account types, or configurations.

## 4. Initial Governance and Security Decisions

Before deployment, the organization must resolve several decisions that influence the architecture, control design, and acceptable residual risk.

### 4.1 Deployment Decision Register

| Decision ID | Decision | Accountable Function | Required Input | Expected Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DEC-001 | Define approved business use cases | Business sponsor with AI governance oversight | Business requirements and proposed use cases | Approved use-case scope |
| DEC-002 | Select the appropriate service arrangement | IT and procurement | Business needs, service capabilities, commercial terms | Documented product and service decision |
| DEC-003 | Define permitted information categories | Information owners with security and privacy input | Data classification and contractual restrictions | AI data-handling rules |
| DEC-004 | Establish identity and access requirements | IAM and IT security | Identity architecture and access risks | Approved access design |
| DEC-005 | Determine required administrative controls | IT service owner and security | Available product controls and risk assessment | Configuration baseline |
| DEC-006 | Establish privacy and legal requirements | Privacy and legal functions | Data flows, purposes, jurisdictions, and contractual terms | Documented legal and privacy assessment |
| DEC-007 | Define human review requirements | Business process owners | Use-case impact and output risks | Review and approval rules |
| DEC-008 | Define monitoring and incident handling | Security operations and service owner | Available telemetry, incident scenarios, response needs | Monitoring and response procedures |
| DEC-009 | Establish supplier assurance requirements | Procurement, GRC, and vendor risk management | Provider documentation and contract terms | Due diligence record and identified gaps |
| DEC-010 | Approve the pilot and expansion criteria | Executive sponsor or delegated governance authority | Risk assessment, control readiness, success criteria | Documented go/no-go decision |

The accountable function may vary by organization. The purpose of this register is to prevent critical decisions from being left implicit.

### 4.2 Minimum Conditions Before a Pilot

Before authorizing a pilot, the organization should establish at least the following:

- A named business sponsor and service owner.
- A defined pilot population and permitted use cases.
- An approved information-handling policy.
- An initial AI system and use-case inventory entry.
- A documented assessment of material security, privacy, and third-party risks.
- Verified identity and access arrangements appropriate to the deployment.
- A process for reporting and investigating AI-related incidents.
- Guidance on verifying AI-generated information.
- A mechanism for recording control gaps and remediation actions.
- Measurable pilot objectives and explicit expansion criteria.

A pilot should not be considered safe merely because it involves a small number of users. A small pilot can still expose highly sensitive information or introduce consequential risks if its scope and safeguards are poorly defined.

### 4.3 Questions for the GRC Practitioner

During initial discovery, ask the following questions.

**Business and scope**
1. What business problems is the deployment intended to solve?
2. Which departments and employee groups will participate?
3. Which use cases are permitted, restricted, or prohibited?
4. Will the service be used only for drafting and analysis, or also for activities that influence consequential decisions?

**Information security and privacy**
5. What information classifications may be submitted?
6. Are personal data, customer records, confidential contracts, or security-sensitive details in scope?
7. What are the provider's applicable data-processing, retention, deletion, and service-improvement terms?
8. What controls prevent accidental submission of credentials, secrets, or restricted information?

**Architecture and access**
9. How will employees authenticate?
10. How are access privileges assigned, reviewed, and revoked?
11. Which administrative controls are available in the selected service arrangement?
12. What evidence is available for monitoring and investigation?

**Governance and assurance**
13. Who owns the service, each use case, and the associated risks?
14. What assessments and approvals are required before rollout?
15. How will AI-generated outputs be validated?
16. What events trigger reassessment or suspension of a use case?
17. What evidence is needed to demonstrate that the defined controls are operating?
18. What conditions must be satisfied before the pilot can expand?

The answers should be documented and linked to the relevant governance records. Where evidence is unavailable, the organization should record the uncertainty rather than assume that the required control exists.

---

## Core Principle

**An enterprise ChatGPT deployment is a governed business capability, not merely a software subscription.** Its security depends on the relationship between approved use cases, information classification, identity and access controls, provider arrangements, human oversight, evidence, and accountable risk decisions. Establishing these foundations is necessary before the organization can make a defensible decision about the scope and conditions of deployment.

## 5. Threat Modeling for Enterprise ChatGPT

Enterprise ChatGPT introduces security risks that extend beyond conventional SaaS security. Although identity management, endpoint protection, network security, and vendor assurance remain essential, organizations must also consider risks arising from natural-language interactions, untrusted input, generated content, information disclosure, and employee reliance on AI-generated recommendations.

The security assessment must consider the entire enterprise AI workflow rather than evaluating the chatbot interface in isolation.

For example, an employee may submit a confidential document, receive an inaccurate summary, and use that summary in a management decision. Alternatively, an employee may ask the AI to analyze a technical log containing an authentication token. The risk is not limited to the model's response; it also involves the original information, the service's processing arrangements, the employee's actions, and the downstream business process.

A structured threat model helps the organization identify these risks, determine their potential impact, and establish proportionate safeguards.

### 5.1 Threat Modeling Scope

For this case study, the threat model covers the following components:

| Component | Security Consideration | Example Threat |
|---|---|---|
| Employee | User behavior, awareness, and authorization | Employee submits restricted information |
| Endpoint | Device security and session protection | Malware captures information entered into the AI interface |
| Identity provider | Authentication and access control | Compromised account enables unauthorized access |
| ChatGPT service | Provider-operated AI processing environment | Data-handling or service-security risk |
| Administrative configuration | Organization-managed service settings | Incorrect configuration weakens access restrictions |
| Uploaded documents | Content, metadata, and embedded secrets | Sensitive information is submitted unintentionally |
| AI-generated output | Accuracy, confidentiality, and integrity | Incorrect or sensitive content is used without validation |
| Enterprise applications | Downstream business processes | AI-generated instructions are used to perform an unsafe action |
| Monitoring and incident response | Detection and investigation | Insufficient records delay investigation |
| Third-party dependencies | Supplier security and resilience | A material provider incident affects the organization |

The exact technical boundaries and available telemetry depend on the selected service arrangement. The organization must validate these details during architecture review and vendor due diligence.

### 5.2 Threat Modeling Method

A practical approach is to combine conventional threat modeling with AI-specific risk analysis.

The organization should:

1. Identify assets and information requiring protection.
2. Map components, data flows, identities, and trust boundaries.
3. Identify threat actors and plausible misuse scenarios.
4. Determine the vulnerabilities and control weaknesses that make each scenario possible.
5. Evaluate confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, safety, and business impacts.
6. Identify preventive, detective, and corrective controls.
7. Assess residual risk after considering the proposed controls.
8. Assign accountable owners and remediation deadlines.
9. Define monitoring indicators and reassessment triggers.
10. Record the results in the enterprise risk register and control matrix.

Threat modeling should be revisited when the deployment expands, new integrations are enabled, sensitive use cases are approved, or material changes occur in the service or its operating environment.

### 5.4 Distinguishing Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risks

These terms should not be used interchangeably.

- **Threat:** A potential cause of an unwanted event, such as a malicious employee or an attacker using a compromised account.
- **Vulnerability:** A weakness that could be exploited, such as inadequate access restrictions or an ineffective process for detecting exposed secrets.
- **Risk scenario:** A description of how a threat could exploit a vulnerability or control weakness, leading to a business impact.
- **Control:** A safeguard intended to prevent, detect, or reduce the likelihood or impact of the scenario.
- **Residual risk:** The risk remaining after relevant controls are considered.

Consider the following example:

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Asset | Confidential corporate acquisition plan |
| Threat actor | Employee acting outside authorization |
| Vulnerability | No effective policy or technical restriction governing the submission of restricted documents |
| Risk scenario | Employee submits an acquisition plan to an AI service without the required authorization |
| Potential impact | Confidentiality breach, contractual exposure, market-sensitive disclosure, and reputational damage |
| Preventive controls | Data classification, approved-use rules, employee training, and suitable technical safeguards |
| Detective controls | Available usage monitoring, incident reporting, and review of relevant records |
| Corrective controls | Incident containment, impact assessment, required notifications, and remediation |
| Residual risk | Depends on the effectiveness of implemented controls and the actual service arrangements |

A risk statement should identify the event and its consequences. Simply writing "ChatGPT data leakage" does not provide enough detail for a defensible risk assessment.

## 6. Enterprise ChatGPT Risk Register

The risk register translates the threat model into records that can be owned, assessed, treated, and reported through the organization's GRC process.

Each risk should have a unique identifier and a clear connection to the relevant AI system, use case, control, evidence, and remediation action.

### 6.1 Risk Assessment Methodology

For this case study, assume the organization uses a five-point likelihood scale and a five-point impact scale.

**Likelihood**

| Score | Rating | Illustrative Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Unlikely under the assessed conditions |
| 2 | Unlikely | Possible but not expected in normal operation |
| 3 | Possible | Could occur under plausible circumstances |
| 4 | Likely | Expected to occur in some operating conditions |
| 5 | Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

**Impact**

| Score | Rating | Illustrative Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | Minimal impact, readily corrected |
| 2 | Minor | Limited disruption or exposure |
| 3 | Moderate | Material business impact requiring management attention |
| 4 | Major | Significant financial, operational, legal, privacy, or reputational consequences |
| 5 | Severe | Potentially critical business impact, major exposure, or substantial harm |

An illustrative risk score can be calculated as:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

| Score | Illustrative Rating |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low |
| 5–9 | Moderate |
| 10–16 | High |
| 17–25 | Critical |

These thresholds are hypothetical. They should not be treated as a universal risk standard. The organization must define its own scoring criteria, escalation thresholds, and risk acceptance authority.

The numerical score should also be supplemented by professional judgment. Certain privacy, safety, legal, or regulatory concerns may require escalation even when a simple multiplication produces a comparatively low score.

### 6.3 Prioritizing Risks

Risk treatment should consider more than the score.

The GRC practitioner should evaluate:

- Whether the risk affects Restricted or Confidential information.
- Whether personal data or vulnerable individuals are involved.
- Whether the use case could cause material financial or operational consequences.
- Whether the organization has effective preventive controls.
- Whether the risk is subject to mandatory legal or contractual requirements.
- Whether the risk can be detected and contained promptly.
- Whether the risk is accepted by an authorized decision-maker.
- Whether compensating controls are practical and demonstrably effective.

For example, a risk involving exposure of production credentials may require immediate containment even if the organization has not completed the formal scoring process.

Likewise, a risk should not be downgraded solely because the organization has published an acceptable-use policy. The organization must determine whether the policy is understood, enforced where feasible, and supported by appropriate operational controls.

### 6.4 Risk Ownership and Treatment Tracking

Each material risk should include:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Risk ID | Unique risk reference |
| AI System ID | Link to the AI system inventory |
| Use-Case ID | Link to the affected business use case |
| Risk Statement | Describe the event and potential consequence |
| Risk Owner | Person accountable for managing the risk |
| Control Owner | Person responsible for the relevant control |
| Inherent Risk | Risk before considering relevant controls |
| Existing Controls | Safeguards already implemented |
| Control Evidence | Records supporting the control assessment |
| Residual Risk | Risk remaining after considering controls |
| Treatment Decision | Mitigate, avoid, transfer where appropriate, or accept |
| Action Owner | Person responsible for a remediation task |
| Target Date | Agreed completion date |
| Approval Authority | Person or body authorized to approve the decision |
| Review Trigger | Event or condition requiring reassessment |
| Status | Current treatment or remediation status |

The risk owner and control owner may be different people. The risk owner remains accountable for ensuring that the risk is appropriately managed, while the control owner is responsible for the design or operation of a specific safeguard.

Risk acceptance should be documented, time-bounded where appropriate, and approved by an authority with sufficient delegated responsibility. Acceptance does not eliminate legal obligations or remove the need to implement mandatory controls.

## 7. Data Protection and Information Classification

Data protection is one of the most important governance areas in an enterprise ChatGPT deployment because the service may be used to process information originating from multiple business functions.

The organization must establish clear rules for selecting information, submitting prompts, uploading files, reviewing outputs, and retaining records.

### 7.1 Corporate Information Classification

Assume the organization uses four information classifications.

| Classification | Description | Illustrative Examples | Initial AI Handling Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public | Information approved for public disclosure | Published product descriptions, public documentation | Permitted for approved business purposes, subject to accuracy and usage rules |
| Internal | Information intended for authorized organizational use | Internal procedures and non-sensitive operational guidance | Permitted where the use case and service arrangements are approved |
| Confidential | Information whose unauthorized disclosure could cause material harm | Commercial proposals, non-public financial reports, confidential contracts | Requires explicit handling rules and assessment of the selected service and use case |
| Restricted | Highly sensitive information requiring stringent protection | Credentials, authentication secrets, highly sensitive personal data, critical security material | Prohibit submission by default unless a specifically approved workflow and adequate safeguards establish that it is permissible |

This table provides an initial governance model, not a universal rule. The organization must align it with its actual classification policy and applicable obligations.

Information classification alone does not determine whether a dataset may be submitted to ChatGPT. The organization must also consider purpose, legal basis where applicable, contractual restrictions, provider terms, processing location where relevant, access controls, and other risk factors.

For example, a document classified as Internal might still contain personal data that requires additional safeguards. Conversely, a particular Confidential dataset may be permitted in a specifically approved workflow if the relevant requirements and safeguards are satisfied.

### 7.2 Data Categories Requiring Particular Attention

**Personal data**

Examples include customer identifiers, employee records, telephone numbers, email addresses, account details, and information about identifiable individuals.

The organization should determine whether the proposed processing is necessary, lawful, proportionate, transparent where required, and consistent with applicable privacy obligations.

**Authentication secrets**

Examples include passwords, API keys, access tokens, private keys, session cookies, and recovery codes.

These should not be pasted into ordinary AI prompts. If a secret is accidentally exposed, the organization should follow its credential-exposure procedure, which may require revocation, rotation, investigation, and assessment of potential misuse.

**Security-sensitive information**

Examples include detailed network configurations, internal attack paths, exploitable vulnerabilities, incident investigation records, and sensitive security architecture.

The organization should determine whether the information can be safely sanitized and whether the selected workflow is approved for its sensitivity.

**Intellectual property**

Examples include proprietary source code, product designs, confidential research, trade secrets, and unreleased commercial plans.

The organization must consider ownership, confidentiality obligations, intellectual property rights, and the relevant service terms.

**Regulated or contractually protected information**

Examples include information governed by financial-sector rules, healthcare confidentiality requirements, customer agreements, or sector-specific security obligations.

The organization should not assume that a generic AI service arrangement satisfies every applicable requirement.

### 7.3 Data Minimization

Data minimization means using only the information reasonably necessary for the approved task.

Suppose an employee wants ChatGPT to summarize a customer complaint and identify possible service issues.

A poor approach would be to submit the complete customer record, including name, address, account number, payment information, authentication details, and unrelated correspondence.

A better approach would be to prepare a minimized case summary containing only the information necessary for the analysis, after confirming that the intended use is permitted.

For example:

**Unnecessarily detailed input**

> Review the complaint from John Smith, account 48392017, at 15 Example Street. His contact number is 600 123 456, and the account contains payment and identity information. Determine why his service was suspended.

**Minimized illustrative input**

> Review this sanitized case summary: A residential customer reported an unexpected service suspension after a billing adjustment. Identify plausible causes, the evidence needed to distinguish them, and the recommended investigation steps.

The second example removes direct identifiers and unnecessary details. However, removing names alone does not guarantee anonymization. Unique circumstances, combinations of attributes, or retained identifiers may still allow a person to be identified.

The organization should distinguish between:

- **Data minimization:** Reducing information to what is necessary for the task.
- **Pseudonymization:** Replacing direct identifiers while retaining a mechanism that can reconnect records to individuals.
- **Anonymization:** Transforming information so that individuals are no longer identifiable, considering reasonably likely means of identification.

Pseudonymized data may remain personal data under applicable privacy laws. The organization's privacy specialists should determine the correct treatment.

### 7.4 Prompt and File Handling Requirements

The organization should establish operational rules for information submitted to the service.

| Control Area | Required Governance Outcome | Example Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt composition | Users understand what information may be submitted | Acceptable-use guidance and contextual training |
| File uploads | Users understand the risks of uploaded documents | Document classification and pre-upload checks |
| Secret protection | Credentials and tokens are not submitted | Secret-scanning tools where feasible, user guidance, and incident procedures |
| Personal data | Processing is limited to approved purposes | Privacy review, minimization, and approved-use rules |
| Confidential documents | Disclosure is consistent with applicable restrictions | Data-owner approval and service-specific assessment |
| Output handling | Generated content receives appropriate review | Verification requirements based on use-case impact |
| Record retention | Relevant records follow approved retention rules | Defined retention and deletion procedures |
| Incident reporting | Suspected exposure can be reported promptly | Established reporting channel and response process |

Technical safeguards should be validated against the selected service's capabilities. For example, the organization must not assume that every product supports identical data loss prevention integrations, content inspection, administrative logging, or retention controls.

### 7.6 Provider Data-Handling Due Diligence

Before approving the deployment, the organization should obtain and evaluate authoritative documentation addressing the selected service arrangement.

3. What contractual terms govern the use of customer content?
4. Under what circumstances, if any, may customer content be used for service improvement or model development?
5. What retention and deletion arrangements apply to prompts, files, outputs, and associated records?
6. What administrative and user controls are available?

12. What limitations apply to the provider's commitments?

The answers must be based on the applicable contractual documents, current service documentation, and the organization's specific configuration.

A marketing statement or generic product description is not a substitute for a documented vendor risk assessment.

## 8. Enterprise ChatGPT Security Control Framework

After identifying the principal risks and establishing information-handling requirements, the organization must define the controls necessary to manage those risks.

For the hypothetical telecommunications enterprise, the objective is to establish a control framework that connects business requirements, AI-specific threats, existing enterprise security capabilities, and governance obligations.

The framework must answer four questions:

1. What risk or requirement does the control address?
2. Who is responsible for implementing and operating the control?
3. What evidence demonstrates that the control is appropriately designed and implemented?
4. How will the organization determine whether the control continues to operate effectively?

A control should not be considered effective merely because a policy exists or a security product has been purchased. The organization must evaluate the control's design, implementation, operation, and supporting evidence.

### 8.1 Control Categories

Enterprise ChatGPT controls can be organized into eight categories.

| Control Category | Primary Objective | Example Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Governance and policy | Establish accountability and permissible use | AI governance policy, acceptable-use policy, use-case approval |
| Identity and access management | Restrict access to authorized users | SSO, MFA where appropriate, access reviews, account lifecycle management |
| Data protection | Reduce unauthorized disclosure | Data classification, minimization, approved-use restrictions, suitable DLP controls |
| AI output assurance | Reduce reliance on inaccurate or inappropriate outputs | Human review, source verification, use-case-specific validation |
| Service configuration | Maintain an approved service configuration | Configuration baseline, administrative access restrictions, change review |
| Monitoring and incident response | Detect and manage misuse or security incidents | Available usage records, incident procedures, escalation processes |
| Third-party risk management | Evaluate the provider and service dependencies | Due diligence, contractual review, assurance evidence |
| Assurance and continuous improvement | Demonstrate control effectiveness | Control testing, audits, remediation tracking, management reporting |

These categories should be integrated with the organization's existing information security management system, enterprise risk management process, privacy governance, and supplier assurance program.

The organization should avoid creating a separate AI control framework that duplicates existing controls without a clear reason. Existing controls may be reused where their scope and effectiveness adequately address the AI-related risk.

### 8.2 Preventive, Detective, and Corrective Controls

Controls can also be classified according to how they manage risk.

**Preventive controls** seek to stop an unwanted event before it occurs.

Examples include:
- Restricting access to approved AI services.
- Defining permitted information classifications.
- Requiring approval before sensitive use cases are introduced.
- Applying appropriate identity and administrative access controls.
- Prohibiting the submission of credentials and authentication secrets.

**Detective controls** identify unwanted events or control failures.

Examples include:
- Reviewing available service usage records.
- Investigating reports of accidental data disclosure.
- Monitoring relevant identity and endpoint security events.
- Reviewing control exceptions and repeated policy violations.
- Testing whether defined access restrictions are operating as intended.

**Corrective controls** reduce the impact of an event or restore an acceptable security posture.

Examples include:
- Revoking compromised accounts or credentials.
- Suspending an affected use case.
- Investigating suspected information disclosure.
- Correcting inappropriate service configurations.
- Implementing remediation actions following an incident or control failure.

A mature control framework combines all three categories. Preventive controls can fail, detective controls may not identify every event, and corrective controls are necessary to manage the consequences of failures that occur.

### 8.4 Control Design, Implementation, and Operating Effectiveness

These three assessment dimensions should be recorded separately.

| Assessment Dimension | Core Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Design effectiveness | Is the control appropriately designed to address the identified risk? | Does the information-handling policy clearly distinguish permitted and prohibited information? |
| Implementation status | Has the control been implemented in the intended environment? | Has the approved policy been published, communicated, and incorporated into the onboarding process? |
| Operating effectiveness | Does the control operate as intended over the relevant period? | Do samples of use-case approvals demonstrate that the required review was consistently completed? |

Consider a control requiring approval before employees use ChatGPT for a sensitive business process.

The organization might have an approved policy, which supports the design assessment. It might also have a workflow in its governance system, which supports implementation.

However, operating effectiveness requires evidence that actual sensitive use cases were reviewed before deployment, that approvals came from authorized decision-makers, and that material conditions were recorded and followed.

**A documented control is not automatically an implemented control, and an implemented control is not automatically an effective control.**

## 9. Mapping Enterprise ChatGPT to Governance Frameworks

The organization should connect its enterprise ChatGPT control framework to relevant standards, risk management frameworks, and applicable legal obligations.

Framework mapping improves traceability and helps identify where one control can support multiple governance objectives.

However, mapping must not be confused with demonstrating compliance. Each framework or legal requirement has its own scope, applicability conditions, and evidence expectations.

### 9.1 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI management system.

For the hypothetical enterprise, the standard can provide a structure for governing the ChatGPT deployment as part of a wider organizational AI management system.

Relevant governance activities include:

- Establishing AI policies and organizational responsibilities.
- Determining the scope of the AI management system.
- Identifying AI-related risks and opportunities.
- Conducting appropriate AI impact assessments.
- Establishing risk treatment processes.
- Managing documented information and operational controls.
- Evaluating performance and conducting internal audits.
- Reviewing management performance and pursuing continual improvement.

The organization should determine which requirements apply to its management-system scope and identify the evidence needed to demonstrate conformity.

For example, a documented AI risk assessment may support risk management activities, but its existence alone does not demonstrate that the organization's entire AI management system conforms to ISO/IEC 42001.

### 9.2 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organizes AI risk management around four core functions:

- **GOVERN:** Establish organizational policies, accountability, processes, and risk management responsibilities.
- **MAP:** Establish context and identify relevant AI system characteristics, stakeholders, and risks.
- **MEASURE:** Analyze and evaluate AI risks using appropriate assessment methods and evidence.
- **MANAGE:** Prioritize, respond to, monitor, and manage identified risks.

Applied to enterprise ChatGPT:

| NIST AI RMF Function | Example Enterprise Activity | Example Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| GOVERN | Establish ownership, policies, oversight, and risk acceptance authority | Governance charter, policy approvals, RACI |
| MAP | Identify use cases, affected stakeholders, information types, and potential impacts | AI system inventory, use-case assessments, data-flow diagrams |
| MEASURE | Evaluate confidentiality, output reliability, access controls, and operational risks | Risk assessments, control test results, documented evaluations |
| MANAGE | Implement safeguards, prioritize remediation, and monitor residual risks | Risk treatment plans, exception records, dashboard reports |

The organization should consult the relevant framework materials when designing its implementation. The table above is an illustrative mapping, not a complete crosswalk.

### 9.3 EU AI Act Considerations

An enterprise ChatGPT deployment may raise questions under the EU AI Act depending on the actual system arrangement, intended purpose, organizational role, and use case.

The organization should not assume that every internal use of ChatGPT is a high-risk AI use case. Equally, it should not assume that using a general-purpose AI assistant means that every downstream use is exempt from additional requirements.

The assessment should distinguish between:

1. The AI service and the roles of the relevant providers.
2. The organization's role as a deployer or other relevant economic operator, depending on the circumstances.
3. The intended purpose of each use case.
4. Whether the use case falls within a legally defined high-risk category or another regulated category.
5. Any applicable transparency, AI literacy, governance, documentation, or other requirements.
6. The dates on which the relevant provisions become applicable.
7. Any applicable exceptions, transitional arrangements, or sector-specific considerations.

For example, using ChatGPT to draft a routine internal announcement is materially different from integrating AI into a system used for employment selection or another potentially regulated decision-making process.

The legal assessment should be based on the actual facts and current applicable law, not on an internal risk label alone.

### 9.4 GDPR and Personal Data Protection

Where personal data is processed, the organization must assess the applicable requirements under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and other relevant privacy laws.

Key considerations may include:

- Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency.
- Purpose limitation.
- Data minimization.
- Accuracy.
- Storage limitation.
- Integrity and confidentiality.
- Accountability.
- Appropriate controller and processor arrangements.
- International data transfers, where relevant.
- Data subject rights.
- Security of processing.
- Whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment is required.

A Data Protection Impact Assessment is not automatically required for every ChatGPT use case. The organization must determine whether the proposed processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals and assess the applicable legal criteria.

Where the use case involves employee or customer information, the organization should involve its privacy function early enough to influence the design rather than treating privacy review as a final administrative step.

### 10.4 Governance Decision Gates

The organization should establish explicit approval gates.

**Gate 1 — Service selection**

The organization confirms that the proposed service meets its business needs and has undergone appropriate procurement, legal, privacy, security, and third-party risk review.

**Gate 2 — Pilot authorization**

The organization confirms that the pilot scope, permitted use cases, information restrictions, access arrangements, incident procedures, and success criteria are documented.

**Gate 3 — Use-case approval**

A specific business process receives the required assessment and authorization before it handles information or performs activities outside the approved baseline.

**Gate 4 — Expansion authorization**

The organization evaluates pilot outcomes, control effectiveness, user behavior, incidents, unresolved findings, and operational readiness before expanding access.

**Gate 5 — Ongoing reassessment**

Material changes to the service, configuration, use cases, data types, provider arrangements, or applicable requirements trigger review under the defined governance process.

Approval should be based on documented evidence and explicit conditions, not on informal agreement that the service appears useful.

## 11. Control Assurance and Evidence Requirements

Governance and compliance assessments require evidence that supports the organization's conclusions.

The evidence should be relevant to the control objective, sufficiently reliable, current, and traceable to the relevant system or process.

### 11.1 Evidence Categories

| Evidence Category | Example | Assurance Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Governance documentation | Approved AI policy and governance charter | Demonstrates defined expectations and accountability |
| Configuration evidence | Relevant administrative settings and configuration records | Supports assessment of implemented safeguards |
| Identity evidence | Access review results and account lifecycle records | Supports assessment of authorization and access governance |
| Assessment records | AI risk assessment and privacy assessment | Demonstrates structured risk analysis |
| Operational evidence | Completed approvals and documented reviews | Supports assessment of actual process execution |
| Monitoring evidence | Relevant service or identity records where available | Supports detection and investigation capability |
| Testing evidence | Control test plans and results | Supports evaluation of design and operating effectiveness |
| Incident evidence | Incident tickets, investigation findings, and remediation records | Demonstrates incident handling and lessons learned |
| Supplier evidence | Contractual documents and provider assurance materials | Supports third-party risk evaluation |
| Training evidence | Training completion and awareness assessment results | Supports evaluation of employee awareness activities |

Evidence must not be assumed to exist merely because the control framework requires it. The organization should verify availability and identify gaps.

### 11.2 Evidence Quality

The GRC practitioner should evaluate evidence using the following criteria:

- **Relevance:** Does the evidence directly support the control being assessed?
- **Authenticity:** Can its source and integrity be established?
- **Currency:** Does it reflect the current service configuration and operating period?
- **Completeness:** Does it cover the required scope, population, and period?
- **Traceability:** Can it be linked to the control, system, owner, and assessment?
- **Reliability:** Is the evidence sufficiently dependable for the intended assurance conclusion?
- **Access protection:** Is the evidence stored and shared appropriately?
- **Reproducibility:** Can an authorized reviewer understand how the conclusion was reached?

A screenshot of a configuration setting may support an implementation assessment but may not demonstrate that the setting remained correct throughout the period under review.

Similarly, a training attendance record does not establish that employees understand or consistently follow the organization's AI usage requirements.

### 11.3 Control Testing Example

Consider GPT-CTL-002: Approved Usage.

**Control objective:** Ensure that employees use ChatGPT only for approved business purposes and in accordance with the organization's defined restrictions.

**Test procedure:**

1. Obtain the current acceptable-use policy and approved-use-case register.
2. Confirm that the policy identifies relevant permitted, restricted, and prohibited activities.
3. Verify that the policy has an accountable owner and an approved version.
4. Review evidence of employee communication or training.
5. Select a risk-based sample of use-case approvals, where such records exist.
6. Confirm that sampled use cases received the required assessment and authorization.
7. Review available evidence of reported violations or exceptions.
8. Determine whether identified gaps were remediated or formally escalated.
9. Document the test period, sample, evidence reviewed, limitations, and conclusion.

**Illustrative test results:**

| Test Element | Example Result |
|---|---|
| Policy approved | Pass |
| Permitted and prohibited uses documented | Pass |
| Training evidence available | Pass |
| Required use-case approvals completed consistently | Fail |
| Exceptions assigned to accountable owners | Partial |
| Overall conclusion | Control requires remediation |

The results are hypothetical. They demonstrate why the existence of an approved policy does not establish that the underlying process operates effectively.

### 11.4 Findings and Remediation

Control findings should include:

- Finding ID.
- Related control and risk IDs.
- Description of the control gap.
- Supporting evidence.
- Potential impact.
- Severity and prioritization rationale.
- Root cause, where established.
- Corrective action.
- Accountable action owner.
- Target completion date.
- Interim safeguards, where required.
- Validation method.
- Closure approval and supporting evidence.

The organization should not close a finding solely because the action owner states that the work is complete. Closure should follow the defined validation and approval process, proportionate to the finding's significance.

## Core Principle

**Enterprise AI governance requires an integrated system of accountable decisions, implemented controls, validated evidence, and independent assurance where appropriate.** Mapping ChatGPT to governance frameworks is useful only when the organization can demonstrate how the relevant requirements are addressed in practice and how material control failures are identified, escalated, and corrected.
