# 21.01 — Enterprise ChatGPT Deployment
## Part 1: Business Context, Enterprise Architecture, and Deployment Model

## 1. Introduction to the Case Study

Enterprise adoption of ChatGPT introduces a combination of business opportunities, information security challenges, data governance requirements, third-party risks, and regulatory considerations. Organizations may deploy ChatGPT to improve employee productivity, accelerate research, support software development, summarize corporate documents, assist customer-facing teams, and improve internal knowledge discovery.

However, deploying an AI assistant across an enterprise is not simply a matter of providing employees with access to a chatbot. The organization must determine what information employees can submit, how enterprise data is protected, which capabilities are enabled, how the service integrates with corporate systems, and who is accountable for the associated risks.

From an AI Security Governance perspective, the central question is:

**How can an organization deploy ChatGPT to improve productivity while maintaining appropriate control over corporate information, access permissions, AI-generated outputs, and operational risk?**

This case study examines a hypothetical multinational enterprise deploying ChatGPT to support employees across business, technology, finance, human resources, legal, customer service, and cybersecurity functions.

The case study focuses on governance and security decisions rather than assuming that a particular product configuration automatically provides sufficient protection.

### 1.1 Case Study Objectives

By examining this scenario, an AI governance or GRC practitioner should be able to:

- Define the business scope and objectives of an enterprise ChatGPT deployment.
- Identify stakeholders and assign governance responsibilities.
- Distinguish consumer AI usage from organization-managed enterprise usage.
- Understand the major components of a typical enterprise AI deployment.
- Identify sensitive information and critical trust boundaries.
- Assess the principal security, privacy, compliance, and operational concerns.
- Establish the foundations for an AI system inventory, risk assessment, and control framework.
- Determine the governance decisions required before broad organizational rollout.

### 1.2 Hypothetical Organization

For this case study, assume the organization has the following characteristics.

| Attribute | Case Study Assumption |
|---|---|
| Organization | Global telecommunications and digital services company |
| Employees | Approximately 25,000 |
| Operating regions | Europe, the Middle East, Asia-Pacific, and North America |
| Industry | Telecommunications, digital services, and enterprise technology |
| Existing IT environment | Microsoft 365, enterprise identity management, cloud platforms, on-premises applications, and enterprise SaaS |
| Information classification | Public, Internal, Confidential, and Restricted |
| AI deployment objective | Provide employees with a managed AI assistant for approved business activities |
| Initial deployment scope | Selected business units and controlled pilot groups |
| Potential expansion | Wider employee access after risk assessment and pilot evaluation |
| Governance functions | IT, cybersecurity, GRC, privacy, legal, procurement, HR, and business leadership |
| Deployment approach | Organization-managed service with centrally defined policies and access controls |

These assumptions are illustrative. They do not describe an actual company or establish the capabilities of any specific ChatGPT subscription or configuration.

### 1.3 Business Problem

Before the enterprise deployment, employees have started using publicly available AI tools independently.

Examples include:

- Copying lengthy reports into AI assistants to generate executive summaries.
- Asking AI to review contracts and supplier proposals.
- Uploading spreadsheets containing business performance information.
- Using AI to draft customer communications.
- Submitting configuration files and troubleshooting logs for technical analysis.
- Asking AI to explain security alerts or vulnerability reports.
- Using AI to generate scripts, queries, and infrastructure configuration examples.
- Providing internal procedures and project documents to obtain recommendations.

These activities can produce productivity benefits, but unmanaged usage creates governance challenges.

The organization may not know which AI services employees use, what information they disclose, whether the service is approved for the relevant data, which contractual protections apply, or whether the information can be accessed or processed in ways inconsistent with corporate requirements.

This creates a potential **shadow AI problem**: employees adopt AI tools outside the organization's approved procurement, security, privacy, and governance processes.

The organization therefore decides to establish a centrally governed ChatGPT deployment.

### 1.4 Business Objectives and Success Criteria

The organization identifies five primary objectives.

| Objective | Intended Benefit | Example Success Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| Improve productivity | Reduce time spent on repetitive knowledge work | Measured reduction in task completion time |
| Improve knowledge work | Support summarization, drafting, analysis, and research | Employee evaluation of output usefulness |
| Improve technical efficiency | Assist with approved troubleshooting and documentation tasks | Reduced time spent preparing technical documentation |
| Strengthen governance | Reduce unmanaged AI usage and establish accountability | Percentage of identified AI use cases registered and assessed |
| Protect information | Reduce inappropriate disclosure and unauthorized access | Number and severity of AI-related data incidents |

These indicators require careful interpretation. Increased usage does not necessarily mean improved productivity, and a low incident count does not prove that information security controls are effective.

The organization should establish baselines, measurement methods, reporting owners, and review periods before evaluating the deployment.

## 2. Defining the Enterprise ChatGPT Use Cases

An enterprise AI deployment should not be treated as one homogeneous business use case. Different activities involve different information types, levels of human oversight, business impacts, and risk profiles.

The organization must define which use cases are permitted, which require additional approval, and which are prohibited.

### 2.1 Initial Use-Case Catalogue

| Use-Case ID | Business Function | Example Activity | Potential Data | Initial Governance Consideration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-UC-001 | Corporate Communications | Draft internal announcements | Internal business information | Validate factual accuracy and disclosure restrictions |
| GPT-UC-002 | Finance | Summarize approved financial reports | Confidential financial information | Restrict access to authorized information and approved workflows |
| GPT-UC-003 | Human Resources | Draft generic job descriptions | Internal HR documentation | Avoid unnecessary employee personal data |
| GPT-UC-004 | Legal | Summarize contract clauses | Confidential contracts | Require legal review and assess confidentiality restrictions |
| GPT-UC-005 | Cybersecurity | Explain security alerts | Logs, indicators, vulnerability details | Remove secrets and assess whether data can be shared with the service |
| GPT-UC-006 | IT Operations | Assist with troubleshooting | System logs and configuration details | Prevent disclosure of credentials, tokens, and sensitive infrastructure details |
| GPT-UC-007 | Software Engineering | Explain code and generate examples | Source code and technical documentation | Assess intellectual property, secrets, licensing, and secure coding requirements |
| GPT-UC-008 | Customer Service | Draft responses for agent review | Customer queries and case information | Protect personal data and retain appropriate human review |
| GPT-UC-009 | Procurement | Summarize supplier proposals | Supplier and commercial information | Respect confidentiality obligations and procurement controls |
| GPT-UC-010 | Enterprise Knowledge | Explain internal policies | Internal knowledge documents | Verify authorization, source quality, and output accuracy |

The table is an initial register, not blanket approval for every activity in each function.

For example, summarizing a publicly available contract template differs materially from analyzing a confidential acquisition agreement. Similarly, explaining a generic security concept differs from submitting production logs containing authentication tokens or customer identifiers.

### 2.2 Use-Case Risk Classification

The organization should assess each use case using defined risk criteria.

Relevant factors include:

1. **Information sensitivity:** What data will be submitted, retrieved, or generated?
2. **Business impact:** Could an incorrect output cause financial loss, service disruption, legal exposure, or reputational damage?
3. **Individual impact:** Could the activity affect employees, customers, applicants, or other individuals?
4. **Decision authority:** Is the AI assisting a person, recommending a decision, or executing an action?
5. **Integration level:** Does the AI service interact with internal applications or external systems?
6. **Autonomy:** Can the system initiate actions without human approval?
7. **Regulatory exposure:** Are specific legal, regulatory, contractual, or sector requirements relevant?
8. **Reversibility:** Can an incorrect output or action be detected and corrected before material harm occurs?
9. **Monitoring capability:** Can the organization detect misuse, unexpected outputs, or policy violations?
10. **Third-party dependency:** What risks arise from the service provider and its supporting infrastructure?

A simple initial classification may use three operational categories.

| Category | Description | Typical Governance Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Limited-impact tasks using public or approved non-sensitive information | Standard acceptable-use requirements and general output verification |
| Moderate | Business activities involving internal information or consequential professional work | Defined use-case approval, additional safeguards, and documented review |
| High | Activities involving sensitive information, material decisions, significant individual impact, privileged system access, or substantial operational consequences | Formal risk assessment, specialist review, explicit approval, enhanced controls, and ongoing monitoring |

This three-level model is illustrative. The organization should establish its own risk methodology and determine whether additional categories are needed.

The classification should also consider applicable legal definitions. An internal operational risk rating does not determine whether a system is legally classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act or another regulatory regime.

### 2.3 Human Oversight Requirements

Human oversight must be proportionate to the consequences of the use case.

Consider three examples:

**Example A — Internal email drafting**

An employee asks ChatGPT to improve the wording of a routine internal announcement. The employee reviews the draft and decides whether to use it.

The human retains responsibility for the final communication.

**Example B — Cybersecurity analysis**

An analyst submits an approved, sanitized alert summary and asks ChatGPT to explain possible causes. The analyst validates the explanation against security telemetry and established incident-response procedures.

The AI provides analytical assistance but does not establish the final incident determination.

**Example C — Customer account action**

An AI workflow proposes suspending a customer's account based on a summarized case. If the system can execute that action, the potential impact is substantially greater than that of a drafting assistant.

The organization must establish appropriate decision authority, validation requirements, authorization controls, and safeguards against erroneous or unauthorized execution.

These examples illustrate an important governance distinction:

**Human review of generated text is not equivalent to human control over an AI system that can execute consequential actions.**

## 3. Enterprise Deployment Models and Security Boundaries

The organization must determine how employees will access ChatGPT and what level of organizational control is required.

Different access arrangements may have materially different identity, administration, data-handling, retention, and security characteristics. The organization must verify the actual features, contractual terms, and configuration of the service it selects.

### 3.1 Consumer Access Versus Organization-Managed Access

A personal account used independently by an employee is not necessarily subject to the organization's identity controls, approved procurement arrangements, administrative policies, or information governance procedures.

An organization-managed deployment can provide a basis for more consistent administration, but it does not automatically make every use case safe or compliant.

| Governance Dimension | Unmanaged Personal Use | Organization-Managed Deployment |
|---|---|---|
| Account administration | May be outside corporate control | Can be centrally administered, depending on the selected arrangement |
| Identity integration | May not use corporate identity controls | May support organization-managed identity and access policies |
| User lifecycle | Corporate joiner/mover/leaver processes may not apply | Can be aligned with enterprise identity processes where supported |
| Data-handling terms | May differ from corporate requirements | Can be evaluated through enterprise procurement and contractual review |
| Usage policies | May be difficult to enforce consistently | Can be supported by organizational policies and technical controls |
| Logging and oversight | Visibility may be limited | Available oversight depends on product capabilities, configuration, and applicable terms |
| Incident response | May be difficult to investigate or contain | Can be integrated into corporate processes where sufficient telemetry and controls exist |
| Compliance evidence | May be incomplete | Can be documented through approved processes, configuration records, and evidence |
| Residual risk | Depends on actual usage and service terms | Remains and must be assessed, treated, and monitored |

The GRC practitioner must avoid treating the phrase *enterprise deployment* as proof that the required controls are present.

The appropriate question is not simply, “Is the company using an enterprise plan?”

It is:

**Which required governance and security outcomes are supported by the selected service, which depend on customer configuration, and which require additional organizational controls?**

The architecture shows several distinct control areas.

The diagram does not assume that the enterprise can inspect every internal model-processing operation or independently control the provider's infrastructure. ### 3.3 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary marks a point where information or control passes between environments with different security assumptions, ownership, or authorization rules.

For enterprise ChatGPT, relevant boundaries include:

1. **Employee-to-device boundary:** The user interacts with a managed or unmanaged endpoint.
2. **Device-to-enterprise-identity boundary:** The organization authenticates the user and evaluates applicable access policies.
3. **Enterprise-to-AI-service boundary:** Information leaves the organization's directly controlled application environment and is submitted to an external service.
4. **User-to-AI-output boundary:** The user receives generated information that may be inaccurate, incomplete, misleading, or inappropriate for the intended task.
5. **AI-output-to-business-process boundary:** Generated content is copied into reports, communications, code, decisions, or other business activities.
6. **Administrative boundary:** Organization-managed configuration and access privileges are exercised by authorized administrators.
7. **Third-party service boundary:** The organization relies on the provider's security, operational resilience, and contractual commitments.

Each boundary should be examined for confidentiality, integrity, availability, authorization, accountability, and failure handling.

The organization should determine what information is processed at each stage and which controls apply.

Potentially relevant data includes:

- Prompt text and uploaded documents.
- Personal data and customer information.
- Corporate reports and commercial information.
- Source code, configuration files, and technical logs.
- Generated responses that reproduce or infer sensitive information.
- Administrative records and usage telemetry.
- Records required for investigation, audit, or regulatory purposes.

The organization must verify the provider's actual data-handling arrangements, including applicable retention, deletion, processing, access, and service-improvement terms. These conditions must not be assumed to be identical across all products, account types, or configurations.

## 4. Initial Governance and Security Decisions

Before deployment, the organization must resolve several decisions that influence the architecture, control design, and acceptable residual risk.

### 4.1 Deployment Decision Register

| Decision ID | Decision | Accountable Function | Required Input | Expected Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DEC-001 | Define approved business use cases | Business sponsor with AI governance oversight | Business requirements and proposed use cases | Approved use-case scope |
| DEC-002 | Select the appropriate service arrangement | IT and procurement | Business needs, service capabilities, commercial terms | Documented product and service decision |
| DEC-003 | Define permitted information categories | Information owners with security and privacy input | Data classification and contractual restrictions | AI data-handling rules |
| DEC-004 | Establish identity and access requirements | IAM and IT security | Identity architecture and access risks | Approved access design |
| DEC-005 | Determine required administrative controls | IT service owner and security | Available product controls and risk assessment | Configuration baseline |
| DEC-006 | Establish privacy and legal requirements | Privacy and legal functions | Data flows, purposes, jurisdictions, and contractual terms | Documented legal and privacy assessment |
| DEC-007 | Define human review requirements | Business process owners | Use-case impact and output risks | Review and approval rules |
| DEC-008 | Define monitoring and incident handling | Security operations and service owner | Available telemetry, incident scenarios, response needs | Monitoring and response procedures |
| DEC-009 | Establish supplier assurance requirements | Procurement, GRC, and vendor risk management | Provider documentation and contract terms | Due diligence record and identified gaps |
| DEC-010 | Approve the pilot and expansion criteria | Executive sponsor or delegated governance authority | Risk assessment, control readiness, success criteria | Documented go/no-go decision |

The accountable function may vary by organization. The purpose of this register is to prevent critical decisions from being left implicit.

### 4.2 Minimum Conditions Before a Pilot

Before authorizing a pilot, the organization should establish at least the following:

- A named business sponsor and service owner.
- A defined pilot population and permitted use cases.
- An approved information-handling policy.
- An initial AI system and use-case inventory entry.
- A documented assessment of material security, privacy, and third-party risks.
- Verified identity and access arrangements appropriate to the deployment.
- A process for reporting and investigating AI-related incidents.
- Guidance on verifying AI-generated information.
- A mechanism for recording control gaps and remediation actions.
- Measurable pilot objectives and explicit expansion criteria.

A pilot should not be considered safe merely because it involves a small number of users. A small pilot can still expose highly sensitive information or introduce consequential risks if its scope and safeguards are poorly defined.

### 4.3 Questions for the GRC Practitioner

During initial discovery, ask the following questions.

**Business and scope**
1. What business problems is the deployment intended to solve?
2. Which departments and employee groups will participate?
3. Which use cases are permitted, restricted, or prohibited?
4. Will the service be used only for drafting and analysis, or also for activities that influence consequential decisions?

**Information security and privacy**
5. What information classifications may be submitted?
6. Are personal data, customer records, confidential contracts, or security-sensitive details in scope?
7. What are the provider's applicable data-processing, retention, deletion, and service-improvement terms?
8. What controls prevent accidental submission of credentials, secrets, or restricted information?

**Architecture and access**
9. How will employees authenticate?
10. How are access privileges assigned, reviewed, and revoked?
11. Which administrative controls are available in the selected service arrangement?
12. What evidence is available for monitoring and investigation?

**Governance and assurance**
13. Who owns the service, each use case, and the associated risks?
14. What assessments and approvals are required before rollout?
15. How will AI-generated outputs be validated?
16. What events trigger reassessment or suspension of a use case?
17. What evidence is needed to demonstrate that the defined controls are operating?
18. What conditions must be satisfied before the pilot can expand?

The answers should be documented and linked to the relevant governance records. Where evidence is unavailable, the organization should record the uncertainty rather than assume that the required control exists.

---

## Core Principle

**An enterprise ChatGPT deployment is a governed business capability, not merely a software subscription.** Its security depends on the relationship between approved use cases, information classification, identity and access controls, provider arrangements, human oversight, evidence, and accountable risk decisions. Establishing these foundations is necessary before the organization can make a defensible decision about the scope and conditions of deployment.
