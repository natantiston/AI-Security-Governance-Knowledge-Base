# 20.01 — AI Governance Policy Template

## Part 1 — Foundations of the AI Governance Policy

### 1. Introduction to the AI Governance Policy

An **AI Governance Policy** is a formal organizational document that establishes the principles, responsibilities, decision-making authority, oversight mechanisms, and minimum requirements governing the acquisition, development, deployment, operation, monitoring, and retirement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems.

The policy defines how an organization ensures that AI is used responsibly, securely, lawfully, transparently, and consistently with its business objectives, risk appetite, and ethical commitments.

Unlike a conventional IT policy that primarily addresses technology infrastructure, access management, or operational security, an AI Governance Policy must address the broader implications of systems that can generate content, make predictions, recommend decisions, classify individuals, automate workflows, or take actions through connected tools.

AI introduces governance challenges that extend beyond traditional technology management, including:

- Probabilistic outputs and unpredictable behavior.
- Bias, discrimination, and potentially unfair outcomes.
- Data protection, privacy, and confidentiality risks.
- Model manipulation, adversarial attacks, and prompt injection.
- Inaccurate, fabricated, or misleading AI-generated information.
- Insufficient transparency and explainability.
- Excessive automation and inappropriate delegation of decisions.
- Third-party AI provider dependencies.
- Intellectual property and data ownership concerns.
- Regulatory obligations associated with particular AI use cases.
- Changes in model behavior, capabilities, and risks over time.
- Accountability for decisions influenced or executed by AI systems.

An effective AI Governance Policy provides the organizational framework for managing these challenges throughout the AI lifecycle.

It does not attempt to describe every technical implementation detail. Instead, it establishes mandatory governance requirements and assigns responsibility for implementing them through supporting standards, procedures, risk assessments, technical controls, and operational processes.

For example, an AI Governance Policy may require every proposed AI use case to undergo a documented risk assessment before production deployment. A supporting AI Risk Management Standard can then define the assessment methodology, scoring criteria, approval thresholds, and required evidence.

This separation makes the policy easier to maintain while allowing technical and operational requirements to evolve.

### 2. Purpose and Objectives of an AI Governance Policy

The purpose of an AI Governance Policy is to establish a consistent, accountable, and risk-based approach to the organization's use of AI.

The policy should enable the organization to obtain business value from AI while maintaining appropriate control over its associated risks.

The principal objectives are summarized below.

| Objective | Description | Expected Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Establish accountability | Assign ownership for AI systems, risks, approvals, and oversight. | Clear responsibility for AI-related decisions. |
| Define acceptable AI use | Establish permitted, restricted, and prohibited uses. | Reduced likelihood of unauthorized or inappropriate AI adoption. |
| Manage AI risks | Require risk identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring. | Risks are addressed according to their severity and business impact. |
| Protect information | Establish requirements for confidentiality, privacy, integrity, and availability. | Reduced exposure of sensitive information. |
| Establish security requirements | Require appropriate AI security controls throughout the lifecycle. | Improved resilience against AI-specific and conventional cyber threats. |
| Support legal and regulatory compliance | Require identification and assessment of applicable obligations. | More systematic compliance management. |
| Promote trustworthy AI | Establish expectations for fairness, transparency, reliability, and human oversight where appropriate. | AI systems are used in a manner consistent with organizational commitments. |
| Control third-party AI | Govern AI products, services, models, and external providers. | Better visibility into supplier dependencies and associated risks. |
| Establish lifecycle oversight | Govern AI from initial proposal through retirement. | Risks are managed beyond initial approval. |
| Enable assurance | Require documentation, evidence, monitoring, and independent review where appropriate. | Greater auditability and demonstrable control effectiveness. |
| Support business alignment | Ensure AI initiatives support approved business objectives and risk appetite. | AI investment is aligned with organizational priorities. |
| Encourage controlled innovation | Provide a defined route for evaluating and approving new AI applications. | Innovation can proceed without bypassing governance requirements. |

These objectives should be translated into enforceable policy statements.

For example, the objective of accountability becomes a requirement to assign an accountable business owner to every registered AI system. The objective of lifecycle oversight becomes a requirement to reassess an AI system when significant changes occur.

A policy objective without an associated requirement, responsible owner, or assurance mechanism may remain an aspirational statement rather than an effective governance control.

### 3. Scope of the AI Governance Policy

The policy must clearly define which organizational activities, entities, technologies, individuals, and third parties fall within its scope.

An ambiguous scope can create governance gaps. For example, an organization may govern internally developed machine-learning models while overlooking AI features embedded in existing SaaS applications.

The scope should therefore be based on AI functionality and organizational use, rather than solely on whether the organization develops its own models.

#### 3.1 Organizational Scope

The policy may apply to:

- The parent organization and its controlled subsidiaries.
- Business units and operational departments.
- IT, cybersecurity, data, engineering, and digital transformation teams.
- Human Resources, Finance, Legal, Procurement, Marketing, and Customer Service.
- Employees, contractors, temporary workers, and other authorized personnel.
- External service providers operating AI systems on the organization's behalf.
- Suppliers and partners where contractual arrangements require compliance with organizational AI governance requirements.

The precise organizational boundary must reflect the organization's legal structure, operating model, and contractual obligations.

For a multinational organization, the policy may establish a global minimum standard supplemented by country-specific requirements.

Local procedures must not weaken mandatory global controls unless a formally approved exception or legally required variation permits it.

#### 3.2 Technology Scope

The policy should cover AI technologies used or acquired by the organization, including:

| AI Technology | Examples | Governance Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Generative AI | Large language models, image generation, audio generation. | Data leakage, hallucinations, intellectual property, prompt injection. |
| Predictive AI | Demand forecasting, customer churn prediction, fraud prediction. | Model accuracy, data quality, bias, operational impact. |
| Classification systems | Spam detection, document classification, transaction classification. | Misclassification, false positives, false negatives. |
| Recommendation systems | Product recommendations, content recommendations, next-best-action engines. | Manipulation, profiling, fairness, unintended influence. |
| Computer vision | Facial analysis, object detection, visual inspection. | Privacy, accuracy, surveillance, environmental limitations. |
| Speech and language AI | Speech recognition, transcription, translation, voice assistants. | Sensitive information exposure, transcription errors, impersonation. |
| AI-enabled SaaS | AI features embedded in CRM, ERP, HR, and collaboration platforms. | Supplier controls, data handling, feature configuration. |
| Retrieval-augmented generation | Enterprise assistants using internal documents and knowledge repositories. | Access-control inheritance, retrieval security, source integrity. |
| AI agents | Systems that invoke tools, access APIs, or execute workflows. | Excessive privileges, unauthorized actions, transaction integrity. |
| Autonomous or semi-autonomous AI | Systems that perform tasks with limited human intervention. | Delegated authority, operational safety, escalation, reversibility. |

The organization should define AI according to an approved internal classification and any applicable legal definitions.

A system does not necessarily fall outside the policy simply because a supplier describes it as an intelligent assistant, automation feature, predictive service, or AI-powered application rather than explicitly labeling it an AI system.

For example, a prototype using synthetic data may require fewer operational controls than a production AI system processing customer financial information. However, experimentation must not be treated as automatically exempt from confidentiality, privacy, security, or other applicable requirements.

The organization should establish risk-based criteria to determine which controls apply at each stage.

#### 3.4 AI Use-Case Scope

The policy should govern both internal and external applications of AI.

Examples include:

- Employees using AI assistants to draft reports or summarize documents.
- Customer-service chatbots interacting with customers.
- AI systems analyzing customer behavior.
- Recruitment systems screening applicants.
- Fraud detection models processing financial transactions.
- AI systems supporting cybersecurity operations.
- AI assistants accessing internal policies and confidential knowledge repositories.
- AI agents creating tickets, updating customer records, or initiating transactions.
- AI-enabled contact centers processing customer conversations.
- AI systems supporting decisions about individuals, employees, or customers.

The level of oversight should be proportionate to the use case, the information processed, the system's capabilities, the potential consequences of errors, and applicable legal requirements.

#### 3.5 Shadow AI and Unapproved AI

**Shadow AI** refers to the use of AI tools or services without appropriate organizational authorization, visibility, or governance.

Examples include an employee uploading a confidential customer database to a personal AI account, a department subscribing to an unapproved AI service, or a developer integrating an external model API without security review.

The policy should establish that:

1. Employees may use only AI services authorized for the relevant purpose and data classification.
2. AI applications and integrations must be registered through the organization's designated governance process.
3. Confidential or restricted information must not be submitted to unauthorized AI services.
4. Business units must disclose AI use cases that fall within the policy's scope.
5. Security and governance teams may monitor authorized environments in accordance with applicable law and internal privacy requirements.
6. Suspected unauthorized AI use must be reported through established channels.
7. Identified violations must be handled through proportionate remediation and the organization's applicable disciplinary or incident-management processes.

The organization should provide an accessible approval route for legitimate business needs. If the approval process is excessively slow or unclear, employees may be more likely to adopt unapproved tools.

### 4. Relationship Between AI Governance and Other Governance Domains

AI governance is not a replacement for established corporate governance, information security, privacy, enterprise risk management, or technology governance.

It connects these disciplines and addresses risks that arise from AI-specific capabilities and use cases.

The AI Governance Policy should define how it interacts with existing organizational policies, standards, and management systems.

| Governance Domain | Primary Focus | Relationship to AI Governance |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate governance | Organizational accountability, strategy, and oversight. | Establishes executive accountability and oversight for material AI risks. |
| Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) | Identification and management of enterprise risks. | Integrates material AI risks into the enterprise risk framework. |
| Information Security | Protection of information and technology assets. | Addresses AI security, access control, threats, vulnerabilities, and incidents. |
| Data Governance | Data ownership, quality, classification, lineage, and permitted use. | Establishes requirements for data used to train, ground, evaluate, or operate AI systems. |
| Privacy Governance | Lawful and appropriate processing of personal data. | Addresses AI-related personal data processing, profiling, transparency, and individual rights. |
| Legal and Regulatory Compliance | Applicable laws, regulations, and contractual obligations. | Determines which AI-related obligations apply to each system and use case. |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Supplier due diligence and ongoing oversight. | Assesses AI vendors, model providers, cloud platforms, and subcontractors. |
| Technology Governance | Architecture, technology selection, change management, and operations. | Integrates AI approval and lifecycle controls into established technology processes. |
| Model Risk Management | Model design, validation, performance, and limitations. | Provides specialized oversight for AI systems whose model behavior creates material risk. |
| Business Continuity and Resilience | Continuity of critical business services. | Addresses AI service outages, model unavailability, provider dependencies, and fallback arrangements. |
| Internal Audit | Independent assurance over governance and controls. | Evaluates whether AI governance is appropriately designed and operating effectively. |

#### 4.2 Avoiding Policy Conflicts

AI governance must not create contradictory requirements.

For example, an AI application may be approved under a business unit's technology governance process but remain prohibited under the organization's data classification rules.

Similarly, an AI vendor may satisfy general procurement requirements but fail an AI-specific security assessment.

The organization should establish a process for resolving conflicts between policies and determining which requirements take precedence.

As a general governance approach:

- Applicable laws and binding regulatory requirements must be respected.
- Contractual obligations must be assessed and fulfilled where applicable.
- Corporate policies and mandatory internal standards must be followed.
- Exceptions must follow a documented approval process and must not authorize unlawful activity.
- Conflicts must be escalated to the appropriate Legal, Compliance, Risk, or executive authority.

The AI Governance Policy should not independently declare that it overrides all other corporate policies. Its precedence must be consistent with the organization's established governance framework.

### 5. Foundational AI Governance Principles

The policy should establish principles that guide decisions across different AI technologies and business applications.

Principles are not substitutes for controls. They express the outcomes and behaviors the organization expects, while enforceable policy requirements specify what must be done.

#### 5.1 Accountability and Responsibility

Every AI system must have clearly assigned accountability appropriate to its business purpose and risk.

The organization should identify:

- The accountable business owner.
- The technical or service owner.
- The data owner, where relevant.
- The individual or function responsible for AI risk management.
- The security and privacy stakeholders.
- The authority responsible for approving deployment.
- The party responsible for ongoing monitoring and reassessment.

Accountability must not disappear merely because the AI capability is supplied by a third party.

A vendor may operate the model, host the service, or provide technical support, but the organization remains responsible for the decisions and obligations that applicable law and its own governance arrangements assign to it.

**Example:** A telecommunications company deploys an AI assistant to support customer-service agents. The supplier operates the underlying model, but the telecommunications company must still govern the use of customer information, agent permissions, response quality, escalation rules, and the business consequences of incorrect recommendations.

#### 5.2 Risk-Based and Proportionate Governance

AI systems should be governed according to their risks rather than through a single uniform approval process.

Relevant factors include:

- The sensitivity and volume of information processed.
- The number and type of people affected.
- The potential for financial, operational, legal, or reputational harm.
- The degree of autonomy.
- The system's access to enterprise resources.
- The consequences of inaccurate outputs.
- The ability to reverse or correct system actions.
- The applicable regulatory classification.
- The availability of human review and effective fallback mechanisms.

A low-impact AI tool used to summarize publicly available information may require a streamlined assessment.

An AI system used to support credit decisions, employment decisions, medical care, or critical infrastructure may require substantially more extensive review.

Risk-based governance does not mean that high-impact systems can ignore baseline security requirements. It means that additional controls and approval requirements are applied where the risk warrants them.

#### 5.3 Transparency and Explainability

The organization should establish appropriate transparency about how AI systems are used and how their outputs influence decisions.

Transparency requirements depend on the application and the audience.

Potential measures include:

- Informing users when they interact with an AI system where appropriate or legally required.
- Documenting the system's intended purpose and limitations.
- Identifying relevant data sources.
- Recording material AI-assisted decisions.
- Explaining significant factors influencing a decision where feasible and appropriate.
- Providing a route for challenging or reviewing consequential outcomes.
- Maintaining sufficient documentation for audit and investigation.

Transparency does not necessarily require disclosing proprietary model weights, confidential algorithms, or every internal computational operation.

The objective is to provide the information necessary for appropriate oversight, accountability, and informed use.

#### 5.4 Fairness and Non-Discrimination

AI systems should be evaluated for unjustified bias and discriminatory outcomes where relevant to their purpose and impact.

Potential sources of unfairness include:

- Unrepresentative training or evaluation data.
- Historical discrimination reflected in source data.
- Proxy variables that indirectly encode protected characteristics.
- Different error rates across relevant groups.
- Inappropriate deployment in a context different from the one for which the model was evaluated.
- Feedback loops that reinforce existing inequalities.

The organization should define appropriate fairness assessment methods based on the use case, affected populations, legal requirements, and available evidence.

Fairness is context-dependent. A metric that is suitable for one application may be inappropriate for another.

#### 5.5 Human Oversight

AI systems must not be granted decision-making authority beyond the level approved for their intended purpose and risk.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely formal.

Depending on the use case, meaningful oversight may require that authorized personnel can:

- Understand the system's intended purpose and material limitations.
- Review relevant outputs and supporting information.
- Challenge or reject recommendations.
- Prevent or reverse consequential actions where feasible.
- Escalate uncertain or high-impact cases.
- Suspend the system when predefined conditions are met.

A nominal human approval step is insufficient if the reviewer lacks the information, authority, competence, or time necessary to exercise independent judgment.

The policy should specify when human review is mandatory, particularly for consequential decisions or actions that cannot easily be reversed.

#### 5.6 Security and Resilience

AI systems must be protected against threats affecting their data, models, interfaces, infrastructure, and operational behavior.

Security requirements may include:

- Strong authentication and authorization.
- Least-privilege access.
- Secure configuration and change management.
- Protection of model and application interfaces.
- Input validation and output handling.
- Protection against prompt injection and malicious instructions.
- Data loss prevention and information classification controls.
- Monitoring for suspicious use and abnormal behavior.
- Vulnerability assessment and remediation.
- Secure integration with APIs and enterprise systems.
- Backup, recovery, and fallback arrangements.
- Incident detection, response, and lessons learned.

The organization should address both conventional cyber threats and AI-specific attack paths.

For example, an AI agent that can create customer accounts, issue refunds, or update billing records requires controls over the actions it can perform, not merely controls over the language model generating its recommendations.

#### 5.7 Privacy and Data Protection

AI systems must process personal data in accordance with applicable data protection requirements and organizational privacy rules.

The organization should assess:

- The purpose and lawful basis of personal data processing, where applicable.
- Data minimization and retention.
- The use of sensitive or special-category data.
- Data-subject transparency and rights.
- Profiling and automated decision-making implications.
- Data transfers and supplier processing arrangements.
- The potential for personal data to be reproduced or inferred through model outputs.
- Whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment or another formal assessment is required.

Privacy risks may arise during training, fine-tuning, retrieval, inference, logging, monitoring, and downstream use of AI-generated information.

The organization should not assume that information becomes anonymous simply because it is processed by an AI model or summarized into a different format.

#### 5.8 Reliability and Accuracy

AI systems must be suitable for their approved purpose and subject to proportionate testing and monitoring.

Governance should address:

- Accuracy and performance.
- Hallucinations and unsupported statements.
- Robustness under unusual or adversarial inputs.
- Model limitations and known failure modes.
- Performance drift.
- Data quality.
- Reproducibility where necessary.
- Human verification of high-impact outputs.
- Safe fallback behavior.

A system that performs well during a limited demonstration may fail under production conditions because of different data, user behavior, workload, integrations, or environmental conditions.

Approval should therefore consider operational suitability rather than relying solely on vendor claims or demonstration results.

#### 5.9 Accountability Across the AI Lifecycle

AI governance must remain effective after initial deployment.

Material changes may include:

- Introducing a new model or model version.
- Changing the intended purpose.
- Connecting the system to additional data sources.
- Expanding the user population.
- Enabling new tools or permissions.
- Increasing the level of automation.
- Changing the hosting environment.
- Modifying prompts, retrieval logic, or business rules in ways that affect risk.
- Introducing a new supplier or subcontractor.

Such changes may require reassessment, retesting, reapproval, or updated documentation.

The level of required review should be determined by the significance of the change and its potential effect on risk.

### 6. Defining the AI Governance Policy's Mandatory Language

A policy must distinguish enforceable obligations from recommendations and aspirational statements.

Inconsistent language can make it difficult to determine whether a requirement is mandatory, conditional, or advisory.

The organization should define its use of policy terms explicitly.

| Term | Intended Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Must | Mandatory requirement. | Every in-scope AI system must have an accountable business owner. |
| Must not | Prohibition. | Confidential information must not be submitted to unauthorized AI services. |
| Shall | Mandatory requirement, if adopted by the organization as equivalent to “must.” | AI risk assessments shall be documented. |
| Should | Recommended practice that may permit justified deviation. | High-risk outputs should receive independent review where appropriate. |
| May | Permitted action or available option. | The AI Governance Committee may request an independent assessment. |
| Where applicable | A requirement that applies when specified conditions are met. | A privacy impact assessment must be completed where applicable. |
| Unless formally approved | A requirement that permits a defined exception process. | A control may be varied only where a formal exception is approved. |

The organization should avoid using terms such as “where possible,” “as needed,” or “when appropriate” without defining who determines applicability and what evidence supports the decision.

For example:

**Weak statement:**

“AI systems should be assessed for security risks when appropriate.”

This statement does not establish a clear mandatory requirement, decision authority, or evidence expectation.

**Stronger statement:**

“All in-scope AI systems must undergo a documented security risk assessment before production deployment. The assessment depth must be proportionate to the system's risk classification. Any exception must be documented and approved by the designated risk authority before deployment.”

The stronger statement identifies the scope, required activity, timing, proportionality principle, and exception mechanism.

#### 6.1 Policy Requirement Quality Test

Each major policy requirement should be assessed against the following questions:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Clarity | Can employees and control owners understand what is required? |
| Scope | Is it clear which systems, activities, or personnel are covered? |
| Accountability | Is a responsible role or function identifiable? |
| Enforceability | Can compliance be assessed objectively? |
| Evidence | Can the organization demonstrate that the requirement has been implemented? |
| Proportionality | Is the requirement appropriate to the relevant risk? |
| Traceability | Can the requirement be mapped to supporting controls or obligations? |
| Exception handling | Is any permitted deviation governed by a defined process? |

A requirement that cannot be tested may still express an important principle, but it should be supplemented with operational requirements that make the expected behavior verifiable.

### 7. Relationship to Recognized AI Governance Frameworks

The organization should determine which recognized frameworks and standards provide the most appropriate structure for its AI governance arrangements.

Frameworks should be selected according to organizational objectives, applicable obligations, industry expectations, and existing management systems.

They should not be treated as interchangeable or as automatically establishing legal compliance.

#### 7.1 ISO/IEC 42001

**ISO/IEC 42001** specifies requirements for an AI management system.

It provides a management-system approach to establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving AI governance arrangements.

Its relevance to an AI Governance Policy includes:

- Establishing organizational context and scope.
- Defining leadership responsibilities and commitments.
- Integrating AI risk management.
- Establishing operational processes and controls.
- Evaluating performance.
- Maintaining documented information.
- Conducting internal audits and management reviews.
- Driving continual improvement.

The policy can establish the organization's high-level commitments, while the AI management system defines the processes, responsibilities, evidence, and review mechanisms needed to implement them.

An organization should not claim ISO/IEC 42001 certification merely because it has written an AI Governance Policy. Certification relates to conformity of the relevant management system with the standard's requirements and the applicable certification process.

#### 7.2 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** provides a voluntary structure for managing risks associated with AI systems.

Its four core functions are:

| Function | Governance Relevance |
|---|---|
| GOVERN | Establishes policies, accountability, organizational culture, and risk management structures. |
| MAP | Establishes context, intended use, affected parties, and potential risks. |
| MEASURE | Evaluates and analyzes identified risks using appropriate methods and evidence. |
| MANAGE | Prioritizes risks and implements responses, monitoring, and treatment activities. |

The AI Governance Policy can establish the organizational commitment to risk-based AI governance, while supporting procedures implement the practical activities associated with these functions.

For example, a policy requirement to assess every in-scope AI use case can support the MAP function. Requirements for validation, performance monitoring, and testing can support MEASURE. Risk treatment, escalation, and ongoing monitoring can support MANAGE.

GOVERN provides the overarching organizational structure that makes these activities consistent and accountable.

#### 7.3 EU AI Act

The **EU AI Act**, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, establishes a legal framework for AI, including obligations that vary according to the applicable role, system classification, use case, and other legal conditions.

For organizations operating in or serving the European Union, AI governance should include a process for determining which provisions apply to each relevant AI system or activity.

Depending on the circumstances, relevant considerations may include:

- Prohibited AI practices.
- AI literacy obligations.
- Requirements applicable to high-risk AI systems.
- Transparency obligations for certain AI interactions or generated content.
- Responsibilities of providers, deployers, importers, distributors, or other actors.
- Technical documentation and record-keeping.
- Human oversight.
- Monitoring and incident handling.
- Applicable implementation dates and transitional arrangements.

The AI Governance Policy should require legal and compliance review rather than attempting to classify every AI system through a generic policy statement.

The organization must verify the current legal requirements and implementation dates applicable to its specific activities. A general AI Governance Policy does not itself establish compliance with the EU AI Act.

#### 7.4 GDPR and Data Protection Requirements

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) remains relevant when AI-related activities involve personal data within its scope.

AI governance should integrate privacy requirements with data governance, security, and AI risk management.

Relevant areas may include:

- Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency.
- Purpose limitation and data minimization.
- Accuracy and storage limitation.
- Integrity and confidentiality.
- Accountability.
- Data protection by design and by default.
- Data protection impact assessments where required.
- Restrictions and safeguards concerning certain solely automated decisions under Article 22, where applicable.

The organization should assess the actual processing activities and applicable legal conditions rather than assuming that all AI-assisted decisions are subject to the same requirements.

#### 7.5 Framework Mapping Principle

A single policy requirement may support multiple frameworks, but the mappings must be justified.

For example, a requirement to maintain an inventory of AI systems may support AI management-system governance, risk identification, regulatory analysis, and internal audit.

However, mapping one internal control to a framework requirement does not automatically demonstrate that the full requirement has been satisfied.

The organization should maintain traceability between:

1. The policy requirement.
2. The applicable external obligation or framework requirement.
3. The supporting internal control.
4. The responsible control owner.
5. The implementation evidence.
6. The assessment of control effectiveness.
7. Any identified gap and remediation action.

This traceability enables the organization to demonstrate how governance commitments translate into operational practice.

### 8. AI Governance Policy Ownership and Approval

An AI Governance Policy requires a clearly defined owner and formal approval authority.

Without ownership, the policy may become outdated, inconsistent with operational practices, or disconnected from enterprise risk decisions.

The organization should distinguish between policy ownership, policy approval, operational implementation, and independent assurance.

#### 8.1 Policy Owner

The policy owner is responsible for maintaining the document and coordinating its governance.

Depending on the organizational structure, ownership may sit with:

- The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO).
- The Chief Risk Officer.
- The Chief Data Officer.
- The Chief AI Officer, where such a role exists.
- An AI Governance Office.
- A designated AI Governance Committee or program owner.

The appropriate owner depends on the organization's operating model and the extent to which AI governance is integrated with information security, enterprise risk, data governance, and compliance.

A single accountable policy owner should be identified even when multiple functions contribute to the document.

#### 8.2 Approval Authority

Approval authority should be proportionate to the policy's organizational significance.

Potential approval authorities include:

- The Board or a designated Board committee for matters requiring Board-level oversight.
- The executive committee.
- The Chief Risk Officer or CISO under formally delegated authority.
- An authorized governance committee.

The policy should specify who approves the initial version, material amendments, and exceptions to mandatory requirements.

A governance committee may recommend approval without possessing the authority to approve the policy itself. These responsibilities must be distinguished.

The policy should include document-control metadata.

### 9. Enterprise AI Governance Operating Model

The AI Governance Policy should operate within a defined governance model that connects executive oversight, risk management, technical implementation, and independent assurance.

A governance model defines how decisions are made, who is accountable, which functions must be consulted, and how unresolved risks are escalated.

The precise structure varies by organization, but responsibilities should be sufficiently clear to avoid duplicated ownership or unassigned control obligations.

#### 9.1 Three Lines Model

Organizations may use the Institute of Internal Auditors' Three Lines Model as a reference for distinguishing operational management, risk and compliance oversight, and independent assurance.

The model should be adapted to the organization's governance structure rather than applied mechanically.

| Governance Layer | Typical AI Governance Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| First line — Business and Technology Management | Owns AI use cases, operates systems, implements controls, manages operational risks, and maintains evidence. |
| Second line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy, and Security Oversight | Establishes methodologies, challenges risk assessments, monitors compliance, defines standards, and provides oversight. |
| Third line — Internal Audit | Provides independent assurance on governance, risk management, and control effectiveness. |

These lines have different responsibilities, but they must collaborate.

For example, the business owner may accept responsibility for operating an AI use case, while the security team evaluates whether the proposed controls are adequate. Internal Audit may later assess whether the governance process is appropriately designed and consistently applied.

Internal Audit should preserve the independence required by its mandate and should not assume responsibility for operating controls that it is expected to audit independently.

#### 9.2 AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee can coordinate material decisions across business, technology, risk, and compliance functions.

Typical responsibilities may include:

- Approving or recommending approval of material AI use cases.
- Reviewing significant AI risks and proposed treatments.
- Resolving cross-functional governance issues.
- Reviewing policy exceptions within delegated authority.
- Monitoring aggregate AI risk exposure.
- Reviewing significant incidents and lessons learned.
- Prioritizing remediation activities.
- Escalating material matters to executive management.
- Monitoring governance performance and maturity.

The committee's authority must be documented.

A committee should not be treated as the owner of every AI system. Business and technical owners remain responsible for their assigned operational obligations.

#### 9.3 Governance Decision Rights

The organization should define which decisions can be made at operational level and which require escalation.

| Decision | Typical Decision Authority |
|---|---|
| Register a low-risk AI use case | Designated business or AI intake owner, following required screening. |
| Approve a standard-risk use case | Authorized business owner and required control functions, according to the approval model. |
| Approve a high-risk or materially consequential use case | Designated senior authority or governance committee, with required specialist reviews. |
| Accept material residual risk | Authorized risk owner within formally delegated risk-acceptance authority. |
| Approve a policy exception | Designated exception authority, subject to applicable law and policy constraints. |
| Suspend an AI system during an incident | Designated incident commander or authorized operational/security authority under established procedures. |
| Approve a material change to a production AI system | Authorized change authority following risk-based reassessment. |
| Retire an AI system | Accountable business owner and relevant technical/data owners, following closure requirements. |

This table is illustrative. Actual decision rights must reflect organizational delegations, regulatory obligations, contractual responsibilities, and the severity of the risks involved.

#### 9.4 Governance Escalation

The policy should establish escalation criteria for situations in which risk exceeds approved thresholds or cannot be resolved at the operational level.

Examples include:

- A proposed AI use case involving prohibited or legally restricted activity.
- A high residual risk that exceeds delegated risk appetite.
- A security vulnerability that creates material exposure.
- A privacy concern requiring formal legal assessment.
- A supplier unable to provide sufficient assurance.
- An AI system producing harmful or discriminatory outcomes.
- An incident involving unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information.
- A material disagreement between the business owner and a control function.
- A proposed exception that exceeds the approver's delegated authority.

Escalation must be timely and documented.

The process should identify the decision required, the responsible authority, the supporting evidence, the available options, and any interim safeguards necessary while the matter remains unresolved.

### 10. From Governance Principles to Enforceable Policy Requirements

The final foundation of an effective AI Governance Policy is the conversion of general principles into requirements that can be implemented and tested.

A principle describes the desired outcome. A policy requirement establishes an obligation. A control specifies the mechanism used to satisfy that obligation. Evidence demonstrates whether the control has been implemented and is operating as intended.

Consider the following example.

| Governance Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Principle | AI systems must be accountable and subject to appropriate oversight. |
| Policy requirement | Every in-scope AI system must have an assigned accountable business owner before deployment. |
| Supporting control | The AI intake process prevents production approval until the accountable owner is recorded and validated. |
| Responsible control owner | AI Governance Operations or the designated intake process owner. |
| Implementation evidence | AI system inventory record and approval workflow. |
| Operating evidence | Periodic sample testing of production AI systems against inventory and ownership records. |
| Failure condition | A production AI system has no identifiable accountable business owner. |
| Remediation | Assign an owner, assess the governance gap, and apply restrictions or escalation where required. |

This distinction is essential because an organization can possess a comprehensive policy without having implemented its requirements.

The policy must therefore establish a basis for translating governance expectations into operational processes, controls, and assurance activities.

#### 10.1 Policy Traceability Model

```text

```

The organization should maintain traceability where it is necessary to demonstrate implementation, manage obligations, and support audits.

This does not mean every principle requires a separate technical control. Some principles may be implemented through multiple coordinated controls, governance decisions, training activities, and management processes.

The traceability model should reflect the actual control environment rather than forcing an artificial one-to-one relationship.

#### 10.2 Minimum Foundation Requirements

Before approving an AI Governance Policy, the organization should confirm that the document establishes, at minimum:

- A defined purpose and scope.
- Clear applicability to relevant AI technologies and use cases.
- Governance principles and mandatory requirements.
- Accountability and policy ownership.
- Approval authority and decision rights.
- A risk-based approach to AI governance.
- Lifecycle oversight requirements.
- Integration with security, privacy, data, legal, and enterprise risk management.
- Requirements for AI inventory and use-case registration.
- Expectations for risk assessment and appropriate control implementation.
- Requirements for monitoring, reassessment, and incident escalation.
- A documented policy exception process.
- Evidence, assurance, and record-keeping expectations.
- A defined review and maintenance process.

These requirements establish the policy's governance foundation. The precise controls and documentation needed to implement them should be proportionate to organizational context and the risks of the AI systems in scope.

**Core Principle**

An AI Governance Policy is effective only when it establishes clear accountability, defines the scope of governed AI activities, sets enforceable requirements, and connects governance principles to operational controls and demonstrable evidence. It must cover the full AI lifecycle, integrate with existing corporate governance disciplines, and apply risk-based oversight without treating third-party AI, embedded AI features, experimentation, or employee use of AI as automatically outside its scope.

**Key Conceptual Distinctions**

- **AI governance vs. AI security:** AI governance establishes accountability, decision rights, oversight, and lifecycle requirements; AI security protects AI systems and their associated information, interfaces, models, and operations.
- **Principle vs. policy requirement:** A principle expresses an expected outcome; a policy requirement establishes a specific obligation.
- **Policy vs. procedure:** A policy defines what must be achieved; a procedure explains how an activity is performed.
- **Policy requirement vs. control:** A requirement establishes the obligation; a control is a mechanism used to satisfy it.
- **Control implementation vs. control effectiveness:** Implementation shows that a control exists; effectiveness assessment determines whether it operates as intended.
- **AI system ownership vs. AI supplier ownership:** A supplier may provide or operate AI technology, but the organization must retain the accountability assigned to it by applicable law, contracts, and its governance arrangements.
- **Risk-based governance vs. reduced accountability:** Proportionate oversight adjusts the depth of assessment and control requirements to the risk; it does not eliminate mandatory obligations.
- **Governance documentation vs. governance assurance:** A written policy records expectations; assurance requires evidence that those expectations have been implemented and are operating effectively.

### 11. Establishing Mandatory AI Governance Requirements

An AI Governance Policy must translate the organization's governance objectives into specific, enforceable requirements that guide how AI systems are proposed, acquired, developed, deployed, operated, monitored, and retired.

The policy should establish a minimum set of obligations applicable to all in-scope AI systems while allowing additional requirements to be imposed according to the system's risk classification, business purpose, technical capabilities, and regulatory context.

A governance requirement should identify what the organization expects, who is responsible for meeting that expectation, when compliance is required, and how compliance can be demonstrated.

For example, stating that the organization supports responsible AI adoption is not sufficient to establish an enforceable control obligation. The policy must translate that commitment into operational requirements, such as mandatory use-case registration, risk assessment, documented approval, and ongoing monitoring.

A practical AI Governance Policy should organize its mandatory requirements into defined control domains.

| Control Domain | Governance Objective | Minimum Policy Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| AI use-case registration | Maintain visibility of organizational AI activities. | All in-scope AI use cases must be registered through the approved governance process. |
| AI classification | Determine the appropriate level of oversight. | Each in-scope AI system must undergo an initial classification before authorization for production use. |
| AI risk management | Identify and address material risks. | AI risks must be assessed, documented, treated, and monitored according to the approved methodology. |
| AI security | Protect AI systems and associated assets. | AI systems must satisfy applicable security requirements before deployment. |
| Data governance | Ensure appropriate data use. | AI data processing must comply with approved data classification, access, quality, retention, and usage requirements. |
| Privacy | Protect personal data and individual rights. | Privacy obligations must be assessed wherever AI processing involves personal data within the scope of applicable requirements. |
| Third-party governance | Control external AI dependencies. | AI suppliers and services must undergo risk-based due diligence before use. |
| Human oversight | Maintain appropriate human authority. | Human oversight must be established where required by risk, system design, organizational policy, or applicable law. |
| AI testing and validation | Establish suitability for intended use. | AI systems must undergo proportionate testing and validation before production authorization. |
| Change management | Control material changes. | Changes that may materially affect AI risk must undergo documented review and approval. |
| Monitoring | Identify changes in risk or performance. | Production AI systems must be monitored according to their risk and operational requirements. |
| Incident management | Respond to AI-related failures and security events. | AI incidents must be reported, assessed, managed, and escalated through established procedures. |
| Documentation | Maintain accountability and traceability. | Required AI governance decisions, assessments, approvals, and evidence must be retained. |
| Exceptions | Control deviations from mandatory requirements. | Policy exceptions must follow the approved exception process and remain subject to documented risk assessment. |
| Retirement | Ensure controlled termination of AI services. | AI systems must be retired through an approved process that addresses access revocation, data handling, records, and dependencies. |

These domains form the policy's control foundation. Supporting standards and procedures should define the detailed requirements needed to implement each domain.

The organization should avoid creating requirements that cannot be assigned, implemented, or verified. Where an obligation depends on organizational context, the policy should identify the authority responsible for determining applicability.

### 12. AI System Registration and Inventory Requirements

An organization cannot govern AI systems effectively if it does not know which systems are being used, who owns them, what information they process, or which business activities depend on them.

The AI Governance Policy should therefore establish a mandatory process for registering in-scope AI systems.

The inventory must cover AI developed internally, purchased from external suppliers, embedded in existing software, accessed through APIs, and used through approved AI services.

It should also address relevant employee-facing AI applications and departmental implementations.

#### 12.1 Mandatory Registration Requirement

A policy requirement could state:

> All in-scope AI systems and use cases must be registered in the organization's designated AI inventory before authorization for production use. Registration must identify the accountable business owner, intended purpose, system owner, supplier or model provider where applicable, data categories, system classification, and current approval status. The inventory must be maintained throughout the AI system lifecycle.

The organization should determine whether particular experimental environments require registration, simplified records, or additional restrictions.

A prototype should not automatically be exempt merely because it is not yet in production. Experiments involving sensitive data, external AI services, consequential decisions, or access to enterprise systems may create material risks before formal deployment.

#### 12.3 Inventory Completeness and Accuracy

Maintaining an inventory requires more than creating a spreadsheet.

The organization should define how inventory completeness is established and monitored.

Potential mechanisms include:

- Mandatory registration within the procurement process.
- Integration with enterprise architecture and application inventories.
- AI-related questions in technology intake and project approval workflows.
- Identification of AI features in SaaS platforms.
- Periodic business-unit attestations.
- Reviews of external AI subscriptions and API integrations.
- Security monitoring for unauthorized AI services where lawful and appropriate.
- Reconciliation between the AI inventory and approved supplier records.
- Periodic validation of ownership and lifecycle status.

An inventory is reliable only when it reflects actual AI usage and is updated when relevant changes occur.

**Control example:**

The AI Governance Office performs a quarterly reconciliation between the AI inventory, approved technology portfolio, and procurement records. Unregistered AI services are investigated, assigned an appropriate risk classification, and either brought into the governance process or restricted according to the organization's incident and remediation procedures.

### 13. AI Use-Case Classification and Approval Requirements

AI systems can create significantly different levels of risk depending on how they are used.

The policy should establish a classification process that determines the appropriate assessment depth, control requirements, approval authority, and monitoring intensity.

Classification should consider the system's actual intended purpose and operating context rather than relying solely on the model's technical category.

For example, the same language model may support low-risk document summarization in one application and materially consequential employee evaluations in another.

#### 13.1 Internal Risk Classification

An organization may use an internal classification model such as the following.

| Internal Classification | Typical Characteristics | Governance Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Limited business impact, low-sensitivity information, no consequential automated decisions, and restricted access. | Simplified assessment, approved-service requirements, baseline security controls, and defined usage restrictions. |
| Medium | Business-relevant outputs, internal or confidential information, integration with enterprise applications, or moderate operational consequences. | Documented risk assessment, security and privacy review where applicable, testing, named ownership, and formal approval. |
| High | Significant potential impact on individuals, critical business processes, sensitive information, or consequential decisions. | Enhanced impact assessment, specialist reviews, stronger validation, explicit approval authority, and more intensive monitoring. |
| Critical | Potential for severe or widespread harm, major disruption of critical operations, or highly consequential actions with limited reversibility. | Executive-level oversight as appropriate, rigorous assurance, strict authorization boundaries, resilience requirements, and explicit operational safeguards. |

These categories are illustrative internal governance tiers. They are not equivalent to the EU AI Act's legal classifications.

An internal system classified as high risk does not automatically constitute a high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act. Conversely, a system cannot be treated as legally low risk merely because the organization assigns it a low internal classification.

Applicable legal classification must be assessed separately.

#### 13.2 Classification Criteria

The classification process should consider several dimensions.

| Assessment Dimension | Questions to Consider |
|---|---|
| Business criticality | Could the system disrupt a critical business process if unavailable or incorrect? |
| Data sensitivity | Does the system process confidential, restricted, personal, or special-category data? |
| Human impact | Could the system influence employment, financial, healthcare, educational, or other consequential outcomes? |
| Autonomy | Can the system execute actions without human approval? |
| Integration | Can it access enterprise applications, privileged interfaces, or sensitive repositories? |
| Decision influence | Can users rely on its outputs when making consequential decisions? |
| Security exposure | Is the system accessible externally or exposed to untrusted inputs? |
| Supplier dependency | Does the organization depend on an external provider for critical AI functionality? |
| Reversibility | Can harmful outputs or actions be detected, contained, and reversed? |
| Regulatory exposure | Are there specific legal, regulatory, or contractual requirements? |
| Scale | Could a failure affect a large population or many transactions? |
| Uncertainty | Are model behavior, limitations, or operational consequences insufficiently understood? |

The organization should define how these dimensions influence the final classification.

A high-impact characteristic may require escalation even when an aggregate score appears moderate. The methodology should therefore include override criteria for prohibited uses, legal requirements, and severe risk scenarios.

### 14. AI Risk Management Policy Obligations

The AI Governance Policy should require AI-related risks to be identified, assessed, treated, accepted where permitted, and monitored throughout the lifecycle.

Risk management must address both risks created by AI and risks that AI systems introduce into existing business processes.

Examples include an AI model generating inaccurate recommendations, a malicious user manipulating a chatbot, an agent executing an unauthorized transaction, or an external provider changing a model in a way that affects business performance.

#### 14.1 Mandatory Risk Assessment

A policy requirement could state:

> All in-scope AI systems must undergo a documented risk assessment before production authorization. The assessment must consider the intended purpose, affected stakeholders, data processing, security threats, operational dependencies, model limitations, foreseeable misuse, and applicable legal obligations. Identified risks must be assigned owners and treated according to the approved risk methodology. Residual risks must be accepted by an authorized risk owner before deployment where acceptance is permitted.

The organization should define reassessment triggers, including:

- Material changes to system purpose or capabilities.
- Significant model or supplier changes.
- Introduction of new data sources.
- Changes in access privileges or tool permissions.
- Significant changes in the affected population.
- New legal or regulatory obligations.
- Security incidents or harmful system behavior.
- Material performance deterioration.
- Changes to the operating environment.

The assessment must be appropriate to the system's actual risk. A generic checklist may support screening, but it should not replace a deeper assessment when significant risks are identified.

#### 14.2 AI Risk Categories

The policy should recognize that AI risks extend beyond conventional information security risks.

| Risk Category | Example Risk | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | AI initiative does not support business objectives. | Wasted investment and poor business outcomes. |
| Operational | AI outputs are unreliable during production use. | Service disruption and process errors. |
| Cybersecurity | Prompt injection manipulates an AI assistant. | Unauthorized disclosure or unintended actions. |
| Data protection | Personal information is processed without appropriate safeguards. | Regulatory exposure and harm to individuals. |
| Confidentiality | Employees submit restricted data to an unauthorized AI service. | Information disclosure and contractual breaches. |
| Model performance | Model accuracy deteriorates after deployment. | Incorrect recommendations and degraded service quality. |
| Bias and fairness | A model produces unjustified disparities between groups. | Unfair outcomes, legal exposure, and reputational damage. |
| Legal and regulatory | The organization misclassifies a system or overlooks an applicable obligation. | Non-compliance and enforcement exposure. |
| Third-party | A supplier changes model behavior or processing arrangements. | Unanticipated risk and reduced control. |
| Intellectual property | AI-generated content or training data creates ownership or infringement concerns. | Disputes, claims, or restrictions on use. |
| Human factors | Users over-rely on AI-generated recommendations. | Inadequate verification and poor decisions. |
| Agent autonomy | An AI agent performs unauthorized actions through connected tools. | Fraud, data alteration, or operational disruption. |
| Resilience | A critical AI service becomes unavailable. | Business interruption and degraded customer service. |
| Reputation | AI generates harmful, misleading, or inappropriate content. | Loss of trust and customer complaints. |
| Environmental and social | AI use creates material unintended social or environmental consequences. | Stakeholder concerns and potential compliance issues. |

Risk categories should be adapted to the organization's industry, operating environment, and risk methodology.

#### 14.3 Risk Treatment Options

The organization should establish approved risk treatment options.

| Treatment Option | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Do not proceed with the proposed use or discontinue the activity. | Reject an AI use case that is prohibited or whose risks cannot be acceptably managed. |
| Mitigate | Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact. | Restrict the model to approved knowledge sources and require human verification. |
| Transfer or share | Allocate defined aspects of risk through contractual or insurance arrangements where appropriate. | Establish contractual incident notification and security obligations with an AI supplier. |
| Accept | Formally accept residual risk through an authorized decision. | Approve a documented residual operational risk within delegated risk appetite. |

Risk transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability or guarantee that a third party will absorb all consequences.

Risk acceptance must not be used to authorize unlawful activity, disregard non-waivable obligations, or bypass mandatory security requirements.

#### 14.4 Risk Ownership and Residual Risk

Each material AI risk should have an identifiable risk owner.

The risk owner is responsible for ensuring that the risk is evaluated, appropriate treatment is selected, and residual exposure is escalated or accepted through the approved process.

The risk owner is not necessarily the person implementing the technical control.

For example:

- The business owner may own the risk that AI-generated customer advice causes business harm.
- The security team may implement prompt-injection defenses and access restrictions.
- The privacy function may advise on personal data processing.
- The AI Governance Office may monitor the assessment process.
- An authorized executive may accept residual risk that exceeds the business owner's delegated authority.

The policy should distinguish risk ownership from control ownership and approval authority.

### 15. AI Security Requirements

The AI Governance Policy should establish that AI systems must comply with applicable information security requirements and any additional controls required by their specific architecture and risk profile.

AI security is not limited to protecting model weights or preventing malicious prompts. It includes securing the complete AI application and its connections to data, users, enterprise services, and operational processes.

#### 15.1 Security-by-Design

AI security requirements should be considered during use-case evaluation, architecture design, supplier selection, implementation, testing, and operational monitoring.

A policy requirement could state:

> All in-scope AI systems must undergo a risk-based security assessment before production deployment. Security controls must address applicable threats to identity, access, data, models, prompts, outputs, integrations, infrastructure, and operational processes. Material security findings must be remediated or addressed through an authorized risk decision before the system is approved for production use.

The required control depth should depend on the architecture and capabilities of the system.

For example, an AI tool that only summarizes approved public documents presents a different threat profile from an AI agent with access to confidential customer records and payment-related workflows.

#### 15.2 Minimum Security Control Domains

| Security Domain | Policy Obligation | Example Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Identity and access management | Access must be authenticated and authorized. | Enterprise identity integration and role-based access control. |
| Least privilege | AI systems and agents must receive only the permissions required for approved tasks. | Separate read-only and transaction-execution permissions. |
| Data protection | Data must be protected according to its classification and applicable obligations. | Encryption, access restrictions, and data loss prevention. |
| Secure integration | Connections to enterprise services must be authorized and controlled. | API gateways, scoped tokens, and server-side authorization. |
| Prompt and input security | Relevant input-manipulation threats must be assessed and mitigated. | Prompt-injection testing and isolation of untrusted content. |
| Output security | AI outputs must be validated before sensitive or consequential downstream use. | Output filtering, schema validation, and human review. |
| Logging and monitoring | Security-relevant activities must be recorded and monitored. | Audit logs, anomaly detection, and incident alerts. |
| Vulnerability management | Relevant vulnerabilities must be identified and remediated. | Security testing and tracked remediation deadlines. |
| Configuration management | Material configuration changes must be controlled. | Approved change workflows and configuration baselines. |
| Model and supplier security | External model dependencies must be assessed. | Supplier due diligence and model-change notifications where available. |
| Resilience | Critical AI services must have appropriate continuity arrangements. | Fallback workflows and recovery procedures. |
| Incident response | AI security incidents must be handled through established response processes. | Incident triage, containment, investigation, and recovery. |

The policy should reference supporting security standards where detailed technical requirements are maintained.

#### 15.3 AI-Specific Threat Considerations

The organization should ensure that relevant AI-specific threats are incorporated into its security assessment methodology.

Examples include:

**Prompt injection:** Malicious instructions embedded in user inputs or retrieved content attempt to override intended behavior or induce unauthorized actions.

**Sensitive information disclosure:** An AI system reveals confidential information through outputs, logs, retrieval, or inappropriate access to enterprise data.

**Insecure output handling:** AI-generated content is passed to another system without sufficient validation, potentially enabling downstream injection or unsafe execution.

**Excessive agency:** An AI system has more authority, tool access, or autonomy than its approved purpose requires.

**Model or data poisoning:** Malicious or manipulated training, fine-tuning, or reference data affects system behavior.

**Supply-chain compromise:** A model, dependency, plugin, connector, or third-party service introduces vulnerabilities or unauthorized behavior.

**Uncontrolled consumption:** Repeated or malicious requests create excessive cost, resource consumption, or service degradation.

**Inference and extraction attacks:** An attacker attempts to infer sensitive information, extract model behavior, or recover information through repeated queries.

The applicable threats and mitigations should be determined through the organization's risk assessment rather than assuming that every AI system is exposed to every threat equally.

### 16. Data Governance and AI Data Use Requirements

AI governance must establish controls over the information used to train, fine-tune, ground, evaluate, operate, and monitor AI systems.

Data-related risks can arise even when the model itself is supplied and operated by a reputable provider.

The policy should require AI data use to comply with the organization's data classification, access control, privacy, retention, and information handling requirements.

#### 16.1 Approved Data Use

A policy requirement could state:

> AI systems may process organizational data only for authorized purposes and through approved environments. Data access, transfer, retention, and disclosure must comply with applicable information classification, privacy, contractual, and security requirements. The use of organizational information for model training, fine-tuning, or provider improvement must be explicitly assessed and authorized where applicable.

This requirement helps prevent assumptions that information may be submitted to any AI service merely because the user has legitimate access to the information.

Access to a document for business purposes does not automatically authorize uploading that document to an external AI provider.

#### 16.2 Data Classification and AI Usage

The organization should map its information classification scheme to permitted AI usage.

| Data Classification | Example | Governance Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Published product information. | May be used in approved AI environments, subject to applicable usage and intellectual property requirements. |
| Internal | Internal procedures and non-sensitive business documentation. | Use only in environments authorized for internal information. |
| Confidential | Customer records, commercial plans, or internal security reports. | Require explicit authorization, approved processing arrangements, and appropriate security controls. |
| Restricted | Highly sensitive personal data, authentication secrets, critical security information, or other specially protected information. | Require strict access restrictions and explicit authorization; some use cases may be prohibited by policy or law. |

These categories are illustrative. The organization's approved classification scheme and specific legal obligations take precedence.

Data classification must be applied to prompts, uploaded documents, retrieval sources, model inputs, generated outputs, telemetry, logs, and stored conversation histories where applicable.

#### 16.4 Data Quality and Provenance

The policy should require appropriate consideration of data quality and provenance when data quality materially affects AI reliability or risk.

Relevant questions include:

- Where did the data originate?
- Is the organization authorized to use it?
- Is the data accurate and sufficiently current?
- Does it contain known gaps or biases?
- Can its origin and transformation history be traced?
- Are the data sources appropriate for the intended purpose?
- Can the organization correct or remove problematic data?
- Are access permissions preserved when data is retrieved by an AI system?

For retrieval-augmented generation systems, access control must apply to retrieved documents as well as the user interface.

An AI assistant must not disclose information to a user merely because the retrieval system can access it. The system should enforce appropriate authorization at retrieval time and, where necessary, during subsequent processing and output generation.

### 17. Privacy and Responsible AI Requirements

The AI Governance Policy should require privacy and responsible-use considerations to be integrated into relevant AI activities.

These requirements should be implemented through the organization's established privacy, legal, ethics, risk, and security processes.

The policy must not assume that a single AI-specific assessment replaces other assessments required by law or internal policy.

#### 17.1 Privacy Assessment

Where AI processing involves personal data, the organization should determine the applicable privacy requirements before deployment.

Depending on the processing activity, this may involve:

- Identifying the purpose and lawful basis of processing.
- Assessing necessity and proportionality.
- Evaluating data minimization and retention.
- Reviewing transparency and data-subject rights.
- Assessing international transfers and supplier arrangements.
- Determining whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment is required.
- Assessing whether the proposed processing involves automated decision-making subject to specific legal restrictions.
- Defining controls for access, disclosure, correction, and deletion where applicable.

The privacy function should be involved at a stage when its findings can still influence the system's design.

A review conducted only after implementation may identify issues that are expensive or impractical to remediate.

#### 17.2 Responsible AI Assessment

The organization should evaluate relevant responsible AI considerations, including fairness, reliability, transparency, accountability, human oversight, and potential harm.

The assessment should be proportionate to the intended purpose and potential consequences.

For example, a customer-service assistant that suggests answers to a human agent may require quality controls, transparency, and escalation procedures. An AI system that materially influences hiring decisions may require more extensive assessment of fairness, data suitability, explainability, human oversight, and applicable legal requirements.

#### 17.3 Human Review and Decision Accountability

The policy should define when human review is required and what the reviewer must be able to do.

A human review requirement should specify:

- Which outputs or actions require review.
- Which roles are authorized to perform the review.
- What information the reviewer must receive.
- What criteria guide acceptance or rejection.
- How disagreements or uncertain results are escalated.
- How the decision and any override are recorded.
- Whether the reviewer can stop or reverse the proposed action.

Human oversight should not be treated as a substitute for adequate technical controls.

For example, an AI agent with unrestricted payment authority should not be considered adequately controlled merely because a manager is nominally responsible for reviewing its activity afterward.

#### 17.4 AI Literacy and Competence

Employees and other authorized users should receive AI literacy and role-appropriate training.

Training should cover:

- Approved AI services and acceptable use.
- Information classification and prohibited data disclosures.
- Limitations of AI-generated outputs.
- Verification of material claims.
- Prompt injection and malicious content risks.
- Privacy and confidentiality.
- Appropriate escalation and incident reporting.
- Human oversight obligations.
- Restrictions on automated decisions and actions.
- The consequences of using unauthorized AI tools.

Training should be proportionate to responsibilities.

A general employee may need awareness of acceptable AI use, while developers, security engineers, model validators, procurement specialists, and AI governance personnel may require more specialized training.

The organization should maintain evidence of required training and periodically evaluate whether the training remains appropriate.

### 18. Third-Party AI and Supplier Governance

AI services frequently depend on external model providers, cloud platforms, SaaS vendors, API providers, data suppliers, and specialist service providers.

The AI Governance Policy should require supplier risks to be assessed before an AI service is acquired or integrated into organizational processes.

Existing suppliers should also be reassessed when material changes in service, processing, or risk occur.

#### 18.1 Supplier Assessment Requirements

A policy requirement could state:

> AI services and suppliers must undergo due diligence proportionate to their intended use, data sensitivity, integration privileges, business criticality, and applicable obligations before authorization. The assessment must address relevant security, privacy, data governance, service resilience, contractual, and AI-specific risks.

Supplier assessment should consider:

- The provider's security and privacy controls.
- Data processing locations and subprocessors.
- Data retention and deletion practices.
- Whether customer data may be used for model training or service improvement.
- Identity and access management.
- Encryption and logging capabilities.
- Security incident notification arrangements.
- Vulnerability management and independent assurance.
- Model documentation and known limitations.
- Service availability and business continuity.
- Model versioning and material-change notification.
- Intellectual property and data ownership terms.
- Audit rights and contractual responsibilities.
- Exit arrangements and data portability.
- Relevant regulatory roles and obligations.

A supplier's marketing statement or security certification should not be treated as sufficient evidence for every risk relevant to the proposed use case.

#### 18.2 AI Supplier Risk Tiers

| Supplier Tier | Example | Assessment Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Low | AI service processing only approved public information with no material enterprise integration. | Basic due diligence and approved-service checks. |
| Medium | AI SaaS processing internal business information. | Security, privacy, contractual, data-handling, and operational assessment. |
| High | AI provider processing confidential customer information or supporting a significant business process. | Enhanced due diligence, specialist review, contractual safeguards, and documented risk decisions. |
| Critical | AI provider supporting critical operations or enabling consequential actions with substantial business impact. | Comprehensive assessment, executive oversight as appropriate, resilience validation, and strong exit and contingency arrangements. |

These are illustrative internal supplier tiers and should be aligned with the organization's third-party risk methodology.

#### 18.3 Shared Responsibility

The organization should document which controls are provided by the supplier and which remain the organization's responsibility.

For a hosted AI service, the provider may manage model infrastructure and platform availability, while the organization remains responsible for user access, permitted data, application configuration, business process design, and appropriate use of outputs.

A shared-responsibility matrix helps prevent control gaps.

| Control Area | Typical Provider Responsibility | Typical Customer Responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Underlying infrastructure | Operate and secure provider-managed infrastructure. | Understand service boundaries and configure customer-managed components. |
| Model service | Maintain the contracted model service. | Select an appropriate service and assess model suitability. |
| User identity | Provide supported authentication capabilities. | Configure identity integration and manage user accounts. |
| Access permissions | Provide authorization features. | Define roles, configure permissions, and review access. |
| Customer data | Implement contracted data-handling and protection measures. | Determine permitted data, classification, and access. |
| Application integration | Provide supported interfaces and platform controls. | Secure API credentials, integrations, and downstream workflows. |
| Monitoring | Provide contracted platform telemetry. | Monitor organizational use and relevant security events. |
| Incident handling | Notify and respond to provider-side incidents under applicable commitments. | Detect customer-side incidents, assess impact, and meet applicable notification duties. |
| Business continuity | Maintain contracted provider resilience arrangements. | Define business fallback, recovery, and exit plans. |

Actual responsibilities depend on the service model, architecture, contract, and configuration.

The organization should document the allocation for each material AI service rather than assuming that the provider is responsible for all AI-related controls.

### 19. AI Testing, Validation, and Production Authorization

An AI Governance Policy should require sufficient testing and validation to establish that an AI system is suitable for its intended use and that material risks are addressed before production deployment.

Testing should be proportionate to the system's potential impact, technical characteristics, and operating environment.

A successful demonstration is not equivalent to production readiness.

#### 19.1 Minimum Testing Categories

| Testing Category | Objective | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Functional testing | Verify that required functionality operates correctly. | Confirm that the assistant can retrieve approved knowledge articles. |
| Accuracy and quality testing | Evaluate output quality and suitability. | Measure factual correctness against an approved evaluation dataset. |
| Security testing | Identify vulnerabilities and attack paths. | Test access controls, prompt injection, and unsafe output handling. |
| Privacy testing | Evaluate personal data handling and disclosure risks. | Verify that unauthorized users cannot retrieve protected personal information. |
| Fairness testing | Identify unjustified disparities where relevant. | Evaluate performance across relevant groups for a consequential decision-support system. |
| Robustness testing | Evaluate behavior under unusual or adverse conditions. | Test ambiguous, malformed, or adversarial inputs. |
| Integration testing | Verify interactions with connected systems. | Confirm that API authorization and error handling operate as intended. |
| Human oversight testing | Confirm that required review and escalation mechanisms work. | Verify that high-impact outputs cannot bypass mandatory approval. |
| Resilience testing | Evaluate failure handling and continuity. | Confirm that a critical workflow enters a safe fallback mode when the model service is unavailable. |
| Regression testing | Detect unintended changes after modifications. | Compare performance and security results after a model or prompt update. |

The organization should establish acceptance criteria before testing begins, rather than defining success after observing the results.

#### 19.2 Production Authorization Criteria

Production deployment should require confirmation that:

1. The use case is registered and classified.
2. Required assessments are complete.
3. Material risks have approved treatment plans.
4. Mandatory security and privacy requirements have been addressed.
5. Required testing has been completed against defined acceptance criteria.
6. Significant findings have been remediated or addressed through an authorized decision.
7. Required human oversight and escalation arrangements are operational.
8. The accountable business owner and technical owner are identified.
9. Monitoring, incident response, and fallback arrangements are established.
10. The required approval authority has authorized deployment.
11. Necessary documentation and evidence have been retained.

The approval process should distinguish between a condition that must be satisfied before deployment and a remediation action that may be permitted after deployment under an explicitly authorized, time-bound arrangement.

A risk acceptance decision must not override legal prohibitions or non-waivable requirements.

### 20. Change Management, Monitoring, and Incident Reporting

AI systems can change through model updates, prompt modifications, new data sources, altered integrations, changes in user behavior, and modifications to tool permissions.

The AI Governance Policy must therefore establish lifecycle controls that remain effective after production approval.

#### 20.1 AI Change Management

Material changes should trigger review before implementation.

Examples include:

- Replacing the underlying model.
- Upgrading to a new model version.
- Changing the system's intended purpose.
- Enabling additional tools or actions.
- Connecting new data repositories.
- Expanding access to new user groups.
- Changing the hosting or processing environment.
- Increasing automation or reducing human review.
- Changing output handling or decision logic.
- Modifying supplier terms that materially affect risk.

The organization should determine whether each change requires a new risk assessment, security testing, privacy review, regression testing, or formal reapproval.

A minor change that does not materially affect risk may follow a streamlined change process. A change that expands the system's authority or alters its intended use may require substantial reassessment.

#### 20.2 Operational Monitoring

The policy should require ongoing monitoring proportionate to the system's risk.

Relevant monitoring areas may include:

- Availability and service performance.
- Output quality and accuracy.
- Changes in model behavior.
- Security events and unauthorized access attempts.
- Prompt-injection indicators.
- Data disclosure incidents.
- Unusual tool usage or transaction activity.
- Human overrides and escalation rates.
- Bias or fairness indicators where relevant.
- Supplier incidents and material service changes.
- Cost and resource consumption.
- Complaints and adverse outcomes.
- Compliance with approved use restrictions.

Monitoring should include defined thresholds and escalation paths.

Collecting metrics without assigning responsibility for reviewing them does not establish effective oversight.

#### 20.3 AI Incident Reporting

The policy should require suspected AI-related incidents to be reported and managed through established incident response arrangements.

Examples include:

- Confidential information disclosed through an AI output.
- An AI agent performing an unauthorized transaction.
- A prompt-injection attack causing unintended system behavior.
- An AI system generating materially harmful or misleading outputs.
- Unauthorized modification of training or retrieval data.
- Significant deterioration in model performance.
- A supplier incident affecting the organization's AI service.
- Failure of a critical AI-dependent business process.

The organization should distinguish between a security incident, a privacy incident, an operational failure, a model-quality issue, and a harmful AI outcome, while recognizing that one event may belong to multiple categories.

Incident handling should establish appropriate containment, investigation, remediation, escalation, evidence preservation, and recovery.

Applicable regulatory or contractual notification deadlines must be assessed separately. The internal incident process should support timely escalation and should not assume that every AI-related event triggers the same external reporting obligation.

#### 20.4 Suspension and Emergency Controls

The organization should define the conditions under which an AI system may be restricted, suspended, or disabled.

Potential triggers include:

- A credible threat of material harm.
- Unauthorized access to sensitive data.
- An AI agent performing unsafe or unauthorized actions.
- A significant vulnerability without adequate mitigation.
- Failure of a mandatory control.
- Materially unreliable outputs in a consequential process.
- A supplier incident that undermines confidence in the service.
- A legal or regulatory concern requiring immediate intervention.

The organization should identify who has authority to initiate suspension and how business continuity will be maintained.

For AI agents, safe suspension may require revoking tool permissions, disabling integrations, invalidating credentials, or placing transactions into a controlled approval queue.

Simply disabling the user interface may be insufficient if background jobs, API integrations, or scheduled actions remain active.

#### 20.5 AI Retirement and Decommissioning

The policy should require controlled retirement of AI systems.

Retirement activities may include:

- Revoking user and service-account access.
- Disabling API integrations and agent tools.
- Revoking credentials and tokens.
- Removing obsolete access permissions.
- Preserving records required for audit or legal obligations.
- Deleting or archiving data according to approved retention requirements.
- Addressing supplier-side data retention and deletion.
- Updating the AI inventory.
- Transferring or closing outstanding risk and remediation records.
- Informing affected users and business stakeholders.
- Validating that dependent business processes remain operational.

Retirement must be documented so that the organization can demonstrate that the system has been removed from service and that residual access, data, and operational dependencies have been addressed.

**Core Principle**

The AI Governance Policy must establish enforceable requirements across registration, classification, risk management, security, data governance, privacy, supplier oversight, testing, approval, monitoring, incident management, change control, and retirement. Each requirement should be proportionate to the system's risk, assigned to an accountable role, supported by operational controls, and capable of being verified through evidence. Governance must remain effective throughout the AI lifecycle rather than ending when a system receives initial production approval.

**Key Conceptual Distinctions**

- **AI inventory vs. AI risk register:** The inventory records which AI systems exist and how they are governed; the risk register records identified risks, their owners, treatment, and residual exposure.
- **Internal risk tier vs. legal risk classification:** An internal tier determines organizational oversight; a legal classification determines obligations under the applicable legal framework.
- **Risk assessment vs. risk acceptance:** Assessment identifies and evaluates risk; acceptance is an authorized decision to retain residual exposure where permitted.
- **Risk owner vs. control owner:** The risk owner is accountable for managing the risk; the control owner is responsible for implementing or operating a specified control.
- **Security testing vs. model validation:** Security testing evaluates threats and control weaknesses; model validation evaluates suitability, performance, and limitations for the intended purpose. The activities may overlap but are not interchangeable.
- **Human oversight vs. human presence:** Meaningful oversight requires sufficient information, competence, authority, and ability to intervene; nominal human involvement does not guarantee effective control.
- **Supplier assurance vs. customer accountability:** Supplier evidence supports due diligence, but the organization must independently assess whether the service and its configuration satisfy its own obligations.
- **Deployment approval vs. lifecycle authorization:** Initial approval authorizes a defined system and use case; material changes, incidents, or changes in risk may require renewed review or suspension.
- **Monitoring vs. assurance:** Monitoring observes ongoing behavior and risk indicators; assurance evaluates whether governance and controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.
- **Policy compliance vs. policy documentation:** A written requirement defines what must happen; compliance requires evidence that the requirement has been implemented and maintained.
