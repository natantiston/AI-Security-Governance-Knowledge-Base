# Chapter 21.04 — AI-Powered Contact Center
## Part 1: Business Context, System Architecture, AI Capabilities, and Governance Foundations

### 1. Introduction to AI-Powered Contact Centers

An AI-powered contact center is a customer-interaction environment that uses artificial intelligence to support or automate activities traditionally performed by human customer-service agents. These activities may include answering customer questions, understanding customer intent, transcribing conversations, summarizing interactions, recommending responses, analyzing customer sentiment, routing calls, and assisting agents with complex requests.

Unlike a traditional contact center, which primarily relies on predefined call flows, interactive voice response (IVR), and human decision-making, an AI-powered contact center can use natural language processing, speech recognition, machine learning, generative AI, and conversational AI to interpret customer requests and generate context-sensitive responses.

The AI capabilities may be embedded into an existing contact center platform or distributed across several services, including a cloud contact center, a customer relationship management (CRM) platform, an AI model provider, a knowledge management system, and enterprise backend applications.

For a telecommunications operator, an AI-powered contact center might support customers who want to:

- Check their mobile or broadband bills.
- Understand their subscription plans.
- Report network connectivity problems.
- Request technical troubleshooting.
- Change a service package.
- Report a lost device or SIM card.
- Request a payment arrangement.
- Open or update a service ticket.
- Speak with a human agent.
- Receive proactive notifications about service disruptions.

These interactions may involve sensitive personal information, authentication credentials, billing records, telecommunications usage data, and account-management functions. The AI system must therefore operate within defined security, privacy, and business-control boundaries.

#### 1.1 Business objectives

Organizations typically introduce AI into contact centers to improve operational efficiency, service quality, and customer experience.

| Business objective | AI capability | Expected benefit | Governance consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reduce waiting times | Automated conversational assistance | More customer inquiries handled simultaneously | Ensure automated responses remain accurate and safe |
| Reduce operational costs | Automated resolution of routine requests | Lower dependence on manual handling for repetitive tasks | Evaluate automation risks and unintended customer impact |
| Improve first-contact resolution | AI-assisted diagnosis and knowledge retrieval | Fewer transfers and repeat contacts | Validate the correctness of recommendations |
| Improve agent productivity | Real-time suggestions and conversation summaries | Less manual documentation | Prevent inaccurate summaries from contaminating customer records |
| Improve service availability | Automated support outside normal working hours | Continuous access to selected services | Maintain fallback arrangements and incident response |
| Improve customer understanding | Natural-language interaction | Customers can describe problems in their own words | Detect ambiguity and escalate sensitive cases |
| Improve service personalization | Contextual recommendations based on authorized customer data | More relevant customer assistance | Enforce data minimization and access restrictions |
| Improve management visibility | Conversation analytics and trend detection | Better insight into customer issues | Control access to recordings, transcripts, and analytics |
| Improve workforce effectiveness | Automated quality monitoring and agent assistance | More consistent service processes | Assess employee privacy and the fairness of performance evaluations |

These benefits are potential outcomes, not guaranteed results. The organization should define measurable success criteria and verify them through controlled evaluation.

#### 1.2 Traditional contact center versus AI-powered contact center

| Dimension | Traditional contact center | AI-powered contact center |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interaction | Human agent, IVR, or scripted workflow | Human agent, AI assistant, autonomous conversational AI, or a combination |
| Language understanding | Primarily predefined menus and agent interpretation | Natural-language processing and conversational context |
| Response generation | Agent-created responses or predefined scripts | AI-generated responses, approved templates, or agent recommendations |
| Knowledge access | Manual search or fixed knowledge menus | Retrieval-assisted search and contextual recommendations |
| Call transcription | Manual notes or separate transcription systems | Automated speech-to-text transcription |
| Interaction summaries | Written by agents | Generated automatically and reviewed where necessary |
| Routing | Rules, queues, and agent selection | Rules combined with AI-based intent classification or routing |
| Quality monitoring | Manual sampling and predefined analytics | Automated analysis across larger volumes of interactions |
| Operational risks | Human error, process failure, and technology outages | Existing risks plus model error, hallucination, prompt injection, and AI-specific data exposure |
| Governance requirements | Conventional IT, security, privacy, and operational controls | Conventional controls supplemented by AI governance and model-behavior oversight |

The introduction of AI does not eliminate conventional contact center risks. Instead, it introduces additional failure modes and changes how certain risks must be assessed.

For example, a traditional IVR may route a customer to the wrong department because of an incorrect menu selection. A generative AI assistant may misunderstand the same request, produce an inaccurate answer, retrieve information from an inappropriate source, or invoke an unsuitable backend operation.

The governance approach must address both categories of risk.

#### 1.3 Degrees of AI autonomy

Not every AI-powered contact center has the same level of autonomy.

**Level 1 — Agent assistance**

AI supports human agents by transcribing conversations, retrieving relevant knowledge, suggesting responses, or preparing summaries. The agent remains responsible for the customer-facing response.

**Level 2 — Human-approved AI actions**

AI prepares a response or proposed transaction, but a human must approve it before execution.

**Level 3 — Bounded automation**

AI independently handles predefined, lower-risk requests within a controlled set of capabilities. Complex, sensitive, or uncertain situations are escalated.

**Level 4 — Tool-enabled conversational automation**

AI can interact with enterprise applications through controlled tools, such as retrieving billing information, opening support tickets, or changing selected service settings.

**Level 5 — Broadly autonomous customer-service operations**

An AI agent can plan and execute multi-step workflows across several systems, subject to its configured permissions and constraints.

These levels are illustrative rather than a universal industry classification. An organization may use different terminology or combine capabilities across levels.

The critical governance distinction is not simply how advanced the model is. It is **what decisions the AI can make, what data it can access, what actions it can execute, and what safeguards constrain those actions**.

A contact center using generative AI only to summarize conversations may present a substantially different risk profile from one that allows AI agents to change subscriptions, issue credits, or modify customer account details.

#### Core Principle

AI-powered contact centers should be governed according to their actual capabilities, data access, customer impact, and level of autonomy. Automation benefits do not remove the organization's responsibility for customer protection, security, privacy, and operational accountability.

### 2. Business Context, Scope, and Stakeholder Responsibilities

An AI-powered contact center is not merely an AI application. It is part of a larger business service involving customer interactions, enterprise data, operational workflows, third-party platforms, and human decision-making.

The organization must define the scope of the system before assessing its risks or approving deployment.

A clearly defined scope prevents teams from overlooking components that influence the AI system's behavior, such as call recordings, speech analytics, CRM integrations, identity services, and agent-assistance tools.

#### 2.1 Define the business use case

The business use case should explain what the system is intended to accomplish, who will use it, and which activities are permitted.

A use-case statement for a telecommunications contact center might read:

> The organization intends to deploy an AI-powered contact center to assist customers with routine billing inquiries, subscription information, technical troubleshooting, service-ticket creation, and customer-service routing. The system may retrieve authorized customer information and execute specifically approved workflows through controlled enterprise integrations. Sensitive account changes, disputed transactions, suspected fraud, and other designated high-risk situations must follow defined authentication, approval, or human-escalation procedures.

The final wording must reflect the actual system design and approved business requirements.

#### 2.2 Define the system boundary

The scope should identify all relevant components, including:

- Telephony and digital customer channels.
- Interactive voice response and conversational interfaces.
- Speech recognition and speech synthesis services.
- AI orchestration and prompt-management components.
- AI models and external model APIs.
- CRM and customer account platforms.
- Knowledge management and retrieval systems.
- Customer identity and authentication services.
- Billing, order management, and service provisioning integrations.
- Call recording and transcription systems.
- Agent desktop and workforce-management platforms.
- Analytics, reporting, and quality-monitoring systems.
- Logging, monitoring, and incident-management services.
- Cloud infrastructure and third-party service providers.

The organization should document which components it operates directly, which are managed by suppliers, and which controls depend on external parties.

A service may be delivered through a single commercial platform while relying on multiple underlying providers. The governance assessment should account for the actual dependencies rather than treating the commercial platform as the entire system.

#### 2.3 Identify stakeholders

A typical enterprise deployment involves the following stakeholders.

| Stakeholder | Primary responsibilities | Governance interest |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Sponsors the business initiative and resolves strategic issues | Business value and material risk |
| Customer-service director | Owns service outcomes and operational requirements | Customer experience, service quality, and escalation |
| Contact center manager | Oversees daily operations and agent workflows | Availability, workload, and operational performance |
| AI system owner | Coordinates the AI service lifecycle and technical accountability | System integrity, performance, and change management |
| AI engineering team | Configures models, prompts, orchestration, and evaluations | Model behavior and technical reliability |
| Contact center platform team | Operates telephony, routing, and platform configuration | Service availability and platform security |
| Cybersecurity team | Defines and evaluates security requirements | Confidentiality, integrity, availability, and abuse prevention |
| Security operations center (SOC) | Monitors relevant security events and investigates incidents | Threat detection and response |
| Data protection officer or privacy function | Advises on applicable privacy obligations and monitors relevant compliance activities | Lawful processing, transparency, and individual rights |
| Legal and compliance teams | Evaluate applicable legal, regulatory, and contractual obligations | Compliance and legal exposure |
| Data owners | Define permitted use and access to enterprise datasets | Data integrity, access, and ownership |
| Knowledge-base owner | Approves and maintains customer-service content | Accuracy, relevance, and source integrity |
| CRM and application owners | Operate systems integrated with the AI service | Transaction integrity and authorization |
| Enterprise architecture | Reviews architecture and integration patterns | Architectural consistency and dependency management |
| Third-party risk management | Assesses external suppliers and service dependencies | Supplier assurance and concentration risk |
| Internal audit | Independently assesses governance and control effectiveness | Assurance over risk management and controls |
| Customer-service agents | Handle escalated interactions and use agent-assistance features | Practical usability and safe customer handling |
| Customers | Interact with the service and provide information | Privacy, fairness, transparency, and reliable assistance |

The organization should not assume that all stakeholders have the same authority. A cybersecurity team may assess a risk without owning the business decision to accept it. Similarly, a business owner may approve a use case but lack authority to waive mandatory security or legal requirements.

#### 2.4 Define accountability for customer-impacting decisions

Some chatbot decisions can directly affect customers, finances, or service availability.

Examples include:

- Suspending or restoring a service.
- Changing a subscription plan.
- Initiating a refund or account credit.
- Changing customer contact details.
- Updating billing arrangements.
- Applying a fraud-related restriction.
- Making a recommendation that affects a customer's access to a service.

The organization should classify these actions according to their impact and define the required safeguards.

A possible control structure is:

| Action category | Example | Illustrative control expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Explain a public tariff | Use approved information and provide an escalation path |
| Authenticated read-only | Retrieve a customer's bill | Verify identity and enforce server-side authorization |
| Low-impact operational | Create a support ticket | Validate required fields and record the action |
| Customer-account modification | Change a contact detail | Require appropriate authentication and transaction validation |
| Financial action | Issue a credit or refund | Enforce transaction limits, authorization, and additional approval where required |
| High-impact or sensitive action | Suspend service for suspected fraud | Apply approved decision rules and appropriate human oversight |
| Exceptional or disputed case | Resolve a contested billing adjustment | Route to an authorized human decision-maker |

These are illustrative categories. The organization must define its own thresholds, approval requirements, and prohibited actions based on its business processes and risk appetite.

#### 2.5 Establish explicit non-goals

The use-case definition should also identify activities the system is not authorized to perform.

Possible restrictions include:

- Accessing customer accounts without appropriate authentication.
- Revealing one customer's information to another customer.
- Making unauthorized account or billing changes.
- Overriding fraud controls or transaction limits.
- Inventing contractual terms, charges, or service commitments.
- Providing unapproved legal, financial, or other specialized advice.
- Making unsupported promises about service restoration.
- Bypassing mandatory human approval.
- Accessing internal systems outside the approved integration scope.
- Treating customer-provided instructions as authorization to override system controls.

These restrictions must be enforced through system architecture, application authorization, workflow design, and operational procedures. A prompt instructing the model not to perform an action is not a sufficient security boundary.

#### Core Principle

The business owner must define the intended use, authorized actions, prohibited activities, and customer-impact thresholds. These decisions establish the boundaries within which the AI system can be designed, tested, and operated.

### 3. AI Capabilities and Technology Components

AI-powered contact centers commonly combine several technologies. Each technology introduces distinct capabilities, dependencies, and potential risks.

Understanding the components is essential for governance professionals because the same customer interaction may pass through multiple AI services and enterprise applications before a response is delivered.

#### 3.1 Automatic speech recognition

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) converts spoken language into text.

In a voice-based contact center, ASR allows the system to interpret a customer's spoken request and provide text to downstream conversational components.

**Example:**

A customer says:

> "My broadband connection has been down since yesterday, and I want to know when it will be fixed."

The ASR component transcribes the speech. The conversational system then interprets the request, identifies the likely technical-support intent, and determines which information or workflow is required.

Potential risks include:

- Incorrect transcription of account numbers or names.
- Misinterpretation of accents, speech patterns, or noisy audio.
- Incorrect recognition of negation or critical details.
- Exposure of recordings or transcripts.
- Unintended retention of voice data.
- Unauthorized use of recordings for model training.

Governance controls should include appropriate accuracy testing, secure handling of recordings, access restrictions, retention rules, and human escalation where transcription uncertainty could affect a consequential action.

#### 3.2 Natural language understanding

Natural language understanding (NLU) interprets the meaning and intent of customer input.

For example, the phrases below may express a similar request:

- "Why is my bill higher this month?"
- "My invoice has increased."
- "I was charged more than usual."
- "Can you explain these extra charges?"

An NLU component may classify these statements as billing inquiries and identify relevant entities, such as invoice period or charge category.

Potential risks include:

- Incorrect intent classification.
- Failure to recognize ambiguous requests.
- Confusion between similar service operations.
- Incorrect extraction of account or transaction details.
- Poor performance for particular languages or customer groups.

A governance programme should define acceptable performance thresholds, test representative customer scenarios, and establish fallback behavior for uncertain classifications.

#### 3.3 Natural language generation and large language models

Natural language generation produces human-readable responses. Generative AI and large language models (LLMs) can create context-sensitive responses based on instructions, conversation history, retrieved information, and available tools.

For example, a customer may ask why an invoice includes an additional charge. An LLM may summarize relevant billing information and explain the charge in natural language.

However, fluent language does not establish factual correctness.

Potential failure modes include:

- Hallucinated billing explanations.
- Unsupported claims about customer entitlements.
- Incorrect interpretation of service policies.
- Disclosure of information from an unauthorized source.
- Inconsistent answers to equivalent questions.
- Manipulation through malicious instructions embedded in user input or retrieved content.

Controls should include approved knowledge sources, grounded-response requirements, server-side authorization, validation of consequential actions, and defined escalation procedures.

#### 3.4 Text-to-speech

Text-to-speech (TTS) converts text into spoken audio.

It enables a virtual agent to respond naturally to customers over telephone or voice-enabled digital channels.

Governance considerations include:

- Whether the spoken response accurately reflects the approved text.
- Whether the system clearly identifies itself as an automated service where appropriate.
- Whether sensitive information is spoken in a suitable context.
- Whether voice recordings or generated voice models are protected.
- Whether synthetic voice capabilities could be misused for impersonation.
- Whether customers can access an alternative interaction channel.

The organization should consider the risks of voice-based disclosure, particularly when calls may be overheard or when identity verification is incomplete.

#### 3.5 Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines information retrieval with generative AI.

Rather than relying exclusively on information learned during model training, a RAG system retrieves relevant content from approved knowledge sources and supplies it to the model as context for generating an answer.

A contact center may use RAG to retrieve:

- Product descriptions.
- Tariff information.
- Troubleshooting procedures.
- Customer-service policies.
- Frequently asked questions.
- Approved service-disruption information.
- Internal agent guidance.

RAG can improve the relevance of answers, but it does not guarantee accuracy or security.

Potential risks include:

- Retrieval of outdated policy documents.
- Inclusion of unauthorized or malicious content.
- Retrieval of information belonging to another customer.
- Excessive exposure of internal instructions.
- Incorrect ranking of retrieved information.
- Generation of unsupported claims from incomplete evidence.

Controls should address source approval, document ownership, versioning, access-control enforcement, retrieval testing, and protection against malicious content.

#### 3.6 AI agents and tool calling

An AI agent may use tools to perform tasks beyond generating text.

For example, a contact center AI agent may be able to:

1. Authenticate a customer through an approved identity workflow.
2. Retrieve the customer's service status.
3. Query billing information.
4. Create a support ticket.
5. Provide the ticket reference.
6. Escalate the interaction if a defined condition is met.

This capability introduces additional risk because the AI system can influence real business operations.

The organization should distinguish between the model proposing an action and the application actually authorizing and executing that action.

A secure design should ensure that:

- Each tool has a defined purpose.
- Tool permissions follow least privilege.
- Customer identity is verified through an appropriate mechanism.
- Backend systems enforce authorization independently.
- Transaction parameters are validated.
- Sensitive actions are subject to appropriate approval.
- Tool execution is logged.
- High-risk operations can be restricted or disabled.
- The model cannot expand its own permissions.

A prompt stating that the model must not issue refunds does not prevent a refund if the application exposes an unrestricted refund API. The application and backend must enforce the business rule.

#### 3.7 Conversation analytics and sentiment analysis

Contact centers may use AI to classify conversations, identify recurring problems, estimate customer sentiment, summarize interactions, and detect potential service-quality issues.

Potential uses include:

- Identifying repeated complaints about a network outage.
- Detecting customers who request escalation.
- Summarizing reasons for customer contact.
- Identifying frequently misunderstood product information.
- Reviewing whether agents follow approved procedures.
- Detecting trends in unresolved service issues.

Potential risks include inaccurate classifications, bias, inappropriate employee monitoring, overinterpretation of sentiment, and secondary use of personal data.

The organization should define the intended purpose, permitted users, evaluation methodology, retention period, and restrictions on consequential decisions based on analytics.

Sentiment analysis should not automatically be treated as a reliable measure of a customer's emotional state or as sufficient evidence for a significant decision about a customer or employee.

#### 3.8 Technology-component inventory

A practical inventory can help the organization identify which capabilities are present and who is responsible for them.

| Component | Primary function | Key security or governance concern | Example control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telephony platform | Connects customer calls | Call interception, routing failure, and availability | Access control, encryption, and resilience |
| ASR service | Converts speech to text | Transcription errors and recording exposure | Accuracy testing and recording protection |
| NLU component | Classifies intent | Incorrect routing or interpretation | Scenario-based testing and fallback |
| LLM service | Generates responses | Hallucination, prompt injection, and disclosure | Approved context, output evaluation, and data controls |
| TTS service | Converts text to speech | Inaccurate spoken output and voice misuse | Output validation and appropriate voice controls |
| RAG service | Retrieves relevant content | Unauthorized retrieval and malicious content | Source governance and access-aware retrieval |
| CRM integration | Retrieves or updates customer records | Unauthorized access or modification | Backend authorization and transaction validation |
| Billing integration | Retrieves billing information or initiates approved actions | Financial error and unauthorized transaction | Least privilege, limits, and approval controls |
| Identity service | Establishes customer identity | Account takeover and authentication failure | Risk-appropriate authentication and session controls |
| Analytics platform | Evaluates interaction patterns | Excessive data use and misleading conclusions | Purpose limitation, access controls, and validation |
| Agent desktop | Supports human agents | Exposure of customer data and incorrect summaries | Role-based access and human review |
| Monitoring platform | Detects service and security events | Blind spots and inadequate alerting | Tested telemetry and response procedures |

#### Core Principle

Each AI technology component must be evaluated according to its function and failure modes. Governance should cover the complete interaction chain rather than concentrating only on the language model.

### 4. Enterprise Architecture and End-to-End Interaction Flow

The enterprise architecture of an AI-powered contact center determines how customer input is received, interpreted, processed, and translated into responses or business actions.

The architecture also determines where authentication, authorization, data validation, logging, and other controls must be enforced.

A governance assessment should therefore examine both the logical components and the trust boundaries between them.

#### 4.2 Architectural trust boundaries

A trust boundary separates components or environments with different security assumptions or levels of authority.

Common trust boundaries in an AI-powered contact center include:

**Boundary 1 — Customer to contact center**

Customer input is untrusted. The system must handle malformed requests, abusive content, attempts to manipulate the AI, and potentially malicious attachments or links.

**Boundary 2 — Contact center to AI provider**

Conversation data may be sent to an externally operated model service. The organization must understand what information is transmitted, how it is processed, what retention applies, and which contractual and technical safeguards are available.

**Boundary 3 — AI orchestration to knowledge repository**

Retrieved documents may influence model output. The system must control which sources can be queried and whether the requesting user or session is authorized to access the retrieved content.

**Boundary 4 — AI system to enterprise applications**

The model or orchestration layer may request information or actions from CRM, billing, ticketing, or provisioning systems. These integrations must not rely solely on model instructions for authorization.

**Boundary 5 — Contact center to human agent**

Escalated conversations and generated summaries may be transferred to agents. The system must ensure that agents receive the appropriate information and that generated summaries are not mistaken for verified facts.

**Boundary 6 — Operational systems to monitoring and analytics**

Logs, transcripts, and analytics records may contain sensitive information. Monitoring and analytics systems therefore require appropriate access controls, retention, and protection against unauthorized disclosure.

#### 4.3 End-to-end customer interaction

Consider a customer who contacts a telecommunications operator because the latest invoice appears higher than expected.

The interaction may follow this sequence:

1. The customer initiates a voice call or opens a digital chat.
2. The contact center establishes a session.
3. If voice is used, the speech-recognition service transcribes the customer's request.
4. The conversational system identifies the request as a billing inquiry.
5. The system determines whether authentication is required before retrieving account-specific information.
6. An approved identity workflow verifies the customer.
7. The application checks the customer's authorization to access the requested billing records.
8. The retrieval or billing integration obtains the permitted information.
9. The AI system uses approved information to prepare an explanation.
10. The response is checked against relevant business rules and available evidence.
11. The customer receives the response through chat or speech.
12. If the request is disputed, ambiguous, or outside the AI's authority, the interaction is escalated to a human agent.
13. Relevant security and operational events are recorded.
14. The interaction is handled according to applicable data-retention and access policies.

Not every interaction requires every step. Public product questions, for example, may not require customer authentication. The sequence must be adapted to the data sensitivity and business action involved.

#### 4.4 Security control placement

Controls should be placed at the appropriate architectural layer.

| Control | Appropriate enforcement point | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Input validation | Customer interface and application gateway | Reduce malformed or inappropriate input before processing |
| Authentication | Approved identity and session-management services | Establish identity using the appropriate verification process |
| Authorization | Backend application and API layer | Enforce permissions independently of model output |
| Data minimization | Data-flow and orchestration layers | Limit information transmitted to AI services |
| Retrieval access control | Retrieval service and source repository | Prevent unauthorized document access |
| Tool permission control | Tool gateway and backend API | Restrict available actions and enforce permissions |
| Transaction validation | Business application and transaction service | Ensure requests meet business rules and limits |
| Output validation | Application response layer where appropriate | Detect prohibited or unsupported response patterns |
| Human approval | Business workflow | Require authorized decisions for designated high-impact actions |
| Audit logging | Application, integration, identity, and security layers | Preserve traceability of relevant events |
| Security monitoring | Central monitoring and incident-management services | Detect suspicious activity and support investigation |
| Change control | Model, prompt, configuration, integration, and release processes | Prevent unreviewed changes from undermining safeguards |

No single control layer is sufficient for every risk.

For example, input filtering may reduce certain malicious prompts, but it cannot replace backend authorization. Similarly, output filtering may identify some prohibited responses, but it cannot guarantee that a billing transaction is correct.

#### 4.5 Architecture review questions

A governance or cybersecurity reviewer should ask:

1. Which components process customer data?
2. Which components are operated by external providers?
3. Does customer information leave the organization's controlled environment?
4. Which components can access customer-specific records?
5. Where is authentication performed?
6. Which system enforces authorization for each backend operation?
7. Can the model invoke tools directly, or must it pass through a controlled application layer?
8. Can retrieved content contain untrusted instructions?
9. Are retrieval permissions aligned with the customer's or agent's authorization?
10. Are generated summaries stored in the CRM, and can errors become persistent customer-record inaccuracies?
11. Which events are logged, and where are logs stored?
12. Can the organization disable an unsafe AI capability without disabling essential customer-service operations?
13. What happens if the model provider, telephony service, or backend application becomes unavailable?
14. How are new model versions and changes to prompts, tools, and data sources reviewed?

These questions should be answered using architecture documentation, implementation evidence, and testing rather than assumptions.

#### Core Principle

The architecture must ensure that trust boundaries, authorization decisions, and business controls are enforced by the appropriate systems. The AI model may interpret requests and propose actions, but enterprise applications must retain control over access to protected data and execution of consequential operations.

### 5. Data Classification, Processing, and Information Flows

AI-powered contact centers may process significantly more information than is apparent from the final customer response.

A single voice interaction may generate an audio recording, transcript, intent classification, conversation history, model input, retrieved documents, generated summary, CRM entry, and quality-monitoring record.

Each artifact may have a different purpose, sensitivity level, access requirement, and retention period.

Data governance must therefore address the entire information lifecycle rather than focusing only on the information visible to the customer.

#### 5.1 Common data categories

| Data category | Examples | Potential sensitivity | Governance considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public business information | Published tariffs and product descriptions | Generally low | Accuracy, version control, and source integrity |
| Customer identity data | Name, address, customer identifier | Potentially high | Access control, data minimization, and privacy |
| Authentication information | Verification responses, session tokens | High | Secure handling, limited retention, and protection against account takeover |
| Billing information | Invoice amounts, charges, payment status | High | Authorization, confidentiality, and transaction integrity |
| Telecommunications service data | Subscription details, service status, usage information | Context-dependent and potentially high | Purpose limitation, access restrictions, and applicable sector rules |
| Voice recordings | Recorded telephone conversations | Context-dependent | Recording notices, access, retention, and permitted use |
| Transcripts | Text generated from conversations | Context-dependent | Accuracy, confidentiality, and retention |
| Conversation history | Previous questions and responses | Context-dependent | Session isolation and controls against cross-customer disclosure |
| AI-generated summaries | Agent handover notes and interaction summaries | Context-dependent | Accuracy checks and correction mechanisms |
| Knowledge documents | Internal procedures, product policies, troubleshooting guides | May be confidential | Document classification, source approval, and access control |
| Operational metadata | Session identifiers, timestamps, routing information | Context-dependent | Monitoring purpose, access, and retention |
| Analytics data | Sentiment indicators, intent categories, interaction statistics | Context-dependent | Purpose limitation, validation, and appropriate aggregation |
| Security telemetry | Authentication failures, tool calls, policy violations | Potentially sensitive | Restricted access and investigation controls |
| Payment information | Payment references or transaction details | High | Applicable payment-security requirements and strict data minimization |

Classification should be based on the actual content, context, applicable requirements, and consequences of disclosure. A transcript may contain several categories of data and should not automatically be treated as low sensitivity because it is stored as text.

#### 5.2 Data lifecycle

A typical data lifecycle includes the following stages:

Controls must apply throughout the lifecycle.

For example, a system may protect a transcript during storage but still expose it by sending unnecessary personal information to an external model provider. Conversely, data may be properly minimized at inference time but retained indefinitely in application logs.

The assessment should identify each location where data is collected, transmitted, transformed, stored, accessed, and deleted.

#### 5.3 Data minimization

Data minimization means processing only the information necessary for the specified purpose.

For example, if a customer asks for an explanation of a published tariff, the system generally should not need to retrieve the customer's full billing history, authentication details, or historical call recordings.

If the customer asks about a specific invoice, the system may need to retrieve relevant account and billing information after the required authorization checks.

A practical review should examine:

- Whether the AI receives unnecessary customer identifiers.
- Whether full transcripts are sent when a short summary would suffice.
- Whether authentication secrets can enter prompts or transcripts.
- Whether logs contain complete personal information unnecessarily.
- Whether third-party services receive data beyond the approved purpose.
- Whether conversation history persists longer than necessary.
- Whether analytics datasets can be appropriately aggregated or de-identified.

Data minimization should be implemented through architecture and configuration, not merely described in a policy.

#### 5.4 Data residency and third-party processing

Cloud-based contact center services may process data across multiple locations or involve several providers.

The organization should determine:

- Where voice recordings and transcripts are processed.
- Where AI inference takes place.
- Where prompts and model outputs may be stored.
- Where customer records and analytics data reside.
- Whether subprocessors are involved.
- Whether data may be transferred across jurisdictions.
- Which contractual commitments apply to retention and deletion.
- Whether provider use of customer data for model training is permitted, prohibited, or subject to specific conditions.
- Whether the organization can obtain sufficient evidence about the provider's data-handling practices.

The answers depend on the actual service configuration and contract. An organization should not assume that choosing a regional cloud environment automatically guarantees that every supporting AI service processes all data exclusively in that region.

#### 5.5 Retention and deletion

Different records may require different retention periods.

For example:

- Call recordings may be subject to defined customer-service, legal, or regulatory retention requirements.
- Security logs may need to be retained for investigation and assurance.
- Conversation transcripts may have a shorter retention period than formal customer records.
- Temporary prompts and intermediate model outputs may not require long-term storage.
- CRM summaries may need to follow the retention rules of the underlying customer record.
- Test datasets may require separate retention and deletion procedures.

The organization should document the purpose, retention rule, owner, storage location, and deletion mechanism for each relevant record type.

Where a legal hold or other lawful retention requirement applies, deletion processes should account for that requirement.

#### 5.6 Data quality and integrity

AI-powered contact centers may transform information several times before it reaches a customer or enterprise application.

Potential errors include:

- Incorrect speech transcription.
- Incorrect extraction of a billing amount.
- Summaries that omit an important customer statement.
- Misclassification of a complaint.
- Incorrect retrieval of a policy version.
- AI-generated content that changes the meaning of source information.

The organization should identify which outputs may become authoritative business records and define appropriate validation requirements.

For example, a generated interaction summary may be useful to an agent, but a material billing adjustment should not be authorized solely because the summary claims that a customer is eligible.

Where generated content is stored in CRM or another system of record, the organization should define how errors are detected, corrected, and traced.

#### Core Principle

Data governance must cover all information generated or processed throughout the contact center lifecycle. The organization should classify data by sensitivity, minimize unnecessary processing, control external transfers, define retention requirements, and protect the integrity of records used for customer-impacting decisions.

### 6. AI Risk Landscape for Contact Centers

AI-powered contact centers combine conventional information-security and operational risks with AI-specific risks arising from probabilistic model behavior, natural-language interaction, retrieval, and tool execution.

The organization should maintain a risk register that reflects its actual architecture and use cases rather than relying on a generic list of AI threats.

#### 6.1 Principal risk categories

| Risk category | Example scenario | Potential impact | Example treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized data disclosure | The chatbot returns another customer's billing details | Privacy breach, regulatory exposure, and customer harm | Backend authorization, session isolation, and access testing |
| Prompt injection | A malicious customer attempts to override instructions or misuse tools | Policy bypass or unauthorized information access | Constrained tool permissions, trusted workflow design, and adversarial testing |
| Hallucination | The AI invents a fee explanation or incorrectly describes a service policy | Customer misinformation and financial disputes | Approved sources, evaluation, and escalation |
| Unauthorized transaction | A model-generated request initiates an unapproved account change | Financial loss or service disruption | Server-side validation, transaction limits, and approval controls |
| Identity fraud | An attacker impersonates a customer | Account takeover or unauthorized disclosure | Risk-appropriate authentication and fraud controls |
| Knowledge-base poisoning | Malicious or inaccurate content is published to a retrieval source | Systematic misinformation or manipulation | Source governance, approval, integrity checks, and change monitoring |
| Model or provider outage | The external AI service becomes unavailable | Reduced service capacity or customer-service disruption | Fallback procedures and continuity planning |
| Integration compromise | An exposed API credential is used to access backend systems | Unauthorized data access or transaction execution | Secret management, scoped credentials, and rotation |
| Excessive data retention | Transcripts and prompts remain available beyond approved periods | Increased exposure and compliance risk | Retention enforcement and deletion validation |
| Inadequate monitoring | Suspicious tool calls are not detected | Delayed containment and investigation | Security telemetry, alerting, and response procedures |
| Biased or uneven performance | Speech recognition performs poorly for certain accents or languages | Unequal service quality and potential customer harm | Representative testing and performance monitoring |
| Inaccurate agent summaries | Generated notes omit a material complaint or invent a detail | Incorrect follow-up and corrupted customer records | Source traceability, human review, and correction processes |
| Third-party dependency | A provider changes service behavior or contractual terms | Control gaps, cost changes, or service disruption | Supplier governance and change notification requirements |
| Excessive AI autonomy | The AI can execute high-impact workflows without appropriate authorization | Uncontrolled customer or business impact | Bounded permissions, approval gates, and action restrictions |
| Misuse of interaction analytics | Sentiment scores are used as definitive evidence of customer behavior | Unfair treatment or inappropriate decisions | Purpose limitation, validation, and human review |

#### 6.2 Inherent risk and residual risk

Inherent risk is the exposure before considering the effect of controls.

Residual risk is the exposure that remains after relevant controls and risk treatments have been considered.

For example:

**Risk scenario:** An AI-powered contact center retrieves customer billing records through an API.

- **Inherent risk:** A vulnerability in identity or authorization handling could expose customer billing information.
- **Control design:** Authentication, server-side authorization, least-privilege API access, logging, and monitoring.
- **Control validation:** Testing verifies that one customer cannot retrieve another customer's records by changing an account identifier.
- **Residual risk:** The remaining exposure depends on the effectiveness of the controls, the architecture, the test evidence, and the surrounding threat environment.

The existence of a control does not automatically justify a lower risk rating. The organization should assess whether the control is implemented and effective.

#### 6.3 Risk assessment dimensions

A practical assessment may consider:

- Confidentiality impact.
- Integrity impact.
- Availability impact.
- Customer harm.
- Financial impact.
- Legal and regulatory exposure.
- Reputational impact.
- Operational disruption.
- Scope of potential exposure.
- Likelihood of exploitation or failure.
- Detectability.
- Effectiveness of existing controls.
- Reversibility of potential actions.
- Degree of AI autonomy.

The assessment method should be consistent with the organization's risk-management framework.

Where appropriate, risks should be assessed at both the overall system level and the individual use-case level. A system may have acceptable risk for public product questions but unacceptable risk for autonomous billing adjustments.

#### 6.4 Risk ownership and treatment

Each material risk should have:

1. A unique identifier.
2. A clearly defined risk scenario.
3. An identified business impact.
4. An assessed likelihood and impact.
5. An assigned risk owner.
6. Existing control descriptions.
7. Control effectiveness evidence.
8. A residual-risk rating.
9. A treatment decision.
10. Remediation actions and deadlines.
11. An approval record where required.
12. A review trigger or review date.

Treatment options may include mitigation, avoidance, transfer where appropriate, or acceptance by an authorized risk owner.

Acceptance should not be used to bypass mandatory legal requirements, non-negotiable security standards, or required approval processes.

#### 6.5 Illustrative risk register entries

| Risk ID | Risk scenario | Inherent risk | Key controls | Evidence required | Residual-risk decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CC-AI-01 | Unauthorized disclosure of customer billing data | High | Authentication, server-side authorization, session isolation | Cross-customer access tests and logs | Assign rating after testing |
| CC-AI-02 | AI provides an incorrect billing explanation | Medium–High | Approved knowledge, grounding, evaluation, and escalation | Accuracy tests and sample reviews | Assign rating after evaluation |
| CC-AI-03 | Prompt injection attempts to invoke unauthorized tools | High | Restricted tool permissions, application validation, and adversarial testing | Red-team report and authorization tests | Assign rating after testing |
| CC-AI-04 | Inaccurate transcript or summary affects a customer record | Medium | Quality validation, traceability, and correction process | Transcription and summary evaluation | Assign rating after evaluation |
| CC-AI-05 | AI provider outage disrupts customer-service operations | Medium–High | Fallback routing, operational runbooks, and resilience testing | Failover exercise and service continuity evidence | Assign rating after testing |
| CC-AI-06 | Malicious knowledge content influences AI responses | High | Source approval, controlled publishing, and integrity monitoring | Content-change records and retrieval tests | Assign rating after testing |
| CC-AI-07 | Excessive personal data is sent to an external AI service | High | Data minimization, configuration controls, and provider review | Data-flow validation and supplier evidence | Assign rating after assessment |
| CC-AI-08 | Unauthorized account changes occur through AI tool execution | High | Least privilege, server-side business rules, transaction limits, and approval controls | Integration tests and transaction audit records | Assign rating after testing |

The ratings are illustrative starting points, not a completed risk assessment. Actual ratings must be based on the deployed architecture, data sensitivity, threat exposure, control effectiveness, and the organization's approved methodology.

#### Core Principle

AI contact center risk management must connect realistic failure scenarios to accountable owners, effective controls, and verifiable evidence. Risk ratings should reflect the actual deployment and should not be reduced merely because a control is documented or a vendor claims that a safeguard exists.

### 7. AI Governance and Applicable Standards

AI-powered contact centers may be subject to multiple layers of governance, including internal policies, cybersecurity standards, privacy requirements, consumer-protection obligations, telecommunications rules, contractual commitments, and AI-specific regulatory requirements.

The applicable requirements depend on the organization's location, customer population, deployment model, use of personal data, business activities, and the functions performed by the AI system.

A contact center should therefore use an applicability assessment rather than assume that one standard or certification addresses every relevant obligation.

#### 7.1 Governance layers

**Layer 1 — Enterprise governance**

Defines business ownership, risk appetite, delegated authority, policy requirements, and escalation procedures.

**Layer 2 — AI governance**

Addresses AI system inventory, use-case approval, lifecycle oversight, evaluation, human oversight, and management of model-related risks.

**Layer 3 — Cybersecurity governance**

Addresses identity and access management, secure architecture, vulnerability management, logging, incident response, resilience, and third-party security.

**Layer 4 — Data governance and privacy**

Addresses personal-data processing, data minimization, access, transparency, retention, data quality, and individual rights where applicable.

**Layer 5 — Operational governance**

Addresses service availability, business continuity, human escalation, change management, service quality, and operational accountability.

**Layer 6 — Independent assurance**

Provides appropriately independent evaluation of whether governance arrangements and controls are designed and operating effectively.

These layers should work together. For example, an AI policy may require human oversight, while the application architecture, operating procedures, and monitoring controls establish how that oversight works in practice.

#### 7.2 Relevant standards and frameworks

The following are potential references for a contact center governance programme.

| Framework or standard | Primary focus | Potential application |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI management systems | Establishing organizational processes for governing AI systems |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information security management systems | Managing information-security risks and controls |
| ISO/IEC 27002 | Information security control guidance | Supporting control design and implementation |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework | AI risk-management activities | Structuring AI risk identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework | Cybersecurity risk-management outcomes | Organizing cybersecurity governance and operational capabilities |
| ISO/IEC 27701 | Privacy information management | Supporting privacy governance where applicable to the organization's implementation and chosen conformity approach |
| Applicable data-protection legislation | Personal-data processing and individual rights | Evaluating lawful processing, transparency, data-subject rights, security, and related obligations |
| Applicable telecommunications regulation | Sector-specific obligations | Evaluating customer protection, service requirements, and relevant sector rules |
| Applicable payment-security requirements | Protection of payment account data | Defining required safeguards when the contact center handles relevant payment data |
| Internal AI and security policies | Enterprise-specific requirements | Translating risk appetite and obligations into enforceable organizational rules |

The presence of a framework in this table does not establish that it applies to every contact center or that the organization is compliant with it.

#### 7.3 EU regulatory considerations

For an organization operating in Spain or elsewhere in the European Union, the compliance assessment should consider relevant EU and national requirements.

Potential areas of analysis include:

- The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), where personal data is processed.
- Applicable provisions of the EU AI Act, based on the system's intended purpose, classification, role of the organization, and applicable implementation dates.
- Consumer-protection and contractual obligations.
- Applicable telecommunications and electronic-communications rules.
- Rules governing recordings, communications confidentiality, and employee monitoring where relevant.
- Applicable cybersecurity and incident-reporting requirements.
- Contractual requirements imposed by customers, partners, and suppliers.

The organization should determine applicability through documented legal and compliance review. It should not assume that every customer-service chatbot is automatically classified as a high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act. Classification depends on the system's intended purpose and the applicable legal criteria.

Similarly, the use of generative AI does not automatically remove the need to assess conventional privacy, cybersecurity, and consumer-protection obligations.

#### 7.4 Translate requirements into controls

Governance frameworks should be translated into operational requirements.

For example:

| Governance requirement | Operational interpretation | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Defined AI accountability | Assign a business owner and system owner | Approved inventory and responsibility matrix |
| AI risk management | Assess AI-specific and conventional technology risks | Risk register and treatment plans |
| Secure system design | Review trust boundaries and integration permissions | Architecture assessment |
| Data protection | Minimize and appropriately protect customer data | Data-flow records and configuration evidence |
| Human oversight | Define when the AI must transfer control to an authorized human | Escalation rules and test results |
| Third-party governance | Evaluate relevant supplier risks and contractual commitments | Supplier assessment and contract review |
| Monitoring | Detect and investigate relevant security and operational events | Logging configuration and alert tests |
| Change management | Evaluate material changes before deployment | Change approvals and regression-test results |
| Accountability and assurance | Preserve evidence of decisions and control operation | Audit records and assurance reports |

The organization should map the applicable requirements to specific controls, responsible owners, evidence, and review mechanisms.

#### 7.5 Governance review questions

Before approving the contact center, reviewers should be able to establish:

1. Has the business use case been formally approved?
2. Has the AI system been inventoried and classified?
3. Have applicable legal and regulatory requirements been assessed?
4. Are the system's permitted actions clearly defined?
5. Are customer-data processing and external-provider dependencies documented?
6. Are AI and cybersecurity risks assessed through an approved method?
7. Are relevant controls mapped to risks and requirements?
8. Has the system been tested against its intended operating scenarios?
9. Are required human-oversight mechanisms operational?
10. Are residual risks accepted by the appropriate authority?
11. Are production approval and ongoing assurance arrangements documented?

#### Core Principle

Governance frameworks provide structure, but effective governance depends on translating applicable requirements into specific responsibilities, controls, and evidence. The organization must document why requirements apply, how they are implemented, and how their effectiveness is verified.

### 8. Foundational Governance Deliverables and Initial Assessment

Before an AI-powered contact center enters detailed implementation or production approval, the organization should assemble a foundational set of governance deliverables.

These documents create a common understanding among business stakeholders, technology teams, cybersecurity, privacy, compliance, and assurance functions.

They also provide the baseline against which later design decisions, testing results, risk treatments, and operational changes can be evaluated.

#### 8.1 Foundational deliverables

| Deliverable | Purpose | Minimum content |
|---|---|---|
| Business use-case assessment | Defines the intended purpose and boundaries | Business objective, users, capabilities, exclusions, and success criteria |
| AI system inventory record | Establishes system ownership and classification | System name, owner, model/provider, deployment context, integrations, and status |
| Stakeholder register | Identifies responsible and consulted functions | Roles, responsibilities, decision authority, and escalation contacts |
| High-level architecture | Documents system components and trust boundaries | Channels, AI services, repositories, integrations, and control points |
| Data-flow inventory | Identifies information movement | Data categories, source, destination, purpose, transfer, and retention |
| Preliminary risk register | Records material threats and uncertainties | Risk scenarios, owners, ratings, controls, and treatment actions |
| Initial privacy assessment | Screens for relevant privacy implications | Data subjects, processing purposes, data categories, and required follow-up |
| Supplier inventory | Identifies external dependencies | Providers, services, data access, criticality, and assurance status |
| Governance RACI | Allocates responsibilities | Responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed parties |
| Security requirements baseline | Defines minimum security expectations | Identity, access, data protection, integration, logging, and incident requirements |
| AI evaluation plan | Defines how behavior will be evaluated | Use cases, test scenarios, quality measures, security tests, and acceptance criteria |
| Production decision criteria | Establishes approval expectations | Required evidence, risk thresholds, decision authority, and prohibited conditions |

The content should be proportionate to the system's complexity and impact. A high-autonomy AI agent with access to billing and provisioning systems requires more detailed assessment than an informational assistant limited to public product information.

#### 8.2 Initial assessment questionnaire

The following questions can be used during discovery workshops with business owners, solution architects, security teams, privacy specialists, and contact center operations.

**Business purpose and autonomy**

1. What customer problem is the AI contact center intended to solve?
2. Which interactions will it handle independently?
3. Which interactions require a human agent?
4. Can the AI initiate account changes, financial transactions, or service-provisioning actions?
5. What activities are explicitly prohibited?

**Data and privacy**

6. Which categories of customer information will the system process?
7. Will voice recordings or transcripts be retained?
8. Will conversation content be sent to an external AI provider?
9. Which provider retention and training conditions apply?
10. How are access, retention, and deletion requirements enforced?

**Architecture and integration**

11. Which enterprise applications can the AI access?
12. How are customer identity and authorization verified?
13. Are backend permissions enforced independently of the model?
14. Which data sources can the retrieval system query?
15. Can the organization disable an unsafe tool or integration independently?

**AI security and evaluation**

16. Has the system been tested for prompt injection and unauthorized tool use?
17. How is the accuracy of generated answers measured?
18. How are hallucinations and unsupported claims handled?
19. How is malicious or outdated knowledge content prevented from influencing responses?
20. What evidence demonstrates that security controls work as intended?

**Operations and accountability**

21. Who owns the business risk?
22. Who operates the service and maintains its controls?
23. Who can approve exceptions and residual-risk acceptance?
24. How are incidents detected, escalated, and investigated?
25. What fallback arrangements exist if the AI service becomes unavailable?

**Compliance and assurance**

26. Which legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements apply?
27. Is a privacy impact assessment or another formal assessment required?
28. Which frameworks and internal policies are in scope?
29. What evidence must be available for audit?
30. Who authorizes production deployment?

#### 8.3 How to interpret discovery findings

The discovery workshop should not be treated as a simple checklist exercise.

Answers should be classified into three categories:

**Confirmed**

The organization has sufficient documentation or evidence to support the answer.

**Partially confirmed**

The answer is known in principle, but implementation details, ownership, or supporting evidence remain incomplete.

**Unknown or unresolved**

The organization lacks sufficient information to make a reliable decision.

Unknowns are not necessarily control failures, but they create uncertainty that must be managed.

For example, if the project team cannot confirm whether the external model provider retains prompts, the organization should not assume that retention is disabled. The issue should be assigned to an owner for contractual and technical verification.

Likewise, if the team cannot establish whether a billing API independently enforces customer-level authorization, this should be treated as an unresolved architectural risk until the design and implementation are verified.

#### 8.4 Initial governance assessment record

A practical assessment record may use the following structure:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Assessment ID | Unique identifier for traceability |
| System name | Name of the AI-powered contact center |
| Business owner | Accountable owner of the intended business use |
| Technical owner | Owner responsible for system implementation and operation |
| Assessment date | Date of the assessment |
| Deployment stage | Discovery, design, pilot, production, or material change |
| Intended use | Approved or proposed business purpose |
| AI capabilities | Speech recognition, generative AI, RAG, analytics, tool calling, or other capabilities |
| Data categories | Types of information processed |
| External dependencies | AI providers, cloud services, and connected systems |
| Preliminary risk level | Initial rating using the organization's approved methodology |
| Unresolved questions | Material unknowns and dependencies |
| Required actions | Remediation or investigation tasks |
| Action owners | Individuals or roles accountable for completion |
| Decision authority | Person or committee authorized to make the relevant decision |
| Review date | Planned reassessment date or trigger |

This record should be maintained as a controlled governance artifact and updated when the system's scope, architecture, data use, or operating conditions change.

#### Core Principle

The foundational assessment establishes the evidence baseline for governing an AI-powered contact center. Unknowns should be recorded transparently, assigned to accountable owners, and resolved or formally addressed before they undermine material security, privacy, or production decisions.

## Part 1 — Core Principle and Conceptual Distinctions

An AI-powered contact center should be treated as an enterprise business service whose behavior depends on AI models, customer channels, data repositories, identity systems, backend applications, external providers, and human operating procedures.

Its governance must therefore address the full service lifecycle rather than the AI model in isolation.

The organization should establish the business purpose, permitted actions, system boundaries, stakeholder responsibilities, data-processing requirements, risk landscape, and applicable governance obligations before making consequential deployment decisions.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **AI capability versus AI autonomy:** A system may use advanced generative AI without having permission to execute business transactions. Autonomy depends on the decisions and actions the system is authorized to perform.
- **Model output versus business authorization:** A model can recommend an action, but the appropriate application and backend controls must determine whether that action is permitted.
- **Authentication versus authorization:** Authentication establishes or verifies identity; authorization determines what the identified user or service may access or do.
- **Conversation data versus system-of-record data:** A transcript or generated summary is not automatically an authoritative customer record. Its reliability and permitted use must be established.
- **Retrieval versus access control:** Retrieving relevant content does not establish that the requester is authorized to see it. Access restrictions must be enforced independently.
- **AI governance versus cybersecurity governance:** AI governance addresses the responsible management of AI capabilities and risks, while cybersecurity governance protects systems, data, and operations. The two must be integrated.
- **Framework mapping versus compliance:** Mapping a control to a standard or regulation does not prove that the requirement applies, that the control is implemented, or that it operates effectively.
- **Inherent risk versus residual risk:** Inherent risk is assessed before controls are considered; residual risk reflects the exposure remaining after relevant controls and treatments are evaluated.
- **Policy instruction versus technical enforcement:** Telling an AI model not to perform an action is not equivalent to preventing that action through application permissions, backend authorization, and transaction controls.
- **Documentation versus evidence:** A documented control describes the intended safeguard; implementation and test evidence demonstrate whether it is present and effective.

**The central governance principle is that every AI-powered contact center must have clearly defined operating boundaries, accountable owners, controlled data flows, enforceable security safeguards, and a risk assessment proportionate to the customer impact and level of automation.**
