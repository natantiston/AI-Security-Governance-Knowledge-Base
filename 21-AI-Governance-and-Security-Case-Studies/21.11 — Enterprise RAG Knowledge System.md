# 21.11 — Enterprise RAG Knowledge System
## Part 1: Business Context, System Architecture, Use Cases, and Governance Foundations

### 1. Introduction to Enterprise Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Systems

A Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system combines information retrieval with a generative AI model to produce responses grounded in information retrieved from approved knowledge sources.

Instead of relying exclusively on information learned during model training, a RAG system retrieves relevant content from enterprise documents, databases, knowledge repositories, and other authorized information sources. The retrieved content is then supplied to the language model as context for generating a response.

Enterprise RAG systems are increasingly used to help employees locate information, understand policies, analyze technical documentation, troubleshoot operational issues, and access organizational knowledge through natural-language interfaces.

However, connecting generative AI to enterprise information introduces security and governance challenges. A system may retrieve confidential information for an unauthorized user, rely on manipulated documents, follow malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content, or generate incorrect conclusions from incomplete evidence.

For this reason, an enterprise RAG system must be governed as a complete information-processing system rather than merely as a chatbot.

#### 1.1 What Is Retrieval-Augmented Generation?

RAG is an architecture in which an AI system retrieves relevant information from an external knowledge source and uses that information to support the generation of a response.

A typical RAG workflow contains the following stages:

1. A user submits a question.
2. The system processes the question into a retrieval query.
3. The retrieval component searches approved knowledge sources.
4. Relevant document passages are selected.
5. Retrieved passages are assembled into a context for the AI model.
6. The model generates a response based on the question and retrieved context.
7. The application applies applicable output controls.
8. The response is returned to the user.
9. Relevant retrieval, access, and generation events are recorded for monitoring and investigation.

The retrieval process may use keyword search, semantic search, vector similarity, metadata filters, or a combination of these methods.

A RAG system can improve the relevance and traceability of responses, but it does not guarantee that the retrieved information is correct, authorized, complete, or interpreted properly.

#### 1.2 Traditional Knowledge Search vs. Enterprise RAG

| Dimension | Traditional Enterprise Search | Enterprise RAG System |
|---|---|---|
| User interaction | Search terms, filters, and document browsing | Natural-language questions and conversational interaction |
| Primary output | Links, document titles, and excerpts | Generated answers supported by retrieved context |
| Information retrieval | Keyword, metadata, or other search methods | Keyword, semantic, vector, or hybrid retrieval |
| Content interpretation | Primarily performed by the user | Partly performed by the AI model |
| Source visibility | Usually displays search results and document locations | May cite or reference retrieved sources, depending on design |
| Answer generation | Generally not required | Central function |
| Hallucination risk | Search can return irrelevant or misleading results | Model may generate unsupported or incorrect statements |
| Authorization requirements | Search results must respect access permissions | Retrieval and generated responses must both respect access permissions |
| Prompt-injection exposure | Primarily conventional search and application risks | Additional exposure from malicious instructions in queries and retrieved content |
| Audit requirements | Search and access logging | Search, retrieval, context construction, generation, and response-related logging |
| Governance complexity | Search quality, access control, and data management | Search quality, data access, model behavior, prompt injection, output validation, and AI governance |

The principal difference is that a RAG system does more than locate information. It uses retrieved information to construct a generated response.

This additional processing introduces risks that traditional search governance alone does not adequately address.

#### 1.3 Why Enterprises Adopt RAG

Organizations may implement RAG to make enterprise knowledge easier to access and use.

Typical business objectives include:

- Reducing the time employees spend searching for information.
- Improving access to approved policies and procedures.
- Supporting technical troubleshooting.
- Helping employees interpret complex documentation.
- Assisting customer-service teams.
- Supporting internal legal, compliance, and risk research.
- Improving knowledge transfer when experienced employees leave.
- Supporting service-desk and IT operations.
- Providing contextual assistance within enterprise applications.
- Reducing repeated requests to specialist teams.

RAG may be particularly useful when organizational information changes frequently. Updated documents can potentially be made available through the retrieval pipeline without retraining the underlying language model.

However, changes to the knowledge base must still be governed. If an outdated or malicious document is indexed, the system may continue to retrieve and rely on it until the problem is identified and corrected.

#### 1.4 Enterprise RAG Business Use Cases

| Use Case | Knowledge Sources | Expected Benefit | Principal Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal policy assistant | HR policies, corporate procedures, approved standards | Faster access to organizational policies | Employees receiving information outside their authorization |
| Cybersecurity knowledge assistant | Security standards, incident procedures, architecture documents | Faster security research and operational guidance | Exposure of sensitive security information |
| IT service-desk assistant | Troubleshooting guides, tickets, approved runbooks | Faster incident diagnosis | Incorrect instructions or disclosure of ticket information |
| Legal and compliance assistant | Approved legal guidance, regulatory material, internal compliance policies | More efficient research and policy interpretation | Unsupported legal conclusions or use of outdated material |
| Human resources assistant | Employee policies, benefits documentation, approved HR guidance | Faster employee self-service | Unauthorized access to personal or employment information |
| Customer-service assistant | Product documentation, FAQs, approved service procedures | More consistent customer support | Disclosure of confidential information or incorrect customer advice |
| Engineering knowledge assistant | Technical manuals, architecture records, approved design standards | Faster engineering research | Exposure of sensitive designs or unsafe technical recommendations |
| Enterprise architecture assistant | Architecture standards, application inventories, dependency records | Faster architecture analysis | Disclosure of infrastructure details and security weaknesses |
| Risk and audit assistant | Risk registers, control descriptions, audit evidence, procedures | More efficient GRC analysis | Inaccurate control interpretations or unauthorized evidence access |
| Network operations assistant | Network diagrams, incident records, configuration standards, runbooks | Faster troubleshooting and knowledge retrieval | Exposure of network topology or unsafe operational instructions |

The intended purpose should be documented separately for each use case. An assistant designed to retrieve internal HR policies should not automatically be permitted to retrieve employee investigation files or confidential management records.

#### 1.5 RAG Does Not Guarantee Truth

A common misconception is that RAG eliminates hallucinations because the model receives retrieved information.

In practice, a RAG system can still produce inaccurate responses for several reasons:

- The retrieval component selects irrelevant documents.
- The knowledge source contains outdated information.
- Relevant documents are missing.
- The document extraction process corrupts the original content.
- The retrieved passages contradict each other.
- The model misinterprets the retrieved content.
- The model introduces unsupported conclusions.
- The response presents an inference as an established fact.
- The source material contains malicious or misleading instructions.
- The model ignores important limitations in the retrieved context.

For example, an enterprise security assistant might retrieve a procedure that was replaced several months ago. Even if the model accurately summarizes that procedure, the answer may still be unsafe because the source is no longer authoritative.

The enterprise must therefore govern both the quality of the knowledge sources and the behavior of the AI system.

#### 1.6 Core Governance Objectives

An enterprise RAG governance program should establish the following objectives.

| Governance Objective | Required Outcome |
|---|---|
| Information confidentiality | Users receive only information they are authorized to access |
| Information integrity | Retrieved information is protected against unauthorized modification |
| Information quality | Knowledge sources are accurate, relevant, current, and fit for purpose |
| Retrieval security | Search and retrieval enforce applicable authorization restrictions |
| AI output reliability | Responses are evaluated against defined quality and safety criteria |
| Prompt-injection resistance | Malicious instructions in user input or retrieved content are prevented from controlling unauthorized behavior |
| Privacy protection | Personal information is processed according to applicable requirements |
| Traceability | Material responses and associated retrieval events can be investigated |
| Accountability | Owners are assigned for the application, knowledge sources, risks, and controls |
| Operational resilience | The service can be restricted, disabled, or recovered when necessary |
| Compliance | Applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements are addressed |
| Lifecycle control | Changes to models, prompts, sources, permissions, and retrieval settings are governed |

**Core Principle:** Enterprise RAG combines information retrieval with AI-generated interpretation. Its governance must protect the underlying information, control retrieval and authorization, evaluate generated responses, and maintain accountability for the complete processing lifecycle.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Retrieval vs. generation:** Retrieval selects information; generation constructs a response using that information and other model capabilities.
- **Relevant information vs. authorized information:** A document may be relevant to a question but still be prohibited from disclosure to the requesting user.
- **Grounded response vs. verified response:** A response that uses retrieved documents is not necessarily correct or independently verified.
- **Knowledge availability vs. knowledge integrity:** Making documents accessible does not establish that they are accurate, current, or trustworthy.
- **Search accuracy vs. answer accuracy:** Correctly retrieving a document does not guarantee that the model will interpret it correctly.
- **RAG deployment vs. RAG governance:** Deploying retrieval and generation components does not establish that the resulting system meets enterprise security and compliance requirements.

### 2. Business Use Cases, Intended Purpose, and Decision Boundaries

An enterprise RAG system should have a clearly defined intended purpose. Governance becomes difficult when an application initially designed for low-risk knowledge retrieval gradually expands into activities involving sensitive information, consequential decisions, or operational actions.

For example, an internal assistant may initially summarize approved IT procedures. Later, users may request security incident recommendations, ask it to interpret employee records, or connect it to systems that can execute operational commands.

Each expansion can change the system's risk profile.

The enterprise should therefore define what the RAG system is permitted to do, which information it may process, who may use it, and what decisions or actions remain outside its authority.

#### 2.1 Define the Intended Purpose

The intended purpose describes the approved business function and operating boundaries of the system.

A complete definition should identify:

- The business problem being addressed.
- The intended users.
- The permitted use cases.
- The information sources that may be accessed.
- The types of questions the system may answer.
- The expected response format.
- The level of accuracy required.
- The consequences of an incorrect answer.
- Whether outputs are advisory or decision-supporting.
- Whether the system can interact with external applications.
- Whether any actions may be executed automatically.
- The limitations that must be communicated to users.
- The conditions that require human review.
- The circumstances under which the service must be restricted or disabled.

The intended purpose should be approved by the business owner and reviewed by the relevant security, data, privacy, architecture, and compliance stakeholders.

#### 2.2 Define RAG Capability Categories

RAG systems can support several categories of enterprise activity.

| Capability Category | Description | Example | Typical Risk Consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Information retrieval | Finds relevant documents or passages | Locate the current access-control policy | Unauthorized retrieval |
| Summarization | Condenses retrieved information | Summarize a network incident report | Omission of critical details |
| Question answering | Answers questions using retrieved context | Explain the company's password requirements | Unsupported or incorrect answers |
| Comparison | Compares multiple sources | Compare two versions of a security standard | Failure to identify important differences |
| Classification | Assigns labels or categories | Classify a support request by topic | Misclassification |
| Recommendation | Suggests a course of action | Recommend a troubleshooting procedure | Unsafe or inappropriate advice |
| Drafting | Produces a draft based on approved material | Draft an audit finding using documented evidence | Fabricated details or incorrect conclusions |
| Decision support | Provides evidence to inform a human decision | Summarize risks for a change advisory board | Automation bias and overreliance |
| Tool-assisted execution | Uses connected tools to perform actions | Create a ticket or retrieve approved system information | Excessive permissions and unauthorized actions |

The risk of the system generally increases when it moves from retrieving information toward influencing consequential decisions or executing actions.

However, the actual risk depends on the use case, information sensitivity, technical controls, and potential impact rather than the capability label alone.

#### 2.3 Establish a Use-Case Classification Model

An enterprise can classify RAG use cases according to their potential impact and sensitivity.

The following model is illustrative and should be aligned with the organization's risk framework.

| Risk Tier | Characteristics | Example | Governance Approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Low impact | Public or low-sensitivity information; limited consequences from error | Assistant for public product documentation | Basic security controls, source validation, and output evaluation |
| Tier 2 — Internal operational | Internal information and routine employee workflows | Internal IT procedure assistant | Authentication, source authorization, logging, and defined accuracy criteria |
| Tier 3 — Sensitive enterprise | Confidential business information or sensitive technical records | Cybersecurity architecture knowledge assistant | Strong access controls, data segregation, enhanced testing, and risk review |
| Tier 4 — High impact | Sensitive personal information, regulated information, or outputs influencing consequential decisions | HR case-support assistant or regulated operational knowledge system | Enhanced privacy, legal, security, human-oversight, and assurance requirements |
| Tier 5 — Critical or action-enabled | RAG integrated with privileged tools or capable of influencing critical operational processes | Assistant connected to production network-management tools | Strict execution boundaries, independent authorization, adversarial testing, recovery controls, and formal risk approval |

These tiers are a practical internal classification approach, not a universal regulatory classification scheme.

The organization should consider whether the system's intended purpose or actual deployment brings it within specific legal or regulatory requirements.

#### 2.4 Establish Explicit Decision Boundaries

The enterprise should distinguish between information support, decision support, and autonomous execution.

For example, a RAG assistant connected to cybersecurity documentation might be allowed to retrieve a runbook and explain its steps. That does not automatically authorize the assistant to execute the runbook against production systems.

| Activity | Permitted by Default? | Required Governance Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieve approved internal documentation | Subject to authorization | Identity, access control, and source approval |
| Summarize an approved policy | Subject to intended purpose | Source fidelity and accuracy evaluation |
| Compare policy versions | Subject to source availability | Version control and completeness |
| Draft an audit finding | Subject to evidence and review requirements | Traceability and human validation |
| Recommend a security configuration | Not automatically authorized | Technical validation and approval |
| Interpret sensitive employee records | Not automatically authorized | Purpose limitation, privacy, and access restrictions |
| Provide a legal or compliance conclusion | Not automatically authoritative | Qualified review and source verification |
| Create an IT service ticket | Only if explicitly authorized | Tool permissions, validation, and audit logging |
| Modify a production configuration | Prohibited unless specifically authorized | Change approval, independent execution controls, and rollback |
| Retrieve another user's restricted documents | Prohibited unless access is explicitly authorized | Authorization enforcement and auditability |
| Override access restrictions because a user requests it | Prohibited | Security policy enforcement |

The system must not treat user instructions as authority to bypass enterprise controls.

#### 2.5 Define Human Oversight Requirements

Human oversight should reflect the consequences of an incorrect response.

| Output Type | Oversight Requirement |
|---|---|
| Low-risk factual summary | Sampling and quality monitoring may be sufficient |
| Internal policy interpretation | User-visible source references and escalation for ambiguity |
| Cybersecurity recommendation | Review by a qualified security professional where impact warrants |
| Audit finding or control assessment | Validation against evidence and review by the responsible assurance professional |
| Legal or regulatory interpretation | Review by an appropriately qualified professional |
| Advice involving sensitive personal information | Authorized access and review appropriate to the purpose |
| Production change recommendation | Review and approval through the established change process |
| High-impact automated action | Explicit authorization and enforceable technical controls |

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than ceremonial.

A reviewer must have enough context, expertise, time, and authority to challenge the output. A process that requires employees to approve every answer without enabling them to evaluate it does not provide effective oversight.

#### 2.6 Define Prohibited and Restricted Uses

The enterprise should document prohibited uses and activities that require additional approval.

Examples may include:

- Retrieving documents outside the user's authorization.
- Exposing confidential information through generated answers.
- Treating retrieved instructions as permission to execute actions.
- Using unapproved repositories containing sensitive information.
- Generating authoritative decisions from unverified sources.
- Using the system for purposes unrelated to its approved business function.
- Allowing external users to access internal knowledge without an approved authorization model.
- Connecting the system to production tools without security review.
- Using sensitive personal information without an approved purpose and lawful basis where required.
- Disabling security controls to improve retrieval coverage.
- Treating generated citations as proof that a statement is correct.
- Allowing users or documents to override system-level security policies.

Restricted uses may be permitted after additional risk assessment, approval, and control implementation.

#### 2.7 Example: Defining a Cybersecurity Knowledge Assistant

Consider an enterprise implementing a RAG assistant for its cybersecurity governance, risk, and compliance team.

**Approved intended purpose:**

The assistant retrieves and summarizes approved cybersecurity policies, standards, control descriptions, risk records, and audit procedures to help authorized employees perform research and prepare draft documentation.

**Permitted activities:**

- Retrieve approved policy documents.
- Summarize control requirements.
- Compare versions of approved standards.
- Locate evidence associated with a control.
- Draft preliminary risk descriptions.
- Identify potential gaps for human investigation.
- Provide references to the source material used.

**Restricted activities:**

- Declaring a control effective without sufficient evidence.
- Making final audit conclusions without required review.
- Accessing confidential investigations outside the user's authorization.
- Treating generated risk ratings as approved enterprise decisions.
- Modifying the authoritative risk register without authorization.
- Executing security changes through connected tools without a separate approval process.

**Required controls:**

- Enterprise authentication.
- Authorization-aware retrieval.
- Approved source repositories.
- Document ownership and version governance.
- Source references in generated answers.
- Evaluation of answer accuracy and source fidelity.
- Logging of relevant retrieval and application events.
- Human validation for material GRC outputs.
- Incident-response and exception-management procedures.

This definition makes the assistant's role clear: it supports the work of GRC professionals but does not replace their accountability or authority.

#### 2.8 Governance Questions for Use-Case Approval

Before approving a RAG use case, stakeholders should answer the following questions:

1. What specific business problem is being addressed?
2. Who are the intended users?
3. Which information sources are required?
4. What is the sensitivity of that information?
5. Are users authorized to access all retrieved material?
6. What happens if the answer is wrong or incomplete?
7. Will users rely on the output for consequential decisions?
8. Does the system connect to external tools or enterprise applications?
9. Can the system modify data or execute actions?
10. Which outputs require human review?
11. What limitations must be communicated to users?
12. How will source accuracy and currency be maintained?
13. How will inappropriate disclosure be detected?
14. Who owns the residual risk?
15. What conditions require the use case to be suspended?

**Core Principle:** A RAG system should operate only within a documented and approved intended purpose. Its permissions, information sources, output controls, human oversight, and action boundaries must be proportionate to the sensitivity of the data and the consequences of error.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Use-case approval vs. platform approval:** Approval of the underlying platform does not automatically authorize every possible application built on it.
- **Information support vs. decision authority:** Helping a user understand evidence does not automatically authorize the system to make the final decision.
- **Recommendation vs. execution:** Suggesting an action is different from having permission to execute it.
- **Human presence vs. effective oversight:** Requiring a person to review an output is insufficient if that person cannot meaningfully assess or challenge it.
- **Restricted use vs. prohibited use:** A restricted activity may become permissible after additional controls and approval; a prohibited activity remains outside the authorized scope.
- **Intended purpose vs. actual use:** The enterprise must monitor how users actually employ the system, not rely exclusively on the original design document.

### 3. Enterprise RAG Architecture and Data Flows

An enterprise RAG system typically includes user-facing applications, identity and authorization services, retrieval components, knowledge repositories, document-processing pipelines, a language model, output controls, and monitoring systems.

The exact architecture depends on the deployment model. A RAG solution may operate within an enterprise cloud environment, use a managed AI service, or combine internal data processing with an external model provider.

From a governance perspective, the critical task is to identify where information enters the system, how it is transformed, which components can access it, how the model receives context, and where generated outputs are delivered.

#### 3.2 Core Architecture Components

| Component | Function | Principal Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Enterprise user interface | Accepts questions and displays responses | Authentication, session security, and appropriate user messaging |
| Identity provider | Authenticates users and provides identity context | Identity assurance and session management |
| Authorization service | Determines permitted access | Correct enforcement of document and data permissions |
| RAG application layer | Coordinates the request and response workflow | Input validation, policy enforcement, and secure orchestration |
| Query processor | Normalizes or transforms user queries | Preservation of intent and resistance to manipulation |
| Retrieval engine | Finds relevant information | Retrieval quality, access filtering, and search manipulation |
| Knowledge repositories | Store authoritative documents and related metadata | Confidentiality, integrity, ownership, and retention |
| Document ingestion pipeline | Extracts and transforms source content | Malicious documents, parsing errors, and unauthorized ingestion |
| Chunking component | Divides documents into retrievable units | Preservation of meaning, context, and document relationships |
| Embedding service | Converts text into numerical representations for semantic search | Data exposure, processing location, and model/provider risk |
| Vector index | Stores embeddings and associated metadata | Access segregation, index integrity, and data leakage |
| Context builder | Selects and assembles retrieved passages | Authorization enforcement and prompt-injection exposure |
| Model gateway | Controls access to the language model | Provider selection, credentials, data transmission, and policy enforcement |
| Language model | Generates the response | Hallucinations, instruction following, and output reliability |
| Output validation layer | Applies response checks and restrictions | Inadequate validation or incorrect blocking decisions |
| Logging and monitoring platform | Records relevant events and detects anomalies | Sensitive data in logs, retention, and investigation capability |
| Governance and administration interface | Manages configuration, sources, permissions, and versions | Privileged access and unauthorized changes |

The enterprise must determine which controls belong in the application, which are enforced by underlying infrastructure, and which depend on external providers.

A control should not be assumed to exist merely because a platform offers a related feature. Its configuration, scope, and effectiveness must be verified.

#### 3.3 Enterprise Knowledge Ingestion Pipeline

Knowledge ingestion is the process through which source material becomes available to the retrieval system.

A typical ingestion pipeline includes:

1. Source identification.
2. Source-owner approval.
3. Permission and classification validation.
4. Document retrieval or extraction.
5. File-type and content validation.
6. Malware and content-security scanning where appropriate.
7. Text extraction and normalization.
8. Document segmentation or chunking.
9. Metadata assignment.
10. Embedding generation where semantic retrieval is used.
11. Index creation or update.
12. Quality and retrieval validation.
13. Publication to the approved knowledge index.
14. Ongoing monitoring and periodic refresh.
15. Removal or re-indexing when information is withdrawn or changed.

The ingestion pipeline should preserve document identity, ownership, source location, version, classification, and applicable access restrictions.

The governance requirement is that unapproved, corrupted, or unauthorized content must not become available for enterprise retrieval simply because it can be processed technically.

RAG systems may process more information than the documents visible to the user.

| Data Category | Examples | Governance Considerations |

| Identity information | User ID, groups, roles, session attributes | Authentication, authorization, minimization |
| Source documents | Policies, reports, manuals, tickets, contracts | Classification, ownership, integrity, access rights |
| Document metadata | Owner, version, department, classification, timestamp | Accuracy and enforcement of metadata-based restrictions |
| Extracted text | Parsed document content | Extraction quality and sensitive-data handling |
| Embeddings | Numerical representations of document content | Confidentiality, access restrictions, and provider exposure |
| Vector index metadata | Document identifiers, permissions, source references | Index integrity and authorization enforcement |
| Retrieved passages | Text selected for a specific query | Disclosure risk and context manipulation |
| Prompt and context | User question plus system instructions and retrieved content | Instruction hierarchy, injection resistance, data minimization |
| Generated response | Summary, answer, recommendation, or draft | Accuracy, confidentiality, and output validation |
| Conversation history | Previous questions and responses | Retention, access control, and context leakage |

| Evaluation data | Test queries, expected answers, annotated results | Access control and representativeness |
| Administrative records | Source changes, access changes, configuration updates | Privileged-access monitoring and accountability |

Embeddings should not automatically be considered harmless merely because they are not ordinary readable text. Their sensitivity depends on the data, embedding method, surrounding metadata, and potential means of inference or reconstruction.

#### 3.6 Trust Boundaries in a RAG Architecture

A trust boundary separates components or environments with different security assumptions.

Important boundaries may exist between:

- The user's device and the enterprise application.
- The application and the identity provider.
- The RAG orchestration layer and the retrieval service.
- The retrieval service and knowledge repositories.
- The ingestion pipeline and external document sources.
- The enterprise environment and an external model provider.
- The model and any connected tools.
- The application and administrative interfaces.
- The production environment and development or testing environments.
- The system and monitoring or logging platforms.

Each boundary should have explicit security requirements.

| Trust Boundary | Example Threat | Required Control Objective |
|---|---|---|
| User to application | Session theft or unauthorized access | Strong authentication and session controls |
| Application to identity service | Forged or improperly validated identity context | Secure identity integration and token validation |
| Application to retrieval service | Unauthorized query or excessive permissions | Service authentication and least privilege |
| Retrieval service to knowledge store | Unauthorized document access | Authorization-aware retrieval and repository controls |
| Ingestion pipeline to source repositories | Malicious or unauthorized documents | Source approval, integrity checks, and ingestion controls |
| Enterprise to model provider | Confidential context exposed externally | Approved provider, secure transmission, and contractual controls |
| Model output to enterprise application | Unsafe or misleading generated content | Output validation and appropriate human review |
| AI application to connected tools | Unauthorized action through tool invocation | Independent authorization and action restrictions |
| Administration interface to production | Unauthorized configuration changes | Privileged access controls and change management |
| Production to logging platform | Sensitive information exposed through logs | Log minimization, access control, and retention governance |

#### 3.7 Authorization-Aware Retrieval

One of the most important architectural requirements is that retrieval respects the user's permissions.

Suppose an enterprise maintains three repositories:

- General corporate policies.
- Confidential security architecture documents.
- Restricted employee investigation records.

An employee authorized to access corporate policies should not gain access to the other repositories merely because the RAG system can retrieve documents from all three.

Authorization should be enforced through a reliable combination of user identity, document permissions, repository controls, and retrieval logic.

The system must not rely solely on the language model to decide whether a retrieved document may be disclosed.

Key requirements include:

- Reliable user identity propagation.
- Access-control metadata associated with documents.
- Permission-aware retrieval.
- Validation of authorization before protected content is supplied to the model.
- Appropriate handling of document-level and, where required, section-level permissions.
- Secure management of group membership and role changes.
- Prompt and conversation isolation between users or tenants.
- Removal or re-indexing of content when access rights change.
- Testing of direct and indirect attempts to retrieve unauthorized information.

Where a user is entitled to a document but not every section within it, the architecture must determine whether section-level controls are required and technically feasible.

| Review Area | Key Questions |
|---|---|
| Application design | Are all components and dependencies documented? |
| Identity | Is the user's identity reliably propagated throughout the request? |
| Authorization | Are permissions enforced before restricted content reaches the model? |
| Knowledge ingestion | Can unauthorized documents enter the index? |
| Data processing | Where are documents, extracted text, embeddings, and prompts processed? |
| Model integration | Is the model provider approved for the information being processed? |
| Context construction | Can retrieved content manipulate model behavior? |
| Output validation | Are unsupported or unauthorized disclosures detected where feasible? |
| Logging | Can relevant events be investigated without unnecessary sensitive-data retention? |
| Administration | Are source, permission, and configuration changes controlled? |
| Resilience | Can the service be disabled or restored safely? |
| Supplier management | Are external dependencies and responsibilities documented? |
| Testing | Have architecture-specific threats been evaluated? |
| Lifecycle management | Are source, model, prompt, and index changes governed? |

**Core Principle:** RAG security depends on the complete architecture and data flow. Authorization, source integrity, context construction, model integration, output handling, and monitoring must be designed as connected controls rather than isolated features.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Document access vs. model-context access:** Permission to view a document in one interface does not prove that the RAG pipeline enforces the same permission correctly.
- **Vector index vs. source repository:** A vector index is a derived representation of source information and requires its own security and lifecycle controls.
- **Data ingestion vs. data publication:** Successfully processing a document does not mean it should be made available for retrieval.
- **Model gateway vs. authorization service:** A gateway may control model access, but it does not necessarily determine whether a user is entitled to each retrieved document.
- **Output filtering vs. retrieval authorization:** Filtering a response cannot reliably compensate for unauthorized information already supplied to the model.
- **Logical architecture vs. verified implementation:** A diagram describes intended design; technical validation is required to demonstrate that the deployed system enforces it.

### 4. Stakeholders, Accountability, and the RAG Governance Model

An enterprise RAG system depends on several organizational functions. Information owners control the source material, data teams manage ingestion and indexing, AI teams maintain retrieval and generation behavior, cybersecurity teams assess threats, and business owners determine the intended use.

Without clear accountability, the system can develop gaps between document ownership, access control, AI behavior, and operational responsibility.

For example, the AI engineering team may be responsible for building the retrieval pipeline but may not have authority to approve access to confidential HR records. Similarly, a knowledge repository owner may approve a document's publication but may not understand how its contents could influence AI-generated responses.

Governance must connect these responsibilities.

#### 4.1 Identify the Principal Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Primary Responsibility | RAG-Specific Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Strategic sponsorship and resources | Business value, material risks, and accountability |
| Business owner | Defines the business objective and intended purpose | Appropriate use, business impact, and output limitations |
| AI system owner | Owns the RAG application and its lifecycle | Model behavior, retrieval quality, and lifecycle changes |
| Enterprise architect | Reviews solution architecture | Trust boundaries, integrations, and enterprise alignment |
| Data owner | Governs the source information | Permitted use, classification, quality, and retention |
| Knowledge manager | Maintains enterprise knowledge resources | Document accuracy, versioning, and publication |
| Data engineering team | Builds ingestion and transformation pipelines | Data integrity, extraction quality, and pipeline security |
| AI engineering team | Develops and maintains retrieval and generation components | Retrieval performance, evaluation, and system behavior |
| Application owner | Operates the application and integrations | Availability, configuration, and application controls |
| Cybersecurity team | Defines and evaluates security controls | Prompt injection, data leakage, access control, and monitoring |
| IAM team | Manages identities and access policies | Identity propagation, role design, and access reviews |
| Privacy officer or DPO, where applicable | Advises on personal-data protection and statutory responsibilities | Lawful processing, minimization, retention, and individual rights |
| Legal team | Interprets legal and contractual obligations | Intellectual property, confidentiality, and legal risk |
| Compliance team | Maps obligations to controls and evidence | Regulatory alignment and compliance assurance |
| Risk management | Oversees risk assessment and treatment | Risk ownership, escalation, and acceptance |
| Procurement and supplier management | Manages providers and contracts | Supplier assurance and contractual responsibilities |
| Records management | Governs retention and disposal | Records lifecycle and defensible deletion |
| Internal audit | Provides independent assurance | Governance and control effectiveness |
| End users | Use the system within approved boundaries | Appropriate reliance, information handling, and incident reporting |

The organization should adapt these roles to its structure and size. Some responsibilities may be combined, but accountability for sensitive information and consequential decisions must remain clear.

#### 4.3 Apply the Three Lines of Accountability

The Three Lines Model helps distinguish operational ownership from independent oversight.

| Line | Typical Functions | Responsibilities for Enterprise RAG |
|---|---|---|
| First line | Business owners, AI system owners, data owners, engineering and operations | Operate the system, manage source information, implement controls, and own operational risks |
| Second line | Risk management, cybersecurity governance, privacy, compliance | Establish requirements, challenge risk assessments, monitor compliance, and oversee risk treatment |
| Third line | Internal audit or equivalent independent assurance | Independently assess governance and control effectiveness |

The precise allocation depends on the enterprise's governance framework.

For example, cybersecurity engineering may implement technical controls, while cybersecurity governance establishes standards and independently challenges whether those controls address the relevant risks.

#### 4.4 Define Accountability Across the RAG Lifecycle

Governance should cover the complete lifecycle, including use-case approval, source onboarding, ingestion, indexing, retrieval, generation, monitoring, and retirement.

| Lifecycle Activity | Primary Accountable Role | Supporting Stakeholders |
|---|---|---|
| Business use-case definition | Business owner | AI system owner, risk, compliance |
| Intended-purpose approval | Business owner or designated authority | Legal, cybersecurity, privacy |
| Source approval | Data or knowledge owner | Data governance, cybersecurity |

| Ingestion pipeline design | AI/data engineering owner | Data owner, cybersecurity |

| AI quality evaluation | AI system owner | Business users, knowledge owners |
| Production approval | Designated enterprise authority | Business owner, risk, security, operations |
| Knowledge-source maintenance | Source owner | Knowledge management, data engineering |
| Operational monitoring | Application or service owner | AI engineering, cybersecurity, business owner |
| Incident management | Designated incident owner | Security, privacy, legal, operations |
| Risk acceptance | Authorized risk owner | Business owner, risk management |
| Retirement and deletion | Service and data owners | Records management, privacy, security |

A single person may hold multiple roles in a smaller organization. Where responsibilities are combined, the enterprise should introduce additional review for decisions with significant security, privacy, or business consequences.

#### 4.5 Define the RACI Matrix

RACI identifies who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed.

- **Responsible (R):** Performs the work.
- **Accountable (A):** Owns the outcome.
- **Consulted (C):** Provides advice or specialist input.
- **Informed (I):** Receives relevant updates.

The following matrix is illustrative and must be aligned with the organization's actual delegation of authority.

| Activity | Business Owner | AI System Owner | Data / Knowledge Owner | AI Engineering | Cybersecurity | Privacy / Legal | Risk / Compliance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Define intended purpose | A | R | C | C | C | C | C |
| Approve use case | A | R | C | I | C | C | C |
| Approve knowledge sources | C | R | A | C | C | C | I |
| Classify source data | I | C | A/R | C | C | C | C |
| Design ingestion pipeline | I | A | C | R | C | C | I |
| Design retrieval architecture | C | A | C | R | C | I | I |
| Design access controls | I | A | C | R | R/C | C | I |
| Conduct AI evaluation | C | A | C | R | C | I | I |
| Conduct security testing | I | C | C | R/C | A/R | C | I |
| Assess privacy implications | C | C | C | I | C | A/R | C |
| Complete risk assessment | C | R | C | C | R/C | C | A/R, subject to governance |
| Approve production deployment | A, subject to delegation | R | C | C | C | C | C |
| Monitor production performance | C | A | C | R | R/C | I | C |
| Manage knowledge-source changes | I | C | A | R | C | C | I |
| Accept residual risk | A or designated risk authority | R/C | C | I | C | C | C |
| Conduct independent audit | I | I | I | I | C | C | C |

Internal audit should retain its independent assurance role and should not be assigned operational ownership of controls it must later audit.

#### 4.6 Define Decision Rights

Different decisions require different approval authorities.

| Decision | Expected Decision Authority | Governance Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Approve a low-risk knowledge source | Authorized data or knowledge owner | Accuracy, classification, and permitted use |
| Add a confidential repository | Designated data owner and relevant security authority | Authorization, sensitivity, and retrieval boundaries |
| Change document-access rules | Authorized data or IAM owner | Impact on all users and existing permissions |
| Change the model provider | Designated AI or architecture authority | Data processing, contracts, security, and model behavior |
| Modify retrieval parameters | AI system owner under change control | Retrieval quality, authorization, and regression testing |
| Change system instructions or prompt templates | AI system owner under change control | Behavior changes, injection resistance, and safety testing |
| Expand to sensitive personal information | Business owner and relevant privacy, legal, and risk authorities | Purpose, lawful basis where applicable, minimization, and impact |
| Connect external tools | Designated application and security authorities | Tool permissions, action boundaries, and auditability |
| Permit consequential automated actions | Authorized enterprise risk and business authority | Impact, independent authorization, and recovery |
| Accept material residual risk | Designated risk authority | Delegation of authority and risk appetite |
| Retire a source or system | Relevant service and data owners | Retention, deletion, dependencies, and continuity |

Approval of one decision does not imply approval of every related capability. For example, approval to connect a RAG system to a knowledge repository does not automatically authorize access to every document within that repository.

#### 4.7 Establish the Risk Ownership Model

Risk ownership should reflect the business consequences of the risk.

Consider a scenario in which a RAG assistant retrieves an outdated network security procedure and recommends an unsafe configuration.

Several teams may contribute to the incident:

- The knowledge owner failed to retire the outdated document.
- The ingestion pipeline did not process the document's status metadata correctly.
- The retrieval component selected the outdated procedure.
- The model generated a recommendation based on the retrieved content.
- The application did not communicate the uncertainty or version conflict.
- The operational user relied on the recommendation.

The organization should identify the accountable risk owner and the control owners responsible for the contributing weaknesses.

A risk register should not assign every RAG risk to the AI engineering team merely because the AI model generated the final response.

#### 4.8 Establish Governance Forums and Escalation

| Forum | Typical Participants | Responsibilities |
|---|---|---|
| AI governance committee | Business, AI, risk, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, compliance | Approve material use cases, review risk, and oversee exceptions |
| Architecture review board | Enterprise architecture, AI engineering, cybersecurity, data architecture | Review design, trust boundaries, integrations, and significant changes |
| Data governance forum | Data owners, knowledge management, privacy, records management | Govern sources, classification, quality, access, and retention |
| Security review forum | Cybersecurity, IAM, application owners, AI engineering | Review security findings, threat models, and remediation |
| Operational service review | Service owner, application operations, AI engineering, business users | Review availability, performance, incidents, and service quality |
| Risk and compliance review | Risk, legal, privacy, compliance, business owners | Review obligations, residual risk, and remediation |
| Incident response team | Security, application operations, AI owner, legal/privacy as needed | Contain incidents, preserve evidence, and coordinate recovery |

Escalation criteria should be defined for incidents involving unauthorized data disclosure, compromised knowledge sources, material AI behavior changes, loss of authorization enforcement, and other events that exceed approved risk thresholds.

**Core Principle:** RAG governance requires clear accountability across business purpose, source information, retrieval, AI behavior, and operational use. The organization must ensure that every material decision has an authorized owner and that operational responsibility remains distinct from independent risk oversight and assurance.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Data ownership vs. AI ownership:** The data owner governs the source information; the AI system owner governs the application that retrieves and uses it.
- **Control implementation vs. risk acceptance:** Engineering teams implement controls, but only authorized decision-makers may accept residual risk.
- **Governance committee vs. operational owner:** Committees establish oversight and resolve material decisions; operational owners maintain the system day to day.
- **Consultation vs. accountability:** A team may provide advice without owning the final decision.
- **Risk oversight vs. independent assurance:** Second-line oversight challenges and monitors risk; third-line assurance independently evaluates governance and control effectiveness.
- **Technical capability vs. delegated authority:** A system's ability to retrieve, generate, or execute does not itself establish permission to do so.

### 5. Foundational Governance Requirements and Enterprise Policy Principles

An enterprise RAG system should operate under a documented governance framework that connects business purpose, information management, security controls, AI evaluation, operational procedures, and compliance requirements.

Policies provide the baseline expectations. Standards define mandatory requirements. Procedures explain how those requirements are implemented. Technical controls enforce them where possible, and assurance activities verify that they work as intended.

A mature governance program should avoid relying exclusively on user guidance or model instructions to protect enterprise information.

#### 5.1 Establish the Enterprise RAG Governance Policy

The organization should define a policy or equivalent set of requirements for enterprise RAG applications.

The policy should address:

- Approved business purposes.
- Permitted and prohibited uses.
- Risk classification and approval.
- Source ownership and authorization.
- Information classification and handling.
- Identity and access management.
- Knowledge ingestion and indexing.
- Model and provider selection.
- Prompt and retrieval configuration.
- Data privacy and retention.
- Security testing and AI evaluation.
- Human oversight.
- Logging, monitoring, and incident response.
- Change management.
- Supplier risk.
- Auditability and evidence retention.
- Risk acceptance and exception management.
- Periodic review and retirement.

The policy should apply to the complete RAG solution, including components operated by external providers where those components process enterprise information or influence the system's behavior.

#### 5.2 Establish Core Policy Principles

| Policy Principle | Requirement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized purpose | Use the system only for approved business purposes | A policy assistant is not repurposed to analyze confidential investigations without review |
| Least privilege | Restrict users, services, and administrators to required access | The retrieval service accesses only approved repositories |
| Source accountability | Assign an owner to every authoritative knowledge source | Each policy repository has an accountable owner |
| Information integrity | Protect documents and metadata against unauthorized modification | Document changes are authenticated and traceable |
| Authorization-aware retrieval | Enforce permissions throughout retrieval and context construction | Restricted passages are not supplied to unauthorized users |
| Data minimization | Process only the information needed for the approved purpose | Avoid sending unrelated personal information to an external model |
| Secure model integration | Control what information reaches the model provider | Use approved processing arrangements and transmission protections |
| Prompt-injection resistance | Treat retrieved content as untrusted input unless validated for its intended use | A document cannot override application security policies |
| Output validation | Evaluate whether responses are supported and safe for their purpose | High-impact recommendations require source verification |
| Human accountability | Preserve human responsibility for consequential decisions | AI-generated audit conclusions are reviewed before formal issuance |
| Traceability | Record relevant events and decisions | Material answers can be linked to retrieved sources and system versions |
| Controlled change | Assess changes to sources, models, prompts, and permissions | Material changes undergo impact assessment and regression testing |
| Resilience | Maintain a safe operating mode when the AI service is unavailable or unreliable | Employees can access approved source documents directly |
| Continuous assurance | Review control effectiveness and system behavior | Security tests and retrieval evaluations are repeated after material changes |

#### 5.3 Establish an AI and Knowledge-Source Inventory

The enterprise should maintain two connected inventories:

1. An AI system and use-case inventory.
2. A knowledge-source inventory.

The AI inventory records what the system does and how it is governed. The knowledge-source inventory records which information the system can retrieve and the conditions under which that information may be used.

**AI system inventory fields:**

| System name | Approved system name |
| Business owner | Accountable business stakeholder |
| AI system owner | Accountable application or AI owner |
| Intended purpose | Approved business function |
| User population | Authorized user groups |
| Risk tier | Internal risk classification |
| Model provider | Internal or external model service |
| Retrieval method | Keyword, semantic, vector, hybrid, or other approach |
| Integrated repositories | Approved source systems |
| External integrations | Model APIs, enterprise applications, and connected tools |
| Autonomy level | Permitted decision-support or action capability |
| Security status | Assessment and control status |
| Production status | Proposed, pilot, approved, suspended, or retired |
| Review date | Next scheduled review |
| Material changes | Recorded changes to scope or architecture |

**Knowledge-source inventory fields:**

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Source ID | Unique source identifier |
| Source name | Repository or information source |
| Source owner | Accountable content owner |

| Version and currency | How current versions are identified |

The inventories should be connected so that a change to a source, provider, or use case can trigger the appropriate risk reassessment.

#### 5.4 Establish Knowledge-Source Approval Requirements

Not every document should automatically become part of the enterprise knowledge base.

Source approval should consider:

- Whether the source is relevant to the approved purpose.
- Whether the organization has the right to process the content.
- Whether the source contains confidential or personal information.
- Whether the source owner has approved the intended use.
- Whether access restrictions can be enforced.
- Whether the content is authoritative and sufficiently current.
- Whether the source contains embedded instructions or potentially malicious content.
- Whether document extraction preserves meaning and structure.
- Whether the source has appropriate retention and deletion rules.
- Whether the source's quality can be monitored.

The approval process should also establish how outdated, disputed, corrupted, or unauthorized documents are removed from retrieval.

A source may be technically accessible but unsuitable for inclusion in a particular RAG use case.

#### 5.5 Establish Minimum Security Requirements

The enterprise should define a baseline control set for all production RAG applications.

| Control Domain | Minimum Governance Requirement |
|---|---|
| Identity | Authenticate users and services through approved mechanisms |
| Authorization | Enforce access restrictions before protected information enters model context |
| Data protection | Apply classification, encryption, and appropriate handling controls |
| Ingestion | Validate sources and restrict unauthorized content |
| Knowledge integrity | Maintain document provenance, versioning, and controlled updates |
| Retrieval | Evaluate retrieval quality and verify permission enforcement |
| Prompt security | Test resistance to malicious user input and instructions embedded in retrieved content |
| Model integration | Approve providers and govern data transmission |
| Output handling | Apply controls appropriate to the response's sensitivity and impact |
| Logging | Record material events while minimizing unnecessary sensitive-data collection |
| Administration | Restrict privileged access and govern configuration changes |
| Testing | Perform security, functional, and AI quality evaluations |
| Monitoring | Detect material security, quality, and operational deviations |
| Incident response | Maintain procedures for data disclosure, prompt injection, and other AI-related incidents |
| Resilience | Provide an appropriate fallback or safe shutdown capability |
| Third-party risk | Assess external providers and contractual responsibilities |
| Assurance | Maintain evidence demonstrating control implementation and effectiveness |

The baseline should be supplemented by stronger controls for systems that process restricted information, support consequential decisions, or connect to operational tools.

#### 5.6 Establish Data Protection and Privacy Principles

Enterprise RAG systems may process personal data in source documents, user queries, retrieved passages, generated responses, and logs.

Where personal data is involved, the organization should assess applicable data-protection requirements, including the GDPR where relevant.

Governance should address:

- A defined and lawful processing purpose.
- Data minimization.
- Appropriate accuracy and currency.
- Retention and deletion.
- Access restrictions.
- Transparency where required.
- Data-subject rights where applicable.
- Controller and processor responsibilities.
- International transfers where relevant.
- Security of processing.
- Data protection impact assessment where required.
- Appropriate handling of personal data in prompts, retrieval context, and logs.

The use of RAG does not automatically require a Data Protection Impact Assessment in every case. The organization should assess the actual processing activities and whether the applicable legal criteria for a DPIA are met.

The enterprise should also determine whether sensitive information is sent to an external model provider and whether the relevant provider arrangements support the intended processing.

#### 5.7 Establish AI Evaluation and Acceptance Criteria

A RAG system should be evaluated against defined acceptance criteria before production deployment.

Evaluation should cover multiple dimensions.

| Evaluation Dimension | Example Question |
|---|---|
| Retrieval relevance | Does the system retrieve the documents needed to answer the question? |
| Retrieval completeness | Does it omit important authoritative sources? |
| Answer correctness | Is the response consistent with validated reference information? |
| Grounding | Are material claims supported by the retrieved context? |
| Citation accuracy | Do the cited sources actually support the associated claims? |
| Currency | Does the system prioritize current authoritative documents? |
| Authorization | Can users retrieve information they are not entitled to access? |
| Privacy | Does the system disclose personal information beyond the approved purpose? |
| Prompt-injection resistance | Can malicious instructions in retrieved documents manipulate the response? |
| Robustness | Does the system behave appropriately when documents conflict or evidence is missing? |
| Refusal and escalation | Does it decline or escalate questions that cannot be answered safely? |
| Operational reliability | Does the service meet its availability and performance requirements? |

Acceptance thresholds should be defined according to the use case. A general policy assistant and a high-impact security operations assistant should not necessarily use identical thresholds.

The organization should test both normal usage and adversarial or unusual conditions.

#### 5.8 Establish Change Management and Lifecycle Governance

The organization should govern changes that can materially affect RAG behavior or risk.

| Change | Required Review |
|---|---|
| Add a new repository | Source approval, data classification, authorization, and risk review |
| Change document permissions | Access-control impact and retrieval authorization testing |
| Change document-processing logic | Extraction quality and ingestion regression testing |
| Change chunking strategy | Retrieval relevance and context-completeness evaluation |
| Change embedding model | Retrieval regression testing and provider review where relevant |
| Change vector database or search service | Architecture, security, performance, and data-processing review |
| Change language model | Model evaluation, security testing, and provider assessment |
| Change system instructions | Behavior evaluation, prompt-injection testing, and approval |
| Change retrieval parameters | Quality and authorization testing |
| Introduce conversation memory | Privacy, retention, isolation, and access-control review |
| Add tool integration | Security architecture, permissions, and action-boundary assessment |
| Change logging behavior | Privacy, evidence, and retention assessment |
| Retire a source | Removal, re-indexing, retention, and dependency review |
| Expand to a new user population | Authorization, privacy, and intended-purpose review |

A material change should not be considered safe solely because the application remains technically available.

#### 5.9 Establish Risk Acceptance and Exception Management

Exceptions should be documented and approved through a defined process.

Each exception should record:

- The requirement being waived or modified.
- The affected system and use case.
- The business justification.
- The risk created by the exception.
- Existing and compensating controls.
- The residual-risk rating.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approving authority.
- The duration of the exception.
- Required monitoring.
- Remediation actions and deadlines.
- Conditions that trigger reassessment or suspension.

An exception should not override a legal requirement or mandatory control that the organization has no authority to waive.

Where a weakness cannot be accepted within the relevant delegated authority, the matter should be escalated or the affected capability restricted.

#### 5.10 Establish Governance Evidence and Auditability

The enterprise should retain evidence that demonstrates how the RAG system is governed and operated.

Typical evidence includes:

- Approved use-case definitions.
- AI and knowledge-source inventories.
- Architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Source-approval records.
- Risk assessments and risk registers.
- Access-control configuration and reviews.
- Model and provider assessments.
- AI evaluation results.
- Security test and red-team reports.
- Change approvals and release records.
- Monitoring dashboards.
- Incident reports and corrective actions.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.
- Production readiness assessments.
- Training and operational handover records.
- Periodic governance reviews.

Evidence collection should be proportionate to risk. The organization should avoid logging excessive confidential content merely to improve traceability.

Where full prompts or retrieved passages are sensitive, the enterprise should determine whether metadata, identifiers, hashes, controlled evidence storage, or other mechanisms can provide sufficient investigative value without unnecessary exposure.

#### 5.13 Management Questions for Governance Approval

Before approving an enterprise RAG system, management should be able to answer:

1. What business purpose does the system serve?
2. Which users and information sources are in scope?
3. Who owns the accuracy and authorization of each source?
4. How are user permissions enforced during retrieval?
5. What prevents confidential information from being exposed through generated responses?
6. How are outdated or malicious documents identified and removed?
7. How is the system tested for prompt injection?
8. How are model providers and external data processing governed?
9. What evidence demonstrates that the system produces sufficiently reliable answers?
10. Which outputs require human review?
11. What events trigger incident response?
12. How are changes to sources, models, and prompts approved?
13. Which residual risks remain?
14. Who is authorized to accept those risks?
15. How can the system be restricted or retired safely?

The organization should not treat the completion of this checklist as automatic proof of compliance. Each requirement must be evaluated against the actual architecture, intended purpose, applicable obligations, and available evidence.

**Core Principle:** Foundational RAG governance must connect enterprise policy with enforceable technical controls, accountable ownership, documented evidence, and continuous review. The organization should be able to demonstrate not only that the RAG system has been deployed, but also that its information sources, access boundaries, AI behavior, and operational risks remain controlled throughout its lifecycle.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Policy vs. technical enforcement:** A written rule establishes an expectation; a technical control helps prevent or detect violations.
- **Source approval vs. source reliability:** Approval establishes permission for use, while reliability requires continuing attention to accuracy, currency, and integrity.
- **AI evaluation vs. security testing:** AI evaluation measures retrieval and answer quality; security testing examines threats, weaknesses, and unauthorized behavior. Both are necessary.
- **Compliance mapping vs. compliance assurance:** Identifying applicable requirements is not equivalent to demonstrating that the organization meets them.
- **Evidence collection vs. excessive logging:** Auditability must be balanced with confidentiality, privacy, minimization, and retention requirements.
- **Risk treatment vs. risk acceptance:** A treatment reduces or otherwise addresses risk; acceptance is a formal decision to retain residual risk within the authority of the designated owner.
- **Initial approval vs. lifecycle governance:** Approval establishes a controlled starting point; ongoing governance ensures the system remains appropriate as data, models, users, and business needs change.
