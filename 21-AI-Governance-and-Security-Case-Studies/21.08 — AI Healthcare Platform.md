# 21.08 — AI Healthcare Platform
## Part 1 — Business Context, System Architecture, Use-Case Definition, and Governance Foundations

## 1. Introduction to Enterprise AI Healthcare Platforms

### 1.1. What Is an AI Healthcare Platform?

An AI healthcare platform is a technology environment that uses artificial intelligence to support healthcare delivery, clinical decision-making, patient administration, medical research, diagnostics, and healthcare operations.

Unlike general enterprise AI applications, healthcare AI operates in an environment where inaccurate outputs, unauthorized data disclosure, system unavailability, or inappropriate automation can directly affect patient safety and clinical outcomes.

An AI healthcare platform may incorporate:

- Machine learning models that analyze medical images.
- Natural language processing systems that extract information from clinical notes.
- Generative AI assistants that summarize patient records.
- Clinical decision-support systems that provide diagnostic or treatment recommendations.
- Predictive models that estimate deterioration, readmission, or disease risk.
- AI-powered virtual assistants that answer patient questions.
- Systems that support hospital resource planning and patient scheduling.
- AI research platforms that analyze clinical trial and biomedical datasets.
- Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems that retrieve information from approved medical knowledge sources.
- AI agents that interact with electronic health record systems or other clinical applications.

An enterprise AI healthcare platform may combine several of these capabilities into a single environment.

For example, a hospital could deploy an AI assistant that retrieves information from a patient's electronic health record, summarizes relevant medical history, identifies possible medication conflicts, and presents a recommendation to a clinician.

Although this may improve efficiency, it introduces several governance questions:

1. Is the AI system authorized to access the patient's medical records?
2. Is the retrieved information accurate, complete, and current?
3. Can the model distinguish verified clinical information from generated content?
4. Does the system make recommendations within its approved intended purpose?
5. Is a qualified healthcare professional required to review its outputs?
6. Can the organization reconstruct how an output was generated?
7. What happens if the system produces an incorrect or potentially harmful recommendation?
8. Does the organization have sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the system is appropriately controlled?

These questions place AI healthcare governance at the intersection of cybersecurity, clinical safety, privacy, AI risk management, regulatory compliance, and operational resilience.

### 1.2. Why Healthcare AI Requires Enhanced Governance

Healthcare organizations process highly sensitive personal information and operate systems that may influence decisions about diagnosis, treatment, medication, and patient care.

The consequences of AI failure therefore extend beyond conventional information security incidents.

Consider the following scenarios.

| Scenario | Potential consequence | Primary governance concern |
|---|---|---|
| AI summarizes a patient record incorrectly | Clinician relies on an inaccurate medical history | Clinical safety, accuracy, human oversight |
| AI assistant exposes patient records to an unauthorized user | Confidential medical information is disclosed | Privacy, access control, cybersecurity |
| AI provides an incorrect medication recommendation | Potential medication-related harm | Clinical validation, intended purpose, safety controls |
| AI model is unavailable during a critical workflow | Clinicians lose access to an expected decision-support capability | Operational resilience, continuity of care |
| Malicious content in a clinical document manipulates an AI assistant | The system produces an unsafe recommendation or attempts unauthorized actions | Prompt injection, input validation, tool security |
| AI model is trained or configured using inappropriate data | Performance degrades or produces unreliable outputs | Data governance, model validation |
| AI system systematically performs worse for a particular patient population | Unequal or inappropriate clinical outcomes may result | Performance evaluation, fairness, clinical governance |
| AI vendor changes its model without adequate notice | Existing validation evidence may no longer reflect the deployed system | Third-party risk, change management |
| AI-generated clinical notes are written into an electronic health record without appropriate review | Incorrect information becomes part of the patient's medical record | Data integrity, authorization, clinical accountability |
| An AI agent modifies a medication order without proper authorization | A potentially dangerous clinical action occurs | Privileged access, human approval, transaction controls |

These scenarios demonstrate that healthcare AI risks are interconnected.

For example, a prompt-injection vulnerability may initially appear to be a cybersecurity issue. If the manipulated output influences treatment, the same event becomes a clinical safety incident. If patient information is disclosed, it may also become a personal-data breach.

A mature governance model must therefore evaluate the full chain of consequences rather than treating cybersecurity, clinical safety, and privacy as separate and unrelated disciplines.

### 1.3. Healthcare AI System Categories

The system's functionality and intended purpose determine the controls and assessments required.

| AI category | Example use case | Principal risk considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical documentation AI | Summarizes consultations and drafts clinical notes | Accuracy, omissions, record integrity, clinician review |
| Diagnostic support AI | Assists with interpreting radiology images | Clinical validity, false negatives, false positives, workflow integration |
| Predictive clinical AI | Estimates the risk of patient deterioration | Calibration, population representativeness, drift, escalation procedures |
| Medication-support AI | Identifies potential drug interactions | Clinical accuracy, source reliability, contraindication handling |
| Patient-facing conversational AI | Answers questions about appointments or approved health information | Medical misinformation, inappropriate reliance, privacy |
| Electronic health record assistant | Retrieves and summarizes patient information | Authorization, patient-record isolation, data provenance |
| Hospital operations AI | Predicts bed demand or staffing requirements | Availability, data quality, operational impact |
| Medical research AI | Analyzes clinical or biomedical datasets | Research governance, consent or other lawful basis, data protection |
| Medical imaging AI | Detects or classifies findings in scans | Diagnostic performance, validation, clinical workflow safety |
| Autonomous or agentic healthcare AI | Executes approved tasks across healthcare applications | Tool permissions, transaction integrity, human authorization, rollback |

The table is illustrative rather than a regulatory classification.

A system's regulatory status cannot be determined from its category name alone. The organization must assess its actual intended purpose, functionality, deployment context, applicable product rules, and relevant legal requirements.

### 1.4. The Difference Between Administrative and Clinical AI

One of the first governance tasks is distinguishing administrative healthcare AI from AI that may influence clinical decisions.

**Administrative healthcare AI** may support appointment scheduling, billing queries, document classification, or nonclinical workflow management.

**Clinical AI** may support or influence diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, triage, medication decisions, or other activities connected to patient care.

This distinction matters because clinical use may introduce additional safety, validation, professional oversight, and regulatory obligations.

However, an administrative label does not automatically make a system low risk.

For example, an AI system that classifies patient referrals could influence which patients receive earlier appointments. Although the system may be described as administrative, its outputs could affect access to care.

Similarly, an AI assistant that answers general patient questions may create clinical risks if patients interpret its responses as personalized medical advice.

The organization should therefore evaluate the system's actual consequences rather than relying solely on its departmental ownership or marketing description.

### 1.5. The Role of AI Security Governance in Healthcare

AI security governance establishes the policies, responsibilities, controls, and assurance mechanisms needed to manage AI-related risks throughout the system lifecycle.

For healthcare, it should integrate at least six governance dimensions.

| Governance dimension | Primary objective | Example governance question |
|---|---|---|
| AI governance | Control intended purpose, accountability, and permitted use | Who authorizes the system and defines its operational boundaries? |
| Clinical safety | Protect patients from unsafe or inappropriate system behavior | How are clinically significant errors identified and managed? |
| Cybersecurity | Protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability | Can unauthorized users access patient data or manipulate AI outputs? |
| Privacy and data protection | Ensure lawful and appropriate processing of personal data | Is access to health information justified and adequately controlled? |
| Model and data governance | Establish data quality, performance, and model accountability | Is the system sufficiently validated for the population and task? |
| Operational resilience | Maintain safe workflows during failures or disruptions | Can clinicians continue essential activities if the AI service becomes unavailable? |

These dimensions should operate together under an integrated governance framework.

A cybersecurity team may establish technical controls, but it should not independently determine whether a clinical recommendation is safe. Similarly, a clinical department may assess medical usefulness, but it should not independently approve a system without appropriate privacy, cybersecurity, and regulatory review.

**Core Principle:** Healthcare AI governance must address both the security of the technology and the consequences of its outputs. A system that protects patient data but produces unsafe clinical recommendations is not adequately governed.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Healthcare AI vs. clinical AI:** Healthcare AI includes administrative and operational applications; clinical AI directly supports or influences activities related to patient care.
- **AI accuracy vs. clinical safety:** A technically accurate model may still be unsafe if its outputs are used in an inappropriate workflow.
- **Cybersecurity risk vs. patient safety risk:** A cybersecurity weakness may become a patient safety hazard when it affects clinical decisions or care delivery.
- **Administrative purpose vs. administrative consequences:** A system labeled administrative may still affect clinical prioritization, access to care, or patient outcomes.

## 2. Business Use Cases, Intended Purpose, and Decision Boundaries

### 2.1. Defining the Business Use Case

Before procuring or deploying an AI healthcare platform, the organization should define the specific problem the system is intended to solve.

A business use case should identify:

- The healthcare problem or operational requirement.
- The intended users.
- The patient population or operational environment affected.
- The data the system will process.
- The outputs it will generate.
- The actions users may take based on those outputs.
- The potential consequences of incorrect outputs.
- The required level of human oversight.
- The system's limitations.
- The applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

Consider a hospital that wants to deploy a generative AI assistant to summarize electronic health records before clinical consultations.

A sufficiently detailed use-case definition could be:

> The system retrieves authorized information from a patient's electronic health record and generates a structured summary for review by an authorized healthcare professional. The summary is intended to support preparation for a consultation and does not independently establish a diagnosis, prescribe medication, or replace the clinician's review of the underlying medical record.

This definition establishes a boundary between information-support functionality and independent clinical decision-making.

It also provides a basis for determining access controls, validation requirements, user training, monitoring, and acceptance criteria.

### 2.2. Intended Purpose as a Governance Control

The intended purpose defines what the system is designed and authorized to do.

In healthcare, it is especially important because seemingly minor changes in functionality can materially change the risk profile.

For example:

**Initial purpose:** Summarize historical patient information.

**Expanded purpose:** Summarize patient information and highlight possible medication interactions.

**Further expanded purpose:** Recommend medication changes based on the patient's medical history.

These capabilities are not equivalent.

The first may primarily involve information retrieval and summarization. The second introduces medication-related decision support. The third may have substantially greater clinical implications and may trigger additional validation or regulatory requirements, depending on its design and intended purpose.

An organization should not treat these changes as ordinary feature enhancements.

A material change in intended purpose should trigger reassessment of:
- Clinical safety.
- Regulatory classification.
- Model validation.
- Data requirements.
- Privacy implications.
- Cybersecurity threats.
- Human oversight.
- User training.
- Operational controls.
- Production authorization.

### 2.3. Defining Permitted, Restricted, and Prohibited Uses

A healthcare organization should maintain explicit rules governing how its AI systems may be used.

The following example concerns a generative AI assistant used to summarize patient records.

| Use classification | Example | Governance treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Permitted | Summarize authorized patient history for clinician review | Allow under approved access, validation, and review controls |
| Permitted with conditions | Identify possible inconsistencies in clinical documentation | Require defined review procedures and documented limitations |
| Restricted | Generate preliminary clinical recommendations | Permit only if specifically assessed, authorized, and supported by appropriate safeguards |
| Restricted | Draft clinical notes for entry into the medical record | Require appropriate verification and authorization before finalization |
| Restricted | Retrieve information from multiple patient records | Require strict authorization, patient-context validation, and data-isolation controls |
| Prohibited under the example deployment policy | Access records outside the user's authorization | Block and investigate |
| Prohibited under the example deployment policy | Independently prescribe medication when the system is not authorized for that function | Prevent the action through technical and procedural controls |
| Prohibited under the example deployment policy | Modify a patient's treatment plan without required authorization | Prevent unauthorized write actions |
| Prohibited under the example deployment policy | Use patient data for an unrelated purpose without appropriate authorization | Block or subject to a separate lawful approval process |

These are example organizational controls, not universal legal classifications. The organization must define the applicable boundaries for each actual system.

A permitted-use policy is most effective when enforced through both technical controls and operational procedures.

For instance, stating that an AI assistant must not modify medical records is insufficient if the assistant has unrestricted write access to the electronic health record system.

### 2.4. Clinical Decision Boundaries

Clinical decision boundaries specify which decisions the AI system may support and which remain subject to professional judgment or other formal authorization.

A healthcare organization should identify whether the system:

1. Retrieves information.
2. Summarizes existing information.
3. Identifies patterns or anomalies.
4. Generates suggestions.
5. Produces risk scores.
6. Recommends a clinical action.
7. Initiates an operational action.
8. Executes a clinical transaction.
9. Makes or materially influences a decision affecting patient care.

The higher the potential consequence, the more carefully the organization should assess the required controls and authorization model.

An example of a bounded workflow is shown below.

```text id="ahp-p1-01"
AUTHORIZED CLINICIAN
        |
        v
AUTHENTICATED ACCESS TO PATIENT RECORD
        |
        v
PATIENT CONTEXT AND PERMISSION VALIDATION
        |
        v
AI RETRIEVES APPROVED CLINICAL INFORMATION
        |
        v
AI GENERATES STRUCTURED SUMMARY
        |
        v
OUTPUT VALIDATION AND SOURCE TRACEABILITY
        |
        v
CLINICIAN REVIEWS SUMMARY AND SOURCE RECORDS
        |
        v
CLINICIAN DETERMINES WHETHER INFORMATION IS USEFUL
        |
        v
AUTHORIZED CLINICAL WORKFLOW
```

The workflow illustrates a system that supports clinician review rather than independently determining a diagnosis or treatment.

Even in this model, human review does not eliminate all risks. The organization should assess whether users can realistically identify errors, whether the underlying information is accessible, and whether the interface encourages excessive reliance on generated summaries.

### 2.5. Human Oversight Must Be Meaningful

Human oversight is a governance requirement that must be implemented in practice, not simply stated in a policy.

A meaningful oversight process should consider:

- Whether the reviewer has the appropriate clinical qualifications.
- Whether the reviewer understands the system's limitations.
- Whether the reviewer can access the underlying source information.
- Whether the reviewer has enough time to evaluate the output.
- Whether the reviewer can reject or override the recommendation.
- Whether the system communicates uncertainty and missing information.
- Whether the reviewer is accountable for the decision being made.
- Whether the organization monitors inappropriate reliance on AI outputs.

For example, a clinician who must approve hundreds of AI-generated recommendations within an unrealistic time limit may not be able to provide meaningful review.

Likewise, a system that displays a confident recommendation without identifying missing or contradictory information may encourage automation bias.

**Automation bias** occurs when a person gives excessive weight to an automated system's output, potentially overlooking contradictory evidence or exercising insufficient independent judgment.

Healthcare organizations should design workflows that support informed review rather than relying on the mere presence of an approval button.

### 2.6. Use-Case Risk Classification

A practical classification process should consider the system's intended purpose, affected population, degree of automation, and consequences of failure.

| Assessment factor | Lower-risk indication | Higher-risk indication |
|---|---|---|
| Intended purpose | Administrative assistance with limited patient impact | Diagnosis, treatment, triage, or other consequential clinical support |
| Output type | Retrieval of clearly identified source information | Clinical recommendation, risk score, or treatment-related suggestion |
| Automation | User-controlled information support | Automated action or substantial influence over clinical decisions |
| Data sensitivity | Limited operational information | Detailed health records, genetic data, or other highly sensitive information |
| Reversibility | Errors are easily detected and corrected before affecting care | Errors may be difficult to detect or may affect urgent treatment |
| Human oversight | Qualified review is feasible and supported | Review is absent, ineffective, or constrained by workflow design |
| Integration | Read-only access to a limited system | Write access to clinical records, orders, or connected medical systems |
| Affected population | Limited internal operational workflow | Broad patient population or particularly vulnerable patients |
| Model uncertainty | Outputs can be readily checked against reliable sources | Outputs are difficult to verify or uncertainty is poorly communicated |
| Regulatory considerations | No special product or high-risk requirements identified after assessment | Potentially applicable medical-device, high-risk AI, or other specialized obligations |

This table supports preliminary triage. It is not a substitute for formal risk assessment or legal classification.

### 2.7. Intended Purpose and Regulatory Classification

An AI healthcare platform may be subject to different regulatory requirements depending on its function.

The organization should determine whether the system is:
- A general-purpose administrative tool.
- A clinical workflow support system.
- A medical device or part of a medical device.
- An AI system subject to specific high-risk requirements.
- A combination of systems with different regulatory roles.

For example, under the EU Medical Device Regulation, software intended for a medical purpose may fall within the medical-device regulatory framework, depending on its intended purpose and applicable classification rules.

The EU AI Act may also impose obligations depending on the system's classification, role in the value chain, intended purpose, and applicable provisions. AI systems that are medical devices or safety components of products covered by specified EU harmonization legislation may be subject to high-risk AI requirements when the relevant legal conditions are met.

The organization should not assume that every healthcare AI application is automatically a medical device or automatically a high-risk AI system. Equally, it should not assume that a system is exempt merely because it is described as an assistant, a productivity tool, or a decision-support application.

The assessment should be documented and reviewed by appropriately qualified regulatory and legal specialists.

### 2.8. Use-Case Approval Record

Before deployment, the organization should maintain a formal use-case approval record.

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Use-case ID | Unique identifier for the proposed AI application |
| Business owner | Accountable healthcare or operational function |
| System owner | Person or function responsible for the deployed platform |
| Intended purpose | Clearly defined purpose and scope |
| Affected users | Clinicians, nurses, administrative staff, patients, or other users |
| Affected population | Patient groups or operational populations potentially affected |
| Data categories | Health records, medical images, patient identifiers, operational data, or other information |
| AI capabilities | Retrieval, summarization, prediction, recommendation, or action execution |
| Decision boundary | Decisions supported, actions permitted, and actions prohibited |
| Human oversight | Required qualifications, review steps, and escalation procedures |
| Regulatory assessment | Applicable regulatory frameworks and documented rationale |
| Risk classification | Organizational risk rating and supporting rationale |
| Required controls | Security, privacy, clinical safety, model, and operational controls |
| Approval status | Proposed, approved, conditionally approved, rejected, or suspended |
| Review triggers | Material changes, incidents, performance degradation, or regulatory developments |

**Core Principle:** The intended purpose and decision boundary determine what the AI system is allowed to do and what safeguards are required. Changes to the system's function or influence over clinical decisions must trigger a proportionate governance reassessment.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Intended purpose vs. actual use:** A formally approved purpose is insufficient if users or integrations cause the system to operate beyond that scope.
- **Information support vs. clinical decision-making:** Summarizing existing information is different from recommending or executing clinical actions.
- **Human approval vs. meaningful human oversight:** A nominal approval step does not guarantee effective review.
- **Organizational risk classification vs. regulatory classification:** An internal risk rating does not replace a formal assessment under applicable legislation.
- **Feature enhancement vs. material scope change:** A new capability may alter clinical, privacy, cybersecurity, or regulatory risk and therefore require renewed approval.

## 3. Enterprise AI Healthcare Architecture and Data Flows

### 3.1. Reference Architecture

An enterprise AI healthcare platform commonly integrates with electronic health records, clinical information systems, medical imaging platforms, identity services, data repositories, and external AI services.

The architecture must protect both the AI components and the surrounding healthcare systems.

A reference architecture may contain the following layers:

1. **User access layer:** Interfaces used by clinicians, nurses, administrators, patients, or researchers.
2. **Identity and access layer:** Authentication, authorization, role management, and privileged access.
3. **Application and orchestration layer:** Workflow services, API gateways, policy enforcement, and AI orchestration.
4. **Data integration layer:** Controlled connections to electronic health records, laboratory systems, imaging platforms, and other approved sources.
5. **AI processing layer:** Models, inference services, retrieval components, and approved AI agents.
6. **Knowledge and data layer:** Clinical documents, approved medical knowledge sources, vector databases, and relevant data stores.
7. **Security and governance layer:** Logging, monitoring, policy enforcement, risk management, model evaluation, and audit evidence.
8. **Operational resilience layer:** Backup, recovery, failover, service continuity, and controlled fallback procedures.

### 3.2. Identity and Access Management

Healthcare AI should use the organization's established identity and access-management controls wherever possible.

Authentication establishes who is accessing the system. Authorization determines what that identity is permitted to access or perform.

These functions must remain distinct.

For example, a clinician may successfully authenticate to an AI assistant but should only retrieve records for patients and purposes permitted by the organization's access policies.

Important controls include:

- Single sign-on where appropriate.
- Multifactor authentication for relevant access scenarios.
- Role-based access control (RBAC).
- Attribute-based access control (ABAC) where contextual authorization is required.
- Least-privilege access.
- Privileged access management.
- Session management.
- Service identity governance.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Prompt revocation of access when roles change.
- Audit logging of sensitive operations.

Healthcare authorization may need to consider more than the user's job title.

Relevant contextual attributes can include the care relationship, treatment team, department, patient assignment, emergency-access status, and purpose of access.

A user should not receive unrestricted access to patient information simply because the AI application has broad technical permissions.

### 3.3. Patient Context and Record Isolation

One of the most important healthcare AI controls is ensuring that the system retrieves information from the correct patient record and does not mix information belonging to different individuals.

Potential failure scenarios include:
- The wrong patient identifier is passed to the retrieval service.
- Search results include records from multiple patients.
- Cached context is reused across user sessions.
- A conversation continues after the active patient changes.
- A retrieval service fails to enforce patient-level authorization.
- An AI agent uses an identifier supplied by an untrusted input without validating it.

The consequences may include privacy breaches, inaccurate clinical summaries, and potentially harmful recommendations.

Required safeguards should include:
1. Validate patient identity and context before retrieval.
2. Enforce authorization at the data-access layer.
3. Bind retrieval requests to an authorized patient context.
4. Prevent unauthorized cross-patient retrieval.
5. Isolate sessions and cached information appropriately.
6. Revalidate context when the active patient changes.
7. Log access to sensitive records.
8. Test cross-patient isolation under adversarial and failure conditions.
9. Fail securely when patient context is missing or ambiguous.
10. Provide a controlled procedure for authorized emergency access where applicable.

The AI model itself should not be the sole mechanism responsible for enforcing patient-data isolation. Authorization must be enforced by trusted application and data-access controls.

### 3.4. Electronic Health Record Integration

Electronic health records (EHRs) contain structured and unstructured information such as diagnoses, medications, allergies, laboratory results, clinical notes, treatment plans, and encounter history.

An AI assistant may access these records through APIs, integration engines, healthcare interoperability interfaces, or other approved mechanisms.

The integration architecture should define:

- Which records the AI application can retrieve.
- Which data fields are necessary for the use case.
- Whether access is read-only or includes write operations.
- How patient identity is validated.
- How authorization is enforced.
- How data is transmitted and stored.
- How errors and incomplete responses are handled.
- How requests and responses are logged.
- How the organization detects unauthorized changes.
- How data retention and deletion are managed.

Where applicable, standards such as HL7 and FHIR may support healthcare data exchange. Using an interoperability standard does not automatically make an integration secure; the organization must still configure authentication, authorization, data minimization, validation, and logging.

### 3.5. Clinical Data Sources and Their Risks

| Data source | Typical information | Principal governance risks |
|---|---|---|
| Electronic health record | Diagnoses, consultations, treatment plans | Unauthorized access, inaccurate summaries, inappropriate disclosure |
| Laboratory information system | Test results and reference ranges | Misinterpretation, incomplete results, stale information |
| Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) | Medical images | Unauthorized image access, incorrect patient association |
| Radiology Information System (RIS) | Imaging requests and reports | Inaccurate report summarization, workflow errors |
| Pharmacy system | Medication orders and dispensing information | Medication errors, unauthorized order changes |
| Clinical knowledge repository | Guidelines, clinical procedures, approved reference documents | Outdated guidance, malicious content, unreliable sources |
| Patient portal | Patient-submitted information and communications | Impersonation, misleading inputs, unauthorized disclosure |
| Medical device data source | Measurements and observations | Device integrity, incorrect timestamps, data-quality issues |
| Research repository | Clinical trial and biomedical data | Purpose limitation, consent or other lawful basis, re-identification |
| External AI service | Model inference and related processing | Supplier dependency, data disclosure, limited transparency |

Data owners should define the approved use of each source and the controls needed before the source is connected to the AI system.

### 3.6. Retrieval-Augmented Generation in Healthcare

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines information retrieval with generative AI.

A healthcare RAG application may retrieve relevant content from authorized patient records or approved clinical knowledge sources and provide that content to a language model as context for generating a response.

A simplified workflow is:

```text id="ahp-p1-03"
AUTHORIZED USER REQUEST
          |
          v
IDENTITY AND PURPOSE VALIDATION
          |
          v
AUTHORIZED RETRIEVAL QUERY
          |
          v
SOURCE ACCESS AND FILTERING
          |
          v
RELEVANT CONTENT RETRIEVED
          |
          v
SOURCE AND CONTEXT VALIDATION
          |
          v
AI GENERATES RESPONSE
          |
          v
OUTPUT VALIDATION AND PROVENANCE
          |
          v
AUTHORIZED USER REVIEWS RESULT
```

RAG can improve access to relevant information, but it does not guarantee factual correctness.

The system may:
- Retrieve irrelevant documents.
- Retrieve outdated clinical guidance.
- Miss important patient information.
- Confuse similar medical terms.
- Use information from the wrong patient record.
- Generate unsupported conclusions.
- Fail to distinguish source statements from model-generated interpretation.

Controls should therefore include source authorization, document freshness, provenance tracking, retrieval-quality evaluation, patient-context isolation, output validation, and appropriate clinical review.

For systems used in clinical contexts, the organization should also define how users verify important statements against authoritative source information.

### 3.7. AI Agents and Clinical System Integrations

Some healthcare AI platforms use agents that can call tools or execute workflow actions.

Possible tools include:
- Retrieving a patient's medication list.
- Checking appointment availability.
- Drafting a clinical note.
- Creating a referral request.
- Preparing a medication order for authorized review.
- Updating an administrative record.
- Sending a message to a healthcare professional.

The risk profile increases when the system can modify records or initiate actions rather than simply retrieve information.

A secure architecture should distinguish between:
- Read operations.
- Draft or preparation operations.
- Actions requiring explicit human approval.
- Actions permitted to execute automatically under narrowly defined rules.
- Actions that the AI system is not authorized to perform.

Each tool should have a narrowly scoped identity and explicit authorization rules.

For example, an AI assistant that summarizes medications may need read access to medication history but should not automatically receive permission to modify prescriptions.

Where write operations are permitted, controls should include transaction validation, authorization checks, approval workflows, audit logging, idempotency where appropriate, and rollback or correction procedures.

### 3.8. Data Flow and Trust Boundaries

An enterprise healthcare AI architecture should identify where sensitive data moves and where trust changes.

Important boundaries may include:
- Clinician workstation to AI application.
- AI application to electronic health record.
- Orchestration service to model provider.
- Retrieval service to clinical knowledge repository.
- Cloud environment to on-premises healthcare systems.
- Internal healthcare network to external supplier.
- AI application to medical record write interface.

Each boundary should be assessed for confidentiality, integrity, availability, authorization, and monitoring.

| Trust boundary | Key question | Example control |
|---|---|---|
| User to application | Is the user authenticated and authorized? | SSO, MFA, session controls |
| Application to EHR | Can the application access only approved records? | API authorization and patient-context enforcement |
| Orchestrator to model | What data is sent to the model, and under what conditions? | Data minimization, approved endpoint, contractual controls |
| Retrieval service to data store | Can retrieval bypass source-level authorization? | Authorization-aware retrieval and access enforcement |
| Internal environment to external provider | Is data disclosure controlled? | Encryption, supplier review, transfer assessment where required |
| AI output to clinical workflow | Can an unverified output directly alter care? | Output validation and required human approval |
| AI agent to tool | Can the agent execute unauthorized actions? | Narrow tool permissions and transaction-level authorization |
| Logging system to analytics environment | Can logs expose unnecessary health information? | Data minimization, access control, retention limits |

### 3.9. Architecture Review Requirements

Before approving the architecture, the review team should confirm:

1. The system's intended purpose is documented.
2. All relevant components and integrations are inventoried.
3. Data flows and trust boundaries are documented.
4. Patient identity and authorization are validated before data retrieval.
5. Sensitive data is minimized in prompts and model requests.
6. External model processing has been assessed and approved.
7. Clinical knowledge sources have defined owners and update procedures.
8. RAG retrieval respects access permissions.
9. AI outputs cannot bypass required clinical approval.
10. Tool permissions are narrowly scoped.
11. Material actions are logged.
12. Security monitoring and incident response are operational.
13. Failure modes and fallback procedures are documented.
14. Model and configuration changes are controlled.
15. The architecture supports the organization's applicable legal and regulatory obligations.

**Core Principle:** Healthcare AI security must be enforced across the entire data and decision flow. The model should not be trusted to enforce patient authorization, protect clinical records, or prevent unauthorized actions on its own.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Authentication vs. authorization:** Confirming a user's identity does not establish permission to access every patient record.
- **Data interoperability vs. data security:** Using healthcare exchange standards does not eliminate the need for access controls and secure integration.
- **RAG vs. verified medical knowledge:** Retrieval provides context but does not guarantee that the information is current, complete, or clinically appropriate.
- **Read access vs. write access:** Retrieving clinical information is materially different from changing medical records or initiating clinical transactions.
- **Model safeguards vs. architectural controls:** Model-level protections are supplementary; critical authorization and transaction controls must be enforced by trusted system components.

## 4. Healthcare AI Stakeholders, Accountability, and Governance Structure

### 4.1. Why Healthcare AI Requires Cross-Functional Governance

Healthcare AI cannot be governed effectively by a single technical team.

The organization must coordinate stakeholders responsible for patient care, clinical quality, information security, privacy, data management, AI model performance, regulatory compliance, procurement, and operations.

Each stakeholder evaluates different consequences of the same system.

For example, a model update may:
- Improve the speed of clinical documentation.
- Change the model's output behavior.
- Introduce new cybersecurity vulnerabilities.
- Alter the processing of personal data.
- Affect the evidence supporting a previous clinical validation.
- Change the regulatory assessment.
- Require additional user training.

The decision to approve the update must therefore consider its complete impact.

A cross-functional governance model should ensure that the right specialists participate in decisions while preserving clear accountability.

### 4.2. Core Stakeholder Groups

**Executive Management and Board-Level Oversight**

Executive management establishes organizational risk appetite, assigns accountability, and ensures that the organization has adequate resources to govern AI.

Responsibilities may include:
- Approving the overall AI governance strategy.
- Reviewing material AI risks.
- Ensuring appropriate clinical safety and cybersecurity oversight.
- Authorizing significant risk decisions within delegated authority.
- Reviewing serious incidents and systemic control weaknesses.

The board or a designated board committee may provide oversight where required by the organization's governance arrangements.

**Clinical Leadership**

Clinical leadership is responsible for clinical governance within its remit.

Responsibilities may include:
- Defining clinical use cases.
- Establishing clinical acceptance criteria.
- Evaluating clinical utility and limitations.
- Approving clinical workflows.
- Defining professional oversight requirements.
- Reviewing clinically significant incidents.
- Determining whether a system remains appropriate for its intended clinical use.

Clinical leadership should not be expected to independently resolve all cybersecurity, privacy, or AI regulatory questions.

**Chief Information Officer and IT Leadership**

IT leadership oversees the technology environment and its integration with enterprise systems.

Responsibilities may include:
- Ensuring that the AI platform fits the approved architecture.
- Managing integration dependencies.
- Establishing operational support.
- Coordinating change management.
- Maintaining availability and recovery capabilities.
- Ensuring that system ownership is clearly assigned.

**Chief Information Security Officer and Information Security**

Information Security is responsible for assessing and managing cybersecurity risks within its mandate.

Responsibilities may include:
- Threat modeling.
- Security architecture review.
- Identity and access control requirements.
- Vulnerability management.
- AI security testing.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Incident response.
- Supplier security assessment.
- Security exception management.

**Data Protection Officer and Privacy Function**

The Data Protection Officer (DPO), where appointed, performs the responsibilities established by applicable law. Other privacy personnel may support operational privacy management.

Responsibilities may include:
- Advising on personal-data processing.
- Reviewing lawful basis and purpose limitation.
- Assessing data minimization and retention.
- Advising on DPIA requirements.
- Reviewing transparency and individual-rights procedures.
- Supporting personal-data breach assessment.
- Advising on international data transfers where relevant.

The DPO's role should not be confused with the responsibility of the controller or other accountable organizational functions for complying with applicable data protection requirements.

**AI Governance and AI Risk Management**

AI Governance coordinates the management of AI-specific risks and the organization's AI control framework.

Responsibilities may include:
- Maintaining AI system inventories.
- Coordinating use-case assessments.
- Defining risk assessment methods.
- Maintaining governance requirements.
- Tracking model limitations and risk treatments.
- Coordinating approvals and reassessments.
- Maintaining evidence of governance decisions.

**Model Owner and Data Science or AI Engineering**

The model owner is responsible for the system's model-related lifecycle within the assigned mandate.

Responsibilities may include:
- Documenting model behavior and limitations.
- Managing evaluation datasets and test procedures.
- Monitoring performance and drift.
- Supporting model validation.
- Managing model versions and configuration changes.
- Investigating unexpected outputs.
- Maintaining technical documentation.

The model owner should not unilaterally approve a clinical use case simply because the model performs well against technical benchmarks.

**Healthcare Data Owners and Data Governance**

Data owners define appropriate access and use of data under their responsibility.

Responsibilities may include:
- Approving data access.
- Establishing data-quality expectations.
- Maintaining provenance and lineage.
- Defining retention and deletion requirements.
- Identifying sensitive data categories.
- Reviewing proposed secondary uses.

**Procurement and Third-Party Risk Management**

These functions assess external providers and establish appropriate supplier controls.

Responsibilities may include:
- Performing due diligence.
- Reviewing supplier security and privacy commitments.
- Assessing subcontractors and dependencies.
- Reviewing data-processing terms.
- Assessing incident-notification commitments.
- Evaluating service continuity and exit arrangements.

**Legal and Regulatory Compliance**

Legal and Compliance assess applicable obligations and support their implementation.

Responsibilities may include:
- Assessing AI regulatory requirements.
- Reviewing contractual commitments.
- Advising on medical-device and other healthcare-specific rules.
- Reviewing employment or professional requirements where relevant.
- Supporting regulatory inquiries and reporting decisions.
- Assessing restrictions on intended use.

**Internal Audit**

Internal Audit independently evaluates the design and effectiveness of governance and controls within its approved scope.

Responsibilities may include:
- Reviewing AI governance arrangements.
- Assessing control evidence.
- Evaluating risk management and accountability.
- Identifying control deficiencies.
- Tracking agreed audit actions.

Internal Audit should preserve its independence and should not become the operational owner of controls it later audits.

### 4.4. Accountability for Clinical and Technical Decisions

The organization should distinguish between different types of approval.

| Decision | Primary accountable role or function | Required consultation |
|---|---|---|
| Approve the business use case | Business or clinical owner | AI Governance, Legal, Privacy, Security |
| Define clinical acceptance criteria | Clinical owner | Relevant clinical specialists, Model Owner |
| Approve technical architecture | Designated technology or architecture authority | Security, Privacy, Data Owners, System Owner |
| Approve access to patient data | Authorized data owner or delegated authority | Privacy, Security, clinical stakeholders |
| Approve model validation approach | Designated model governance authority | Clinical specialists, AI Risk, independent validators where required |
| Approve cybersecurity controls | Designated Security authority | System Owner, IT Operations, AI Engineering |
| Approve privacy assessment | Controller's accountable governance process, informed by the Privacy function and DPO where applicable | Legal, Security, system owner |
| Approve supplier onboarding | Authorized procurement or third-party risk authority | Security, Privacy, Legal, business owner |
| Accept material residual risk | Authorized risk owner under delegated authority | Relevant control owners and governance functions |
| Authorize production deployment | Designated production or business approval authority | Clinical, AI Governance, Security, Privacy, IT Operations |
| Suspend unsafe functionality | Designated operational or incident authority under documented procedures | Clinical Safety, Security, System Owner, AI Governance |
| Approve restoration after a serious incident | Authorized recovery or change authority | Relevant clinical, security, privacy, and operational stakeholders |

The final decision structure must be aligned with applicable law, organizational policy, and the system's regulatory status.

### 4.5. Three Lines of Accountability

An organization may use a three-lines model to distinguish operational ownership, risk oversight, and independent assurance.

**First line — Operational ownership**

Includes clinical teams, system owners, IT Operations, data owners, and AI engineering teams responsible for operating the system and implementing controls.

**Second line — Risk and compliance oversight**

Includes functions such as AI Governance, Enterprise Risk, Information Security oversight, Privacy, Compliance, and relevant clinical safety oversight functions, depending on the organization's structure.

These functions establish requirements, challenge risk decisions, monitor compliance, and support escalation.

**Third line — Independent assurance**

Internal Audit or another appropriately independent assurance function evaluates the design and effectiveness of governance and controls.

The three-lines model does not mean that only the second line manages risk. Operational owners remain responsible for managing risks arising from their activities.

### 4.6. Governance Forums and Escalation

The organization should establish forums that match the system's complexity and risk.

| Forum | Purpose | Typical participants | Escalation triggers |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI governance committee | Oversee AI use cases and material AI risks | Executive sponsor, AI Governance, clinical leadership, risk functions | Material residual risk, significant use-case changes |
| Clinical safety review | Evaluate clinical hazards and safety evidence | Clinical owner, patient-safety specialists, model owner | Potential patient harm, unsafe outputs |
| Security architecture review | Assess technical design and cybersecurity controls | Security architects, IT, AI engineering | Critical design weaknesses, unauthorized access paths |
| Privacy review | Assess personal-data processing | Privacy specialists, DPO where applicable, legal, system owner | High privacy risk, material changes in data use |
| Model validation review | Evaluate model suitability and limitations | Model owner, clinical experts, independent validators where appropriate | Material performance degradation or failed validation |
| Operational service review | Monitor availability, incidents, and service performance | System owner, IT Operations, Security | Service disruption, recurring incidents |
| Executive risk escalation | Resolve material risk and resource issues | Authorized executive decision-makers | Risk beyond delegated authority or appetite |

A governance forum should produce documented decisions, assigned actions, deadlines, and escalation records.

### 4.7. Governance Records and Decision Traceability

For a healthcare AI platform, governance records should enable the organization to reconstruct the decisions that shaped the system's use.

Relevant records may include:
- Approved intended purpose.
- System inventory entry.
- Risk classification and rationale.
- Clinical safety assessment.
- AI risk assessment.
- Security and privacy assessments.
- Supplier due diligence.
- Model validation and evaluation reports.
- Human-oversight procedures.
- Production approval.
- Material change approvals.
- Risk acceptance records.
- Incident and corrective-action records.
- Monitoring and reassessment reports.

The organization should define which records are required, who owns them, where they are maintained, who can access them, and how long they must be retained.

Recordkeeping should itself respect privacy and data minimization. Auditability does not justify indiscriminate collection of patient information in logs.

**Core Principle:** Effective healthcare AI governance assigns distinct responsibilities for clinical safety, technical security, privacy, model performance, and business accountability while ensuring that these functions make coordinated decisions.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Clinical accountability vs. technical accountability:** A technical team may manage the model, but clinical owners remain responsible for decisions assigned to their clinical governance remit.
- **DPO advice vs. controller accountability:** The DPO advises and monitors as required by law; the controller remains responsible for compliance with its applicable obligations.
- **Operational risk ownership vs. second-line oversight:** Control owners manage risks in their activities, while oversight functions challenge and monitor the adequacy of risk management.
- **Governance committee vs. independent assurance:** A committee makes or oversees decisions; independent assurance evaluates whether governance and controls are effective.
- **Consultation vs. approval authority:** Participating in a review does not automatically confer the right to authorize a system or accept its risks.

## 5. Foundational Governance Requirements and Enterprise Policy Principles

### 5.1. Establishing a Healthcare AI Governance Policy

An enterprise healthcare AI governance policy establishes the mandatory principles and responsibilities governing the acquisition, development, deployment, use, monitoring, and retirement of AI systems.

The policy should apply proportionately to the organization's AI use cases, taking into account their purpose, risk, affected population, and applicable requirements.

It should define:
- Which systems are considered AI for governance purposes.
- Which organizational functions are covered.
- Who can propose and approve AI use cases.
- How risks are assessed and classified.
- What approvals are required before deployment.
- How patient data may be accessed and used.
- How model validation and clinical safety are addressed.
- How human oversight is established.
- How cybersecurity and privacy controls are implemented.
- How third-party AI systems are assessed.
- How material changes are managed.
- How incidents are reported and investigated.
- How risks and performance are monitored.
- How systems are suspended, withdrawn, or retired.

The policy should be supported by detailed standards, procedures, and technical controls.

### 5.2. Core Policy Principles

**Principle 1 — Patient Safety First**

AI systems that may influence patient care must be assessed for the potential consequences of inaccurate, incomplete, misleading, or unavailable outputs.

Clinical safety requirements should be proportionate to the system's intended purpose and potential impact.

**Principle 2 — Defined and Authorized Intended Purpose**

AI systems must operate within a documented and approved scope.

Material changes to functionality, automation, data sources, or clinical influence must trigger reassessment.

**Principle 3 — Lawful and Appropriate Data Processing**

Patient information must be processed in accordance with applicable privacy and healthcare requirements.

Access should be limited to authorized purposes, and data collection should be proportionate to the use case.

**Principle 4 — Security by Design**

AI systems must incorporate appropriate controls for identity, authorization, data protection, secure integration, vulnerability management, logging, and incident response.

Security requirements should be addressed during procurement and design, not added only after deployment.

**Principle 5 — Evidence-Based Validation**

The organization must establish that the system is sufficiently evaluated for its intended purpose.

Evaluation should consider relevant accuracy measures, limitations, failure modes, population characteristics, and the consequences of incorrect outputs.

**Principle 6 — Meaningful Human Oversight**

Where human oversight is required, the process must enable appropriate reviewers to understand, challenge, and respond to AI outputs.

The level of oversight should reflect the system's function and potential impact.

**Principle 7 — Accountability and Traceability**

Each AI system must have designated owners, documented decision rights, and records supporting material governance decisions.

**Principle 8 — Third-Party Accountability**

Outsourcing AI development or hosting does not automatically eliminate the organization's own responsibilities.

Supplier roles, security commitments, data-processing arrangements, incident notification, change management, and exit provisions should be assessed.

**Principle 9 — Continuous Monitoring**

AI governance must continue after production approval.

The organization should monitor relevant performance, security, privacy, clinical safety, and operational indicators.

**Principle 10 — Controlled Change and Withdrawal**

Material changes must undergo appropriate review before implementation.

The organization must also be able to restrict, suspend, or retire AI functionality when risks become unacceptable or required controls can no longer be maintained.

### 5.3. Minimum Governance Requirements by Lifecycle Stage

| Lifecycle stage | Minimum governance requirement | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Proposal | Define the business need and intended purpose | AI use-case request |
| Initial assessment | Identify affected stakeholders, data, and potential risks | Preliminary risk assessment |
| Procurement | Assess supplier capabilities, obligations, and limitations | Supplier due-diligence report |
| Design | Define architecture, trust boundaries, and control requirements | Security and data-flow diagrams |
| Development or configuration | Apply approved data, security, and model governance controls | Configuration records and development evidence |
| Validation | Evaluate system suitability and relevant risks | Testing and validation reports |
| Deployment | Verify readiness and obtain required approvals | Production readiness assessment |
| Operation | Monitor performance, incidents, and control effectiveness | Monitoring dashboard and incident records |
| Change | Reassess material changes and authorize releases | Change request and approval |
| Retirement | Revoke access, manage data retention, and complete secure decommissioning | Retirement and deletion records |

### 5.4. Risk-Based Control Proportionality

Not every AI healthcare application requires identical controls.

The organization should calibrate its requirements to:
- The system's intended purpose.
- The severity and likelihood of potential harm.
- The sensitivity of the information processed.
- The degree of automation.
- The potential for clinical influence.
- The reversibility of incorrect outputs.
- The affected patient population.
- The system's regulatory status.
- The complexity of its integrations.
- The organization's ability to detect and correct failures.

For example, an AI tool that categorizes nonclinical administrative emails may require a different level of clinical validation from a model that supports treatment decisions.

However, proportionality should not be interpreted as permission to omit controls that are mandatory under applicable law or essential to manage a material risk.

### 5.5. Governance Documentation Hierarchy

A practical policy structure can be organized into five levels.

**Level 1: Enterprise AI Governance Policy**

Defines executive commitments, governance principles, accountability, and mandatory organizational expectations.

**Level 2: Healthcare AI Governance Standard**

Defines common requirements for healthcare AI use cases, including risk assessment, validation, human oversight, and monitoring.

**Level 3: Domain-Specific Requirements**

Contains specialized requirements for clinical safety, cybersecurity, privacy, data governance, and model risk management.

**Level 4: Operating Procedures and Control Guides**

Explains how teams perform assessments, approve changes, respond to incidents, and carry out required controls.

**Level 5: System-Specific Evidence**

Contains records demonstrating how requirements are implemented for a particular AI system.

This hierarchy connects organizational policy to operational practice and auditable evidence.

### 5.6. Policy Exceptions and Risk Acceptance

The policy should define how exceptions are requested, assessed, approved, monitored, and closed.

An exception request should include:
- The requirement for which an exception is requested.
- The business justification.
- The affected system and scope.
- The risk created by the exception.
- Potential patient safety implications.
- Privacy and regulatory implications.
- Compensating controls.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The authorized approver.
- The expiration date.
- The review and closure criteria.

An exception should not be used to authorize an unlawful use, bypass a mandatory requirement, or ignore an unacceptable patient safety hazard.

Where an essential control cannot be implemented, the organization should consider restricting the functionality, selecting an alternative solution, or rejecting the use case.

### 5.7. Governance Metrics and Management Reporting

The policy should require periodic reporting of meaningful governance indicators.

Possible metrics include:

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Percentage of AI healthcare systems with approved intended purposes | Measures governance coverage |
| Percentage of systems with completed risk assessments | Measures risk-assessment completeness |
| Percentage of systems with current validation evidence | Measures validation governance |
| Number of overdue high-risk remediation actions | Highlights unresolved risk |
| Number of unauthorized patient-data access incidents involving AI | Measures relevant access-control failures |
| Number of material model changes without completed approval | Identifies change-control weaknesses |
| Number of significant AI-related clinical safety incidents | Tracks clinically significant events |
| Percentage of required staff completing AI-specific training | Measures training coverage |
| Percentage of systems with tested incident response and fallback procedures | Measures operational readiness |
| Percentage of material suppliers with current risk assessments | Measures third-party governance coverage |
| Number of systems operating outside their approved intended purpose | Identifies use-boundary violations |
| Percentage of required governance reviews completed on time | Measures governance execution |

Metrics should have defined owners, calculation methods, reporting periods, thresholds, and escalation requirements.

A low incident count should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of safety. It may reflect weak detection, incomplete reporting, or limited monitoring.

Similarly, a high number of identified issues may reflect improved detection rather than deteriorating control effectiveness. Metrics should be interpreted in context.

### 5.8. Management Review and Continual Improvement

Management should periodically assess whether the governance framework remains appropriate and effective.

The review should consider:
- Changes in healthcare operations.
- New AI use cases.
- Changes in applicable regulations.
- Clinical safety incidents.
- Cybersecurity incidents.
- Privacy events.
- Model performance and drift.
- Supplier changes.
- Audit findings.
- Control exceptions.
- Staff training and competency.
- Patient or clinician feedback.
- Effectiveness of corrective actions.

The outcome should include documented decisions, updated priorities, assigned actions, and review dates.

Continual improvement should focus on reducing risk and improving the organization's ability to detect, prevent, and respond to failures.

### 5.9. Foundational Governance Acceptance Criteria

Before an AI healthcare use case progresses into detailed design or deployment, the organization should be able to demonstrate:

1. A defined business need and intended purpose.
2. An accountable business or clinical owner.
3. An identified system owner.
4. An initial assessment of patient safety implications.
5. An initial privacy and cybersecurity assessment.
6. A documented regulatory applicability assessment or a plan to complete it before the relevant decision gate.
7. Identified data sources and access requirements.
8. Defined decision boundaries and human-oversight expectations.
9. A preliminary supplier and dependency assessment.
10. A documented risk-management approach.
11. Clear approval and escalation routes.
12. A plan for validation, monitoring, and incident management.

These criteria establish the minimum foundation for controlled progression. They do not constitute production approval.

**Core Principle:** Enterprise healthcare AI governance must connect policy, risk assessment, clinical safety, cybersecurity, privacy, model validation, and operational accountability throughout the system lifecycle. The organization should be able to demonstrate not only that requirements exist, but also that they are implemented, monitored, and enforced.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Policy vs. procedure:** A policy defines mandatory expectations; a procedure explains how those expectations are implemented.
- **Risk-based proportionality vs. control exemption:** Controls can be calibrated to risk, but proportionality does not override mandatory legal requirements.
- **Governance coverage vs. governance effectiveness:** Having an inventory or policy is not proof that risks are adequately managed.
- **Risk acceptance vs. legal authorization:** An internal approval cannot make a prohibited activity lawful.
- **Initial assessment vs. production approval:** Early governance approval permits controlled progression, not automatic authorization for clinical deployment.
- **Monitoring vs. continual improvement:** Monitoring identifies changes and failures; continual improvement uses the findings to update controls, procedures, and governance decisions.
