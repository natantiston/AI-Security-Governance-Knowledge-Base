# 21.07 — AI Recruitment Platform
## Part 1 — Business Context, System Architecture, Use-Case Definition, and Governance Foundations

## 1. Introduction to AI-Powered Recruitment Platforms

### 1.1. What Is an AI Recruitment Platform?

An AI recruitment platform is a technology system that uses artificial intelligence to support or automate activities throughout the employee recruitment lifecycle. These systems may analyze CVs, extract candidate information, rank applicants, match candidates to job descriptions, conduct assessments, generate interview questions, summarize interviews, or recommend hiring decisions.

Organizations deploy AI recruitment systems to manage high application volumes, reduce administrative workload, improve candidate matching, standardize recruitment workflows, and support talent acquisition teams.

However, AI-assisted recruitment creates significant governance challenges because its outputs can influence access to employment opportunities. An inaccurate or biased recommendation may disadvantage qualified candidates, reinforce historical discrimination, or cause recruiters to overlook applicants whose experience does not conform to the patterns learned by the model.

Consequently, an AI recruitment platform must be governed as more than an ordinary HR application. Its governance should address:

- Employment-related decision-making.
- AI model risk and performance.
- Discrimination and fairness.
- Personal data protection.
- Cybersecurity and confidentiality.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Human oversight and accountability.
- Third-party and vendor risk.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Auditability and candidate redress.

The central governance question is not simply whether the AI can identify promising candidates. It is whether the organization can demonstrate that the system is appropriate for its intended recruitment purpose, operates within approved boundaries, protects candidates' rights, and produces outcomes subject to effective oversight.

### 1.2. Common AI Recruitment Capabilities

AI recruitment platforms may contain several capabilities with different risk profiles.

| AI capability | Description | Potential benefit | Principal governance concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| CV parsing | Extracts employment history, qualifications, skills, and other information from CVs | Reduces manual data entry | Incorrect extraction, excessive data collection, and inaccurate candidate profiles |
| Candidate-job matching | Compares candidate profiles against job requirements | Helps recruiters identify potentially suitable applicants | Poor matching criteria and systematic exclusion of qualified candidates |
| Applicant ranking | Assigns scores or ranks to applicants | Prioritizes recruiter review | Discrimination, unjustified ranking criteria, and automation bias |
| Candidate screening | Recommends whether applicants should progress to the next stage | Reduces screening workload | Unfair rejection and insufficient human review |
| Skills assessment | Evaluates technical, cognitive, or role-specific capabilities | Supports structured evaluation | Invalid assessments and unequal performance across candidate groups |
| AI interview analysis | Analyzes interview responses, transcripts, or other recorded information | Helps organize interview evidence | Privacy risks, unreliable inferences, and inappropriate behavioral or emotional analysis |
| Interview summarization | Produces summaries of interviews and candidate responses | Saves recruiter time | Hallucinations, omitted information, and distorted summaries |
| Candidate chatbot | Answers candidate questions and supports scheduling | Improves accessibility and responsiveness | Disclosure of confidential information and inaccurate recruitment guidance |
| Talent sourcing | Searches candidate databases and identifies potential applicants | Expands sourcing capabilities | Unlawful or excessive data collection and inappropriate profiling |
| Recruitment forecasting | Estimates hiring demand, recruitment timelines, or candidate availability | Supports workforce planning | Inaccurate forecasts and overreliance on historical patterns |
| Generative job descriptions | Drafts job advertisements and role requirements | Accelerates content preparation | Exclusionary language, fabricated requirements, and inconsistent job criteria |
| Automated recommendation generation | Produces candidate recommendations or proposed hiring decisions | Supports recruiter decision-making | Recommendations being treated as authoritative without sufficient scrutiny |

The organization should not classify every capability as having the same risk level. A system that extracts dates and qualifications from a CV may create different risks from a system that scores applicants and automatically rejects those below a threshold.

Risk classification should reflect the system's actual functionality, intended purpose, operational context, degree of automation, and potential impact on candidates.

### 1.3. AI Recruitment System vs. Traditional Applicant Tracking System

An Applicant Tracking System (ATS) manages recruitment workflows, candidate records, communications, interview schedules, and hiring-stage administration.

An AI recruitment platform may be integrated into an ATS or may operate as a separate service. The presence of AI capabilities changes the governance requirements when the system generates predictions, rankings, classifications, or recommendations that influence recruitment outcomes.

| Dimension | Traditional ATS | AI Recruitment Platform |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Manage recruitment workflows and records | Perform recruitment administration and potentially AI-driven analysis |
| Candidate evaluation | Primarily based on recruiter-defined criteria and manual review | May use models to score, classify, rank, or recommend candidates |
| Decision logic | Often based on configured workflows and explicit rules | May include statistical models, machine learning, or generative AI |
| Explainability | Rules and workflow configurations may be directly inspectable | Model outputs may require additional explanation and validation |
| Bias risk | Can reflect biased policies or recruiter practices | Can reproduce existing bias or introduce new patterns of discrimination |
| Data requirements | Candidate records and recruitment information | May additionally use derived features, historical data, embeddings, and model inputs |
| Monitoring | Workflow performance and service availability | Workflow performance plus model, data, fairness, and output-quality monitoring |
| Change management | Application and configuration changes | Application, configuration, model, feature, prompt, and training-data changes where applicable |
| Governance | HR, IT, privacy, security, and compliance | Those functions plus appropriate AI and model-risk governance |

**Important distinction:** Traditional systems are not automatically fair or low-risk, and AI-enabled systems are not automatically unsuitable. Governance must assess the specific function, context, and consequences of each system.

## 2. Business Use Cases, Intended Purpose, and Decision Boundaries

### 2.1. Defining the Intended Purpose

The intended purpose describes what the AI recruitment system is designed and authorized to do.

It should be documented before procurement, configuration, development, or production deployment. A vague purpose such as "improve recruitment efficiency" is insufficient because it does not establish which activities the system may perform or how its outputs may affect candidates.

A sufficiently detailed intended-purpose statement should identify:

1. The recruitment activities supported by the system.
2. The candidate population and roles covered.
3. The types of information processed.
4. The outputs generated.
5. The decisions influenced by those outputs.
6. The degree of automation.
7. The role of human reviewers.
8. The permitted and prohibited uses.
9. The expected operational benefits.
10. The measurable criteria used to evaluate success.

**Example intended-purpose statement**

The organization deploys an AI recruitment platform to extract job-related information from candidate applications, compare documented qualifications against approved role requirements, and provide structured recommendations to authorized recruiters. The system supports recruiter assessment but does not independently make final hiring decisions. Recruiters remain responsible for evaluating recommendations, verifying material information, documenting relevant decisions, and following approved recruitment policies.

This statement establishes a defined boundary. If the organization later intends to use the platform for automated rejection, personality inference, emotion analysis, or employee performance prediction, it must assess whether the changed purpose is permitted and what additional controls and approvals are required.

### 2.2. Recruitment Use-Case Classification

Each AI-enabled recruitment activity should be assessed individually.

| Use case | Example system output | Key risk questions | Initial governance consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| CV data extraction | Extracted qualifications and work history | Are extracted fields accurate? Are sensitive or irrelevant details captured? | Data quality, privacy, and human verification |
| Skills matching | Match percentage against a job description | Are job criteria valid, necessary, and job-related? | Criteria governance and matching validation |
| Candidate ranking | Ranked list of applicants | Does the ranking systematically disadvantage particular groups? | Fairness testing, explainability, and oversight |
| Automated rejection | Applicants rejected below a score threshold | Is automated rejection legally permissible and appropriately governed? | High scrutiny of legal basis, impact, validity, and human oversight |
| Interview transcription | Text transcript of an interview | Is recording lawful, transparent, secure, and proportionate? | Privacy, access control, retention, and accuracy |
| Interview summarization | Summary of candidate responses | Does the summary omit or distort relevant information? | Output validation and recruiter review |
| Video interview analysis | Scores derived from video, speech, or behavior | Are the inferences valid, necessary, and legally permissible? | Elevated legal, ethical, privacy, and scientific-validity scrutiny |
| Candidate chatbot | Answers to candidate questions | Could the chatbot disclose personal data or provide misleading information? | Access boundaries, approved knowledge sources, and escalation |
| Internal mobility matching | Recommendations for existing employees | Could recruitment data be repurposed for employment decisions without appropriate authority? | Purpose limitation and employment-data governance |
| Talent-pool segmentation | Groups candidates by inferred characteristics or suitability | Are segmentation criteria appropriate and transparent? | Profiling, fairness, and data protection review |

The classification is a starting point, not a substitute for a formal risk assessment.

### 2.3. Defining Decision Boundaries

A decision boundary specifies what the AI system may recommend, what it may execute, and which decisions remain with authorized people.

The distinction is particularly important when the system influences hiring, shortlisting, or rejection.

A recruitment platform may be configured to:

- Extract information without making recommendations.
- Identify apparent matches against approved criteria.
- Rank applicants for recruiter review.
- Recommend candidates for an interview.
- Recommend rejection based on configured criteria.
- Automatically reject applicants.
- Generate a proposed final hiring decision.

These capabilities have different implications. Increasing automation may increase efficiency, but it can also reduce opportunities to identify errors, challenge inappropriate recommendations, or correct unfair outcomes.

A decision-boundary matrix should specify the permitted behavior.

| Activity | Permitted AI role | Human responsibility | Governance requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extracting CV information | Extract and organize information | Verify material fields where needed | Accuracy testing and correction process |
| Comparing qualifications | Identify matches against approved criteria | Assess the relevance and completeness of the comparison | Criteria approval and validation |
| Ranking candidates | Generate an advisory ranking where approved | Evaluate candidates in accordance with recruitment policy | Fairness monitoring and review |
| Recommending rejection | Provide a recommendation only if specifically approved | Review and make the decision under the approved process | Legal review, documented criteria, and enhanced oversight |
| Making final hiring decisions | Only if the intended purpose, applicable law, and governance framework permit the arrangement | Retain the responsibilities required by law and policy | Formal approval, safeguards, and appropriate decision traceability |
| Evaluating personality or emotions | Prohibit by default unless specifically assessed and lawfully authorized | Apply approved, job-relevant assessment methods | Specialized legal, validity, and impact assessment |
| Communicating with candidates | Provide approved information and perform authorized workflow actions | Handle exceptions and sensitive questions | Approved content, escalation, and privacy controls |

Human involvement should be meaningful rather than ceremonial. A recruiter who routinely accepts AI recommendations without reviewing the underlying evidence may not provide effective oversight.

### 2.4. Defining Prohibited Uses

The organization should establish prohibited or restricted uses to prevent function creep and inappropriate decision-making.

Examples of uses that should be prohibited or subjected to explicit legal and governance approval include:

- Using protected characteristics as unauthorized selection criteria.
- Using proxy variables to reproduce prohibited discriminatory decisions.
- Inferring a candidate's emotions, personality, or psychological condition from facial expressions or voice without a specific and defensible legal basis.
- Collecting unrelated personal information simply because it is available.
- Scraping candidate information from external sources without assessing lawful access, transparency, relevance, and purpose.
- Using recruitment data for unrelated employee monitoring.
- Allowing an AI system to invent qualifications, employment history, or assessment results.
- Rejecting candidates solely because their CV formatting differs from the formats commonly represented in historical data.
- Using a model outside the job families, languages, locations, or candidate populations for which it has been appropriately assessed.
- Allowing recruiters to override approved controls through unauthorized spreadsheets, scripts, or manual scoring processes.
- Using candidate data to train or improve a third-party model without appropriate authorization and legal assessment.

Prohibitions should be translated into enforceable controls, configuration restrictions, contractual requirements, training, monitoring, and escalation procedures.

### 2.5. Defining Business Success Without Creating Harmful Incentives

Organizations often measure recruitment success through time-to-hire, cost-per-hire, recruiter productivity, and application-processing volume.

These metrics are useful but insufficient. If the organization optimizes only for speed and cost, it may inadvertently encourage the system to reject more candidates, reduce human review, or prioritize profiles resembling historical hires.

A balanced performance framework should include:

| Performance dimension | Example indicator | Governance purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Efficiency | Median time from application to initial review | Measures workflow improvement |
| Accuracy | Correctness of extracted candidate information | Detects parsing and data-quality failures |
| Job relevance | Relationship between recommendations and approved job criteria | Assesses the relevance of AI-generated recommendations |
| Fairness | Differences in relevant outcomes across appropriately assessed groups | Identifies potential disparities requiring investigation |
| Human oversight | Percentage of required reviews completed and documented | Measures whether oversight procedures operate |
| Candidate experience | Complaint rate and candidate feedback | Identifies operational and communication problems |
| Privacy | Unauthorized disclosures and retention exceptions | Measures privacy-control effectiveness |
| Security | Relevant security incidents and unresolved vulnerabilities | Measures security exposure |
| Reliability | Service availability and failed-processing rate | Measures operational resilience |
| Accountability | Percentage of material decisions with required traceability | Supports investigation and auditability |

Metrics must be interpreted in context. A difference in outcomes between groups does not automatically establish unlawful discrimination, but it may indicate a problem requiring investigation. Conversely, similar aggregate outcomes do not prove that a system is fair.

## 3. Enterprise AI Recruitment Architecture and Data Flows

### 3.1. Reference Architecture

An enterprise AI recruitment platform typically connects candidate-facing channels, recruitment workflow systems, candidate data repositories, AI services, external providers, and enterprise identity and monitoring systems.

The architecture should make trust boundaries and data flows explicit because candidate information may move between several organizations and technical environments.

This architecture is illustrative. Actual deployments may use an ATS with embedded AI capabilities, external AI APIs, cloud-hosted models, or organization-managed models.

The security and governance assessment must follow the actual implementation rather than assuming that every component is separate.

### 3.2. Major Architecture Components

**Candidate-facing channels**

These are the points through which candidates submit applications, upload CVs, answer screening questions, or communicate with recruiters and chatbots.

Key concerns include:
- Unauthorized access to applications.
- Malware and malicious file uploads.
- Exposure of candidate information.
- Abuse of application or chatbot interfaces.
- Accessibility and language-related issues.
- Inadequate notice concerning AI-assisted processing.

**Applicant Tracking System**

The ATS maintains candidate records, recruitment stages, interview arrangements, recruiter assignments, and workflow history.

Key concerns include:
- Excessive recruiter permissions.
- Inappropriate access between business units or geographic regions.
- Inaccurate candidate records.
- Unauthorized changes to recruitment status.
- Incomplete audit trails.
- Inadequate retention and deletion.

**Data governance layer**

This layer ensures that candidate information is appropriately sourced, validated, classified, protected, and retained.

Key concerns include:
- Unverified data provenance.
- Incorrect CV extraction.
- Collection of irrelevant information.
- Uncontrolled feature engineering.
- Inappropriate use of historical hiring decisions.
- Inconsistent data retention across systems.

**AI recruitment services**

These services process candidate information and generate extracted fields, matches, scores, summaries, or recommendations.

Key concerns include:
- Biased training or evaluation data.
- Inappropriate model assumptions.
- Prompt injection against document-processing or generative AI components.
- Unauthorized model changes.
- Data leakage to external services.
- Inadequate testing for different candidate populations.

**Decision support layer**

This layer combines model outputs with approved recruitment criteria and presents recommendations to recruiters.

Key concerns include:
- Undocumented ranking logic.
- Unauthorized threshold changes.
- Excessive reliance on AI-generated scores.
- Missing explanations or reason codes.
- Uncontrolled automated rejection.
- Failure to record human overrides.

**Recruiter workspace**

Recruiters review candidate information, interview records, model recommendations, and hiring-related information.

Key concerns include:
- Excessive privileges.
- Automation bias.
- Disclosure of candidate information.
- Inconsistent review procedures.
- Failure to document reasons for material decisions.

### 3.3. Candidate Data Classification

The organization should classify candidate information according to sensitivity, purpose, and applicable legal requirements.

| Data category | Examples | Principal governance considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Identity information | Name, contact details, candidate identifier | Access control, minimization, retention |
| Employment information | Job history, employer names, dates of employment | Accuracy, relevance, source verification |
| Education and qualifications | Degrees, certifications, training | Accuracy and job relevance |
| Skills and experience | Technical skills, professional experience, language proficiency | Validity of extraction and matching criteria |
| Recruitment assessments | Interview notes, test results, recruiter evaluations | Confidentiality, access, retention, and fairness |
| AI-generated information | Extracted skills, match scores, summaries, recommendations | Accuracy, traceability, and appropriate use |
| Special-category personal data | Health information or other data covered by applicable special-category rules | Strict legal assessment and additional safeguards where applicable |
| Background-check information | Information obtained through authorized checks | Legal basis, relevance, access, and retention |
| Video and audio data | Interview recordings and transcripts | Necessity, transparency, security, and retention |
| System and audit data | Access logs, model versions, decision records | Integrity, controlled access, and proportional retention |

The organization should not assume that every item in a CV is appropriate for AI processing merely because the candidate submitted it. The data must be assessed against the recruitment purpose, relevant legal requirements, and the organization's policies.

### 3.4. Data-Flow Analysis

A complete data-flow analysis should document how candidate information enters the platform, how it is transformed, where it is stored, which services process it, and how outputs affect recruitment workflows.

A simplified flow is:

```text
CANDIDATE SUBMITS APPLICATION
              |
              v
APPLICATION RECEIVED AND VALIDATED
              |
              v
CANDIDATE DATA STORED IN ATS
              |
              v
AUTHORIZED DATA EXTRACTED
              |
              v
AI SERVICE PROCESSES DATA
              |
              v
EXTRACTED FIELDS / MATCH SCORE / SUMMARY
              |
              v
OUTPUT VALIDATION AND POLICY CHECKS
              |
              v
RECRUITER REVIEWS RELEVANT EVIDENCE
              |
              v
AUTHORIZED RECRUITMENT DECISION
              |
              v
DECISION RECORD AND CANDIDATE COMMUNICATION
              |
              v
RETENTION, DELETION, OR RESTRICTED ARCHIVING

```

The institution should separately document data flows for:
- External AI APIs.
- Model training and evaluation.
- Analytics and reporting.
- Interview transcription and recording.
- Third-party assessment tools.
- Cross-border transfers.
- Backups and disaster recovery.
- Model monitoring and audit logs.
- Candidate correction and deletion requests.

The analysis should distinguish candidate data used for an individual recruitment decision from data used to develop, fine-tune, evaluate, or improve a model. These purposes may have different legal and governance requirements.

### 3.5. Trust Boundaries and Security Zones

A trust boundary separates components or environments with different security assumptions.

Typical boundaries include:

1. **Public-facing boundary:** Candidate portals, application interfaces, and chatbots.
2. **Application boundary:** ATS and recruitment workflow services.
3. **Data boundary:** Candidate databases, document repositories, and assessment records.
4. **AI processing boundary:** Model endpoints, inference services, prompts, and derived outputs.
5. **External-provider boundary:** Third-party AI services, assessment providers, and recruitment vendors.
6. **Administrative boundary:** Privileged accounts, model configuration, and production deployment.
7. **Monitoring boundary:** Security logs, audit records, and monitoring systems.

Each boundary should have appropriate authentication, authorization, data protection, logging, and monitoring controls.

For external AI services, the organization should determine whether submitted information may be retained, used for service improvement, accessed by provider personnel, or processed in other geographic regions. These details should be verified through the applicable service configuration, contract, and technical documentation rather than assumed.

### 3.6. Architecture-Level Threat Scenarios

| Threat scenario | Example | Potential impact | Governance response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Malicious CV upload | An applicant uploads a document containing malicious content | Compromise of document-processing components or downstream systems | File validation, malware scanning, sandboxing, and secure processing |
| Prompt injection through CV content | A CV includes instructions designed to manipulate a generative AI parser | Incorrect extraction, manipulated recommendations, or unauthorized tool behavior | Treat documents as untrusted input, isolate instructions from content, restrict tools, and validate outputs |
| Candidate data leakage | CVs are transmitted to an unapproved AI service | Confidentiality breach and possible legal noncompliance | Approved-provider controls, data minimization, contractual safeguards, and egress monitoring |
| Ranking manipulation | An unauthorized user changes scoring weights or thresholds | Unfair or unauthorized candidate prioritization | Role-based access, approval workflows, configuration logging, and change control |
| Model poisoning | Training or evaluation data is manipulated | Degraded or systematically biased recommendations | Data provenance, dataset access controls, integrity checks, and validation |
| Excessive recruiter privileges | A recruiter accesses records outside their recruitment responsibility | Unauthorized disclosure or misuse of candidate data | Least privilege, access reviews, segregation of duties, and monitoring |
| Model drift | Candidate profiles or labor-market conditions change | Reduced matching accuracy or unexpected outcome disparities | Performance monitoring, periodic validation, and reassessment |
| Insecure integration | ATS APIs accept unauthorized requests | Exposure or alteration of candidate records | Strong authentication, authorization, input validation, rate limiting, and API monitoring |
| Unauthorized model update | An unapproved model version enters production | Unvalidated recruitment recommendations | Controlled release pipelines, artifact integrity, approval records, and rollback |
| Audit-trail manipulation | A user alters recruitment records or deletes evidence of a change | Reduced accountability and inability to investigate decisions | Protected logging, restricted administrative access, and retention controls |

Architecture reviews should consider both traditional cybersecurity threats and AI-specific failure modes.

## 4. Stakeholders, Accountability, and the Recruitment Governance Model

### 4.1. Why Cross-Functional Governance Is Necessary

AI recruitment affects several organizational functions simultaneously.

Human Resources understands recruitment processes and job requirements. Technology operates the platform. Data teams manage information quality and integration. Cybersecurity evaluates technical threats. Privacy and Legal assess personal-data processing and legal obligations. Compliance identifies relevant regulatory requirements. AI governance and model-risk specialists evaluate the system's intended use, limitations, and broader risks.

No single department possesses all the expertise required to approve and operate a high-impact recruitment system responsibly.

A formal governance model should therefore establish clear responsibilities, decision rights, escalation paths, and reporting obligations.

### 4.2. Key Stakeholders and Responsibilities

| Stakeholder | Primary responsibilities | Governance evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Provides sponsorship, resolves escalated issues, and ensures appropriate resources | Program approval and executive decisions |
| Head of Talent Acquisition | Owns recruitment objectives, approved use cases, and recruitment-process integration | Business requirements and use-case approval |
| HR process owner | Defines recruitment workflows, assessment criteria, and operating procedures | Recruitment process documentation |
| AI system owner | Maintains accountability for the system's lifecycle and approved operation | AI system inventory and lifecycle records |
| Model owner | Oversees model documentation, limitations, performance, and change management | Model documentation and monitoring records |
| Recruiters and hiring managers | Review candidates and apply approved recruitment procedures | Review records and documented decisions |
| AI Governance Lead | Coordinates AI governance requirements, assessments, and oversight | AI risk assessment and governance records |
| Model Risk Management | Provides model-risk challenge and validation according to institutional policy | Independent validation and model-risk findings |
| CISO or Security Lead | Oversees cybersecurity assessment and security control requirements | Security assessment and remediation records |
| Data Protection Officer or Privacy Lead | Advises on applicable data-protection obligations and privacy risks | Privacy advice and assessment records |
| Legal and Compliance | Assesses applicable employment, AI, privacy, and sector requirements | Legal assessment and compliance mapping |
| Data Governance Lead | Oversees data quality, lineage, classification, and permitted use | Data inventory and lineage records |
| Data Science or AI Engineering | Develops, configures, evaluates, or integrates AI capabilities | Model development and testing evidence |
| MLOps or Platform Engineering | Controls deployment, model versioning, monitoring, and rollback | Release and operational records |
| Procurement | Coordinates supplier selection and contractual governance | Procurement and due-diligence records |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Assesses vendor security, resilience, and other supplier risks | Supplier risk assessment |
| Internal Audit | Provides independent assurance within its approved mandate | Audit reports and findings |
| Candidate Support or HR Operations | Handles candidate inquiries, corrections, complaints, and escalation | Case records and resolution evidence |

The institution should assign named roles to these responsibilities. A role title alone does not establish accountability unless its authority and obligations are documented.

### 4.4. Governance Artifacts

The organization should maintain a controlled set of governance artifacts.

| Artifact | Purpose | Typical owner |
|---|---|---|
| AI recruitment system inventory | Records system identity, purpose, owner, provider, and deployment context | AI system owner |
| Intended-purpose statement | Defines approved use and operational boundaries | Business owner |
| AI governance assessment | Identifies relevant AI risks and required oversight | AI Governance |
| Model documentation | Describes model design, intended use, limitations, and performance | Model owner |
| Data inventory and lineage | Records data sources, transformations, and destinations | Data Governance |
| Fairness assessment | Evaluates relevant risks of unequal or inappropriate outcomes | Credit-independent recruitment risk, HR, Compliance, and specialists as assigned |
| Privacy assessment | Evaluates personal-data processing and applicable obligations | Privacy function and responsible business owner |
| Security assessment | Identifies threats, vulnerabilities, and security controls | Security function |
| Vendor assessment | Evaluates external service and supplier risks | Third-Party Risk Management |
| Human oversight procedure | Defines how recruiters review, challenge, and escalate AI outputs | HR process owner |
| Model change log | Records material model and configuration changes | Model owner |
| Incident response plan | Defines handling of AI, privacy, security, and recruitment incidents | Relevant operational and risk owners |
| Production approval record | Demonstrates that required authorities approved deployment | Authorized approval body |
| Monitoring dashboard | Tracks model, operational, security, and relevant outcome indicators | Model and system owners |
| Audit evidence repository | Maintains records supporting independent review | Designated control and evidence owners |

The organization should avoid duplicating documentation without a clear purpose. Where a single assessment can support multiple governance processes, it may be reused if its scope, evidence, and conclusions genuinely address the requirements of each process.

### 4.5. Accountability for Human Oversight

Human oversight should be designed into the recruitment workflow.

A recruiter should be able to:
- Understand the intended purpose and limitations of the AI output.
- Access relevant candidate information needed for the assessment.
- Recognize that a score or recommendation may be incorrect.
- Challenge or disregard an AI recommendation where justified.
- Escalate suspected bias, data errors, or system defects.
- Record material overrides and the rationale for significant decisions.
- Follow approved procedures when the AI service is unavailable.
- Report patterns of unexpected or harmful outcomes.

The organization should monitor whether the oversight process is meaningful. Indicators may include review completion, override patterns, escalation rates, time spent reviewing recommendations, and evidence that recruiters examine relevant supporting information.

A high override rate is not automatically evidence of model failure, and a low override rate is not automatically evidence of model quality. Both should be interpreted alongside decision quality, workflow design, reviewer behavior, and other evidence.

## 5. Foundational Governance Requirements and Enterprise Policy Principles

### 5.1. Establish an AI Recruitment Governance Policy

The organization should maintain an AI governance policy or equivalent controlled framework that defines how AI may be used in recruitment.

The policy should align with the organization's wider AI governance, HR, cybersecurity, privacy, risk-management, and supplier-management frameworks.

At a minimum, it should establish requirements for:

- AI use-case registration and approval.
- Intended-purpose definition.
- Risk classification and assessment.
- Candidate data governance.
- Fairness and discrimination risk management.
- Model validation and performance monitoring.
- Human oversight and decision accountability.
- Cybersecurity and access management.
- Third-party and external AI service management.
- Transparency and candidate communication.
- Records retention and auditability.
- Incident response and remediation.
- Change management and periodic reassessment.
- Exceptions and risk acceptance.

The policy should be supported by operational procedures. A policy that states "AI must be fair and secure" is not sufficient without measurable requirements, accountable owners, implementation procedures, and evidence of control effectiveness.

### 5.2. Policy Principle 1 — Purpose Limitation

Candidate information should be processed only for approved and appropriate recruitment purposes, subject to applicable legal requirements.

The organization should prevent recruitment information from being repurposed for unrelated employee monitoring, profiling, marketing, or model training without the necessary authorization and legal assessment.

**Example:** A CV submitted for a particular job vacancy should not automatically be used to train an external AI model merely because the recruitment platform makes that function available.

### 5.3. Policy Principle 2 — Job-Relevant Criteria

AI recommendations should be based on approved criteria that are relevant to the role and appropriate for the recruitment purpose.

The organization should document:
- Why each material criterion is relevant.
- How the criterion is measured.
- Whether the criterion can create unjustified exclusion.
- How missing or ambiguous information is handled.
- Who approves changes to the criterion.

**Example:** A platform should not automatically downgrade a candidate solely because the candidate uses an unfamiliar job title when their documented skills and responsibilities may be equivalent to the stated requirements.

### 5.4. Policy Principle 3 — Fairness and Non-Discrimination

The organization should assess whether the system could produce inappropriate or unlawful differences in outcomes.

This assessment should consider:
- Historical recruitment data and its limitations.
- Candidate population differences.
- The relevance of selection criteria.
- Data representation and missing information.
- Potential proxy variables.
- Model performance across relevant populations where lawful and appropriate to assess.
- Recruitment outcomes and complaint patterns.
- The effects of human overrides and downstream decision rules.

Fairness testing must use a defensible methodology and be interpreted in context. No single statistical metric establishes that a recruitment system is fair in every legal, ethical, or operational sense.

### 5.5. Policy Principle 4 — Human Accountability

The organization must define who is accountable for recruitment decisions and what responsibilities remain with human decision-makers.

Human oversight procedures should specify when review is mandatory, what information must be considered, how concerns are escalated, and how decisions are documented.

The organization should not treat a human review step as effective merely because a recruiter clicks an approval button.

### 5.6. Policy Principle 5 — Transparency and Explainability

Candidates and recruiters should receive the information required by applicable law and organizational policy.

Recruiters should have enough information to understand the intended meaning, limitations, and appropriate use of AI-generated recommendations.

Where a model generates a score or ranking, the organization should determine whether it can provide meaningful, reliable explanations of the factors or criteria that influenced the result. Explanations must not imply a level of certainty or causal understanding that the system cannot support.

Candidate-facing transparency should be designed in coordination with HR, Legal, Privacy, and Compliance.

### 5.7. Policy Principle 6 — Data Quality and Integrity

The organization should implement controls to ensure that candidate information is sufficiently accurate, relevant, and complete for the intended purpose.

Controls should address:
- CV parsing errors.
- Duplicate records.
- Inconsistent job titles.
- Missing employment dates.
- Incorrect qualifications.
- Data-source reliability.
- Feature transformations.
- Data lineage.
- Unauthorized changes.
- Model input integrity.

The organization should define correction procedures so that material inaccuracies can be investigated and addressed.

### 5.8. Policy Principle 7 — Privacy and Confidentiality

The organization should assess the lawful basis, purpose, transparency, minimization, security, retention, and other applicable requirements associated with candidate-data processing.

Sensitive information should not be collected or processed merely because it might improve a model's apparent predictive performance.

Where special-category personal data or other highly sensitive information is involved, the organization should obtain appropriate legal and privacy guidance before processing.

The organization should also assess whether external providers may access candidate data, retain it, transfer it internationally, or use it for purposes beyond the organization's instructions.

### 5.9. Policy Principle 8 — Cybersecurity by Design

AI recruitment systems should be protected through appropriate security controls across the application, data, model, infrastructure, and integration layers.

Core controls may include:
- Least-privilege access.
- Strong authentication.
- Privileged-access management.
- Encryption in transit and at rest.
- Secure API integration.
- Secrets management.
- Input validation and file scanning.
- Vulnerability and dependency management.
- Secure model and configuration deployment.
- Protected logging.
- Monitoring and incident response.
- Backup, recovery, and rollback.

Security controls should address AI-specific attack surfaces, including malicious document content, prompt injection, unauthorized model changes, data leakage, and manipulation of model outputs.

### 5.11. Policy Principle 10 — Accountability and Auditability

The organization should retain records sufficient to demonstrate how the system was approved, configured, tested, deployed, and monitored.

Records may include:
- Use-case approvals.
- Risk assessments.
- Model and data documentation.
- Validation reports.
- Configuration and change records.
- Access reviews.
- Material recommendation and decision records.
- Incident reports.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Monitoring results.
- Remediation evidence.

Retention should be proportionate to the purpose, applicable legal requirements, and approved recordkeeping policies. Auditability should not become a justification for retaining candidate personal data indefinitely.

## Core Principle

An AI recruitment platform must be governed according to the consequences of its outputs, not merely the sophistication of its technology. A system that influences access to employment opportunities requires a clearly defined purpose, job-relevant criteria, reliable data, appropriate privacy and security controls, meaningful human oversight, and evidence that risks are being managed effectively.

The organization remains responsible for the recruitment process even when a third-party vendor supplies the AI model or the platform generates recommendations automatically.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Recruitment automation vs. recruitment decision-making:** Automating administrative work does not necessarily involve making decisions about candidates.
- **AI recommendation vs. authorized hiring decision:** A recommendation is an input to a governed process, not automatically a legitimate decision.
- **Model accuracy vs. recruitment validity:** A model can produce consistent predictions without measuring the qualities genuinely required for a role.
- **Human involvement vs. meaningful human oversight:** Human participation is effective only when reviewers have the ability, information, authority, and competence to challenge outputs.
- **Fairness testing vs. proof of non-discrimination:** Testing provides evidence about defined risks; it does not establish universal fairness or automatically determine legal compliance.
- **Data availability vs. permission to process:** Information accessible to the organization is not automatically appropriate or lawful to use for every recruitment purpose.
- **Vendor assurance vs. organizational accountability:** Supplier documentation supports due diligence but does not eliminate the deploying organization's responsibilities.
- **Policy approval vs. operational effectiveness:** A policy establishes requirements; implementation evidence demonstrates whether controls work in practice.
