# 21.10 — AI-Powered Network Operations
## Part 1: Business Context, System Architecture, Use Cases, and Governance Foundations

### 1. Introduction to AI-Powered Network Operations

AI-powered network operations refers to the use of artificial intelligence, machine learning, generative AI, and automated decision-support capabilities to monitor, analyze, troubleshoot, optimize, and maintain enterprise and telecommunications networks.

Traditional network operations rely on predefined rules, threshold-based alerts, network-management platforms, performance counters, configuration monitoring, and human-led troubleshooting. Although these mechanisms remain essential, modern networks generate increasingly large volumes of telemetry and operate across complex environments involving physical infrastructure, virtualized network functions, cloud platforms, software-defined networking, wireless access, and distributed applications.

AI can help network operations teams interpret these signals, identify anomalies, correlate events, predict potential failures, recommend remediation, and automate selected operational tasks.

However, introducing AI into network operations also creates new risks. Incorrect recommendations, manipulated telemetry, unauthorized configuration changes, model drift, or excessive automation can affect network availability, data confidentiality, service integrity, and business continuity.

In telecommunications environments, these risks may affect mobile networks, fixed broadband, voice services, enterprise connectivity, customer-facing digital services, and critical communications infrastructure.

From a Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) perspective, the objective is not simply to make network operations faster. It is to establish an AI-enabled operating model in which the organization can demonstrate that AI capabilities are authorized, appropriately controlled, secure, measurable, and accountable.

#### 1.1 What Is AI-Powered Network Operations?

AI-powered network operations applies AI capabilities to activities such as:

- Network performance monitoring.
- Anomaly detection and event correlation.
- Predictive maintenance.
- Root-cause analysis.
- Capacity planning and forecasting.
- Network configuration recommendations.
- Fault diagnosis and troubleshooting.
- Network traffic optimization.
- Security-related network anomaly detection.
- Change-impact analysis.
- Automated ticket classification and incident reporting.
- Controlled remediation of recurring network faults.

The exact capabilities depend on the network architecture, the available telemetry, the quality of operational data, and the degree of automation authorized by the organization.

For example, an AI system may analyze interface utilization, packet loss, latency, routing events, and device logs to identify a likely cause of service degradation. It may then recommend adjusting a configuration or rerouting traffic.

The recommendation should not automatically become an executed change. The organization must establish whether the action is technically valid, authorized, reversible, and safe for the affected services.

#### 1.2 Traditional Network Operations vs. AI-Powered Network Operations

| Dimension | Traditional Network Operations | AI-Powered Network Operations |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring | Thresholds, predefined rules, and dashboards | Traditional monitoring enhanced by anomaly detection and learned patterns |
| Event correlation | Static correlation rules and manual analysis | AI-assisted correlation across multiple telemetry sources |
| Troubleshooting | Engineer-led investigation using established procedures | AI-generated hypotheses supported by network evidence |
| Root-cause analysis | Manual examination of logs, metrics, topology, and changes | Automated analysis of relationships among events, dependencies, and historical incidents |
| Predictive maintenance | Scheduled maintenance and threshold-based alerts | Forecasting of potential failures based on available historical and current data |
| Capacity planning | Historical trends and manual forecasting | AI-assisted forecasting and scenario analysis |
| Configuration management | Approved scripts, templates, and change procedures | AI-assisted configuration analysis and, where authorized, controlled automation |
| Incident handling | Human-led triage and predefined workflows | AI-assisted prioritization, investigation, and remediation recommendations |
| Network optimization | Engineer-designed changes and optimization policies | AI-assisted optimization subject to constraints and validation |
| Operational accountability | Primarily assigned to engineers and network-management teams | Shared across network operations, AI system owners, security, risk, and governance stakeholders |
| New risk exposure | Misconfiguration, hardware failure, software defects, and operational error | Existing risks plus model error, adversarial manipulation, data poisoning, automation misuse, and AI-specific failures |
| Auditability | Configuration records, tickets, logs, and change approvals | Traditional evidence plus model versions, AI recommendations, tool calls, validation, and AI-related decision records |

AI does not eliminate traditional network-management requirements. It introduces additional capabilities and additional control obligations.

A well-governed deployment integrates AI with established network-management disciplines rather than bypassing them.

#### 1.3 Business Drivers for AI-Powered Network Operations

Organizations may introduce AI into network operations for several reasons.

| Business Driver | Operational Challenge | Potential AI Contribution | Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operational efficiency | Large volumes of repetitive troubleshooting tasks | Automated analysis and investigation assistance | Incorrect recommendations may create additional incidents |
| Service availability | Failures may affect multiple dependent services | Predictive indicators and faster fault correlation | False predictions or missed failures may affect availability |
| Mean time to repair | Engineers must examine multiple data sources | Evidence-based root-cause hypotheses | Unsupported conclusions may lead to inappropriate remediation |
| Network complexity | Distributed infrastructure creates many dependencies | Cross-domain event correlation | Incomplete topology information may produce misleading conclusions |
| Capacity management | Demand changes over time | Forecasting and resource optimization | Incorrect forecasts may cause congestion or unnecessary expenditure |
| Predictive maintenance | Failures may be difficult to anticipate | Identification of patterns associated with degradation | Poor data quality may create unreliable predictions |
| Configuration quality | Large networks contain many configuration objects | Configuration analysis and policy checks | AI-generated changes may violate approved standards |
| Security monitoring | Network behavior changes continuously | Anomaly detection and investigative support | Adversarial activity may manipulate telemetry or detection results |
| Customer experience | Network degradation affects customer-facing services | Early detection of performance deterioration | Optimization decisions may unfairly or incorrectly affect service allocation |
| Cost management | Manual operations and inefficient resource allocation increase costs | Resource forecasting and operational recommendations | Cost optimization must not undermine security or service obligations |

Business benefits should be expressed as measurable objectives.

For example, a project may target a reduction in median fault-investigation time while maintaining or improving the accuracy of fault diagnosis. Measuring speed alone would be insufficient because faster but incorrect remediation could increase service disruption.

#### 1.4 The AIOps Concept

AI for IT Operations, commonly referred to as AIOps, uses AI and machine learning to enhance IT operations through data analysis, event correlation, anomaly detection, forecasting, and automation.

AIOps can support infrastructure operations, application monitoring, service management, cloud operations, and network operations.

AI-powered network operations is a specialized application of this broader concept.

A typical AIOps capability may:

1. Collect telemetry from multiple operational systems.
2. Normalize and correlate events.
3. Identify unusual behavior.
4. Estimate likely causes or future failures.
5. Generate recommendations.
6. Trigger an approved workflow.
7. Measure the result.
8. Retain evidence for subsequent analysis.

The organization must distinguish between an analytical AIOps platform and an autonomous operational control system.

An analytical platform may recommend actions without changing the network. An autonomous control system may execute changes that directly affect routing, traffic handling, network configuration, or service availability.

The second capability generally requires more stringent controls because it can create direct operational consequences.

#### 1.5 Key AI Technologies Used in Network Operations

| Technology | Function | Example Network Operations Application | Important Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supervised machine learning | Learns patterns from labeled examples | Classifying network faults by probable cause | Performance depends on representative labels and data |
| Unsupervised learning | Identifies patterns without predefined labels | Detecting unusual traffic or performance behavior | Anomaly does not necessarily mean a fault or attack |
| Time-series forecasting | Estimates future values from historical observations | Predicting link utilization or equipment degradation | Unexpected events may invalidate historical patterns |
| Graph analytics | Examines relationships among connected entities | Analyzing dependencies between network components | Incomplete topology data can distort conclusions |
| Natural language processing | Processes operational text | Classifying incident tickets and summarizing engineering notes | Ambiguous or inaccurate text can produce incorrect classifications |
| Generative AI and large language models | Generates explanations, summaries, and recommendations | Producing troubleshooting hypotheses from logs and runbooks | Generated explanations may be plausible but unsupported |
| Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) | Grounds generation in retrieved information | Using approved network documentation and operational procedures | Retrieved information may be outdated, unauthorized, or maliciously manipulated |
| Reinforcement learning | Learns policies through feedback or rewards | Researching or optimizing selected network-control strategies | Poorly designed objectives can produce unsafe operational behavior |
| Digital twins and simulation | Represents a system for testing or analysis | Evaluating network changes in a simulated environment | Simulation results may not fully represent production conditions |
| Optimization algorithms | Searches for solutions within defined objectives and constraints | Capacity allocation and routing optimization | Incomplete constraints may produce operationally unacceptable solutions |

These technologies are not interchangeable. Each requires a suitable validation method and a clear definition of its permitted use.

For instance, a language model that summarizes a troubleshooting guide should not automatically be trusted to calculate a safe routing configuration. A forecasting model should not be treated as a deterministic guarantee that a device will fail.

### 2. Business Use Cases, Intended Purpose, and Decision Boundaries

A central AI governance requirement is to define the intended purpose of each AI use case.

A general statement such as "use AI to optimize the network" is insufficient for risk management. It does not identify the systems affected, the decisions being influenced, the potential consequences, or the permissions required.

Each use case should specify what the AI system is intended to do, which data it can access, what decisions it may support, what actions it may perform, and which activities remain prohibited.

#### 2.1 AI Network Operations Use-Case Catalogue

| ID | Use Case | AI Capability | Potential Business Benefit | Principal Risk | Initial Governance Position |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ANO-UC01 | Network anomaly detection | Identifies unusual telemetry patterns | Earlier identification of degradation or suspicious behavior | False positives and missed anomalies | Permit with performance monitoring |
| ANO-UC02 | Fault correlation | Correlates alarms and network events | Reduced investigation effort | Incorrectly merging unrelated incidents | Permit with evidence validation |
| ANO-UC03 | Root-cause analysis | Generates hypotheses about the underlying fault | Faster troubleshooting | Incorrect diagnosis leading to ineffective remediation | Recommendation only until validated |
| ANO-UC04 | Predictive maintenance | Forecasts potential equipment or link failures | Reduced unplanned downtime | Inaccurate forecasts and unnecessary interventions | Permit with independent validation |
| ANO-UC05 | Capacity forecasting | Predicts future network demand | Better resource planning | Underprovisioning or unnecessary expenditure | Permit with human review |
| ANO-UC06 | Configuration compliance | Compares device configurations against approved standards | Improved configuration consistency | Incomplete checks or false assurance | Permit with deterministic policy checks |
| ANO-UC07 | Configuration recommendation | Suggests changes to device configurations | Faster remediation and optimization | Unsafe or incompatible configurations | Require technical validation and change approval |
| ANO-UC08 | Automated ticket management | Classifies, summarizes, and routes incidents | Reduced administrative workload | Incorrect severity or routing | Permit with workflow controls |
| ANO-UC09 | Network optimization | Recommends changes to traffic distribution or routing | Improved performance and resource utilization | Service degradation or unintended traffic changes | Controlled pilot and approval requirements |
| ANO-UC10 | Automated fault remediation | Executes approved actions for recognized faults | Faster recovery from recurring incidents | Cascading failures and unauthorized changes | Restrict to explicitly approved playbooks |
| ANO-UC11 | Network security analytics | Identifies suspicious network behavior | Improved detection and investigation | Evasion, false positives, and manipulated telemetry | Permit with security validation |
| ANO-UC12 | Operational knowledge assistant | Retrieves and explains approved network documentation | Faster access to engineering knowledge | Prompt injection, stale guidance, or unauthorized disclosure | Permit with access controls and source traceability |
| ANO-UC13 | Change-impact analysis | Estimates potential effects of a proposed network change | Improved change planning | Missing dependencies and inaccurate impact estimates | Require engineering review |
| ANO-UC14 | Network incident reporting | Drafts incident reports and post-incident summaries | More consistent documentation | Unsupported conclusions or disclosure of sensitive information | Permit with accountable review |
| ANO-UC15 | Autonomous network control | Executes selected network changes based on changing conditions | Potentially faster and more adaptive control | Large-scale disruption or loss of control | Separate high-assurance assessment and authorization |

The initial governance positions are illustrative. Actual approval requirements depend on network criticality, action reversibility, the organization's risk appetite, and the applicable legal and contractual environment.

#### 2.2 Define the Intended Purpose

For every AI network operations use case, the organization should document:

- **Business objective:** The problem the system is intended to solve.
- **Operational environment:** The network domains, devices, and services in scope.
- **Users:** The people or operational teams who use the capability.
- **Data sources:** The telemetry, logs, configurations, topology information, and documentation involved.
- **AI function:** Detection, classification, prediction, generation, recommendation, or control.
- **Decision supported:** The operational decision influenced by the output.
- **Permitted actions:** The tasks the AI system may perform.
- **Prohibited actions:** Activities outside its approved authority.
- **Human oversight:** Required review, approval, and escalation.
- **Performance criteria:** How effectiveness and safety will be measured.
- **Failure conditions:** Situations requiring fallback, restriction, or suspension.
- **Accountability:** The system owner, operational owner, and risk owner.

For example, consider an AI assistant used to investigate packet loss across an enterprise network.

Its intended purpose might be to correlate interface errors, latency measurements, routing changes, and recent incident records to produce evidence-linked troubleshooting recommendations.

Its initial authorization might permit read-only access to approved telemetry and ticketing data. It would not be authorized to modify routing tables, change firewall rules, restart network equipment, or alter production configurations.

This distinction defines a manageable scope and limits the consequences of incorrect recommendations.

#### 2.3 Establish Network Operations Autonomy Levels

The organization should classify AI capabilities according to their operational authority.

The following model is an illustrative governance classification, not an official universal standard.

| Level | Name | Description | Example | Governance Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A0 | No operational AI | Network operations rely on traditional monitoring and human-led analysis | Engineers investigate alarms using dashboards and runbooks | Existing operational and security controls |
| A1 | AI-assisted observation | AI analyzes data and produces information without making operational recommendations | AI summarizes historical interface utilization | Data protection and output-quality controls |
| A2 | AI recommendation | AI recommends investigations or actions, but authorized personnel decide what to do | AI recommends checking a routing adjacency after identifying packet loss | Evidence validation and human review |
| A3 | Approved workflow execution | AI triggers predefined, bounded workflows under established authorization rules | AI opens an incident ticket or executes an approved read-only diagnostic workflow | Scoped permissions, audit logging, and workflow controls |
| A4 | Conditional autonomous remediation | AI executes specific preapproved remediation actions when defined conditions are satisfied | AI restarts an approved noncritical service after validated conditions are met | Strong safeguards, action constraints, rollback, monitoring, and tested failure handling |
| A5 | Adaptive autonomous network control | AI dynamically adjusts selected network behaviors within a formally approved control boundary | AI optimizes selected traffic paths within validated constraints | High-assurance engineering, independent validation, robust containment, and explicit authorization |

A higher level of autonomy generally increases the potential consequences of model errors, manipulated inputs, unexpected system behavior, and control failures.

However, autonomy level alone does not determine risk. A read-only AI assistant handling highly sensitive infrastructure information may still create significant confidentiality risk.

#### 2.4 Define Decision Boundaries

The organization should explicitly distinguish analytical decisions from operational decisions.

| Decision Category | Example | Can AI Perform It? | Required Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive analysis | Summarize latency measurements | Generally, yes | Preserve data accuracy and source traceability |
| Anomaly identification | Flag unusual interface errors | Yes, with validation | Define performance requirements and false-positive handling |
| Diagnostic hypothesis | Suggest a likely routing issue | Yes, as a recommendation | Identify supporting evidence and uncertainty |
| Change recommendation | Suggest a routing configuration update | Yes, subject to validation | Require engineering review and change control |
| Low-impact administrative action | Create or update an incident ticket | Potentially | Restrict permissions and validate workflow behavior |
| Production configuration change | Modify routing, switching, or firewall settings | Only under explicitly approved controls | Apply authorization, validation, testing, and rollback |
| Critical service intervention | Change a network component supporting critical services | Exceptional and tightly controlled | Require an appropriate safety and operational risk assessment |
| Emergency response | Execute a predefined containment action during a major incident | Only if specifically authorized | Define emergency authority, constraints, monitoring, and recovery |
| Risk acceptance | Accept the risk of a potentially disruptive network change | No | A designated accountable human authority must decide |

AI can support decision-making, but organizational risk acceptance and accountability must remain with the appropriately authorized people or governance bodies.

#### 2.5 Example: AI-Recommended Routing Change

Suppose an AI system identifies increasing latency and packet loss on a primary network path. It recommends shifting traffic to an alternative path.

The recommendation could be beneficial, but several questions must be answered before execution:

1. Is the telemetry accurate and sufficiently recent?
2. Is the identified congestion the actual cause of the problem?
3. Is the alternative path available and capable of carrying the additional traffic?
4. Will the change affect latency-sensitive or business-critical applications?
5. Are there routing policies, security inspection requirements, or contractual constraints?
6. Could the proposed change create an asymmetric traffic path?
7. Has the alternative path been validated against current network conditions?
8. Is the proposed change within the approved change window?
9. Can the change be reversed safely?
10. Who is authorized to approve execution?

The AI system may provide supporting analysis, but the organization must enforce the required validation and approval controls.

**Key distinction:** A technically plausible recommendation is not necessarily an operationally safe change.

### 3. Enterprise AI Network Operations Architecture and Data Flows

An enterprise AI-powered network operations platform commonly integrates network telemetry, observability platforms, network-management systems, configuration repositories, IT service management, AI models, orchestration tools, and human operational workflows.

The architecture must protect both the AI components and the underlying network infrastructure.

Unlike a conventional analytical dashboard, an AI-enabled platform may interpret untrusted data, generate instructions, and interact with operational tools. This creates trust boundaries between data ingestion, model inference, recommendation generation, authorization, and execution.

The architecture should prevent untrusted network data from becoming an unauthorized operational command.

#### 3.2 Architecture Component Responsibilities

| Component | Primary Function | Security and Governance Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Network infrastructure | Produces operational state and carries traffic | Device hardening, access control, secure management interfaces |
| Telemetry collection | Collects network events and measurements | Authenticated sources, protected transport, appropriate collection scope |
| Data ingestion | Validates and normalizes incoming information | Schema validation, malformed-input handling, source verification |
| Network operations data platform | Stores historical and current operational information | Data classification, access control, retention, integrity, and lineage |
| AI analytics layer | Detects patterns and produces analysis or recommendations | Model governance, validation, version control, and controlled access |
| Knowledge and retrieval layer | Supplies approved documentation and historical evidence | Source authorization, freshness, provenance, and prompt-injection controls |
| Decision and policy gateway | Determines whether proposed actions satisfy approved rules | Policy enforcement independent of model-generated instructions |
| Human workflow | Supports review, approval, escalation, and accountability | Role-based access, meaningful review, and approval traceability |
| Orchestration layer | Executes approved operational workflows | Scoped permissions, allowlisted actions, bounded execution, and rollback |
| Network management interfaces | Provide access to controllers and operational platforms | Strong authentication, API security, authorization, and change controls |
| Monitoring and audit layer | Records system activity and evaluates control performance | Integrity-protected logs, alerting, traceability, and evidence retention |

A key architectural principle is to separate AI inference from operational authorization.

The model may generate a recommendation, but a separate policy-enforcement mechanism should determine whether the recommendation can proceed. The policy mechanism must not simply accept an action because the AI model labels it safe or necessary.

#### 3.3 Network Operations Data Sources

AI network operations may consume several categories of information.

| Data Category | Examples | Operational Use | Potential Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Performance telemetry | Latency, jitter, packet loss, interface utilization | Anomaly detection and capacity forecasting | Incorrect or manipulated measurements |
| Device logs | System events, interface errors, routing messages | Fault correlation and diagnosis | Malicious or misleading log content |
| Flow records | NetFlow, IPFIX, traffic summaries | Traffic analysis and anomaly detection | Sensitive traffic metadata exposure |
| Configuration data | Routing policies, interface settings, firewall rules | Configuration compliance and change-impact analysis | Disclosure of sensitive architecture or credentials |
| Network topology | Device relationships, links, dependencies, paths | Root-cause analysis and impact assessment | Incorrect topology can produce unsafe recommendations |
| Change records | Approved changes, deployment history, rollback records | Correlating incidents with recent changes | Incomplete or outdated records |
| Incident tickets | Fault descriptions, actions, outcomes, lessons learned | Troubleshooting assistance and knowledge retrieval | Sensitive information and untrusted embedded instructions |
| Performance history | Historical utilization and fault data | Forecasting and predictive maintenance | Data drift and historical bias |
| Threat intelligence | Indicators, advisories, attack techniques | Security analytics and investigation | Poisoned, outdated, or inaccurate intelligence |
| Operational documentation | Runbooks, procedures, network standards | Retrieval-augmented troubleshooting | Prompt injection, unauthorized content, or stale procedures |
| Service dependencies | Application-to-network mappings and service criticality | Impact analysis and prioritization | Incomplete dependencies can underestimate business impact |
| Asset inventory | Device identity, version, ownership, lifecycle status | Asset-aware troubleshooting and risk prioritization | Inaccurate inventories can cause incorrect targeting |

The organization should not assume that every available data source is necessary for every use case.

Data access should be limited to what the approved purpose requires. A system designed to summarize interface utilization may not need access to complete firewall configurations or customer-identifiable records.

#### 3.5 Trust Boundaries and Security Zones

An enterprise architecture should distinguish between data collection, AI processing, decision-making, and operational execution.

| Trust Boundary | Information or Action Crossing the Boundary | Primary Control |
|---|---|---|
| Network device to collection platform | Telemetry and events | Source authentication, protected transport, and validation |
| Collection platform to data platform | Normalized operational records | Schema validation, access control, and integrity checks |
| Data platform to AI layer | Operational data and retrieved documents | Data authorization, minimization, and source provenance |
| AI layer to decision gateway | Recommendations and proposed actions | Output validation and policy enforcement |
| Decision gateway to human workflow | Recommendations requiring review | Authenticated users and traceable approval |
| Decision gateway to orchestration | Authorized action requests | Allowlisting, policy checks, and scoped execution |
| Orchestration to network management | Commands and configuration changes | Strong authentication, change controls, and action limits |
| AI platform to external provider | Prompts, telemetry extracts, or other processed data | Supplier assessment, data protection, and approved transfer controls |

The architecture should be designed so that compromise of one component does not automatically grant control over the entire network.

For example, access to a knowledge base should not confer permissions to change routing configurations. Similarly, a model endpoint should not possess broad administrative credentials simply because some use cases require limited operational integration.

#### 3.6 Architectural Failure Scenarios

| Scenario | Potential Consequence | Required Design Response |
|---|---|---|
| Telemetry source becomes unavailable | Incomplete analysis and delayed fault detection | Detect missing sources and disclose analysis limitations |
| Data ingestion accepts malformed records | Incorrect correlation or processing failures | Validate schemas and quarantine invalid records |
| AI produces an unsupported root cause | Engineers may perform ineffective remediation | Require supporting evidence and validate recommendations |
| Knowledge base contains malicious instructions | AI may produce unsafe or manipulated recommendations | Treat retrieved content as untrusted data and enforce policy boundaries |
| AI credentials are compromised | Unauthorized access to operational tools | Use scoped identities, secret rotation, monitoring, and revocation |
| Decision gateway is bypassed | Unauthorized actions may reach network systems | Enforce authorization at the execution interface as well |
| Orchestration workflow repeats an action | Repeated changes may cause instability | Use bounded retries, idempotency where appropriate, and execution limits |
| AI service becomes unavailable | Loss of AI-assisted functionality | Maintain a defined fallback to established operational processes |
| Configuration change causes degradation | Service disruption | Validate outcomes and support rollback or compensating action |
| Audit logging fails | Reduced ability to reconstruct consequential decisions | Detect logging failures and restrict affected high-risk operations where necessary |

**Architecture principle:** The AI model must not be the only security boundary protecting the network. Independent access controls, policy enforcement, execution safeguards, and operational monitoring must constrain its behavior.

### 4. Stakeholders, Accountability, and AI Network Operations Governance Model

AI-powered network operations affects multiple organizational functions. Network engineering may own infrastructure changes, operations teams may own service continuity, cybersecurity may own security requirements, and AI governance or enterprise risk functions may oversee the responsible use of AI.

A governance model should define how these responsibilities interact.

Without clear accountability, the organization may encounter situations in which the network team assumes that the AI supplier is responsible for model risk, the AI team assumes that network engineers validate all recommendations, and the risk function assumes that production approvals include all relevant safeguards.

#### 4.1 Key Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Primary Responsibility | AI Network Operations Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Owns the business case and executive-level accountability | Benefits, costs, service risk, and investment decisions |
| Network operations director | Owns operational performance and network service delivery | Availability, incident handling, and operational readiness |
| Network engineering | Designs, configures, and maintains network infrastructure | Technical correctness and safe configuration changes |
| Network operations center (NOC) | Monitors network health and coordinates fault resolution | Alert quality, troubleshooting, and service restoration |
| Network architecture | Defines target network architecture and technical standards | Architectural integrity, dependencies, and change impact |
| AI system owner | Owns the AI capability throughout its lifecycle | Intended purpose, system performance, and operational controls |
| AI or data science team | Develops or configures models and analytical workflows | Model quality, limitations, evaluation, and drift |
| Cybersecurity | Assesses threats and protects systems and data | Unauthorized access, manipulation, data exposure, and integration security |
| Security operations center (SOC) | Detects and responds to cybersecurity incidents | AI-related security alerts and incident coordination |
| Enterprise risk management | Coordinates enterprise risk assessment and escalation | Residual risk, risk appetite, and risk acceptance |
| AI governance function | Coordinates AI policy, accountability, and lifecycle governance | Approved use, human oversight, and AI risk controls |
| IAM team | Manages human and machine identities | Least privilege, credential protection, and access reviews |
| IT service management | Manages incidents, problems, and changes | Workflow integrity and operational traceability |
| Change advisory board (CAB), where used | Reviews changes according to the organization's change process | Service impact, authorization, and rollback readiness |
| Privacy officer or DPO, where applicable | Advises on applicable data protection obligations | Personal data, monitoring, retention, and lawful processing |
| Legal and compliance | Evaluates applicable legal and contractual obligations | Regulatory exposure, contractual requirements, and accountability |
| Procurement and third-party risk | Assesses suppliers and contractual protections | Provider reliability, subcontractors, data use, and exit arrangements |
| Internal audit | Independently evaluates governance and controls | Evidence quality, control effectiveness, and governance assurance |
| Business service owners | Own the services dependent on the network | Business impact, maintenance windows, and service-level obligations |
| External network or AI provider | Supplies technology, services, or support | Service commitments, product limitations, security, and incident notification |

The same person may hold more than one role in a smaller organization. However, material risk acceptance, privileged change execution, and independent assurance should not be combined in a way that undermines effective oversight.

#### 4.2 Three Lines of Accountability

A practical model separates operational responsibility, risk oversight, and independent assurance.

**First line — Operations and technology delivery**

The first line includes network operations, network engineering, AI system owners, platform administrators, and teams responsible for implementing and operating controls.

Responsibilities include:

- Defining operational requirements.
- Implementing approved safeguards.
- Managing network and AI configurations.
- Maintaining access controls.
- Validating AI-assisted recommendations.
- Monitoring performance and operational risks.
- Managing incidents and corrective actions.
- Maintaining evidence of control operation.

**Second line — Risk, compliance, and AI governance oversight**

The second line provides policy, risk oversight, challenge, and governance monitoring.

Responsibilities include:

- Defining risk-management requirements.
- Reviewing material AI risks.
- Establishing governance and control expectations.
- Monitoring risk acceptance and exceptions.
- Assessing applicable compliance obligations.
- Challenging the adequacy of risk treatment.
- Reviewing material changes and escalated incidents.
- Reporting significant exposures to management.

**Third line — Independent assurance**

Internal audit or another appropriately independent assurance function evaluates whether governance, risk management, and controls are designed and operating effectively.

Responsibilities include:

- Reviewing governance accountability.
- Evaluating the risk-management process.
- Testing selected controls independently.
- Assessing the quality of retained evidence.
- Identifying systemic weaknesses.
- Reporting findings through established independent channels.

Internal audit should not become the operational owner of the AI system or approve routine production changes. Its independence is necessary for credible assurance.

#### 4.4 Establish Decision Rights

The organization should explicitly identify who can:

- Approve new AI network operations use cases.
- Approve access to sensitive telemetry or configuration data.
- Authorize model deployment.
- Approve changes to model behavior or operational configuration.
- Grant or expand machine permissions.
- Authorize production network changes.
- Approve automated remediation workflows.
- Accept material residual risk.
- Approve exceptions to internal policy.
- Suspend an unsafe AI capability.
- Authorize restoration after a material incident.
- Approve the introduction of a new provider or external AI service.

The decision-rights model should align with existing network change management and security governance.

For example, an AI system owner may be authorized to approve a model update after validation, but that approval should not automatically authorize a change to the network controller's permissions or permit a new autonomous remediation capability.

#### 4.5 Example RACI Matrix

RACI means:

- **R — Responsible:** Performs the activity.
- **A — Accountable:** Owns the outcome.
- **C — Consulted:** Provides input.

### 5. Foundational Governance Requirements and Enterprise Policy Principles

Before deploying AI-powered network operations into production, the organization should establish a governance baseline that defines acceptable use, risk ownership, security requirements, operational restrictions, and ongoing assurance.

These requirements should be incorporated into existing enterprise policies wherever practical. The objective is to establish enforceable operational controls, not merely create additional AI documentation.

#### 5.1 Establish an AI Network Operations Policy

The policy should define:

- The purpose and scope of AI use in network operations.
- Approved AI technologies and deployment environments.
- Requirements for registering AI use cases.
- Ownership and accountability requirements.
- Rules governing access to network telemetry and configuration information.
- Minimum identity and access-control requirements.
- Restrictions on production configuration changes.
- Human approval and oversight requirements.
- Requirements for model validation and performance monitoring.
- AI security testing requirements.
- Logging and evidence-preservation requirements.
- Incident reporting and response obligations.
- Supplier and external AI service requirements.
- Risk acceptance and exception-management procedures.
- Change management and periodic reassessment.
- Conditions for suspending or retiring AI capabilities.

The policy should distinguish mandatory requirements from recommended practices.

For example, "AI should use least privilege" is a broad principle. An enforceable requirement would specify that an AI service identity may access only the approved telemetry sources and operational APIs necessary for its authorized use cases, with privileged actions restricted by independent authorization controls.

#### 5.2 Maintain an AI Network Operations System Inventory

Every material AI capability should be recorded in an inventory.

| Inventory Field | Description |
|---|---|
| System ID | Unique identifier for the AI capability |
| System name | Name used by the organization |
| Business owner | Person accountable for the business purpose |
| Technical owner | Person responsible for technical operation |
| Intended purpose | Approved operational objective |
| AI technology | Model, analytical method, or AI service used |
| Deployment environment | On-premises, private cloud, public cloud, or SaaS |
| Network domains | Infrastructure and services within scope |
| Data sources | Telemetry, logs, configurations, tickets, and documentation |
| Data classification | Sensitivity and handling requirements |
| Connected systems | SIEM, NMS, SDN controller, ITSM, CMDB, or other integrations |
| Access level | Read-only, recommendation, workflow execution, or configuration control |
| Autonomy level | Approved autonomy classification |
| Human oversight | Required review and approval controls |
| Risk rating | Current assessed risk level |
| Model version | Model or service version where available |
| Configuration version | Relevant configuration and policy version |
| Supplier | External provider, if applicable |
| Validation status | Current evaluation and approval status |
| Known limitations | Documented weaknesses and operational constraints |
| Monitoring requirements | Required metrics and alerting |
| Approval status | Development, pilot, approved, restricted, suspended, or retired |
| Review date | Date of the latest formal review |

The inventory should be updated when the system's intended purpose, permissions, data sources, supplier, or operational behavior changes materially.

#### 5.3 Establish Core Governance Principles

| Principle | Requirement | Practical Application |
|---|---|---|
| Accountability | Every material AI capability has a designated owner | Assign a named system owner and operational risk owner |
| Intended-purpose limitation | AI must operate within an approved scope | Prohibit unapproved network-control activities |
| Least privilege | AI receives only necessary permissions | Separate read-only telemetry access from configuration-change privileges |
| Human oversight | Consequential actions have appropriate review and approval | Require authorized approval for material production changes |
| Defense in depth | Multiple independent safeguards constrain system behavior | Combine model validation, policy enforcement, API authorization, and monitoring |
| Data minimization | Access and processing are limited to what the purpose requires | Avoid providing full device configurations when selected metrics are sufficient |
| Traceability | Material recommendations and actions can be reconstructed | Link outputs to source evidence, model versions, approvals, and execution records |
| Secure by design | Security controls are integrated into architecture and lifecycle processes | Apply secure integration patterns and protected management interfaces |
| Resilience | Network operations remain manageable when AI capabilities fail | Maintain established troubleshooting and response procedures |
| Change control | Material changes are assessed and authorized | Validate model, configuration, and permission changes before deployment |
| Continuous monitoring | Performance and risk are monitored during operation | Track drift, incorrect recommendations, unauthorized actions, and service impact |
| Supplier accountability | External dependencies are assessed and governed | Evaluate data handling, service commitments, incident notification, and exit options |
| Proportionate governance | Control rigor reflects risk and potential impact | Apply stronger approval and validation to autonomous changes affecting critical services |
| Reversibility | High-impact actions should have defined recovery options where feasible | Test rollback and compensating controls before enabling automated remediation |

#### 5.4 Define Mandatory Approval Boundaries

The organization should establish a minimum approval model for different categories of activity.

| Activity | Minimum Governance Expectation |
|---|---|
| Reading approved telemetry | Authorized access and appropriate monitoring |
| Summarizing operational data | Data protection, output validation, and traceability |
| Generating a diagnostic hypothesis | Evidence-based recommendation and uncertainty handling |
| Creating an incident ticket | Scoped workflow permissions and audit logging |
| Executing a read-only diagnostic command | Approved command allowlist, scoped credentials, and execution limits |
| Changing a noncritical network configuration | Formal authorization, testing, change control, and rollback planning |
| Changing routing or firewall policy | Network engineering review and applicable security/change approval |
| Modifying critical network infrastructure | Enhanced impact assessment and authorization appropriate to the service criticality |
| Enabling autonomous remediation | Separate risk assessment, validation, bounded permissions, monitoring, and recovery safeguards |
| Changing AI permissions | Access review and authorization by the designated access owner |
| Introducing a new model or provider | Risk, security, privacy, and supplier assessment as applicable |
| Accepting material residual risk | Formal acceptance by the authorized risk owner or delegated authority |

An emergency procedure may permit expedited action, but the organization should define the conditions, authority, documentation, and retrospective review requirements in advance.

#### 5.5 Establish an AI Risk Acceptance Framework

Risk acceptance should be based on documented evidence and authority.

A risk acceptance record should include:

- Risk identifier.
- Description of the risk and its potential consequences.
- Affected network services and business processes.
- Inherent risk rating.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness assessment.
- Residual risk rating.
- Applicable risk appetite or tolerance.
- Available treatment options.
- Rationale for acceptance.
- Compensating controls.
- Accountable risk owner.
- Approval authority.
- Acceptance date and expiry or review date.
- Conditions that trigger reassessment.
- Evidence supporting the decision.

The organization should not accept a risk merely because remediation is inconvenient or the AI system delivers significant business benefits.

For example, if an AI remediation workflow can execute a production network change without the required authorization, the organization should first determine whether the authorization control can be corrected or the capability disabled. Formal risk acceptance must not be used to bypass mandatory legal requirements or controls that organizational policy makes non-negotiable.

#### 5.6 Define Prohibited or Restricted AI Activities

The organization should maintain a clear list of prohibited and restricted activities.

Examples of activities that should ordinarily be prohibited unless separately assessed and explicitly authorized include:

- Executing arbitrary model-generated commands against production network devices.
- Allowing AI-generated instructions to bypass network change management.
- Using broad administrative credentials for routine AI analysis.
- Modifying critical routing or firewall configurations without the required authorization.
- Disabling security monitoring to improve AI performance.
- Deleting or modifying audit evidence without authorized retention and disposal procedures.
- Treating unverified AI-generated root-cause hypotheses as confirmed findings.
- Allowing retrieved documents or telemetry to override system authorization policies.
- Deploying a new model or expanding its autonomy without the required validation.
- Using unapproved external AI services to process confidential network configurations.
- Continuing an unsafe autonomous remediation workflow after a defined suspension trigger has been met.

Restrictions should be enforced through technical controls wherever feasible. Documentation alone is insufficient when the platform can technically perform a prohibited action.

#### 5.7 Establish Governance Review Frequency and Triggers

Governance should operate throughout the AI lifecycle.

| Review Activity | Illustrative Frequency | Event-Driven Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Operational performance review | Daily or weekly, according to criticality | Material degradation or unusual operational behavior |
| AI risk and control review | Monthly or quarterly | New material risk, incident, or control failure |
| Privileged access review | According to access policy and risk | Role change, credential compromise, or permission expansion |
| Model performance review | Defined by use case and risk | Drift, changed data patterns, or repeated incorrect recommendations |
| Architecture review | Periodically and before material changes | New integration, network domain, or execution capability |
| Supplier review | According to third-party risk policy | Material service change, incident, or supplier ownership change |
| Formal governance review | At the defined management-review interval | Material change in intended purpose, autonomy, or risk profile |
| Independent assurance | Based on the audit plan and risk | Significant control weakness or repeated audit findings |

The exact cadence should reflect network criticality, the rate of change, the consequences of failure, and applicable requirements.

#### 5.8 Governance Maturity Assessment

Organizations can assess their AI network operations governance maturity using a staged model.

| Level | Maturity | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Ad hoc | AI tools are introduced experimentally, with limited ownership and inconsistent controls |
| Level 2 | Defined | Initial policies, use-case records, and basic risk assessments exist |
| Level 3 | Controlled | Access restrictions, validation, change management, and human oversight are implemented |
| Level 4 | Measured | Performance, risks, control effectiveness, and incidents are measured systematically |
| Level 5 | Adaptive | Governance integrates continuous assurance, threat-informed testing, controlled optimization, and evidence-based improvement |

The model is an internal maturity aid, not a certification standard.

The objective is not necessarily to achieve the highest level for every use case. The organization should establish a maturity target proportionate to the business importance and risk of each AI capability.

For example, a read-only assistant that summarizes routine network documentation may require a different assurance level from an autonomous system that modifies routing behavior across a critical telecommunications network.

### Core Principle

AI-powered network operations must be governed as part of the enterprise's operational technology and IT service environment. AI can improve anomaly detection, troubleshooting, forecasting, and network optimization, but its recommendations and actions must remain within defined authorization boundaries, validated technical constraints, and accountable operational processes.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **AIOps vs. autonomous network control:** AIOps can analyze and recommend; autonomous network control can directly alter network behavior.
- **Telemetry access vs. configuration authority:** Permission to observe network state does not imply permission to change it.
- **Anomaly detection vs. confirmed diagnosis:** An unusual pattern is evidence for investigation, not proof of a particular fault.
- **AI recommendation vs. approved change:** A technically plausible suggestion must still satisfy operational and security requirements.
- **Model accuracy vs. operational safety:** A model can perform well on evaluation data and still produce unsafe results in a specific production context.
- **Human oversight vs. nominal approval:** Effective oversight requires sufficient information, authority, and time to challenge an AI recommendation.
- **Risk reduction vs. risk acceptance:** Controls reduce exposure; formal acceptance requires an authorized decision regarding the remaining risk.
- **Governance documentation vs. enforceable controls:** Policies must be translated into technical restrictions, operational procedures, and verifiable evidence.
