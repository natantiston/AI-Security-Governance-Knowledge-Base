# Chapter 21.06 — AI Credit-Scoring System
## Part 1: Business Context, System Architecture, Use-Case Definition, and Governance Foundations

### 1. Introduction to AI Credit-Scoring Systems

An AI credit-scoring system uses artificial intelligence, statistical models, machine learning, or related analytical techniques to estimate the creditworthiness of an individual, business, or other borrowing entity.

The system evaluates information associated with an applicant or existing customer and produces an output that supports decisions about lending, credit limits, repayment terms, pricing, or ongoing credit-risk management.

Traditional credit-scoring systems commonly rely on predefined statistical relationships and established scoring methodologies. AI-based systems may use more complex models to identify patterns across larger and more varied datasets, potentially improving predictive performance while introducing additional governance, security, explainability, and fairness challenges.

An AI credit-scoring system should not be treated merely as a mathematical model. It is part of a broader credit decision-making process involving customer data, financial records, model outputs, lending policies, human oversight, regulatory requirements, and operational controls.

From a Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) perspective, the central question is not simply whether the model predicts default accurately. The organization must also establish whether the system is lawful, secure, reliable, explainable to the extent required, appropriately supervised, and suitable for the decisions it supports.

#### 1.1 What is credit scoring?

Credit scoring is the process of evaluating the likelihood that a borrower will meet specified financial obligations.

Depending on the use case, the system may estimate:

- The probability of default within a defined period.
- The likelihood of delinquency.
- The probability of repayment under specified conditions.
- The risk associated with a proposed credit limit.
- The likelihood of an existing customer experiencing financial distress.
- The relative credit risk of a portfolio or group of applicants.

A common model output is the **Probability of Default (PD)**, which estimates the probability that a borrower will default within a defined time horizon under a specified definition of default.

For example, a model may estimate that an applicant has a 4% probability of defaulting within the next twelve months.

This estimate is not a guarantee that the applicant will repay the loan, nor does it mean that default is certain when the probability is high. It is a statistical estimate that must be interpreted within the model's assumptions, validation results, and intended use.

A credit institution may combine the model output with other information, including affordability assessments, lending policies, collateral, regulatory requirements, and human review.

#### 1.2 Traditional credit scoring versus AI-based credit scoring

Traditional and AI-based credit-scoring systems can both use statistical models. The distinction is not simply that traditional systems use mathematics while AI systems use artificial intelligence.

Traditional credit scoring often relies on established techniques such as logistic regression, scorecards, and predefined variable transformations. AI-based implementations may additionally use methods such as gradient-boosted trees, random forests, neural networks, or other machine-learning approaches.

The appropriate method depends on the intended use, data quality, interpretability requirements, performance expectations, and governance constraints.

| Dimension | Traditional credit scoring | AI-based credit scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Modeling approach | Often scorecards and established statistical methods | May use statistical methods and more complex machine-learning models |
| Data relationships | Often based on predefined variables and transformations | May identify complex nonlinear relationships and interactions |
| Explainability | Scorecards can provide relatively direct explanations | Explainability varies by model and explanation method |
| Data requirements | Depends on the methodology and intended use | May require larger, more diverse, or more carefully governed datasets |
| Validation | Statistical validation and established model-risk controls | Statistical validation plus assessment of model-specific risks and limitations |
| Fairness assessment | Required where applicable to the use case | May require additional investigation into complex interactions and differential outcomes |
| Change management | Controlled model and scorecard updates | Controlled updates to models, features, training data, and deployment pipelines |
| Security | Conventional application, data, and model security | Conventional security plus relevant machine-learning and data-manipulation threats |
| Operational monitoring | Portfolio and model-performance monitoring | Portfolio monitoring plus applicable drift, feature, and model-behavior monitoring |

Neither approach is inherently fair, secure, compliant, or accurate in every environment. Those characteristics depend on design, data, implementation, validation, and ongoing governance.

#### 1.3 Why organizations adopt AI credit scoring

Organizations may adopt AI-based credit scoring to improve risk estimation, process applications more efficiently, and identify patterns that existing approaches do not adequately capture.

Potential benefits include:

1. **Improved risk differentiation:** Distinguishing between applicants with different estimated levels of credit risk.
2. **Operational efficiency:** Supporting faster processing of applications and routine assessments.
3. **Consistent decision support:** Applying defined assessment criteria across large application volumes.
4. **Portfolio monitoring:** Identifying emerging risk patterns in existing credit portfolios.
5. **Alternative data analysis:** Assessing additional data sources where their use is lawful, appropriate, and justified.
6. **Early-warning capabilities:** Identifying indicators associated with potential repayment difficulties.
7. **Risk-based credit management:** Supporting the review of credit limits, repayment arrangements, or other authorized actions.

These benefits are potential outcomes, not guaranteed results. A more complex model may perform worse than a simpler model when data is inadequate, market conditions change, or the implementation is poorly controlled.

#### 1.4 Why AI credit scoring creates significant governance risk

Credit-scoring systems can influence access to financial services and materially affect individuals or businesses.

Incorrect or inadequately governed decisions may lead to:

- Unfair denial of credit.
- Inappropriate credit limits.
- Mispriced credit products.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Inaccurate assessments of repayment capacity.
- Excessive collection or review activity.
- Exposure of sensitive financial information.
- Regulatory breaches.
- Reputational damage.
- Financial losses for the lender.
- Customer complaints and disputes.

The risk increases when the organization cannot adequately explain how the system is used, verify the reliability of its data, challenge questionable outputs, or determine who is accountable for the resulting decisions.

A credit-scoring system should therefore be governed as a potentially high-impact decision-support capability whose actual obligations depend on its intended purpose, jurisdiction, deployment context, and applicable law.

#### 1.5 The role of GRC in AI credit scoring

GRC provides the organizational framework for managing the risks associated with the credit-scoring lifecycle.

| GRC discipline | Application to AI credit scoring |
|---|---|
| Governance | Defines accountability, decision rights, policies, oversight, and approval requirements |
| Risk management | Identifies, assesses, treats, and monitors credit, model, cybersecurity, privacy, and operational risks |
| Compliance | Determines applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal requirements |
| Security | Protects applicant data, model assets, APIs, infrastructure, and decision processes |
| Privacy | Governs lawful processing, data minimization, transparency, retention, and individual rights |
| Model risk management | Evaluates model suitability, assumptions, limitations, performance, and validation |
| Fairness and responsible AI | Assesses potential discriminatory effects and establishes appropriate safeguards |
| Audit and assurance | Verifies whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively |

A mature governance approach connects these disciplines instead of allowing each team to operate independently.

**Core Principle**

AI credit scoring is a decision-support capability with financial, legal, security, and customer-impact implications. Its suitability depends not only on predictive performance but also on lawful data use, effective controls, appropriate oversight, and accountable decision-making.

### 2. Business Use Cases, Intended Purpose, and Decision Boundaries

The intended purpose of an AI credit-scoring system determines what the model is designed to assess, how its outputs may be used, and which governance requirements must be considered.

A model used to estimate default probability does not automatically have the authority to approve a loan, reject an application, set an interest rate, or reduce an existing credit limit.

The organization must distinguish the analytical function performed by the model from the business decision that consumes its output.

#### 2.1 Define the intended purpose

The intended purpose should describe:

- The business problem being addressed.
- The population being assessed.
- The financial product or credit relationship involved.
- The decisions the system supports.
- The types of data used.
- The model outputs produced.
- The permitted downstream uses.
- The level of automation.
- The human oversight arrangements.
- The limitations and excluded uses.

For example:

> The system estimates the probability of twelve-month default for eligible applicants seeking unsecured personal loans. Its output supports a governed credit assessment process that combines model results with affordability requirements, lending policies, applicable legal obligations, and authorized decision rules.

This statement defines a more controlled purpose than a general declaration that the system uses AI to approve loans.

It also creates a reference point against which changes can be assessed.

#### 2.2 Common AI credit-scoring use cases

**Consumer lending**

A bank or lender may use AI to support assessments for personal loans, credit cards, vehicle financing, or other consumer credit products.

The system may estimate default risk, support credit-limit recommendations, or contribute to an authorized lending decision.

**Mortgage lending**

A lender may use analytical models as one component of mortgage-risk assessment. The overall process may also include affordability analysis, income verification, property valuation, collateral requirements, and other applicable conditions.

A model output should not replace required assessments or mandatory lending rules.

**Small and medium-sized enterprise lending**

An organization may assess business financial statements, repayment history, cash-flow indicators, and other permitted business information to estimate credit risk.

The quality and availability of business financial data may vary significantly, requiring explicit data-quality and uncertainty controls.

**Credit-card limit management**

A lender may use models to support the assessment of appropriate credit limits or identify changes in customer credit risk.

Changes affecting existing customers require particular attention to contractual obligations, applicable consumer-protection requirements, customer impact, and appropriate review mechanisms.

**Early-warning systems**

A lender may monitor existing credit exposures to identify indicators of potential deterioration.

An early-warning indicator should not automatically be treated as proof that a borrower is unable or unwilling to repay.

**Portfolio risk assessment**

Models may support analysis of credit-risk concentrations, expected losses, or emerging trends across a portfolio.

Portfolio-level outputs should be distinguished from individual customer decisions because the consequences and required controls may differ.

#### 2.3 Distinguish scoring from decision-making

An enterprise credit decision may involve several stages.

1. The applicant submits information.
2. The organization validates the application and checks required documentation.
3. Authorized data sources provide relevant financial and credit information.
4. The model estimates credit risk.
5. A decision policy combines the score with applicable lending criteria.
6. Additional checks or human review may be required.
7. An authorized decision is recorded.
8. The applicant receives the appropriate communication.
9. Relevant records are retained for monitoring, review, and dispute handling.

The model may be responsible only for step four.

The organization remains responsible for ensuring that the overall process operates lawfully and according to its approved policies.

#### 2.5 Define permitted and prohibited uses

The organization should establish explicit boundaries around the use of model outputs.

| Potential use | Governance consideration |
|---|---|
| Estimate probability of default | Validate the model for the defined population and prediction horizon |
| Support a credit application assessment | Confirm that downstream decision rules are authorized and appropriate |
| Recommend a credit limit | Assess customer impact, affordability requirements, and applicable constraints |
| Support interest-rate determination | Evaluate pricing governance, fairness, transparency, and applicable requirements |
| Identify potentially deteriorating exposures | Establish escalation and review procedures |
| Trigger additional verification | Define proportionate criteria and appropriate customer-handling procedures |
| Automatically reject every application above a model threshold | Assess legal requirements, decision consequences, validation, and the appropriateness of fully automated decision-making |
| Use unrelated personal information without an established legal basis | Prohibit unauthorized processing |
| Extend the model to a new product without reassessment | Require appropriate change assessment and approval |
| Use the model as the sole authority for decisions outside its approved purpose | Prohibit unless separately assessed, authorized, and lawful |

The table illustrates governance considerations rather than making a universal legal determination about every possible use.

#### 2.6 Define the consequences of errors

Credit-scoring errors can produce different forms of harm.

A **false positive**, in a fraud-detection context, typically means legitimate activity is incorrectly flagged. In credit scoring, terminology can be less consistent, so the organization should define the relevant error explicitly.

For a binary credit-risk model, one possible convention is:

- **False negative for default risk:** An applicant who will default is assessed as low risk.
- **False positive for default risk:** An applicant who will not default is assessed as high risk.

These errors have different consequences.

| Error scenario | Potential consequence |
|---|---|
| High-risk applicant incorrectly assessed as low risk | Increased credit losses and potentially inappropriate lending |
| Low-risk applicant incorrectly assessed as high risk | Unnecessary rejection, reduced credit access, or less favorable terms |
| Default probability inaccurately calibrated | Incorrect portfolio-risk estimates or pricing decisions |
| Applicant data incorrectly matched | Decision based on another person's or entity's information |
| Model output interpreted incorrectly | Inappropriate decision despite an otherwise valid score |
| Outdated score used after material changes | Decision based on an assessment that no longer reflects relevant circumstances |

The organization should define acceptable performance according to the consequences of each error rather than optimizing a single accuracy metric.

#### 2.7 Establish decision boundaries and human oversight

Human oversight should be designed according to the nature of the decision and the risks involved.

Possible arrangements include:

- Human review of specified applications.
- Additional verification when information is inconsistent.
- Escalation of cases outside the model's validated scope.
- Review of unusual or uncertain model outputs.
- A process for correcting inaccurate data.
- A documented mechanism for challenging decisions.
- Restrictions on the use of automated recommendations.
- Monitoring of overrides and their outcomes.

Human involvement is not automatically meaningful simply because a person clicks an approval button.

Reviewers need appropriate authority, sufficient information, relevant training, and the practical ability to challenge the model's output.

The organization should also monitor whether human reviewers consistently defer to automated recommendations without adequate scrutiny.

#### 2.8 Define out-of-scope conditions

The model should identify situations in which its output is unreliable or outside its approved scope.

Examples may include:

- Applicants from populations not represented adequately in validation data.
- Missing critical financial information.
- Data sources that fail required quality checks.
- Products with materially different risk characteristics.
- Prediction horizons that differ from the validated use.
- Changes in market conditions that invalidate key assumptions.
- Model outputs that fail required validation checks.

The system should have a defined response for these conditions, such as requesting additional information, routing the case for review, or declining to produce a score.

It should not silently treat an unreliable score as equivalent to a validated result.

**Core Principle**

The intended purpose establishes the boundaries of an AI credit-scoring system. The model's output must be used only within an assessed and approved decision process, with explicit treatment of error consequences, human oversight, and out-of-scope conditions.

### 3. Enterprise AI Credit-Scoring Architecture and Data Flows

An enterprise AI credit-scoring platform typically connects customer-facing applications, identity and verification services, credit-information sources, internal financial systems, data-processing services, model infrastructure, decision engines, and operational monitoring.

Each connection introduces potential risks involving data confidentiality, integrity, availability, authorization, and traceability.

The architecture must protect both the data used to generate a score and the downstream process through which that score influences a credit decision.

#### 3.1 Identify the principal architecture components

**Customer and application channels**

These include online applications, mobile applications, branch systems, broker portals, and business lending interfaces.

They collect application information and provide an entry point into the credit-assessment process.

**Identity and verification services**

These services validate applicant identity and may verify relevant attributes or documentation.

The system should prevent an incorrect identity match from causing financial information to be associated with the wrong applicant.

**External credit-information providers**

Depending on the jurisdiction and product, a lender may obtain permitted credit-history information, credit-bureau data, or other approved external financial information.

The organization must evaluate the provider's reliability, contractual terms, data provenance, security arrangements, and lawful basis for the relevant processing.

**Internal financial systems**

These may include account history, payment records, existing credit exposures, customer relationship systems, and other approved internal data sources.

Access should be restricted to information necessary for the intended purpose.

**Data ingestion and validation services**

These components receive, normalize, validate, and prepare data for downstream processing.

They should detect malformed records, unexpected schema changes, invalid values, missing critical information, and unauthorized modifications.

**Feature engineering and feature storage**

These components transform approved source data into model inputs.

For example, the system may calculate historical payment ratios, debt-to-income measures where appropriate, or repayment-history indicators.

Feature definitions, lineage, transformations, and ownership should be documented.

**Model training and validation environment**

This environment supports model development, evaluation, validation, and controlled experimentation.

It should be separated appropriately from production, with restricted access to sensitive data and production assets.

**Model registry and deployment pipeline**

These components manage model versions, validation status, approvals, artifact integrity, and controlled deployment.

**Model inference service**

The inference service loads an approved model and evaluates validated inputs to produce a score or related output.

**Credit decision engine**

The decision engine applies authorized lending policies and other required conditions to the model output.

It should not permit a model response to bypass mandatory eligibility or authorization requirements.

**Case management and review services**

These services support manual review, additional verification, exception handling, complaints, and evidence collection.

**Security and operational monitoring**

These functions collect relevant security events, service-health metrics, model-performance indicators, and audit records.

The model development environment should be governed separately from the production inference environment.

Training data, experiments, model artifacts, and deployment permissions should not be accessible without appropriate authorization simply because they belong to the same AI platform.

#### 3.3 Establish trust boundaries

A trust boundary separates components with different security assumptions or authority.

Relevant boundaries include:

- Applicant devices to public-facing applications.
- Application services to internal financial systems.
- Internal systems to external credit-information providers.
- Data ingestion to governed data storage.
- Data-processing services to the feature store.
- Model-development environments to production environments.
- Model registry to deployment pipeline.
- Inference service to the credit decision engine.
- Credit decision engine to customer communication services.
- Production infrastructure to administrative interfaces.
- Operational systems to security monitoring platforms.

For each boundary, the organization should document the identities involved, data exchanged, permitted operations, validation controls, monitoring requirements, and expected failure behavior.

#### 3.4 Protect data integrity throughout the lifecycle

Data integrity is especially important because a credit model can produce a technically valid result from incorrect information.

Consider a case in which a data pipeline mistakenly associates one applicant's repayment history with another applicant. The model may calculate a score correctly using the supplied inputs, but the overall assessment is invalid.

Controls should therefore include:

- Applicant identity and record matching.
- Source validation.
- Schema and type checks.
- Completeness checks for required fields.
- Detection of unexpected values.
- Validation of derived features.
- Data lineage.
- Controlled corrections.
- Versioned datasets.
- Monitoring for unexpected changes.
- Reconciliation with authoritative sources where appropriate.

Data quality failures should trigger defined responses rather than being silently converted into apparently valid values.

#### 3.5 Separate training, validation, and production environments

The model lifecycle should use controlled environments with clearly defined permissions.

| Environment | Purpose | Principal controls |
|---|---|---|
| Development | Explore features and candidate models | Restricted data access, controlled experimentation, versioning |
| Validation | Independently assess candidate model performance and limitations | Controlled datasets, documented methods, appropriate independence |
| Pre-production | Test integration, configuration, deployment, and operational behavior | Representative environment, controlled access, security testing |
| Production | Generate authorized credit-scoring outputs | Approved artifacts, restricted administration, monitoring, change control |

A model should not enter production simply because it performs well in a developer's environment.

The organization should establish a formal promotion process that confirms the exact artifact, associated configuration, validation status, and required approvals.

#### 3.6 Protect the inference-to-decision boundary

The model inference service should return a defined output under an approved interface contract.

The decision engine should verify that the response:

- Comes from the expected service.
- Corresponds to the correct application.
- Uses an approved model version.
- Contains the expected output fields.
- Meets validation requirements.
- Is not expired or inconsistent with the current assessment.
- Is interpreted according to approved policy.

The decision engine should also handle timeouts, unavailable services, invalid outputs, and inconsistent results according to documented rules.

A model should not be permitted to directly modify a customer's account, change a contractual term, or execute another high-impact action unless the architecture and governance arrangements explicitly authorize that capability.

#### 3.7 Establish traceability between application and decision

For a material credit decision, authorized reviewers should be able to reconstruct the relevant processing path.

The evidence may include:

- Application reference.
- Assessment timestamp.
- Relevant source-data references.
- Data-quality validation results.
- Feature-processing version.
- Model identifier and version.
- Model output.
- Applicable decision-policy version.
- Final decision.
- Human review or override, where applicable.
- Reason codes or explanations required by the process.
- Relevant approval and audit records.

Records should be protected against unauthorized alteration and retained according to applicable requirements.

Traceability does not require indiscriminate retention of every personal-data field. The organization should retain the information necessary for its legitimate operational, legal, and assurance purposes while applying appropriate minimization and access controls.

#### 3.8 Define secure failure behavior

The architecture should specify how the system behaves when a critical component is unavailable or unreliable.

Examples include:

- External credit-data provider outage.
- Data-quality validation failure.
- Feature-store unavailability.
- Model inference timeout.
- Model artifact integrity failure.
- Authentication-service failure.
- Decision-engine failure.
- Logging or audit-recording failure.

Possible responses include requesting additional information, routing the application for manual review, delaying the assessment, or using an approved alternative process.

The organization should not automatically interpret missing data as low risk or assume that a previous score remains valid after material changes.

The selected response should reflect the consequences of an incorrect decision, applicable obligations, and approved business-continuity requirements.

**Core Principle**

The architecture must preserve the integrity and traceability of the entire credit-assessment process. Secure data flows, controlled trust boundaries, separation of environments, and explicit failure handling help ensure that model outputs are based on appropriate information and are consumed only by authorized decision processes.

### 4. Stakeholders, Accountability, and the Credit-Scoring Governance Model

AI credit scoring requires coordination among business owners, credit-risk professionals, model developers, data owners, cybersecurity teams, privacy specialists, legal functions, and operational personnel.

The governance model must ensure that each material risk has an owner and that decisions are made by individuals with appropriate authority.

A common governance failure occurs when the technology team assumes that it owns all AI risks, while the business team assumes that the model has already been approved by specialists. The result can be an operationally deployed system with unclear accountability for customer impact, model suitability, or compliance.

#### 4.1 Identify the principal stakeholders

**Executive management and risk committees**

Provide strategic oversight, establish risk appetite, and make or delegate material risk decisions.

**Credit-risk management**

Defines credit-risk objectives, evaluates risk exposure, and ensures that the scoring approach supports the organization's credit-risk framework.

**Business product owner**

Owns the intended business use, product requirements, operational outcomes, and business justification.

**Model development team**

Develops candidate models, documents assumptions, and maintains relevant model-development artifacts.

**Independent model validation or model risk management**

Assesses model suitability, performance, limitations, and validation evidence according to the organization's model-risk framework.

**Data owners and data governance**

Establish permitted data use, ownership, lineage, quality, access, and retention requirements.

**Cybersecurity and security architecture**

Assesses system security, identity and access controls, architecture, APIs, infrastructure, and threat exposure.

**Privacy and legal teams**

Evaluate applicable legal requirements, personal-data processing, transparency, individual rights, contractual terms, and regulatory implications.

**Technology and platform operations**

Operate the platform, maintain infrastructure, manage deployments, and implement availability and recovery controls.

**Customer service and complaints management**

Handle customer questions, complaints, disputes, and escalation procedures.

**Compliance and regulatory affairs**

Monitor applicable regulatory obligations and support compliance assessments.

**Internal audit or independent assurance**

Provides independent assessment of governance and control effectiveness within its mandate.

#### 4.2 Distinguish business risk from model risk

Business risk and model risk are related but not identical.

**Business risk** concerns the consequences of the organization's credit-related decisions, including losses, inappropriate lending, customer impact, and operational outcomes.

**Model risk** concerns the possibility that model design, assumptions, data, implementation, or use produce incorrect or misleading outputs that affect decisions.

For example, a credit model may be statistically well validated but used with an unauthorized dataset. This creates a data-governance or compliance concern even if the model's predictive performance remains strong.

Similarly, a model may be implemented securely but have poor predictive performance for a relevant applicant population. This creates a model-suitability concern despite adequate cybersecurity controls.

Governance must evaluate both risk categories and their interactions.

#### 4.3 Establish decision rights

The organization should identify who may:

- Approve the intended purpose.
- Approve the use of data sources.
- Approve the model-development methodology.
- Complete or approve independent validation.
- Approve production deployment.
- Change model thresholds or decision policies.
- Authorize human-review exceptions.
- Accept residual risk within delegated authority.
- Suspend model use.
- Approve a new model version.
- Approve changes to customer-impacting processes.
- Determine when reassessment is required.

The person who develops a model should not automatically have unrestricted authority to validate, approve, and deploy it without the independent review required by the organization's governance framework.

#### 4.4 Define initial accountability assignments

| Activity | Primary function | Governance objective |
|---|---|---|
| Define credit-risk objectives | Credit-risk management and business owner | Ensure the system supports approved business requirements |
| Approve intended purpose | Business owner and relevant governance authority | Establish clear system boundaries |
| Approve data use | Data owners, privacy, legal, and relevant business owners | Ensure data use is authorized and appropriate |
| Develop candidate models | Model development team | Produce documented and reproducible model artifacts |
| Independently validate the model | Model risk management or independent validator | Assess suitability and limitations |
| Assess security architecture | Security architecture and cybersecurity | Identify and address security weaknesses |
| Operate the production platform | Technology operations | Maintain secure and reliable service |
| Approve production deployment | Designated production decision authority | Confirm readiness and acceptability of residual risks |
| Monitor credit outcomes | Credit-risk management and business operations | Identify changes in credit performance |
| Monitor security events | Security operations | Detect and respond to cybersecurity incidents |
| Handle customer disputes | Customer service and relevant decision owners | Ensure complaints and correction requests are addressed |
| Conduct independent assurance | Internal audit or independent assurance | Assess control effectiveness independently |

These assignments are illustrative. The final governance model must reflect the organization's structure, regulatory obligations, and delegated authorities.

#### 4.5 Establish an accountability principle

Every material AI credit-scoring decision should have a traceable chain of accountability.

That chain should identify:

- Who approved the system's intended purpose.
- Who owns the credit-risk methodology.
- Who is responsible for the data used.
- Who validated the model.
- Who approved deployment.
- Who operates the service.
- Who monitors its performance.
- Who investigates incidents.
- Who may authorize changes.
- Who accepts residual risk.
- Who can suspend or withdraw the system.

Accountability should remain explicit when vendors, cloud providers, model suppliers, or external credit-information providers participate in the solution.

Outsourcing a technical component does not automatically transfer the organization's responsibility for its lending decisions or applicable obligations.

**Core Principle**

AI credit-scoring governance must assign clear ownership across business decisions, model risk, data, cybersecurity, privacy, operations, and assurance. No single technical team should be assumed to own every risk, and material decisions must remain traceable to authorized decision-makers.

### 5. Foundational Governance Requirements and Enterprise Policy Principles

Before deploying an AI credit-scoring system, the organization should establish the governance requirements that apply throughout the system's lifecycle.

These requirements provide the foundation for risk assessment, architecture design, model validation, operational controls, and ongoing assurance.

The objective is to translate general principles such as fairness, transparency, security, and accountability into requirements that can be assigned, implemented, tested, and evidenced.

#### 5.1 Establish an AI credit-scoring policy

The organization should define an approved policy or incorporate the relevant requirements into its existing AI governance, model-risk, credit-risk, information-security, and data-governance policies.

The policy should address:

- Permitted uses of AI credit scoring.
- Ownership and accountability.
- Risk classification and assessment.
- Data governance and lawful processing.
- Model development and independent validation.
- Fairness and customer-impact assessment.
- Explainability and decision documentation.
- Security architecture and access control.
- Change management.
- Monitoring and incident response.
- Human oversight.
- Customer complaints and correction procedures.
- Third-party risk.
- Audit evidence.
- Risk acceptance.
- Suspension and retirement.

The policy should distinguish mandatory requirements from guidance and should identify the roles responsible for implementation and oversight.

| Governance requirement | Minimum expectation |
|---|---|

#### 5.4 Establish fairness and responsible-use principles

Credit-scoring governance should examine whether the model and the broader decision process produce unjustified or discriminatory outcomes.

The assessment may consider:

- Data representativeness.
- Historical bias in training data.
- Differences in data availability.
- Model performance across relevant populations.
- Proxy variables that may reproduce sensitive distinctions.
- Differences in false-positive and false-negative rates.
- Whether decision rules have a defensible purpose.
- Whether customer correction and review mechanisms are available.

Fairness assessment must be legally informed and context-specific. Different jurisdictions restrict different practices, and the collection or use of protected-characteristic data for testing may itself be subject to legal and governance constraints.

A model's use of seemingly neutral variables does not automatically eliminate discriminatory effects.

#### 5.5 Establish transparency and customer-impact principles

The organization should determine what information must be communicated to applicants and existing customers.

Depending on the product, jurisdiction, and decision process, relevant information may include:

- That an application or assessment is being evaluated.
- The categories of information used.
- The role of automated processing.
- The outcome and applicable reasons where required.
- Available correction or complaint mechanisms.
- How an applicant can request review where applicable.

Explanations should be accurate and consistent with the actual decision process.

An organization should not invent a simple explanation that misrepresents how a complex model and downstream policy contributed to a decision.

#### 5.6 Establish model and data ownership

Each production system should have identifiable owners for:

- The overall AI system.
- The credit-risk model.
- Training and validation datasets.
- Production feature definitions.
- External data feeds.
- Decision rules and thresholds.
- Technical infrastructure.
- Security controls.
- Operational monitoring.
- Residual risks.

Ownership should include practical responsibilities, not merely a name in a register.

Owners should understand their duties, have the authority and resources to fulfill them, and know when escalation is required.

#### 5.7 Establish controlled exceptions

Exceptions may be necessary when a control cannot be implemented immediately or when an alternative approach is justified.

However, an exception should not become an informal route around governance.

A formal exception should identify:

- The requirement being excepted.
- The reason for the exception.
- The affected system and scope.
- The risk created.
- Existing and additional safeguards.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approving authority.
- The start date.
- The expiry or review date.
- Required remediation.
- Conditions for withdrawal.

Exceptions must not override mandatory legal or regulatory requirements.

#### 5.8 Establish governance evidence requirements

The organization should maintain evidence that supports material lifecycle decisions.

Examples include:

- Approved policy and governance charter.
- AI system inventory.
- Risk assessments.
- Data inventories and lineage.
- Model documentation.
- Validation reports.
- Fairness assessments.
- Privacy assessments where required.
- Security architecture reviews.
- Access-control reviews.
- Deployment approvals.
- Monitoring records.
- Incident reports.
- Change records.
- Customer complaint and correction records.
- Risk acceptance and exception records.

Evidence should be protected, appropriately retained, and accessible to authorized reviewers.

#### 5.9 Establish policy review triggers

The governance framework should be reviewed when material conditions change.

Examples include:

- New credit products.
- New applicant populations.
- New data sources.
- Material model changes.
- New automated decision capabilities.
- Major security incidents.
- Significant changes in performance.
- New legal or regulatory requirements.
- Material changes to supplier arrangements.
- Evidence that existing controls are ineffective.

A policy that is formally approved but no longer reflects the system's actual operation cannot provide reliable governance.

**Core Principle**

Foundational governance requirements turn broad principles into accountable and verifiable obligations. An AI credit-scoring system should operate under an approved lifecycle framework that defines permitted use, risk ownership, data and model controls, customer safeguards, change management, and evidence requirements.

## Part 1 — Core Principle and Conceptual Distinctions

AI credit scoring must be governed as an enterprise credit-decision capability rather than as an isolated predictive model. Its intended purpose, architecture, data sources, model outputs, and downstream decisions must be clearly defined and controlled.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Credit scoring versus credit decision-making:** Scoring estimates risk; decision-making applies approved lending policies and other required conditions.
- **Traditional statistical scoring versus AI-based scoring:** Both use quantitative methods, but model complexity, data requirements, and explainability characteristics can differ.
- **Probability of default versus certainty of default:** A probability is a statistical estimate over a defined horizon, not a guarantee about an individual outcome.
- **Predictive performance versus responsible use:** A model may predict outcomes accurately without automatically satisfying fairness, privacy, security, or legal requirements.
- **Model risk versus business risk:** Model risk concerns weaknesses in model design, data, implementation, or use; business risk concerns the consequences of the organization's decisions and operations.
- **Model output versus authorized action:** A model should not independently acquire authority to change credit terms or execute consequential actions.
- **Data availability versus permitted data use:** Data being accessible does not establish that its use is lawful, necessary, accurate, or appropriate.
- **Human involvement versus meaningful human oversight:** A human reviewer must have sufficient information, authority, and practical ability to challenge an automated recommendation.
- **Technical implementation versus governance approval:** A functioning model does not establish that the system is ready or authorized for production.
- **Documentation versus assurance:** Documents describe requirements and decisions; assurance evaluates whether the relevant controls have been implemented and operate effectively.
- **Deployment approval versus continuing suitability:** A model approved for a defined purpose must be reassessed when material assumptions, data, risks, or operating conditions change.

**The central principle is that trustworthy AI credit scoring requires validated models, governed data, secure architecture, appropriate customer safeguards, explicit accountability, and controlled use of model outputs within an authorized credit-decision process.**

## Part 2 — Risk Assessment, Data Governance, Model Risk, Fairness, and Regulatory Compliance

### 6. AI Credit-Scoring Risk Assessment Methodology

AI credit-scoring systems introduce interconnected business, financial, operational, cybersecurity, privacy, model, and regulatory risks. Unlike conventional IT systems, their outputs can influence whether an individual receives a loan, qualifies for a mortgage, obtains a credit card, or receives particular lending terms.

A governance assessment must therefore evaluate both the security of the technology and the consequences of its decisions.

A technically secure system can still create unacceptable risk if its training data is biased, its model is poorly calibrated, its decisions cannot be explained, or its outputs are used beyond their approved purpose.

#### 6.1.1 Objectives of the Risk Assessment

The assessment should establish whether:

- The credit-scoring use case has a clearly defined and approved purpose.
- Data sources are lawful, appropriate, accurate, relevant, and sufficiently representative.
- The model is independently validated before production use.
- Model outputs are reliable enough for the decisions they support.
- Discriminatory outcomes and unjustified differences in treatment are identified and addressed.
- Personal data is protected throughout the model lifecycle.
- Human review and customer appeal mechanisms are appropriate to the decision's impact.
- Cybersecurity controls protect the model, training data, features, APIs, and decision thresholds.
- Third-party models and data providers are subject to appropriate due diligence.
- The organization can demonstrate compliance through documented evidence.

#### 6.1.2 Risk Assessment Lifecycle

A structured assessment should follow a repeatable process.

```text
STEP 1: DEFINE THE USE CASE
        |
        v
STEP 2: IDENTIFY ASSETS, DATA, MODELS, AND DECISION POINTS
        |
        v
STEP 3: IDENTIFY THREATS, FAILURES, AND CUSTOMER IMPACTS
        |
        v
STEP 4: ASSESS INHERENT RISK
        |
        v
STEP 5: IDENTIFY AND TEST EXISTING CONTROLS
        |
        v
STEP 6: ASSESS RESIDUAL RISK
        |
        v
STEP 7: DEFINE TREATMENT ACTIONS AND RISK OWNERS
        |
        v
STEP 8: OBTAIN APPROVAL FROM AUTHORIZED GOVERNANCE BODIES
        |
        v
STEP 9: MONITOR RISK AND REASSESS WHEN CONDITIONS CHANGE
```

The assessment must cover the entire lifecycle, from data acquisition and model development to deployment, monitoring, retraining, retirement, and deletion of retained data where required.

#### 6.1.3 Risk Categories

| Risk category | Description | Example | Potential impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic | The model does not support the approved business objective | A model optimized for repayment prediction is used to maximize short-term loan sales | Poor business decisions and conduct risk |
| Credit | The model misestimates a borrower's credit risk | High-risk applications receive favorable scores | Increased defaults and financial losses |
| Model | The model is inaccurate, unstable, or inadequately validated | A model performs well during training but poorly on new applicants | Unreliable lending decisions |
| Fairness | The model produces unjustified differences in outcomes | A particular customer group experiences substantially higher rejection rates | Discrimination exposure and customer harm |
| Data | Input data is inaccurate, incomplete, biased, or outdated | Incorrect income information reduces an applicant's score | Unfair or incorrect decisions |
| Cybersecurity | Attackers manipulate data, models, or decision processes | An attacker alters model features through an exposed API | Fraud, financial loss, and compromised decisions |
| Privacy | Personal data is processed without an appropriate basis or safeguard | An unnecessary behavioral dataset is incorporated into scoring | Privacy violations and regulatory exposure |
| Explainability | Decisions cannot be adequately explained | Staff cannot identify the principal factors behind a rejection | Complaints, weak oversight, and potential compliance failures |
| Operational | The scoring service becomes unavailable or produces invalid outputs | A failed deployment causes all applicants to receive a default score | Lending disruption and incorrect decisions |
| Third-party | An external model or data provider introduces unmanaged risk | A provider changes its model without notifying the lender | Uncontrolled changes in credit decisions |
| Regulatory | The organization fails to meet applicable legal obligations | A high-risk AI system is deployed without required conformity measures | Enforcement action and remediation costs |
| Reputational | Customers or the public lose confidence in lending practices | Evidence emerges that a scoring model systematically disadvantages a group | Customer attrition and reputational damage |

#### 6.1.4 Inherent Risk Scoring

A practical risk assessment can use a five-point likelihood scale and a five-point impact scale.

**Inherent Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

| Score | Likelihood | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Negligible |
| 2 | Unlikely | Minor |
| 3 | Possible | Moderate |
| 4 | Likely | Major |
| 5 | Almost certain | Severe |

Illustrative risk bands:

| Score | Risk level | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low | Maintain controls and monitor |
| 5–9 | Moderate | Define proportionate treatment and an accountable owner |
| 10–16 | High | Prioritize mitigation and obtain documented risk-owner oversight |
| 17–25 | Critical | Escalate immediately; do not proceed where the risk exceeds approved tolerance |

These thresholds are an example of an enterprise risk methodology, not a universal regulatory standard. An organization should align them with its approved risk appetite and assessment policy.

For AI credit scoring, a numerical score must not be the only decision criterion. A low-frequency event may still require critical treatment if it could produce severe discriminatory outcomes, large-scale exposure of personal data, or systemic lending errors.

#### 6.1.5 Control Effectiveness and Residual Risk

Inherent risk describes exposure before considering the effect of existing controls. Residual risk is the exposure remaining after those controls have been considered.

For example:

| Risk | Inherent risk | Existing controls | Control assessment | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized modification of credit-scoring thresholds | Critical | Role-based access, approval workflow, logging | Controls tested and operating effectively | Moderate |
| Biased training data | Critical | Data-quality checks and fairness testing | Testing does not cover all relevant populations | High |
| Model drift | High | Performance monitoring and periodic validation | Monitoring detects deterioration but lacks timely alerts | Moderate–High |
| Inaccurate rejection explanations | High | Explanation engine and manual review | Explanation quality has not been independently validated | High |

The ratings above are illustrative. A control should not be treated as effective merely because it exists in a policy or technical design. The assessment must consider implementation, operating effectiveness, coverage, and supporting evidence.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Risk assessment methodology and scoring criteria.
- Approved AI credit-scoring use-case description.
- Risk register with accountable owners.
- Control design and operating-effectiveness results.
- Model validation and fairness assessment reports.
- Risk treatment plans and target completion dates.
- Formal risk acceptance records, where permitted.
- Evidence of approval by authorized decision-makers.

#### 7.1.2. Risk Treatment Prioritization

Not all risks should be treated in the same way. Prioritization should consider severity, customer impact, legal obligations, exposure, control maturity, and the feasibility of reducing the risk.

| Priority | Example | Expected action |
|---|---|---|
| P1 — Immediate | Evidence of systematic discriminatory outcomes or unauthorized threshold manipulation | Escalate, investigate, contain exposure, and consider suspending the affected decision process |
| P2 — High | Model validation is incomplete before production approval | Block production release until required validation and approval criteria are satisfied |
| P3 — Planned | Monitoring lacks an agreed early-warning threshold | Assign an owner, define a measurable control, and establish a deadline |
| P4 — Continuous improvement | Model documentation could be clearer although minimum requirements are met | Improve documentation through the controlled enhancement backlog |

Risk treatment options include:
- **Avoid:** Do not use the AI model for a particular decision or population.
- **Mitigate:** Implement controls that reduce likelihood or impact.
- **Transfer or share:** Allocate certain contractual or financial consequences to another party where appropriate; this does not eliminate the institution's own legal obligations.
- **Accept:** Formally approve a residual risk within delegated authority and applicable legal constraints.

Risk acceptance cannot legitimize unlawful discrimination, waive mandatory regulatory requirements, or substitute for controls required by law.

#### 7.1.3. Risk Ownership and Escalation

A credit-scoring system should not have a single undifferentiated risk owner.

- **Business owner:** Accountable for the business purpose and lending outcomes.
- **Credit risk owner:** Accountable for credit-risk methodology and lending risk appetite.
- **Model owner:** Accountable for the model's intended purpose, performance, and lifecycle.
- **Data owner:** Accountable for approved use, quality, and governance of relevant data.
- **CISO or security owner:** Accountable for cybersecurity risk management within the security function.
- **Privacy owner or DPO, where appointed:** Provides privacy advice and performs the applicable oversight responsibilities.
- **Compliance and Legal:** Interpret and monitor relevant legal and regulatory obligations.
- **Independent Model Risk Management:** Challenges model design, validation, and performance.
- **Internal Audit:** Provides independent assurance according to the audit plan.

An individual can hold multiple responsibilities in a smaller institution, but incompatible duties—particularly model development, independent validation, and final approval—should be separated where practicable.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Approved and maintained risk register.
- Named accountable owners and control owners.
- Documented risk-treatment decisions.
- Risk acceptance and escalation records.
- Evidence of closure and independent verification of remediation.
- Periodic reporting to the appropriate risk committee.

### 8. Data Governance, Data Quality, and Feature Management

Data governance is a foundational control for AI credit scoring. A model's outputs depend on the data used to develop, validate, and operate it. If that data is inaccurate, incomplete, unrepresentative, unlawfully obtained, or improperly transformed, model sophistication cannot reliably correct the underlying problem.

Data governance must cover both conventional applicant information and derived features created by data-processing pipelines.

#### 8.1.1. Data Sources and Provenance

Common data sources include:

| Data source | Example | Principal governance concern |
|---|---|---|
| Customer-provided information | Income, employment, liabilities | Accuracy, verification, purpose limitation |
| Internal banking records | Account history, existing obligations | Access authorization, relevance, retention |
| Credit bureau information | Existing credit commitments and repayment history | Accuracy, permitted use, correction procedures |
| Transactional data | Payment patterns and account activity | Proportionality, purpose compatibility, feature validity |
| Public or external records | Permitted public financial or legal information | Reliability, lawful acquisition, contextual accuracy |
| Derived features | Debt-to-income ratio, payment volatility | Reproducibility, validity, unintended proxies |
| Alternative data | Selected behavioral or commercial signals | Heightened scrutiny of necessity, legality, relevance, and fairness |

The organization should maintain a data inventory recording the source, owner, purpose, classification, lawful basis where applicable, quality requirements, retention rules, transformations, recipients, and systems in which the data is used.

**Data provenance** describes where the data originated and how it was obtained.

**Data lineage** records how data moves and changes across systems, including transformations, feature engineering, model training, inference, and downstream decision-making.

Both are important for investigating incorrect decisions and demonstrating that model inputs can be traced to approved sources.

#### 8.1.2. Data Quality Dimensions

| Dimension | Definition | Credit-scoring example | Control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Data correctly represents the relevant fact | Income is recorded incorrectly | Source verification and reconciliation |
| Completeness | Required information is present | Existing liabilities are missing | Completeness checks and controlled handling of missing values |
| Consistency | Data agrees across relevant records | Monthly income differs between application and servicing records | Reconciliation rules |
| Timeliness | Data is sufficiently current | An old employment record is treated as current | Freshness thresholds and periodic updates |
| Validity | Data conforms to expected formats and business rules | Debt-to-income ratio is outside its valid range | Schema and business-rule validation |
| Representativeness | Data appropriately reflects the population for the intended use | Historical training data excludes important applicant segments | Population analysis and sampling review |
| Traceability | Data can be linked to its origin and transformations | A feature cannot be traced to its source | Lineage records and reproducible pipelines |

Missing information must not automatically be treated as evidence of high credit risk unless that treatment has a valid, documented, and appropriately assessed rationale.

A missing value may reflect an incomplete application, a technical integration failure, a reporting gap, or a genuine lack of information. These causes have different implications and should not be conflated.

#### 8.1.3. Feature Engineering and Feature Governance

Feature engineering converts raw information into variables used by a model.

For example:

```text
RAW INPUTS
  |
  +--> Verified monthly income
  |
  +--> Monthly debt obligations
  |
  +--> Existing credit exposure
  |
  v
DATA VALIDATION AND NORMALIZATION
  |
  v
FEATURE ENGINEERING
  |
  +--> Debt-to-income ratio
  |
  +--> Repayment-history indicators
  |
  +--> Credit utilization
  |
  v
FEATURE VALIDATION AND APPROVAL
  |
  v
CREDIT-SCORING MODEL
  |
  v
SCORE, RISK ESTIMATE, OR RECOMMENDATION
  |
  v
GOVERNED LENDING DECISION PROCESS
```

A feature registry should document each material model input.

| Feature attribute | Required information |
|---|---|
| Feature name | Unique identifier |
| Business definition | What the feature represents |
| Source | Original data system or provider |
| Transformation | Formula, logic, or processing method |
| Intended purpose | Why the feature is relevant to credit assessment |
| Data owner | Accountable person or function |
| Model usage | Models and decisions using the feature |
| Quality requirements | Validation and freshness criteria |
| Privacy classification | Personal or sensitive-data considerations |
| Fairness assessment | Potential discriminatory effects or proxy risks |
| Approval status | Approved, restricted, under review, or prohibited |
| Version and change history | Evidence of modifications and approvals |

Features that are strongly correlated with protected characteristics, or that may act as proxies for them, require careful evaluation. Correlation alone does not establish unlawful discrimination, but neither does the absence of an explicitly protected feature demonstrate that a model is fair.

The institution should assess the feature's purpose, predictive contribution, legal permissibility, effects on relevant groups, and availability of less problematic alternatives.

#### 8.1.4. Data Leakage and Label Quality

**Data leakage** occurs when model development uses information that would not legitimately be available at the time the model makes its prediction, or when the training process inadvertently incorporates information that reveals the outcome being predicted.

For example, a model intended to estimate default risk at loan origination could be trained using a variable created only after the borrower defaults. This may produce misleadingly strong test results while failing in real-world use.

Controls should include:
- Explicit definition of the prediction timestamp.
- Separation of features available at prediction time from later information.
- Time-based training and validation splits where appropriate.
- Independent review of feature construction.
- Reproducible data preparation pipelines.
- Documentation of the target variable and observation window.
- Tests for label contamination and post-outcome information.

Label quality also requires scrutiny. Historical labels such as default, delinquency, or repayment status may reflect inconsistent operational practices, incomplete observation periods, or changes in lending policy.

Where outcomes are observed only for applicants previously approved for credit, the resulting dataset may not fully represent the risk characteristics of rejected applicants. This limitation should be documented and addressed using a method appropriate to the model's purpose.

#### 8.1.5. Data Lifecycle Controls

| Lifecycle stage | Principal risks | Governance controls |
|---|---|---|
| Collection | Excessive or unauthorized data collection | Purpose definition, source approval, minimization |
| Ingestion | Data manipulation or integration errors | Authentication, schema validation, integrity checks |
| Storage | Unauthorized disclosure or alteration | Encryption, access control, monitoring |
| Preparation | Incorrect transformations or leakage | Version-controlled pipelines, peer review, validation |
| Training | Biased or contaminated datasets | Dataset approval, provenance, sampling review |
| Validation | Unrepresentative test data | Independent test datasets and documented limitations |
| Inference | Invalid, stale, or manipulated input data | Runtime validation and anomaly detection |
| Retention | Excessive storage or unnecessary reuse | Retention schedules and access reviews |
| Deletion | Data remains accessible after required deletion | Verified deletion processes and backup-retention controls |
| Archiving | Loss of evidence or uncontrolled access | Restricted access and documented retention rationale |

Retention requirements should distinguish between operational data, training datasets, model artifacts, decision records, audit evidence, and data subject to legal holds. Deletion obligations and recordkeeping obligations must be reconciled under applicable law and the institution's documented retention policy.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Data inventory and lineage documentation.
- Approved feature registry.
- Data-quality rules and monitoring results.
- Training, validation, and test dataset documentation.
- Evidence of leakage testing and label-quality review.
- Privacy and data-retention assessments.
- Records of feature approval and change control.

### 9. Model Risk Management, Validation, Calibration, and Performance

Model Risk Management (MRM) establishes how an organization identifies, measures, controls, and monitors the risks created by models used in material business decisions.

For AI credit scoring, MRM should cover model development, independent validation, approval, deployment, monitoring, change management, and retirement.

A model should not be considered reliable merely because it achieves a high accuracy score. Credit risk is often characterized by imbalanced outcomes: most applicants may not default, meaning a model that predicts no defaults could achieve superficially high accuracy while providing little practical value.

#### 9.1.1. Model Inventory and Classification

The organization should maintain a model inventory containing, at minimum:

- Model identifier and name.
- Business owner and technical owner.
- Intended purpose and prohibited uses.
- Model type and version.
- Input data and principal features.
- Output definition and downstream decisions.
- Materiality and risk classification.
- Development and validation status.
- Production environment and dependencies.
- Performance thresholds and monitoring frequency.
- Known limitations and compensating controls.
- Last validation date and next review date.
- Approved change history and retirement status.

Models used to approve or reject loans, determine credit limits, or materially influence the terms offered to individuals may warrant a high internal model-risk classification because of their financial and customer impact.

Internal materiality classification should not be confused with the legal classification of an AI system under the EU AI Act. The organization must assess each applicable framework independently.

#### 9.1.2. Independent Model Validation

Validation should challenge whether the model is suitable for its intended purpose and whether the evidence supports its use.

| Validation area | Key questions | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Conceptual soundness | Is the methodology appropriate for the stated objective? | Model design document and independent challenge |
| Data suitability | Are the datasets relevant, sufficiently reliable, and appropriately governed? | Data-quality and representativeness reports |
| Statistical performance | Does the model perform adequately on unseen data? | Out-of-sample evaluation results |
| Calibration | Do predicted risk estimates align with observed outcomes? | Calibration curves and backtesting |
| Stability | Does performance remain acceptable across time and relevant conditions? | Stability and drift analysis |
| Fairness | Are outcomes and errors appropriately assessed across relevant groups? | Fairness evaluation and documented limitations |
| Explainability | Can material drivers of a score be understood and communicated appropriately? | Explanation validation and case testing |
| Robustness | Does the model remain reliable under plausible input variations? | Stress tests and sensitivity analysis |
| Implementation | Does the deployed implementation match the approved model? | Version verification and regression testing |
| Governance | Are ownership, approvals, limitations, and monitoring defined? | Approval records and model documentation |

Validation independence should be proportionate to risk and consistent with the institution's governance requirements. The individuals who independently validate a material model should not simply approve their own development work without an effective independent challenge.

#### 9.1.3. Performance Metrics

Different metrics measure different aspects of performance. No single metric is sufficient for all credit-scoring systems.

| Metric | What it measures | Governance interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Precision | Proportion of predicted positive cases that are actually positive | Useful when false-positive predictions create significant costs |
| Recall (sensitivity) | Proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified | Useful when missing positive cases is costly |
| Specificity | Proportion of actual negative cases correctly identified | Helps assess the treatment of negative cases |
| F1 score | Harmonic mean of precision and recall | Provides a combined measure of precision and recall |
| ROC-AUC | Ability to rank positive cases above negative cases across thresholds | Measures discrimination, not calibration or fairness |
| Precision–Recall AUC | Precision–recall performance across thresholds | Particularly informative when the positive class is uncommon |
| Brier score | Mean squared error of predicted probabilities for binary outcomes | Assesses probabilistic prediction quality |
| Calibration error | Difference between predicted and observed outcome rates | Indicates whether predicted probabilities align with observed frequencies |
| Population Stability Index (PSI) | A commonly used measure of distributional change | Can flag shifts in input or score distributions; thresholds require contextual interpretation |
| Default-rate analysis | Observed default frequency for a defined population | Supports credit-performance monitoring, subject to observation-window limitations |

The meaning of a positive class must be defined explicitly. In default prediction, the positive class may represent a default event; in an approval recommendation model, it may represent a different outcome. Metric interpretation depends on that definition.

A model can have strong ROC-AUC but poor calibration. It can also have strong aggregate performance while performing materially worse for a particular population. Model validation must therefore assess ranking, probability quality, operational consequences, and fairness separately.

#### 9.1.4. Threshold Governance

Many credit-scoring processes use a threshold to convert a score or risk estimate into an action.

For example:

```text
APPLICANT DATA
      |
      v
CREDIT-SCORING MODEL
      |
      v
ESTIMATED RISK OR CREDIT SCORE
      |
      v
APPROVED DECISION POLICY
      |
      +----------------------------+
      |                            |
      v                            v
Within approved criteria     Outside approved criteria
      |                            |
      v                            v
Potential approval,          Further review, decline,
subject to other checks      or another defined outcome
```

The diagram is illustrative. Actual lending decisions may combine model outputs with affordability assessments, policy rules, regulatory requirements, manual review, and other factors.

Thresholds should not be modified informally to improve approval rates, reduce operational workloads, or achieve a commercial target without evaluating the resulting credit, fairness, customer, and regulatory implications.

A controlled threshold-change process should require:
1. A documented business rationale.
2. Analysis of expected approval, default, and customer-impact effects.
3. Relevant fairness and compliance testing.
4. Independent challenge where required.
5. Formal approval by authorized decision-makers.
6. Controlled implementation and versioning.
7. Post-implementation monitoring and rollback capability.

#### 9.1.5. Model Drift and Economic Change

Model performance can deteriorate because the applicant population, macroeconomic conditions, product mix, fraud patterns, or data-generating processes change.

Two important concepts are:

- **Data drift:** A change in the distribution of input features.
- **Concept drift:** A change in the relationship between inputs and the outcome being predicted.

A change in input distributions does not automatically mean the model is invalid. It should trigger investigation to determine whether performance, calibration, fairness, or the assumptions underlying the model have deteriorated.

Monitoring should consider:
- Input and score distributions.
- Missing-value rates and feature quality.
- Default rates and observed outcomes, once sufficient outcome data is available.
- Calibration and discrimination.
- Performance by relevant population segments.
- Approval and rejection patterns.
- Changes in product policy or applicant composition.
- Data-pipeline failures and model-version changes.

#### 9.1.6. Model Change Management and Rollback

Model changes can include retraining, feature modifications, threshold adjustments, dependency updates, infrastructure changes, or replacement with another model.

A governed change process should classify changes according to materiality and require appropriate testing and approval.

| Change type | Example | Expected governance |
|---|---|---|
| Minor technical change | Non-functional logging update | Change approval and regression testing proportionate to impact |
| Feature change | Introduction of a new financial indicator | Feature approval, data assessment, model testing, fairness review |
| Model retraining | New training dataset and fitted parameters | Revalidation appropriate to the change and model-risk policy |
| Threshold change | Lowering the score required for approval | Impact assessment, risk review, approval, monitoring |
| Model replacement | Replacing a traditional scorecard with a new AI model | Full lifecycle assessment, independent validation, and production approval |
| Emergency rollback | Returning to a previously approved model after a failure | Controlled incident procedure, authorization, evidence, and follow-up review |

The organization should retain the ability to identify the model version that produced a material decision, subject to applicable recordkeeping and data-protection requirements.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Model inventory and classification.
- Model development and validation documentation.
- Performance, calibration, and stability reports.
- Threshold approval records.
- Fairness and robustness testing results.
- Change approvals, release records, and rollback test evidence.
- Monitoring alerts, investigation records, and remediation actions.

### 10. Fairness, Discrimination, Explainability, and Customer Impact

Fairness is a central governance concern in AI credit scoring because model outputs can affect access to essential financial services and influence the economic opportunities available to individuals.

A model does not need to explicitly use a protected characteristic to produce potentially discriminatory outcomes. Other variables may correlate with protected characteristics or reflect historical patterns of unequal access to financial services.

At the same time, differences in outcomes do not automatically prove unlawful discrimination. A rigorous assessment must examine the applicable legal requirements, the decision context, the data, the model's behavior, the business rationale, and the consequences for affected individuals.

#### 10.1.1. Sources of Unfair Outcomes

| Source | Example | Governance response |
|---|---|---|
| Historical bias | Past lending decisions reflect unjustified historical practices | Assess historical labels and establish appropriate validation criteria |
| Representation bias | Some relevant populations are underrepresented in training data | Evaluate coverage, data limitations, and performance by population |
| Measurement bias | Income or repayment behavior is measured inconsistently | Validate data collection and measurement methods |
| Proxy variables | Geographic or behavioral variables correlate with protected characteristics | Investigate the variable's relevance, effects, and legal permissibility |
| Label bias | Historical outcomes reflect prior decisions rather than an unbiased view of creditworthiness | Examine how labels were generated and what they actually measure |
| Selection bias | Performance is evaluated only on applicants previously granted credit | Document the limitation and use appropriate evaluation methods |
| Deployment bias | A model developed for one product is applied to a different population | Reassess intended purpose, representativeness, and performance |
| Human override bias | Staff consistently override model results for particular groups | Monitor overrides, rationale, and downstream outcomes |

#### 10.1.2. Fairness Assessment Dimensions

A fairness assessment should define the population, decision, protected or relevant groups, outcome, and evaluation method before selecting metrics.

Potential measures include:

| Measure | Main question | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Approval-rate comparison | Do approval rates differ between relevant groups? | Differences may require investigation but do not establish their cause or legal status |
| False-positive-rate comparison | Are certain groups more frequently incorrectly classified as high risk or low risk, depending on the target definition? | Interpretation depends on the positive class and decision process |
| False-negative-rate comparison | Are actual cases of a target outcome missed at different rates? | Requires reliable outcome labels and adequate observation periods |
| Calibration by group | Do predicted risk estimates correspond to observed outcomes across groups? | Requires sufficient, reliable outcome data and careful statistical analysis |
| Error-rate analysis | Do model errors differ materially across populations? | Aggregate error measures can conceal different error types |
| Feature-contribution analysis | Do certain variables drive outcomes differently across groups? | Feature attribution does not, by itself, prove fairness or explain causality |
| Counterfactual analysis | Would a decision change under a carefully defined change to an input? | Counterfactual results depend on assumptions and may not represent realistic alternatives |

Fairness metrics can conflict. A model may be calibrated across groups while exhibiting different error rates, particularly where underlying outcome rates differ. The organization should not assume that one metric resolves all fairness concerns.

The selected metrics, their rationale, limitations, and acceptable thresholds should be documented and approved by the relevant risk and compliance functions.

#### 10.1.3. Proxy Discrimination and Feature Review

Proxy analysis should evaluate whether a feature acts as an indirect indicator of a protected characteristic or produces an unjustified disadvantage.

A structured review should ask:

1. Is the feature relevant to the approved credit-risk objective?
2. Is the feature sufficiently accurate and reliable?
3. Is the feature legally permissible for this purpose?
4. Could it encode historical exclusion or socioeconomic disadvantage?
5. Does its use materially change decisions for relevant groups?
6. Is a less intrusive or less problematic alternative available?
7. Can the feature's effect be explained and independently challenged?
8. Are the associated risks proportionate to the expected benefit?

For example, geographic information may provide legitimate information in certain carefully defined risk assessments, but it may also act as a proxy for socioeconomic or demographic patterns. Its use requires context-specific analysis rather than a blanket conclusion that all geographic features are either acceptable or unacceptable.

Where protected-characteristic data is considered for fairness testing, its collection and use must itself be assessed for legality, necessity, access restrictions, and retention. The organization should not assume that collecting such data is automatically permissible simply because it could help measure disparities.

#### 10.1.4. Explainability and Decision Reasons

Explainability has several audiences:

- **Model developers:** Need to understand model behavior and limitations.
- **Model Risk Management:** Needs to challenge assumptions and evaluate reliability.
- **Credit officers:** Need to interpret scores and apply approved lending policy.
- **Compliance and Legal:** Need to assess decision processes against applicable obligations.
- **Internal Audit:** Needs traceable evidence of design, controls, and operation.
- **Customers:** May need meaningful information about a decision and available review mechanisms, depending on applicable law and the decision context.

Different explanation techniques serve different purposes. A global explanation describes overall model behavior, while a local explanation attempts to explain an individual prediction.

A feature-attribution method may indicate which variables contributed to a particular score. However, it does not necessarily establish causation, prove legal compliance, or provide a complete customer-facing explanation.

A decision-reason process should therefore be validated to ensure that:
- Reasons accurately reflect the actual decision process.
- Explanations are consistent with the approved model and policy.
- Reasons are understandable to their intended audience.
- Explanations do not disclose information that would undermine fraud controls or create an avoidable security risk.
- Explanations are not misleading, overly generic, or generated from unrelated variables.
- Customer communications comply with applicable legal requirements.

#### 10.1.5. Human Oversight and Customer Review

Human oversight should be meaningful, not merely ceremonial.

A reviewer should have:
- Access to relevant information needed to evaluate the case.
- Authority to request additional evidence or challenge the model output.
- Clear criteria for overrides and escalation.
- Sufficient training to understand model limitations.
- Protection against pressure to approve automated outcomes without scrutiny.
- A process for documenting the rationale for a material override.

Human review is particularly important where the decision has a significant impact, the model's evidence is uncertain, data is disputed, or an applicant raises a credible concern about accuracy.

However, adding a human reviewer does not automatically eliminate discrimination, bias, or legal obligations. Reviewers may rely excessively on model recommendations or reproduce the same underlying assumptions.

The institution should monitor override rates, reviewer consistency, reasons for overrides, and resulting outcomes.

#### 10.1.6. Customer Impact and Remediation

When a model produces an incorrect or potentially unfair decision, remediation should address both the individual case and any underlying systemic problem.

A response may include:
- Rechecking the applicant's source data.
- Correcting inaccurate records.
- Reassessing the decision using an approved process.
- Providing an appropriate explanation or review route.
- Identifying other customers who may have been affected.
- Correcting the model, features, threshold, or decision policy where necessary.
- Reporting material incidents to the relevant governance functions.
- Assessing whether external notification or regulatory reporting is required.

The institution should retain evidence showing the complaint, investigation, decision, remediation, and any broader corrective action, subject to applicable retention and privacy requirements.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Fairness assessment methodology.
- Group-level performance and outcome analyses where lawful and appropriate.
- Proxy-feature assessments.
- Explanation validation results.
- Human-review and override procedures.
- Customer complaint and remediation records.
- Documented approval of residual fairness risks.

### 11. Privacy, Automated Decision-Making, and Regulatory Compliance

AI credit scoring may involve extensive processing of personal financial information and may materially affect individuals. Privacy governance and regulatory compliance must therefore be integrated into the system's design and operating model.

The applicable requirements depend on the institution, the lending product, the jurisdiction, the role of each organization, and the actual use of the model. A regulatory assessment must distinguish binding legal obligations from voluntary standards, supervisory expectations, and internal policy.

#### 11.1.1. GDPR and Personal Data Processing

Where the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies, the institution should assess the relevant principles and obligations throughout the processing lifecycle.

| GDPR consideration | Credit-scoring application | Governance action |
|---|---|---|
| Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency | Personal data must be processed under an appropriate legal basis and with required transparency | Document the lawful basis and provide appropriate notices |
| Purpose limitation | Data must be collected for specified purposes and not reused incompatibly | Approve purposes and assess secondary use |
| Data minimization | Data should be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary | Review each data source and feature |
| Accuracy | Inaccurate personal data must be addressed under applicable requirements | Establish correction and verification procedures |
| Storage limitation | Personal data should not be retained longer than necessary, subject to applicable obligations | Define retention and deletion rules |
| Integrity and confidentiality | Personal data requires appropriate security | Implement access controls, encryption, monitoring, and incident response |
| Accountability | The controller must be able to demonstrate compliance | Maintain records, assessments, approvals, and control evidence |
| Data subject rights | Individuals may have rights concerning their personal data | Establish request-handling and escalation procedures |
| Privacy by design and default | Privacy safeguards should be incorporated into system design | Include privacy requirements in architecture and change reviews |

The organization should identify its role as controller, processor, or potentially joint controller for each relevant processing activity. Contractual labels alone do not determine these roles; the actual allocation of purposes and means of processing is important.

#### 11.1.2. Automated Decision-Making and GDPR Article 22

GDPR Article 22 addresses certain decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produce legal effects concerning an individual or similarly significantly affect them, subject to the provision's scope, exceptions, and safeguards.

Not every use of an AI score automatically falls within Article 22. The assessment must establish how the score is used, whether the decision is solely automated in the relevant sense, and the effect on the individual.

For example, a system that produces a score for an employee to review may require a different legal analysis from a system that automatically rejects a loan without meaningful human involvement.

Where Article 22 applies, the institution must assess the relevant legal conditions and safeguards. Depending on the circumstances, this may include requirements concerning the permitted basis for the decision and safeguards such as the ability to obtain human intervention, express a point of view, and contest the decision.

A nominal human approval step does not necessarily prevent a process from being considered solely automated. The practical role and authority of the human reviewer matter.

**Governance questions:**
- Does the model make the decision, or does it only support a broader decision process?
- Is human involvement substantive and capable of changing the outcome?
- Does the decision produce legal or similarly significant effects?
- Which Article 22 conditions and safeguards, if any, apply?
- What information must be provided to the individual?
- How can the individual challenge data accuracy or the decision?
- Has the legal analysis been documented and approved?

These questions require assessment of the actual processing arrangement rather than a blanket classification based solely on the presence of AI.

#### 11.1.3. Data Protection Impact Assessment

A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) may be required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing that produces legal or similarly significant effects is one of the categories specifically addressed by GDPR Article 35(3)(a).

The institution should document whether a DPIA is required and the rationale for its conclusion.

A DPIA for AI credit scoring should generally consider:
- The nature, scope, context, and purposes of processing.
- Categories of individuals and personal data involved.
- Data sources and the lawful basis for processing.
- Necessity and proportionality of the proposed processing.
- Potential discrimination and other impacts on individuals.
- Risks of inaccurate, opaque, or inappropriate decisions.
- Cybersecurity threats and potential data exposure.
- Measures to address identified risks.
- Roles of controllers, processors, and other relevant parties.
- Residual risks and the need for further action.

A DPIA is not a substitute for model validation, cybersecurity risk assessment, or AI Act compliance assessment. These activities may share evidence, but they address different legal and technical questions.

#### 11.1.4. EU AI Act: Creditworthiness and High-Risk Classification

The EU AI Act includes certain AI systems used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score within the high-risk category under Annex III, subject to the applicable provisions and exceptions. Systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud are treated differently under that Annex.

This distinction matters because an institution may operate both:
- A fraud-detection model intended to identify suspicious transactions; and
- A creditworthiness model intended to evaluate a person's ability or likelihood to repay credit.

These systems should not be treated as legally equivalent simply because both operate in a financial institution.

For a creditworthiness system, the organization should assess:
- The system's actual intended purpose.
- Whether the system falls within the relevant Annex III category.
- Whether a stated exception is applicable and all its conditions are met.
- The role of the institution in the AI value chain.
- Applicable high-risk AI requirements and conformity obligations.
- Required technical documentation, logging, human oversight, and monitoring.
- Quality management, risk management, and post-market obligations where applicable.
- Whether the system is subject to additional requirements because of its deployment context.

An institution should not assume that an exception applies simply because a system supports fraud prevention, includes a human reviewer, or is described internally as a decision-support tool. The system's intended purpose and actual function must be evaluated against the applicable law.

The EU AI Act has phased application dates and transitional provisions. The institution must confirm which obligations apply at the time of development, deployment, or operation rather than relying on a static checklist.

#### 11.1.5. Other Financial and Security Requirements

Depending on the institution and service, other requirements may apply.

| Requirement or framework | Relevance | Assessment approach |
|---|---|---|
| Applicable banking and consumer-credit law | Lending decisions, affordability, disclosures, fair treatment, and creditworthiness assessments | Determine obligations for the institution, product, and jurisdiction |
| European Banking Authority (EBA) guidance and applicable supervisory expectations | Governance, internal controls, credit risk, and model risk, depending on the relevant guidance | Identify applicable instruments and supervisory expectations |
| Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) | ICT risk management, incident handling, resilience testing, and third-party ICT risk for entities and services within scope | Map AI-related dependencies into the institution's ICT risk framework |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI management system and organizational AI governance | Use as a governance framework where adopted |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information security management | Map relevant information-security risks and controls |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework | AI risk management across governance, mapping, measurement, and management | Use as a risk-management reference where adopted |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework | Cybersecurity governance and risk-management outcomes | Map applicable security outcomes |
| Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) | Cardholder-data environments, if the relevant systems and activities fall within scope | Assess actual card-data flows and scope rather than assuming applicability to all scoring systems |

These frameworks do not have identical legal status. A standard may be voluntary unless incorporated into a contract, policy, certification commitment, or other applicable obligation. A regulation or binding legal requirement must be assessed according to its own scope and provisions.

#### 11.1.6. Regulatory Obligations Mapping

A regulatory obligations register should translate legal requirements into accountable, testable obligations.

| Obligation area | Example control requirement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| AI system classification | Document the intended purpose and classification rationale | Approved classification assessment |
| AI risk management | Identify, assess, treat, and monitor relevant AI risks | Risk register and treatment records |
| Data governance | Maintain appropriate data quality, provenance, and governance controls | Dataset documentation and data-quality reports |
| Technical documentation | Maintain documentation appropriate to the applicable legal requirements | Controlled technical documentation |
| Logging and traceability | Preserve relevant records needed for oversight and investigation | Logs, decision records, and retention controls |
| Human oversight | Establish appropriate oversight measures where required | Procedures, training, and review records |
| Accuracy and robustness | Define and test appropriate performance requirements | Validation and monitoring reports |
| Cybersecurity | Protect the system against relevant threats and vulnerabilities | Security assessments and remediation evidence |
| Transparency | Provide required information to relevant users and affected individuals | Approved notices and communications |
| Monitoring and incident handling | Detect, investigate, and respond to relevant failures or incidents | Monitoring records and incident procedures |
| Supplier management | Establish appropriate controls over relevant providers and dependencies | Due-diligence and contract records |
| Accountability | Assign responsibilities and retain evidence of compliance | Governance charter, RACI, and approvals |

The organization should assign an owner to each obligation, define how compliance is demonstrated, and establish a process to reassess requirements when legislation, guidance, products, or system capabilities change.

**Required governance evidence:**
- GDPR applicability and lawful-basis assessment.
- Records of processing activities where required.
- DPIA screening and completed DPIA where required.
- Automated decision-making legal assessment.
- AI Act classification and applicability assessment.
- Regulatory obligations register.
- Approved transparency and customer-review processes.
- Evidence of compliance monitoring and remediation.

### 12. Third-Party, Cloud, and External Data Provider Risk

AI credit-scoring systems often rely on external services, including cloud infrastructure, credit bureau data, fraud-prevention services, hosted models, data enrichment providers, and model development platforms.

Third-party involvement does not eliminate the financial institution's responsibility to manage the risks associated with its lending decisions. The organization should understand the service, the provider's role, the data flows, the dependencies, and the controls that remain under its own responsibility.

#### 12.1.1. Third-Party Risk Categories

| Risk category | Example | Governance concern |
|---|---|---|
| Model opacity | A provider supplies a score without sufficient documentation | Inability to validate suitability, limitations, or material behavior |
| Data quality | A credit bureau provides outdated or incorrect records | Incorrect assessment of an applicant |
| Model changes | A provider updates its model without adequate notification | Uncontrolled changes to lending outcomes |
| Privacy | Personal data is processed by a provider for an incompatible purpose | Unlawful or excessive processing |
| Cybersecurity | A supplier's compromised environment exposes applicant information | Data breach or model compromise |
| Concentration | Several critical lending services rely on one provider | Correlated failure and reduced resilience |
| Availability | A hosted scoring service becomes unavailable | Lending disruption |
| Jurisdiction | Data or support access crosses relevant legal boundaries | Transfer, confidentiality, and regulatory concerns |
| Subcontracting | A provider introduces an unassessed subprocessor | Reduced visibility and unmanaged dependencies |
| Exit risk | The institution cannot migrate away from a provider | Operational dependency and weak negotiating position |
| Intellectual property | Contractual terms restrict access to required documentation | Limits on auditability, validation, or transition |
| Integrity | External model artifacts or datasets are altered | Incorrect or malicious model behavior |

#### 12.1.2. Third-Party Due Diligence

Due diligence should be proportionate to the criticality of the service and the sensitivity of the data.

**Business and service assessment**
- What specific function does the provider perform?
- Does the service materially influence creditworthiness assessments?
- What happens if the service is inaccurate or unavailable?
- Is the provider substitutable within a reasonable period?
- Does the service support a critical or important business function under the institution's applicable framework?

**Model and data assessment**
- What is the model's intended purpose?
- What are the principal data sources and known limitations?
- Is sufficient information available for independent validation?
- How are model changes communicated and controlled?
- How are data quality and model performance monitored?
- Can the provider demonstrate appropriate data provenance and integrity?

**Cybersecurity assessment**
- How does the provider authenticate users and systems?
- How is privileged access managed?
- How are customer data and model artifacts protected?
- What logging, monitoring, and incident-response capabilities exist?
- How are vulnerabilities assessed and remediated?
- What independent security assurance reports are available, and what are their scope and limitations?

**Privacy and legal assessment**
- What role does the provider play in the processing?
- What data is processed and for what purpose?
- Where is data stored and accessed?
- Are international data transfers involved?
- Are subcontractors or subprocessors used?
- What contractual obligations govern deletion, security, confidentiality, and data-subject requests?
- Can the institution meet its own regulatory obligations while using the service?

#### 12.1.3. Contractual Governance Requirements

Contracts should establish clear and enforceable obligations appropriate to the service.

| Contractual area | Requirement to consider |
|---|---|
| Scope and intended purpose | Define the service and authorized use |
| Data processing | Define relevant roles, instructions, purposes, and safeguards |
| Confidentiality | Protect applicant information, model artifacts, and institutional information |
| Security | Specify appropriate security obligations and incident cooperation |
| Model transparency | Define documentation, limitations, and information required for oversight |
| Change notification | Require notification of material changes affecting service behavior or risk |
| Performance | Define service levels and, where appropriate, model-performance commitments |
| Audit and assurance | Establish appropriate rights to obtain evidence and assess controls |
| Incident reporting | Define notification timelines and cooperation obligations consistent with applicable requirements |
| Subcontracting | Establish requirements for notification, authorization, and flow-down obligations as applicable |
| Data retention and deletion | Define handling of data at termination and throughout the service lifecycle |
| Business continuity | Define resilience, recovery, and contingency arrangements |
| Exit and portability | Establish transition assistance, data export, and migration arrangements |
| Regulatory cooperation | Support access to evidence and cooperation needed to meet applicable obligations |

Contractual assurances are not a substitute for technical testing or independent risk assessment. A supplier's certification or assurance report should be reviewed for its scope, exclusions, period covered, and relevance to the actual service being used.

#### 12.1.4. Cloud Shared Responsibility

Cloud deployment introduces a shared-responsibility model. The allocation of duties depends on the service model, configuration, contract, and applicable legal requirements.

```text
FINANCIAL INSTITUTION
  |
  +--> Lending purpose and decision policy
  +--> Credit risk appetite and governance
  +--> Lawful and appropriate data use
  +--> Model acceptance and validation oversight
  +--> Customer communications and review processes
  +--> User access and tenant configuration, where applicable
  +--> Business continuity and exit planning
  |
  v
CLOUD OR AI SERVICE PROVIDER
  |
  +--> Contracted infrastructure and service operation
  +--> Provider-side security controls
  +--> Service availability commitments
  +--> Provider-managed platform components
  +--> Contracted incident and assurance support
  |
  v
JOINTLY GOVERNED DEPENDENCIES
  |
  +--> Identity and access integration
  +--> Data movement and API security
  +--> Logging and incident coordination
  +--> Model and service changes
  +--> Recovery, portability, and exit
```

The diagram illustrates typical responsibilities, not a universal allocation. The institution must verify which controls it operates, which controls the provider operates, and which require joint implementation.

For example, a cloud provider may secure the underlying infrastructure while the institution remains responsible for access permissions, data classification, the approved use of the scoring model, and its own decision-making process.

#### 12.1.5. Ongoing Supplier Monitoring

Third-party risk assessment should continue after onboarding.

Monitoring should include:
- Changes to provider ownership, financial condition, or service scope.
- Security incidents and material vulnerabilities.
- Changes in model methodology, data sources, or performance.
- Availability and service-level performance.
- Relevant audit findings and remediation.
- Changes in subprocessors or data-processing locations.
- Changes in applicable laws or supervisory expectations.
- Concentration and dependency risk.
- Provider exit readiness and availability of alternative arrangements.

A material provider change should trigger reassessment where it could affect the system's intended purpose, model performance, privacy, security, regulatory status, or customer outcomes.

#### 12.1.6. Exit and Contingency Planning

Exit planning should be established before a provider becomes operationally indispensable.

A practical plan should address:
1. Identification of critical provider dependencies.
2. Data and model-artifact portability.
3. Alternative service providers or internal capabilities, where feasible.
4. Contract termination and transition requirements.
5. Secure transfer, retention, and deletion of data.
6. Preservation of required decision and audit records.
7. Validation of any replacement model or service.
8. Customer and operational impact during migration.
9. Recovery testing and approval of the replacement arrangement.
10. Confirmation that residual third-party risks remain within approved tolerance.

Replacing a model or data provider may materially change the institution's credit decisions. The organization should not assume that a replacement service is equivalent merely because it performs a similar function.

**Required governance evidence:**
- Third-party and service dependency inventory.
- Supplier due-diligence assessments.
- Model and data provider documentation.
- Contractual control mapping.
- Cloud shared-responsibility matrix.
- Supplier monitoring and assurance records.
- Material-change assessments.
- Business continuity and exit plans.
- Evidence that replacement arrangements are tested and approved.

## Core Principle

AI credit-scoring governance must integrate risk management, data governance, model validation, fairness, privacy, regulatory compliance, and third-party oversight. These are interdependent disciplines: weaknesses in one area can undermine the reliability and legitimacy of the entire lending decision process.

The institution must be able to demonstrate not only that a model produces useful predictions, but also that the model is appropriate for its intended purpose, supported by governed data, independently assessed, monitored throughout its lifecycle, and operated within applicable legal and risk boundaries.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- **Inherent risk vs. residual risk:** Exposure before controls versus exposure remaining after controls are assessed.
- **Data quality vs. data representativeness:** Whether data is accurate and complete versus whether it appropriately reflects the intended population.
- **Model performance vs. model fairness:** Predictive effectiveness versus the assessment of potentially unjustified differences in outcomes and errors.
- **Discrimination indicators vs. legal conclusions:** Statistical disparities can identify issues requiring investigation but do not, by themselves, determine whether unlawful discrimination has occurred.
- **Explainability vs. accountability:** Understanding model behavior does not replace clear ownership, decision authority, or documented responsibility.
- **Model score vs. lending decision:** A score is an input to a governed decision process, not necessarily the final decision itself.
- **Human involvement vs. meaningful human oversight:** A human's presence does not automatically establish effective review or prevent a process from being considered solely automated under applicable law.
- **Privacy assessment vs. AI risk assessment:** Privacy analysis addresses personal-data processing and individual rights, while AI risk assessment covers a broader range of system, model, operational, and societal risks.
- **Regulatory obligation vs. voluntary framework:** Binding legal requirements must be distinguished from standards and frameworks adopted as governance references.
- **Supplier assurance vs. institutional accountability:** External certifications and contractual commitments provide evidence but do not remove the institution's responsibility to manage its own risks.
