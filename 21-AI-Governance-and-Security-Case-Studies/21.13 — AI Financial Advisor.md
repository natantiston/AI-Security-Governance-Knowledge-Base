# 21.13 — AI Financial Advisor: Enterprise Use Cases, Business Context, Architecture, and Risk Landscape
## Part 1 — Business Context, AI System Architecture, Data Flows, and Governance Foundations

### 1. Introduction to AI-Powered Financial Advisory Systems

An AI-powered financial advisor is a technology system that uses artificial intelligence to analyze financial information, interpret customer requirements, generate financial insights, and support or automate certain financial advisory activities.

Depending on its design and regulatory classification, the system may provide educational information, help users understand their financial position, recommend investment products, construct portfolios, support retirement planning, or execute transactions through integrated financial platforms.

AI financial advisory systems can combine several technologies:

- **Large language models (LLMs):** Interpret natural-language questions and generate explanations, summaries, and conversational responses.
- **Predictive machine learning:** Estimate financial outcomes, customer behavior, market-related variables, or other defined targets.
- **Recommendation engines:** Match customer profiles and stated preferences with potentially suitable products or strategies.
- **Portfolio optimization models:** Evaluate possible portfolio allocations against specified objectives and constraints.
- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG):** Retrieve information from approved financial documents, product disclosures, policies, and research materials to support generated answers.
- **Rules engines:** Enforce eligibility conditions, product restrictions, regulatory requirements, and predefined business rules.
- **Financial data integrations:** Retrieve balances, transactions, market information, portfolio holdings, and other authorized financial data.
- **Agentic AI:** Coordinate multiple steps, such as collecting information, analyzing a portfolio, preparing recommendations, and initiating approved workflows.

These capabilities create business opportunities but also introduce material governance concerns. A financial advisor may influence a person's savings, investments, debt management, retirement planning, or ability to meet long-term financial objectives.

Incorrect advice, biased recommendations, unauthorized disclosure of financial information, or uncontrolled transactions can cause direct financial harm.

Consequently, an AI financial advisor must be governed as a potentially high-impact decision-support or decision-making system rather than merely as a conversational chatbot.

#### 1.1 Common Types of AI Financial Advisors

| System Type | Primary Function | Example | Principal Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial education assistant | Explains financial concepts and terminology | Explaining compound interest or diversification | Inaccurate information and misleading explanations |
| Personal finance assistant | Helps users understand income, expenses, and budgets | Identifying recurring expenses and preparing a budget | Financial-data confidentiality and inaccurate categorization |
| Investment research assistant | Summarizes financial reports and market information | Comparing investment products using approved documentation | Unsupported claims and misleading analysis |
| Robo-advisor | Provides automated portfolio recommendations or management | Recommending an asset allocation based on a customer profile | Suitability, model risk, and regulatory compliance |
| Retirement planning assistant | Estimates future retirement income and savings requirements | Modeling different retirement contribution scenarios | Incorrect assumptions and overconfident projections |
| Credit and lending assistant | Supports borrowing decisions or provides loan guidance | Estimating affordability or explaining loan eligibility | Discrimination, inaccurate assessments, and regulatory exposure |
| Wealth management assistant | Supports portfolio analysis and wealth-planning activities | Identifying concentration risk in a customer's investments | Incomplete information and inappropriate recommendations |
| Autonomous financial agent | Performs approved financial workflows using connected tools | Preparing and submitting a transaction subject to authorization | Unauthorized actions, excessive permissions, and direct financial loss |

These categories are not mutually exclusive. A single platform may combine several capabilities.

The governance requirements depend on the actual functionality, customer population, jurisdictions, data processed, degree of automation, and consequences of the system's outputs.

#### 1.2 Advisory Support Versus Regulated Financial Advice

One of the most important governance distinctions is the difference between providing general financial information and providing personalized recommendations that may constitute regulated financial advice.

Consider the following examples:

**Example A — General financial education**

A user asks an AI assistant to explain the difference between stocks and bonds.

The system provides a general explanation of ownership, debt, risk, and potential returns.

**Example B — Personalized investment recommendation**

A user provides their income, savings, age, financial obligations, investment experience, and risk tolerance. The system recommends specific financial products and suggests how much of the user's savings to invest in each product.

The second scenario presents substantially greater governance and regulatory concerns because the recommendation is based on personal circumstances and may influence a consequential financial decision.

**Example C — Automated portfolio management**

The system analyzes a customer's portfolio, decides which investments to buy or sell, and submits transactions through a connected brokerage platform.

This scenario introduces additional requirements involving transaction authorization, suitability or appropriateness where applicable, operational resilience, execution controls, and the prevention of unauthorized financial actions.

The precise legal classification depends on the service, the nature of the recommendation, the jurisdiction, and applicable exemptions. A disclaimer stating that the system is for informational purposes does not, by itself, determine its regulatory status.

#### 1.3 Why AI Financial Advisors Require Strong Governance

The principal risks arise from the combination of sensitive information, uncertain predictions, consequential recommendations, and possible integration with financial systems.

| Risk Area | Description | Potential Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Financial accuracy | The model generates incorrect calculations or explanations | Customers make decisions based on inaccurate information |
| Recommendation suitability | The system recommends products inconsistent with the customer's circumstances or objectives | Financial losses or inappropriate investment exposure |
| Hallucination | The LLM invents financial products, fees, regulatory requirements, or market information | Misleading or fabricated advice |
| Market uncertainty | The system presents uncertain forecasts as reliable predictions | Excessive confidence in uncertain outcomes |
| Data confidentiality | Financial records are exposed to unauthorized parties | Privacy violations, fraud, and reputational damage |
| Algorithmic bias | Recommendations or eligibility decisions disadvantage certain groups | Unfair outcomes and possible legal violations |
| Model drift | Performance deteriorates as customer behavior, products, or market conditions change | Increasingly unreliable recommendations |
| Prompt injection | Malicious content manipulates the model or its tool-use behavior | Unauthorized disclosure or improper actions |
| Third-party risk | External models, market-data providers, or financial APIs fail or introduce vulnerabilities | Service disruption or incorrect recommendations |
| Unauthorized transactions | An agent initiates a financial action without proper authorization | Direct financial loss |
| Regulatory non-compliance | The service fails to meet applicable financial-services or data-protection requirements | Enforcement action, financial penalties, or restrictions |
| Explainability limitations | Customers and reviewers cannot understand why a recommendation was generated | Weak accountability and difficulty challenging decisions |
| Conflicts of interest | Commercial incentives influence product recommendations | Recommendations may not serve the customer's interests |
| Operational resilience | The system becomes unavailable or produces unreliable outputs during a critical period | Disrupted customer service or financial workflows |

These risks must be evaluated in relation to the system's intended purpose. A general financial education assistant does not necessarily require the same controls as a system that makes personalized investment recommendations or executes transactions.

### 2. Business Scenario: An Enterprise Deploys an AI Financial Advisor

Consider a hypothetical financial services company, **Aurelia Wealth Services**, that plans to introduce an AI-powered financial advisory platform for retail customers.

The company wants to improve customer experience, increase advisory capacity, reduce the time required to analyze financial information, and provide more accessible financial guidance.

The proposed platform combines an LLM-based conversational interface with financial data integrations, a portfolio analytics engine, a product recommendation service, and a human-advisor escalation process.

The company operates in the European Union and serves customers with different income levels, investment experience, financial objectives, and risk profiles.

The platform may eventually support personalized investment recommendations, but the initial deployment is restricted to financial education, financial-position summaries, and decision support for authorized human advisors.

This distinction is important: **the initial approval covers only the capabilities actually assessed and authorized.** Future expansion into autonomous recommendations or transaction execution requires a separate assessment of the additional risks and regulatory obligations.

#### 2.1 Business Objectives

The organization defines the following business objectives.

| Objective | Business Rationale | Success Measurement |
|---|---|---|
| Improve customer accessibility | Provide timely answers to common financial questions | Customer satisfaction and response time |
| Increase advisor productivity | Reduce manual preparation and document-review effort | Advisor preparation time |
| Improve information consistency | Use approved financial information and standardized explanations | Accuracy and consistency testing |
| Support personalized financial analysis | Summarize customer financial information for authorized advisory workflows | Completeness and accuracy of summaries |
| Reduce repetitive administrative work | Automate low-risk information-gathering tasks | Reduction in manual processing time |
| Strengthen compliance oversight | Maintain traceability of recommendations and relevant decisions | Evidence completeness and control-testing results |
| Improve customer engagement | Make financial information easier to understand | Engagement and customer feedback metrics |
| Control operational costs | Reduce unnecessary manual processing while maintaining quality | Cost per completed advisory workflow |

Business objectives must not create incentives to maximize customer transactions, promote unsuitable products, or increase revenue at the expense of customer interests.

For example, measuring success solely by the value of investment products purchased through the platform could create a conflict of interest if the AI system begins optimizing its recommendations for sales conversion rather than customer suitability.

#### 2.2 Proposed System Capabilities

The proposed platform has the following capabilities:

1. **Conversational financial education:** Explains financial concepts using approved information.
2. **Financial-position summarization:** Summarizes authorized information about income, expenses, assets, liabilities, and investments.
3. **Financial document analysis:** Extracts and summarizes information from approved statements and financial documents.
4. **Portfolio analytics:** Calculates portfolio allocation, concentration, fees, and historical performance.
5. **Scenario modeling:** Illustrates potential financial outcomes under clearly stated assumptions.
6. **Product information retrieval:** Retrieves current information from approved product documentation.
7. **Advisor support:** Prepares structured summaries for review by authorized human financial advisors.
8. **Recommendation preparation:** Generates draft recommendations for review where the organization has explicitly authorized this functionality.
9. **Workflow integration:** Creates case records, schedules follow-ups, and routes requests to authorized teams.
10. **Controlled transaction support:** Potential future functionality requiring separate authorization, stronger controls, and a dedicated risk assessment.

#### 2.3 Initial Deployment Boundaries

For the initial deployment, the company establishes the following restrictions:

- The AI assistant may explain general financial concepts.
- The system may summarize customer financial information only when access is authorized.
- Financial calculations must use validated calculation services rather than relying solely on LLM-generated arithmetic.
- Product information must be retrieved from approved, current sources.
- Market-sensitive information must have an identifiable source and relevant timestamp.
- The system must communicate uncertainty and avoid presenting projections as guaranteed outcomes.
- Personalized recommendations must not be provided unless the relevant capability has been assessed and approved.
- The AI assistant must not independently execute investment transactions.
- High-impact or ambiguous cases must be escalated to an authorized human advisor.
- Customer information must be handled according to applicable privacy, security, and retention requirements.
- The organization must maintain sufficient records to investigate material complaints, errors, and incidents.

These boundaries should be implemented through technical controls and workflow restrictions where feasible, rather than relying exclusively on user instructions or prompts.

#### 2.4 Business Stakeholders

| Stakeholder | Responsibility | Key Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Executive Management | Approves business objectives, investment, and risk appetite | Business value and enterprise exposure |
| Chief Risk Officer | Oversees financial and non-financial risk management | Risk acceptance and control effectiveness |
| Chief Information Security Officer | Oversees cybersecurity requirements | Confidentiality, integrity, availability, and AI-specific threats |
| Chief Technology Officer | Oversees technical architecture and implementation | Reliability, integration, scalability, and maintainability |
| AI Governance Committee | Coordinates AI-related governance decisions | Intended use, accountability, risk classification, and ongoing oversight |
| Compliance Department | Assesses applicable financial-services obligations | Regulatory compliance and supervisory expectations |
| Legal Department | Reviews contracts, liability, consumer protection, and other legal issues | Legal exposure and contractual obligations |
| Data Protection Officer | Advises on applicable data-protection obligations and monitors compliance within the DPO's mandate | Personal-data processing and individuals' rights |
| Financial Advisors | Review customer circumstances and provide authorized advice | Recommendation quality and customer suitability |
| Product Management | Defines product functionality and customer experience | Business requirements and product boundaries |
| Data Science and Model Risk Teams | Validate models, assumptions, and performance | Accuracy, reliability, bias, and model limitations |
| Engineering and Platform Teams | Build and operate integrations and controls | Secure implementation and operational stability |
| Security Operations | Monitors relevant security events and coordinates security response | Threat detection and incident response |
| Internal Audit | Independently assesses governance and control effectiveness | Evidence, assurance, and accountability |

The organization should avoid assigning incompatible responsibilities to the same person without appropriate safeguards. For example, the individual responsible for achieving a commercial sales target should not automatically have sole authority to approve the suitability controls designed to protect customers.

### 3. AI Financial Advisor Architecture and Core Components

The architecture of an AI financial advisor should separate language generation from authoritative financial calculations, customer-data authorization, regulatory decision rules, and transaction execution.

An LLM can help interpret questions and explain results, but it should not be treated as the authoritative source for customer balances, live market prices, financial calculations, or regulatory eligibility decisions.

Those functions should be supported by validated systems, approved data sources, and controlled business processes.

The essential requirement is that authorization, financial rules, and critical calculations must not depend solely on the LLM's generated response.

#### 3.2 Customer Interface Layer

The customer interface is the entry point for financial questions and requests.

It may include:

- Web-based financial advisory portals.
- Mobile banking or investment applications.
- Internal dashboards for human advisors.
- Customer service interfaces.
- Approved communication channels.

Governance controls include:

1. Authentication appropriate to the sensitivity of the service.
2. Session management and protection against account takeover.
3. Clear communication about the system's capabilities and limitations.
4. Protection against unauthorized access to customer information.
5. Accessible routes for complaints, corrections, and human assistance.
6. Appropriate controls against automated abuse and denial-of-service attacks.
7. Clear separation between general information and personalized advisory functions.

A conversational interface should not create the impression that every answer is personalized, professionally reviewed, or guaranteed to be correct.

#### 3.3 API Gateway and Identity Control

The API gateway and identity layer enforce access between the customer interface and backend services.

Key controls include:

- Strong authentication.
- Role-based or attribute-based access control where appropriate.
- Customer-level authorization.
- API authentication and authorization.
- Rate limiting and abuse detection.
- Secure session handling.
- Input validation.
- Service-to-service authentication.
- Authorization checks for each sensitive operation.

A critical distinction exists between authenticating a customer and authorizing the customer to access a specific financial record.

For example, successful authentication establishes that a person is using an authenticated account. It does not automatically authorize access to another person's portfolio, a joint account outside the granted permissions, or a financial advisor's restricted administrative functions.

Authorization should be enforced by trusted backend systems. The LLM should never be the sole authority deciding whether a customer can retrieve a balance, inspect a transaction, or initiate a financial workflow.

#### 3.4 AI Orchestration Layer

The orchestration layer coordinates the model, approved data sources, financial services, and workflow controls.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Classifying user intent.
- Determining whether a request falls within the approved use case.
- Selecting approved tools.
- Enforcing workflow restrictions.
- Retrieving relevant context.
- Applying output validation.
- Routing ambiguous requests to a human.
- Recording appropriate audit events.
- Preventing prohibited operations.

For example, if a customer asks, "Should I sell all my retirement investments today?", the orchestration layer should recognize that the request may involve a consequential personalized financial decision.

Depending on the approved system scope, it might provide general educational information, prepare a request for a qualified advisor, or route the matter to an authorized advisory workflow.

It should not automatically convert the request into an investment transaction merely because a brokerage API is technically available.

#### 3.5 Large Language Model Layer

The LLM processes natural-language requests and generates explanations, summaries, and other text.

Potential responsibilities include:

- Explaining financial terminology.
- Summarizing financial documents.
- Interpreting customer questions.
- Explaining outputs produced by validated analytics services.
- Generating structured summaries for human advisors.
- Identifying when a question falls outside the approved scope.

The LLM should not independently determine authoritative account balances, invent market prices, or substitute generated calculations for validated financial computations.

Relevant controls include:

- Approved model and configuration management.
- Restricted access to tools and sensitive data.
- Input and output validation.
- Testing for hallucinations and misleading certainty.
- Evaluation of financial reasoning limitations.
- Prompt-injection testing.
- Monitoring for inappropriate disclosures.
- Defined fallback behavior when the model cannot provide a reliable answer.

The organization should also establish procedures for reviewing material model changes and assessing whether new model behavior affects the system's approved use cases.

#### 3.6 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Layer

A RAG service retrieves relevant information from approved sources and supplies it to the LLM as context.

Potential sources include:

- Official product documentation.
- Approved financial product disclosures.
- Internal advisory policies.
- Customer-authorized financial documents.
- Approved financial education materials.
- Regulatory and compliance guidance.
- Approved market research.
- Relevant fee schedules and contractual information.

The RAG system should distinguish between authoritative information and documents that are merely informative.

For example, a current official product disclosure should generally carry more authority for product-specific terms than an old internal presentation or an unverified external article.

Controls should address:

1. Source approval and ownership.
2. Document versioning and freshness.
3. Access permissions at retrieval time.
4. Document classification.
5. Index security.
6. Protection against cross-customer retrieval.
7. Detection of malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content.
8. Traceability from generated answers to retrieved sources.
9. Removal or correction of obsolete information.
10. Evaluation of retrieval accuracy and completeness.

RAG can improve grounding, but it does not guarantee correctness. A model may misinterpret a retrieved document, omit a critical qualification, or combine information from incompatible sources.

#### 3.7 Financial Analytics and Calculation Services

Financial analytics services should perform calculations using validated algorithms and controlled data inputs.

Examples include:

- Portfolio allocation calculations.
- Investment fee calculations.
- Historical return calculations.
- Compound-interest calculations.
- Retirement savings projections.
- Loan repayment calculations.
- Cash-flow analysis.
- Risk-exposure summaries.
- Scenario analysis.

For example, a retirement projection should identify the assumptions used, including contribution amounts, time horizon, inflation assumptions, and any assumed investment returns.

The result should not be presented as a guaranteed future balance.

The organization should validate the calculation logic, input handling, rounding, treatment of missing data, and consistency with approved business rules.

For consequential financial calculations, test cases should cover normal inputs, boundary conditions, invalid inputs, missing values, and relevant extreme scenarios.

#### 3.8 Financial Rules and Recommendation Controls

The rules layer applies defined business requirements and restrictions.

Depending on the service, these may include:

- Product eligibility.
- Customer eligibility.
- Investment restrictions.
- Risk-profile constraints.
- Required disclosures.
- Approval thresholds.
- Jurisdictional restrictions.
- Conflict-of-interest controls.
- Suitability or appropriateness requirements where applicable.
- Human-review requirements.

The rules layer should be independently governed and tested.

An LLM-generated recommendation should not override a mandatory restriction because the model produces persuasive language or interprets a customer request as an instruction to ignore the rule.

Where rules are incomplete, contradictory, or unavailable, the system should follow a defined fallback process rather than improvising an authorization decision.

#### 3.9 Enterprise Integration Layer

The integration layer connects the AI financial advisor to authoritative financial systems.

| System | Data or Capability | Principal Security Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Customer relationship management (CRM) | Customer profile, preferences, service history | Unauthorized access and excessive disclosure |
| Core banking system | Account balances and transaction records | Financial confidentiality and integrity |
| Brokerage platform | Holdings, orders, and investment transactions | Unauthorized or incorrect transactions |
| Portfolio management system | Asset allocation, valuation, and performance | Incorrect analysis and inconsistent data |
| Market-data provider | Prices, indices, and market information | Data freshness, integrity, and licensing |
| Product information system | Fees, terms, eligibility, and product disclosures | Outdated or incorrect product information |
| Document management system | Statements, contracts, and financial documents | Access-control failures and document tampering |
| Identity provider | Authentication and identity information | Account compromise and privilege misuse |
| Compliance platform | Compliance cases, restrictions, and monitoring | Incomplete enforcement and unauthorized changes |
| Audit and security monitoring platform | Logs, alerts, and investigation evidence | Excessive logging, insufficient coverage, and unauthorized access |

Each integration should have a defined business purpose, approved data scope, identity model, access restrictions, and monitoring requirements.

The system should not be given broad financial-platform permissions merely because future use cases might require them.

#### 3.10 Human Advisor and Escalation Layer

Human oversight is particularly important when a request involves complex circumstances, uncertain information, significant financial consequences, or an activity outside the AI system's approved scope.

Escalation triggers may include:

- Conflicting customer information.
- Missing information necessary for an authorized assessment.
- Uncertainty about product eligibility.
- High-impact or complex financial decisions.
- Customer complaints about advice.
- Potential conflicts of interest.
- Suspected fraud or account compromise.
- A request to perform an unauthorized transaction.
- A model response that fails validation.
- A request requiring professional judgment beyond the system's approved capabilities.

Human review must be meaningful. A process that merely asks an advisor to approve every AI recommendation without sufficient time, evidence, or authority may create the appearance of oversight without providing effective control.

**Core Principle:** The architecture should separate conversational intelligence from authoritative data, validated financial calculations, access authorization, and consequential financial decisions.

### 4. Customer Data, Financial Information, and Data Governance

AI financial advisors process information that can reveal a person's financial position, spending habits, investments, liabilities, personal circumstances, and future plans.

Such information may be highly sensitive even when it does not fall into a special category of personal data under a particular privacy law.

Effective data governance must therefore address confidentiality, integrity, availability, lawful processing, data minimization, access control, retention, and accountability.

#### 4.1 Common Data Categories

| Data Category | Examples | Primary Governance Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Identity data | Name, address, date of birth, customer identifier | Identity theft and unauthorized disclosure |
| Account information | Account numbers, balances, transaction history | Financial confidentiality and fraud |
| Income information | Salary, business income, pensions, recurring payments | Privacy and inaccurate affordability analysis |
| Expense information | Rent, utilities, subscriptions, debt payments | Behavioral profiling and incorrect financial assessments |
| Investment information | Securities, funds, portfolio holdings, purchase prices | Unauthorized disclosure and inappropriate recommendations |
| Liability information | Loans, mortgages, credit-card balances | Financial profiling and incorrect risk assessments |
| Risk-profile information | Investment experience, stated risk tolerance, investment horizon | Inappropriate recommendations and stale customer profiles |
| Financial objectives | Retirement plans, savings goals, major purchases | Inaccurate planning and inappropriate product selection |
| Financial documents | Bank statements, tax documents, contracts, product disclosures | Excessive retention and unauthorized document access |
| Behavioral data | Questions, product interactions, usage patterns | Profiling, secondary use, and privacy concerns |
| Authentication information | Session tokens, authentication events, recovery information | Account takeover and unauthorized access |
| Advisory records | Generated summaries, recommendations, advisor comments | Record integrity, confidentiality, and accountability |
| Model interaction data | Prompts, retrieved context, responses, feedback | Unintended disclosure, retention, and training-use risks |

Data classification should reflect both the intrinsic sensitivity of the information and the consequences of unauthorized access or incorrect use.

#### 4.2 Data Classification Model

The organization may establish a four-level classification model.

| Classification | Description | Example | Typical Control Expectations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public | Approved for unrestricted public distribution | Published financial education material | Integrity controls and source verification |
| Internal | Intended for authorized internal use | General internal operating procedures | Access restrictions and approved-use requirements |
| Confidential | Information whose disclosure could harm the organization or an individual | Internal research, non-public product information, limited customer-service records | Strong access controls, approved processing, monitoring, and retention rules |
| Restricted | Highly sensitive information requiring enhanced safeguards | Detailed customer financial records, authentication secrets, highly sensitive account information | Strict authorization, strong encryption, data minimization, enhanced monitoring, and tightly controlled retention |

This is an illustrative classification scheme. The organization should align it with its established information-classification policy and applicable legal requirements.

Authentication secrets, private keys, and session tokens require specialized protection even when the general classification framework does not explicitly list them.

#### 4.3 Data Minimization

Data minimization means processing only the information necessary for the approved purpose.

Suppose a customer asks the AI assistant to explain compound interest.

The system generally does not need access to the customer's full transaction history, account credentials, tax records, or investment portfolio.

By contrast, a service that has been specifically authorized to analyze the customer's investment allocation may need access to relevant holdings, valuations, investment objectives, and other information required by its approved methodology.

The organization should ask the following questions for each use case:

1. What information is necessary?
2. Why is it necessary?
3. Which system is the authoritative source?
4. Who is authorized to access it?
5. Can the task be completed with less detailed or aggregated information?
6. Is the information sent to an external model provider?
7. How long is the information retained?
8. Is it used for model training or another secondary purpose?
9. Can the information be removed when no longer required?
10. How can the organization demonstrate compliance with its decision?

The fact that information is technically available through an API does not mean it should be supplied to the LLM.

#### 4.4 Data Accuracy and Integrity

Incorrect financial information can produce misleading summaries and recommendations even when the AI model itself behaves as designed.

The organization should establish controls for:

- Reconciliation with authoritative financial systems.
- Validation of account and portfolio data.
- Identification of missing or stale information.
- Currency and timestamp handling.
- Consistent treatment of fees and taxes where relevant.
- Detection of duplicate or inconsistent records.
- Correction of inaccurate customer information.
- Prevention of unauthorized changes to source records.
- Traceability of data transformations.

For example, a portfolio analysis may be misleading if it combines holdings valued at different timestamps without identifying the discrepancy.

Similarly, a retirement projection may be unreliable if it uses outdated contribution amounts or ignores material liabilities.

The system should distinguish between verified facts, customer-provided information, estimated values, and assumptions.

#### 4.5 Data Lineage and Provenance

Data lineage describes how information moves from its source through processing, transformation, and eventual use.

For an AI financial advisor, lineage may connect:

1. The authoritative customer or financial-data source.
2. The data retrieved for the request.
3. Any transformations or calculations performed.
4. The documents retrieved by the RAG service.
5. The model input constructed by the orchestration layer.
6. The financial analytics output.
7. The generated response or advisory summary.
8. The final human decision, where applicable.

#### 4.6 Data Retention and Deletion

Retention requirements should distinguish between different data types.

| Data Type | Governance Question |
|---|---|
| Customer financial records | What retention obligations apply to the underlying financial service? |
| Customer prompts | Are prompts needed for service delivery, investigation, or compliance? |
| Model responses | Which responses must be retained to demonstrate the basis of a material decision? |
| Retrieved documents | Can the system retain references rather than copies of sensitive documents? |
| Temporary context | When should information be removed from active processing or session memory? |
| Security logs | What evidence is necessary for investigation, monitoring, and compliance? |
| Model evaluation datasets | Are data subjects' rights, licensing terms, and confidentiality obligations addressed? |
| Training or fine-tuning data | Is use authorized, lawful, necessary, and consistent with applicable commitments? |
| Backups | How are deletion requirements reconciled with backup retention and recovery obligations? |

The organization should document retention periods and deletion procedures for each relevant category.

It should also verify what happens to information processed by external providers, including provider-side retention, diagnostic data, abuse-monitoring records, backups, and any permitted model-improvement processes.

Provider assurances should be validated against the actual contractual terms, service configuration, and technical capabilities.

#### 4.7 Data Governance Responsibilities

| Role | Data Governance Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Data Owner | Defines business use, access requirements, and acceptable data quality for the relevant domain |
| Data Steward | Maintains definitions, quality rules, lineage, and data-handling procedures |
| System Owner | Ensures the application implements approved data controls |
| Data Protection Officer | Advises on applicable privacy obligations and monitors compliance within the DPO's remit |
| Information Security | Defines and assesses confidentiality, integrity, access, and monitoring controls |
| Model Risk Management | Evaluates the quality and appropriateness of data used for model development or validation |
| AI Service Owner | Ensures data use remains consistent with the approved AI use cases |
| Third-Party Risk Management | Assesses provider handling of organizational and customer information |
| Internal Audit | Independently evaluates whether governance and controls operate effectively |

Data ownership should be explicit. The technical team operating a data platform does not automatically become the business owner of every dataset stored within it.

**Core Principle:** An AI financial advisor should access only authorized, necessary, sufficiently accurate information and preserve appropriate traceability from source data to consequential outputs.

### 5. AI Financial Advisor Data Flow and Trust Boundaries

A data-flow assessment identifies where financial information originates, how it is processed, which components can access it, and where it crosses organizational or technical boundaries.

This is especially important when the platform uses external LLM providers, third-party market-data services, cloud-hosted analytics, or financial APIs.

A system may have strong authentication and encryption while still exposing sensitive data through excessive retrieval permissions, insecure application logic, poorly controlled model context, or provider configurations that do not match organizational requirements.

#### 5.1 End-to-End Data Flow

Consider a customer asking:

> "Can you help me understand how my current savings and investments compare with my retirement goal?"

The system may need to retrieve authorized financial information, calculate current allocations, obtain the customer's stated objective, and generate an explanation.

A controlled workflow could operate as follows:

1. The customer authenticates through the approved application.
2. The application verifies the customer's authorization to access the relevant records.
3. The orchestration layer determines that the request falls within an approved use case.
4. The system retrieves the minimum required financial information.
5. Data-quality checks identify missing, inconsistent, or outdated values.
6. A validated analytics service calculates the relevant financial metrics.
7. The RAG service retrieves approved retirement-planning guidance.
8. The LLM generates an explanation using the validated results and relevant retrieved information.
9. Output controls check for unsupported claims, prohibited recommendations, and disclosure violations.
10. The system presents the response with relevant assumptions and limitations.
11. If the request requires personalized regulated advice outside the approved scope, the system escalates it to an authorized advisor.
12. Appropriate audit records are retained under the approved evidence and retention policy.

This process separates the generation of natural-language explanations from the systems responsible for authorization and financial calculations.

#### 5.2 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary is a point at which information or control passes between components with different security assumptions, permissions, ownership, or levels of trust.

Relevant boundaries may include:

- Customer device to enterprise application.
- Enterprise application to identity provider.
- AI orchestration service to external LLM provider.
- RAG service to document repositories.
- AI service to customer financial systems.
- Analytics service to market-data providers.
- AI-generated output to a human advisor.
- Advisory workflow to transaction-processing systems.
- Application logs to security monitoring platforms.

Each boundary should be assessed for authentication, authorization, input validation, encryption, monitoring, data minimization, and failure behavior.

#### 5.5 External Model Provider Risk

When an external LLM provider is involved, the organization should assess:

- What data is transmitted.
- Whether prompts and responses are retained.
- Whether data may be used for model training or service improvement.
- Which personnel or subprocessors may access the information.
- Where processing and storage occur.
- What security controls are available.
- How data deletion is implemented.
- How the provider handles security incidents.
- How model and service changes are communicated.
- Whether the provider supports required audit and regulatory obligations.
- What happens when the service is unavailable.
- How the organization can terminate the service and retrieve or delete relevant data.

The organization should not assume that all enterprise AI offerings have identical data-handling terms or that a provider's marketing statements are sufficient evidence of contractual compliance.

The actual service, configuration, contract, and data-processing arrangements must be reviewed.

#### 5.6 Failure Modes and Safe Degradation

The system should define what happens when a required component fails or returns unreliable information.

| Failure Scenario | Unsafe Behavior | Recommended Response |
|---|---|---|
| Financial API unavailable | Inventing or estimating current account balances without disclosure | State that the information is unavailable and avoid unsupported calculations |
| Market data stale | Presenting outdated prices as current | Identify the timestamp or withhold time-sensitive conclusions |
| RAG source unavailable | Generating authoritative-sounding product terms from model memory | Restrict the response or explain that current source information cannot be verified |
| Analytics service fails | Asking the LLM to substitute unvalidated calculations for a critical financial calculation | Stop the affected analysis and escalate or retry safely |
| Authorization service unavailable | Allowing access based on previously supplied customer identifiers alone | Fail closed for sensitive operations unless an explicitly approved safe alternative exists |
| Model output fails validation | Presenting the response despite a detected policy violation | Block or revise the response and apply the defined fallback |
| External LLM unavailable | Switching automatically to an unapproved provider | Use an approved fallback or provide a controlled service-unavailable response |
| Audit logging unavailable | Continuing high-impact operations without required evidence | Restrict affected operations according to the approved resilience policy |
| Human advisor unavailable | Performing a prohibited advisory action automatically | Queue the request or provide an approved informational response |

Fail-safe behavior should be defined according to the operation's risk. A temporary failure in a low-risk educational feature may permit a limited response, while an authorization failure affecting sensitive financial data should generally prevent the affected access.

The enterprise architecture and GRC teams should maintain:

- Current architecture diagrams.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Trust-boundary definitions.
- Data inventories and classifications.
- External provider and subprocessor inventories.
- API and service-identity inventories.
- Data-retention and deletion requirements.
- Logging and monitoring requirements.
- Approved-use-case mappings.
- Failure-mode and recovery procedures.
- Risk assessments for material integrations.

These artifacts should be updated when the organization introduces new data sources, changes providers, expands model access, or enables additional capabilities.

**Core Principle:** Every material data flow and trust boundary must have a defined purpose, authorized access model, appropriate safeguards, and a documented response to failure.

### 6. Governance Frameworks and Regulatory Landscape

An AI financial advisor may be subject to overlapping requirements involving financial-services regulation, data protection, cybersecurity, operational resilience, consumer protection, outsourcing, and AI governance.

The organization must determine which requirements apply to its specific service, jurisdiction, legal entity, customer population, and business model.

No single AI framework replaces the need to assess the applicable financial-services rules.

#### 6.1 Governance and Risk Frameworks

| Framework or Standard | Relevance to an AI Financial Advisor | Typical Application |
|---|---|---|
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) | Provides a structured approach to identifying and managing AI-related risks | AI governance, context definition, measurement, and risk treatment |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | Establishes requirements for an AI management system | AI policies, roles, risk processes, operational controls, and continual improvement |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) | Supports enterprise cybersecurity risk management | Governance, identification, protection, detection, response, and recovery |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Provides requirements for an information security management system | Information security risk management and control governance |
| ISO/IEC 23894 | Provides guidance on AI risk management | Identifying, analyzing, evaluating, and treating AI risks |
| ISO/IEC 27701 | Supports privacy information management | Privacy governance where the standard is adopted and applicable |
| COBIT | Supports governance and management of enterprise information and technology | Accountability, control objectives, performance, and oversight |
| Three Lines Model | Clarifies management, risk oversight, and independent assurance roles | Separation of operational ownership, risk oversight, and internal audit |
| Model Risk Management Framework | Supports validation and oversight of quantitative and predictive models | Model inventory, validation, limitations, monitoring, and change control |

These frameworks serve different purposes. The organization should select and combine them according to its existing governance model rather than treating every framework as an independent compliance checklist.

#### 6.2 European Union AI Act

The European Union AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems. Its applicability to an AI financial advisor depends on the system's intended purpose, functionality, role in the relevant activity, and the specific obligations applicable to the provider, deployer, or other regulated actor.

The organization should not automatically assume that every AI financial advisor is classified as a high-risk AI system. Nor should it assume that a financial-services use case is exempt from AI Act obligations.

The assessment should consider:

- The intended purpose of the system.
- Whether it falls within a legally defined high-risk category.
- Whether it is used as a component of another regulated system.
- The organization's role as provider, deployer, or another relevant actor.
- Transparency requirements that apply to the actual use.
- Requirements concerning human oversight, documentation, and monitoring where applicable.
- Applicable AI literacy obligations.
- Relevant implementation dates and transitional provisions.
- Any subsequent legal amendments or official guidance.

The organization should document the legal classification rationale and reassess it when the intended purpose or functionality changes.

#### 6.3 GDPR and Personal Financial Data

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) may apply when the system processes personal data relating to identifiable individuals in the European Union, subject to its territorial and material scope.

Relevant governance questions include:

- What is the lawful basis for each processing purpose?
- Are the purposes specific and transparent?
- Is the information necessary for the stated purpose?
- Are customers informed about the processing?
- Are data-subject rights supported?
- Are retention and deletion practices appropriate?
- Are data processors and international transfers governed correctly?
- Is a data protection impact assessment required?
- Does the system make decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects?
- Are profiling, transparency, and human-intervention obligations relevant?

Article 22 of the GDPR may be particularly relevant where a decision based solely on automated processing produces legal effects concerning a person or similarly significantly affects that person, subject to the provision's scope and exceptions.

Not every AI-generated financial explanation or recommendation automatically falls within Article 22. The organization must evaluate the actual decision process, its consequences, the degree of human involvement, and the applicable legal conditions.

Human review should be substantive when relied upon as a safeguard. A nominal approval step that does not allow the reviewer to understand, challenge, or change the recommendation may be insufficient.

#### 6.4 Financial-Services Regulation

Financial-services requirements depend on the organization's activities and authorizations.

For example, investment firms providing investment services in the European Union may need to consider the applicable requirements under MiFID II and associated measures, including relevant obligations concerning suitability, appropriateness, client information, conflicts of interest, and organizational controls.

The precise obligations depend on the service provided and the applicable legal provisions.

A platform that provides general educational information may have a different regulatory position from a platform that gives personalized investment recommendations or manages portfolios on behalf of customers.

The organization should assess:

1. Whether the activity constitutes a regulated financial service.
2. Which legal entity provides the service.
3. Which jurisdictions and customer categories are involved.
4. Whether suitability or appropriateness requirements apply.
5. How conflicts of interest are identified and managed.
6. What records must be maintained.
7. What disclosures and customer protections are required.
8. Which activities require qualified or authorized personnel.
9. How complaints and disputes are handled.
10. How regulatory obligations apply to automated workflows.

#### 6.5 Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)

DORA establishes requirements concerning digital operational resilience for financial entities within its scope.

Where applicable, an AI financial advisor may form part of the financial entity's ICT environment and may rely on third-party ICT services.

Relevant assessment areas can include:

- ICT risk management.
- Incident management and reporting.
- Digital operational resilience testing.
- ICT third-party risk management.
- Contractual arrangements with ICT service providers.
- Business continuity and recovery.
- Monitoring of critical technology dependencies.
- Concentration and dependency risks associated with external providers.

The organization should determine whether the entity and the particular arrangements fall within DORA's scope and which requirements apply to the service.

An external AI provider should not be assumed to be outside the enterprise's operational-resilience governance merely because it supplies a software service rather than a traditional financial product.

#### 6.6 Financial Crime and Fraud Controls

An AI financial advisor may interact with customer data and workflows that are relevant to fraud prevention, anti-money-laundering controls, or customer due diligence.

The organization should assess whether the service could:

- Reveal information that helps an attacker impersonate a customer.
- Be manipulated to bypass established customer-verification procedures.
- Produce misleading explanations of account restrictions.
- Interfere with fraud alerts or compliance workflows.
- Be used to generate deceptive financial communications.
- Expose sensitive information about monitoring or investigation activities.

AI-generated text should not override mandatory identity-verification, transaction-monitoring, or financial-crime controls.

#### 6.7 Regulatory Applicability Matrix

| Requirement Area | Applicability Question | Evidence Required |
|---|---|---|
| AI Act | What is the system's intended purpose and legal classification? | Documented classification assessment and legal review |
| GDPR | What personal data is processed, and for what purposes? | Data inventory, lawful-basis analysis, privacy notices, and relevant assessments |
| Financial-services rules | Does the service provide regulated advice, investment services, or other regulated activities? | Legal classification and documented control mapping |
| DORA | Is the financial entity and relevant ICT arrangement within scope? | Applicability assessment, ICT risk records, and provider documentation |
| Consumer protection | Could customers be misled about the system's capabilities, limitations, or recommendations? | User disclosures, response testing, and complaint procedures |
| Outsourcing and third-party risk | Does the provider relationship create material operational or regulatory dependency? | Due diligence, contractual review, and ongoing monitoring |
| Cybersecurity | Are security requirements defined and tested for the system and its integrations? | Risk assessment, control matrix, and security-test results |
| Recordkeeping | Which customer interactions, decisions, and supporting evidence must be retained? | Retention policy, evidence design, and audit records |
| Financial crime | Could the system affect customer verification, fraud controls, or regulated monitoring? | Control mapping and scenario testing |
| Model governance | Are relevant models inventoried, validated, monitored, and controlled? | Model documentation, validation records, limitations, and change history |

The applicability matrix should identify a responsible owner for each requirement, the rationale for applicability or non-applicability, the associated control, and the evidence needed to demonstrate compliance.

#### 6.8 Framework Mapping and Avoiding Compliance Gaps

The organization should map its chosen frameworks and legal requirements to a unified control framework.

For example, a requirement to protect customer financial information may connect:

- GDPR data-protection obligations, where applicable.
- ISO/IEC 27001 information security controls.
- NIST CSF cybersecurity outcomes.
- ISO/IEC 42001 AI management-system processes.
- Internal financial-services security policies.
- Provider contractual obligations.

These requirements may overlap, but they are not identical. A control designed to protect confidentiality does not automatically satisfy requirements concerning lawful processing, customer suitability, model validation, or automated decision-making.

A unified control matrix should preserve the distinction between legal obligations, contractual commitments, framework recommendations, and internal policy requirements.

**Core Principle:** AI governance frameworks support a structured control environment, but regulatory applicability must be determined from the actual financial service, system purpose, data processing, jurisdiction, and business model.

**Key Conceptual Distinctions**

- **General financial information vs. regulated financial advice:** The legal significance depends on what the service actually does, not merely how it is labeled.
- **AI framework alignment vs. legal compliance:** Alignment with a voluntary framework does not automatically satisfy statutory obligations.
- **Data confidentiality vs. data protection:** Preventing unauthorized access does not, by itself, establish that processing is lawful, necessary, transparent, and appropriately limited.
- **Model accuracy vs. recommendation suitability:** A calculation can be technically correct while the resulting recommendation remains inappropriate for a particular customer.
- **Provider assurance vs. enterprise accountability:** Third-party controls support risk management but do not eliminate the organization's responsibility for its deployment and use.
- **Human review vs. meaningful oversight:** A review process is effective only when the reviewer has sufficient information, competence, time, and authority to challenge the output.
- **Risk assessment vs. risk acceptance:** Identifying a risk does not authorize the organization to proceed; acceptance must follow the applicable governance process.
- **Initial approval vs. continuing compliance:** Governance must remain active as the model, provider, customer population, data flows, and permitted financial activities change.

### 7. AI Financial Advisor Risk Assessment Methodology

An AI financial advisor introduces a combination of traditional financial-services risks, information security risks, data protection risks, model risks, and AI-specific threats. These risks must be assessed together because a failure in one component can create consequences across the entire advisory process.

For example, an incorrect portfolio calculation may appear to be a technical defect. However, if an LLM presents that calculation as a reliable basis for a personalized investment recommendation, the incident may also involve customer harm, regulatory non-compliance, inadequate human oversight, and reputational damage.

The Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) function should therefore assess risks across the complete AI-enabled financial advisory lifecycle.

#### 7.1 Risk Assessment Objectives

The risk assessment should establish:

1. Whether the AI system's intended purpose is clearly defined.
2. Whether the proposed use cases are legally and operationally permissible.
3. Whether customer information is accurate, authorized, and sufficient.
4. Whether model outputs are reliable for their intended purpose.
5. Whether recommendations can cause material financial harm.
6. Whether cybersecurity controls address AI-specific attack paths.
7. Whether customers can understand and challenge consequential outputs.
8. Whether human oversight is appropriate and effective.
9. Whether external providers create unacceptable dependencies.
10. Whether the organization can detect, investigate, and recover from failures.
11. Whether residual risks fall within approved risk appetite.
12. Whether sufficient evidence exists to support a deployment decision.

The assessment must evaluate both the likelihood of an adverse event and the severity of its consequences. Low-frequency events may still require strong controls when the potential impact is substantial.

#### 7.2 AI Financial Advisor Risk Categories

| Risk Category | Example Risk | Potential Business Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic risk | AI capability does not align with the organization's approved advisory strategy | Wasted investment and inappropriate automation |

| Model risk | The model produces unreliable outputs under certain conditions | Poor recommendations and loss of confidence |
| Data risk | Customer financial information is incomplete or outdated | Misleading analysis |

| Conduct risk | Recommendations are influenced by commercial incentives | Conflicts of interest and customer detriment |
| Compliance risk | The system performs an activity without required controls or authorization | Regulatory action and remediation costs |
| Operational risk | An integration fails or produces inconsistent data | Service disruption and incorrect outputs |
| Third-party risk | An AI provider changes its service or experiences an outage | Loss of availability or degraded performance |

| Regulatory-change risk | Changes in law or regulatory interpretation are not reflected in system controls | Compliance gaps |

#### 7.4 Inherent Risk, Control Effectiveness, and Residual Risk

These concepts must remain distinct.

**Inherent risk** is the exposure before considering the effect of controls.

**Control effectiveness** describes whether controls are appropriately designed and operate as intended.

**Residual risk** is the exposure remaining after the effects of implemented controls are considered.

For example, an AI financial advisor that can retrieve customer portfolios and generate personalized investment recommendations may have substantial inherent risk because an incorrect recommendation could affect a customer's savings.

Strong access controls, validated financial calculations, recommendation restrictions, human review, and monitoring may reduce that risk.

However, the residual risk is not automatically low simply because the organization has documented these controls. Their effectiveness must be demonstrated through testing, operational evidence, and ongoing monitoring.

#### 7.5 Example Risk Scoring Model

A financial-services organization may use a five-point scoring model.

**Likelihood**

| Score | Rating | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Unlikely under normal operating conditions |
| 2 | Unlikely | Possible, but not expected in most circumstances |
| 3 | Possible | Could occur under identifiable conditions |
| 4 | Likely | Expected to occur periodically without effective controls |
| 5 | Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

**Impact**

| Score | Rating | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | Minimal operational effect and negligible customer impact |
| 2 | Minor | Limited disruption or low-severity customer impact |
| 3 | Moderate | Meaningful remediation, customer complaints, or operational disruption |
| 4 | Major | Significant customer harm, material financial loss, or serious compliance consequences |
| 5 | Severe | Extensive customer harm, major financial consequences, or potentially critical regulatory and operational impact |

The organization may calculate a preliminary risk score as:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

This produces a score between 1 and 25.

An illustrative classification is:

| Score | Rating | Indicative Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | Low | Manage through routine controls and monitoring |
| 5–9 | Moderate | Assign an owner and implement proportionate treatment |
| 10–16 | High | Require formal treatment and appropriate management oversight |
| 17–25 | Critical | Escalate for urgent decision; prohibit deployment or activity until required safeguards are established |

These thresholds are illustrative, not universal regulatory requirements. The organization must define its own risk methodology and approval thresholds.

A simple numerical score should not override mandatory legal requirements or risk appetite constraints. A risk involving unauthorized investment transactions may require a strict prohibition even if a simplified calculation produces a score below the critical threshold.

### 8. Financial Recommendation Governance and Customer Suitability

Financial recommendation governance is one of the most consequential areas of AI financial advisory risk management.

A recommendation may be technically plausible and based on accurate market information while still being inappropriate for a particular customer.

For example, an investment product may have historically produced strong returns but expose a customer to a level of volatility inconsistent with their financial objectives, liquidity needs, investment horizon, or capacity to absorb losses.

Recommendation governance must therefore evaluate more than whether the model generated a convincing explanation.

#### 8.1 Recommendation Lifecycle

A controlled recommendation process may include the following stages:

1. **Request classification:** Determine whether the user requests general information, personalized guidance, a regulated service, or a financial action.
2. **Customer identification:** Establish which customer or account is involved.
3. **Authorization:** Confirm that the request and relevant data access are permitted.
4. **Information collection:** Retrieve the information required for the approved advisory process.
5. **Data validation:** Identify missing, stale, inconsistent, or unverified information.
6. **Customer-profile assessment:** Apply the approved methodology to relevant customer circumstances.
7. **Product and strategy evaluation:** Assess potential options against applicable criteria.
8. **Rule enforcement:** Apply eligibility restrictions, investment constraints, and mandatory controls.
9. **Recommendation generation:** Produce an output consistent with the authorized process.
10. **Validation:** Check the recommendation against approved requirements and supporting evidence.
11. **Human review:** Obtain qualified review when required by the risk level, legal obligations, or operating model.
12. **Customer communication:** Present relevant information, assumptions, risks, and limitations.
13. **Decision recording:** Preserve the evidence required to reconstruct the recommendation and decision.
14. **Monitoring:** Track outcomes, complaints, exceptions, and changes affecting recommendation quality.

The exact workflow depends on the type of financial service and applicable regulatory requirements.

#### 8.2 Customer Information Required for Personalized Advice

Depending on the service and applicable requirements, relevant customer information may include:

- Financial objectives.
- Investment horizon.
- Investment experience and knowledge.
- Risk tolerance.
- Capacity to bear financial losses.
- Income and recurring expenses.
- Assets and liabilities.
- Liquidity requirements.
- Existing portfolio allocation.
- Relevant investment restrictions.
- Tax considerations where appropriately within scope.
- Relevant personal circumstances.
- Product eligibility and applicable legal restrictions.

The organization must distinguish between **risk tolerance** and **capacity to bear losses**.

Risk tolerance concerns the customer's willingness to accept investment uncertainty and potential losses.

Capacity to bear losses concerns the customer's financial ability to withstand those losses without unacceptable consequences.

A customer may express a willingness to take substantial investment risks while having limited financial capacity to absorb losses. The system should not treat these concepts as interchangeable.

The exact information required must be defined by the applicable advisory methodology and regulatory obligations.

#### 8.3 Suitability and Appropriateness Are Not Interchangeable

In regulated investment services, suitability and appropriateness are distinct concepts under the applicable rules.

**Suitability** generally involves assessing whether a recommendation is appropriate in light of the relevant client information and the requirements applicable to the investment service.

**Appropriateness** generally concerns whether a client has the necessary knowledge and experience to understand the risks of certain investment products or services, where the applicable rules require that assessment.

Their precise application depends on the service and legal framework.

An AI financial advisor should not substitute a generic questionnaire or a model-generated risk score for the assessment required by the relevant legal and business process.

#### 8.4 Recommendation Control Matrix

| Control Area | Governance Requirement | Example Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Intended use | Define the financial activities the system may support | Approved use-case specification |
| Customer profiling | Use an approved methodology for relevant customer circumstances | Profiling methodology and validation records |
| Data completeness | Identify information gaps that could affect the result | Data-quality checks and exception logs |
| Product eligibility | Prevent recommendations involving prohibited or ineligible products | Rules-engine configuration and test results |
| Conflict management | Identify and manage relevant conflicts of interest | Conflict register and control testing |
| Financial calculations | Use validated calculation services | Calculation specifications and test evidence |
| Recommendation explanation | Provide relevant reasoning, assumptions, and limitations | Output evaluation and sample advisory records |
| Human review | Route cases requiring qualified judgment to an authorized reviewer | Workflow records and review decisions |
| Customer disclosures | Present required information and warnings | Approved disclosure templates and presentation tests |
| Recordkeeping | Retain required evidence of the advisory process | Audit records and retention configuration |
| Change control | Reassess the methodology when relevant models or rules change | Change assessments and approval records |
| Ongoing monitoring | Identify systematic recommendation errors and customer complaints | Monitoring reports and remediation records |

#### 8.5 Preventing Commercial Bias

AI systems may create or amplify conflicts of interest if their objectives, training data, recommendation rules, or business integrations favor commercially beneficial outcomes over customer interests.

Potential scenarios include:

- Ranking products primarily by commission or revenue.
- Promoting products from affiliated providers without appropriate safeguards.
- Giving greater visibility to products that generate higher sales conversion.
- Treating customer engagement as a proxy for financial suitability.
- Recommending unnecessary transactions to increase activity.
- Using customer vulnerability or financial anxiety to encourage purchases.

Controls should include documented recommendation criteria, transparent commercial incentives, appropriate conflict management, testing for outcome disparities, and independent review of material recommendation logic.

Where applicable, the organization must comply with the relevant legal obligations concerning acting in the customer's best interests and managing conflicts of interest.

#### 8.6 Recommendation Explainability

An AI financial advisor should provide explanations that are sufficiently accurate, relevant, and understandable for the decision being supported.

A useful explanation may identify:

- The customer's stated objective considered by the process.
- The relevant financial information used.
- The key assumptions.
- The material factors affecting the result.
- The principal risks and limitations.
- The reason an alternative was not selected, where this can be explained reliably.
- The circumstances in which the output should be reviewed by a human.

The explanation should not claim that the system considered factors that were not actually used.

For example, if a recommendation engine does not evaluate tax consequences, the LLM must not state that the recommendation is tax-optimized.

Similarly, a fluent narrative is not proof that a model's reasoning is complete, accurate, or causally faithful to the process that generated the result.

#### 8.7 Customer Challenge and Correction Mechanisms

Customers should have appropriate ways to question an output, correct relevant information, request assistance, and raise complaints.

The process should support:

1. Identification of the disputed output or recommendation.
2. Collection of relevant evidence.
3. Verification of the data used.
4. Review of calculations and applicable rules.
5. Escalation to an authorized human.
6. Correction of inaccurate information or records.
7. Assessment of whether other customers may be affected.
8. Remediation of the underlying model or control issue.
9. Documentation of the resolution.
10. Communication of the outcome to the customer where appropriate.

A complaint should not be treated solely as a customer-service issue if it may reveal a systemic model defect, an unauthorized data flow, or a failure in a mandatory financial control.

**Core Principle:** A financial recommendation must be governed by the customer's relevant circumstances, validated information, applicable rules, and appropriate oversight—not by the fluency or confidence of the AI-generated response.

### 9. Model Risk Management and Financial Accuracy

Model risk arises when decisions or processes depend on models that may be incorrect, misused, poorly understood, or inadequately validated.

In an AI financial advisor, model risk may originate from traditional predictive models, portfolio optimization algorithms, financial calculators, recommendation engines, and LLMs.

These components have different failure modes and should not be assessed as though they were one interchangeable model.

#### 9.1 Model Inventory and Classification

The organization should maintain an inventory of models and AI components used in the financial advisory service.

| Component | Primary Function | Principal Risk |
|---|---|---|
| LLM | Interprets questions and generates natural-language outputs | Hallucination, inconsistent responses, and prompt injection |
| Customer risk-scoring model | Estimates defined customer or portfolio risk measures | Miscalibration, bias, and inappropriate interpretation |
| Recommendation engine | Matches products or strategies with defined criteria | Unsuitable recommendations and conflicts of interest |
| Portfolio optimizer | Evaluates portfolio allocations against specified objectives | Incorrect assumptions and unstable optimization |
| Financial calculation engine | Calculates returns, fees, projections, or repayments | Formula defects and incorrect input handling |
| Market prediction model | Estimates future market-related variables | Forecast error and overconfidence |
| Fraud detection model | Identifies potentially suspicious activity | False positives, false negatives, and evasion |
| Retrieval-ranking model | Ranks relevant financial documents | Omission of critical information |
| Document extraction model | Extracts values from statements or contracts | Misread amounts, dates, or contractual conditions |
| Speech or sentiment model | Interprets customer speech or other interaction signals | Misclassification and inappropriate downstream use |

The inventory should identify each component's owner, intended purpose, criticality, dependencies, validation status, limitations, approval status, and monitoring requirements.

A model's classification should reflect its potential consequences, not simply its technical complexity.

#### 9.2 LLM Hallucination Risk

An LLM may generate plausible but false statements, including:

- Invented investment products.
- Incorrect fees or product terms.
- Unsupported claims about historical returns.
- Incorrect descriptions of financial regulations.
- Misinterpretation of a product disclosure.
- Fabricated market events.
- Incorrect explanations of portfolio performance.
- Unsupported claims about the customer's eligibility.

Potential controls include:

- Retrieval from approved, current sources.
- Source attribution where appropriate.
- Validation against authoritative product data.
- Structured output requirements.
- Restrictions on unsupported claims.
- Clear communication of uncertainty.
- Automated checks for critical fields.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Defined fallback behavior when evidence is insufficient.

A source citation is useful only if the cited material genuinely supports the associated statement.

#### 9.3 Financial Calculation Validation

LLMs should not be treated as authoritative financial calculators for consequential calculations.

A financial calculation service should have documented formulas, validated inputs, defined precision, and reproducible outputs.

For example, compound-interest calculations may use the following simplified formula:

\[
FV = P(1+r)^n
\]

Where:

- \(FV\) is the future value.
- \(P\) is the initial principal.
- \(r\) is the assumed return per period.
- \(n\) is the number of periods.

The formula assumes a simplified compounding structure and does not, by itself, account for additional contributions, fees, taxes, inflation, or changing returns.

A retirement-planning service must therefore specify the assumptions and calculation method appropriate to its use case.

For investment projections, the system should distinguish clearly between illustrative scenarios and guaranteed outcomes. Historical returns should not be presented as assurance of future performance.

#### 9.4 Model Validation Requirements

A validation process may include:

1. Review of the model's intended purpose.
2. Assessment of conceptual soundness.
3. Review of training and evaluation data where relevant.
4. Assessment of data representativeness and quality.
5. Independent testing of calculations or predictions.
6. Benchmark comparison.
7. Boundary and stress testing.
8. Testing for systematic errors and relevant bias.
9. Evaluation of uncertainty and calibration where applicable.
10. Review of limitations and prohibited uses.
11. Verification of reproducibility where feasible.
12. Review of monitoring and change-management arrangements.

The depth of validation should be proportionate to the component's role and potential impact.

A deterministic calculator and a generative model require different testing techniques, even when both contribute to the same advisory workflow.

#### 9.5 Evaluation Metrics

Different model types require different evaluation metrics.

| Component | Example Metrics | Governance Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Financial calculator | Numerical error, formula-test pass rate, reconciliation accuracy | Whether outputs meet defined mathematical and business requirements |
| Predictive model | Calibration, precision, recall, error measures appropriate to the task | Whether predictions are sufficiently reliable for the approved purpose |
| Recommendation engine | Rule-compliance rate, suitability-review findings, constraint violations | Whether recommendations respect defined requirements |
| LLM | Factual accuracy, unsupported-claim rate, groundedness, refusal quality | Whether responses remain within approved boundaries |
| RAG system | Retrieval precision, retrieval recall, source freshness | Whether relevant and authoritative evidence is retrieved |
| Document extraction | Field-level accuracy, critical-field error rate | Whether extracted financial values are reliable |
| Fraud model | False-positive rate, false-negative rate, detection effectiveness | Whether fraud detection balances missed threats and unnecessary interventions |
| Customer-facing system | Complaint rate, correction rate, escalation rate | Whether the service produces acceptable customer outcomes |

No single metric demonstrates that an AI financial advisor is safe or compliant.

For example, a high factual-accuracy score does not establish that recommendations are suitable, that data processing is lawful, or that customers are protected against unauthorized transactions.

#### 9.6 Model Drift and Change Management

Model performance may change because of:

- Changes in customer behavior.
- New financial products.
- Market conditions.
- Changes in source documents.
- Shifts in data distributions.
- Provider model updates.
- Changes in system prompts or orchestration logic.
- New integrations.
- Changes in regulatory requirements.
- Changes to recommendation rules.

The organization should distinguish between **data drift**, where input data distributions change, and **performance drift**, where the system's measured quality deteriorates.

For an LLM, behavioral changes may occur even when the organization's own application code remains unchanged, particularly when a provider updates the underlying model or service configuration.

Change controls should require impact assessment, appropriate regression testing, approval, and rollback or fallback procedures.

#### 9.7 Model Risk Acceptance

The business owner should not be permitted to accept every model risk without appropriate independent challenge.

A risk acceptance process should consider:

- The severity of potential customer harm.
- The applicable legal and regulatory obligations.
- The effectiveness of existing controls.
- The availability of safer alternatives.
- The limits of model validation.
- The ability to monitor the risk in production.
- The authority of the proposed risk acceptor.
- The conditions under which acceptance must be reconsidered.

A model may be acceptable for summarizing public financial education material but unacceptable for making autonomous investment decisions.

The same underlying model can therefore have different approval status depending on the use case and controls.

**Core Principle:** Model approval must be specific to the intended use, supported by appropriate validation, and maintained through controlled changes and continuous monitoring.

### 10. Responsible AI, Fairness, Transparency, and Customer Protection

Responsible AI governance ensures that the system's design and operation account for accuracy, fairness, transparency, privacy, accountability, security, and the potential for customer harm.

For financial advisory systems, these principles must be translated into measurable requirements and enforceable operational controls.

#### 10.1 Fairness and Disparate Outcomes

An AI financial advisor may produce different outcomes for different customer groups because of differences in input data, historical patterns, model design, recommendation criteria, or the way customers interact with the system.

Potential problems include:

- Different levels of service quality across customer groups.
- Systematic errors for customers with limited financial records.
- Inappropriate use of demographic or proxy variables.
- Disparate treatment resulting from historical data.
- Unequal access to human assistance.
- Language-related differences in response quality.
- Inconsistent explanations for similar financial circumstances.

Fairness testing should be designed around the actual service and the applicable legal requirements.

The organization should identify relevant customer groups, define appropriate comparison methods, investigate material disparities, and document justified differences in outcomes.

Not every difference in outcome establishes unlawful discrimination. Equally, the absence of an explicitly protected attribute from the model inputs does not guarantee that discriminatory effects are absent.

#### 10.2 Transparency and Customer Understanding

Customers should receive information appropriate to the nature of the service.

Depending on the applicable legal requirements and use case, this may include:

- That they are interacting with an AI system.
- What the system is designed to do.
- Which functions it cannot perform.
- Whether outputs are informational or form part of an advisory service.
- Whether a human advisor reviews the output.
- The principal assumptions and limitations.
- How to request assistance or challenge an outcome.
- How relevant personal data is processed.

Transparency should be meaningful rather than merely extensive.

A lengthy disclaimer does not compensate for a misleading user interface, a recommendation that appears guaranteed, or a system that hides material uncertainty.

#### 10.3 Human Oversight

Human oversight should be proportionate to the risk and integrated into the actual workflow.

Relevant requirements include:

- Clearly defined cases requiring human review.
- Reviewers with appropriate competence.
- Access to the evidence supporting the AI output.
- Sufficient time and authority to challenge recommendations.
- Ability to reject or modify an output.
- Escalation routes for uncertain or high-impact cases.
- Documentation of material review decisions.
- Monitoring for excessive reliance on automation.

Human oversight should not become a ceremonial approval process.

For example, an advisor who sees only a final recommendation without the relevant assumptions, source information, or identified limitations may be unable to evaluate it properly.

#### 10.4 Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when a person gives excessive weight to an automated system's output, even when contradictory information is available.

In financial advisory work, automation bias may lead an advisor to accept:

- An incorrect portfolio analysis.
- An unsuitable investment recommendation.
- A fabricated explanation of a financial product.
- An inaccurate customer-risk assessment.
- A recommendation based on stale information.

Mitigation measures include:

1. Training advisors to recognize AI limitations.
2. Presenting supporting evidence and relevant assumptions.
3. Highlighting missing or inconsistent information.
4. Requiring independent checks for defined high-impact decisions.
5. Measuring override and correction patterns.
6. Reviewing whether human decisions systematically reproduce AI errors.
7. Ensuring that performance targets do not discourage appropriate challenge.

A low human-override rate is not necessarily evidence of high model quality. It may also indicate over-reliance, insufficient review, or organizational pressure to accept AI recommendations.

#### 10.5 Customer Vulnerability and Financial Harm

Some customers may be particularly vulnerable to misleading financial explanations because of limited financial literacy, language barriers, financial distress, or urgent personal circumstances.

The organization should evaluate whether the system:

- Encourages customers to take risks they do not understand.
- Presents uncertain projections as reliable outcomes.
- Exploits urgency or fear to influence financial decisions.
- Encourages unnecessary borrowing or investment activity.
- Fails to recognize requests outside its approved scope.
- Provides misleading reassurance about financial products.
- Makes it difficult to reach a qualified human advisor.

The system should not infer sensitive personal characteristics unnecessarily or use customer vulnerability to increase sales.

Where appropriate, the service should use clear language, communicate uncertainty, and direct customers to qualified human assistance.

#### 10.6 Responsible AI Control Matrix

| Principle | Control Requirement | Example Test |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Verify factual claims and critical calculations | Compare outputs with authoritative references and independently validated calculations |
| Fairness | Evaluate relevant outcome differences | Test representative customer scenarios and investigate material disparities |
| Transparency | Communicate the system's role and limitations | Review customer disclosures and sample conversations |
| Accountability | Assign owners and approval authority | Verify that each critical control has an accountable owner |
| Privacy | Limit access and processing to approved purposes | Test access restrictions and review data-flow records |
| Security | Resist manipulation and unauthorized tool use | Conduct adversarial testing and permission-boundary tests |
| Human oversight | Enable meaningful review and intervention | Inspect review records and test escalation workflows |
| Contestability | Allow appropriate challenge and correction | Test complaint, correction, and escalation procedures |
| Reliability | Monitor performance and manage changes | Review production metrics, regression tests, and rollback evidence |
| Traceability | Preserve relevant decision evidence | Reconstruct a sampled advisory workflow from retained records |

#### 10.7 Customer Protection and Incident Remediation

If the organization discovers that the AI advisor has generated materially incorrect financial information, it should evaluate the scope of the issue and determine whether customers may have relied on the affected outputs.

A controlled remediation process may include:

1. Restricting the affected functionality where necessary.
2. Preserving relevant evidence.
3. Identifying the affected time period and customer population.
4. Determining the technical and governance root cause.
5. Assessing actual or potential customer harm.
6. Involving legal, compliance, risk, and relevant business owners.
7. Determining applicable notification or reporting obligations.
8. Correcting the underlying defect.
9. Reviewing affected customer cases.
10. Providing appropriate remediation where required.
11. Testing the correction before restoring the functionality.
12. Updating risk assessments and preventive controls.

The organization should avoid assuming that a model defect affects only the single interaction in which it was discovered. A shared prompt, model configuration, retrieval index, calculation service, or recommendation rule may affect many customers.

**Core Principle:** Responsible AI in financial advisory services requires measurable safeguards for accuracy, fairness, transparency, human oversight, and customer protection throughout the system lifecycle.

**Key Conceptual Distinctions**

- **Inherent risk vs. residual risk:** Inherent risk describes the exposure before controls; residual risk reflects the exposure remaining after controls are considered.
- **Model accuracy vs. suitability:** Correct model outputs do not automatically produce appropriate financial recommendations.
- **Risk tolerance vs. capacity to bear losses:** Willingness to accept risk is different from the financial ability to absorb losses.
- **Suitability vs. appropriateness:** These are distinct regulatory concepts whose application depends on the relevant service and legal requirements.
- **Validation vs. monitoring:** Validation evaluates whether a model is suitable for its intended purpose; monitoring evaluates whether its performance and risk remain acceptable in operation.
- **Data drift vs. performance drift:** Changes in input distributions differ from deterioration in the model's measured performance.
- **Transparency vs. explainability:** Informing customers that AI is used differs from providing a reliable explanation of a specific output or decision.
- **Human involvement vs. meaningful oversight:** A human step is effective only when the reviewer can understand, challenge, and influence the outcome.
- **Fairness testing vs. proof of non-discrimination:** Testing can identify risks and disparities but does not, by itself, establish compliance with every applicable legal requirement.
- **Risk identification vs. risk acceptance:** Documenting a risk does not authorize deployment or make the remaining exposure acceptable.

## Part 3 — Enterprise Security Architecture, Financial Data Protection, and Operational Resilience

### 11. Security Architecture for an Enterprise AI Financial Advisor

An enterprise AI financial advisor must operate within a security architecture that protects customer financial information, prevents unauthorized actions, maintains the integrity of recommendations, and supports regulatory compliance.

Unlike a conventional chatbot, an AI financial advisor may interact with customer portfolios, financial transactions, investment products, account information, identity services, and external market-data providers. These integrations create trust boundaries that must be explicitly defined and enforced.

The security architecture should not assume that an LLM can reliably enforce authorization policies, validate financial calculations, or determine whether an action is permitted. Those responsibilities must be implemented through deterministic controls, established enterprise security services, and governed business workflows.

#### 11.1 Security Architecture Objectives

The architecture should provide the following capabilities:

1. **Confidentiality:** Prevent unauthorized access to financial information and personal data.
2. **Integrity:** Protect customer records, financial calculations, recommendation inputs, and decision evidence from unauthorized modification.
3. **Availability:** Maintain the availability of critical advisory services and supporting systems.
4. **Authentication:** Verify the identity of customers, advisors, administrators, and service identities.
5. **Authorization:** Restrict each user and component to explicitly permitted resources and actions.
6. **Segregation of duties:** Separate recommendation generation, financial authorization, administrative access, and independent oversight where appropriate.
7. **Data minimization:** Process only the information necessary for the approved purpose.
8. **Traceability:** Maintain sufficient records to investigate material outputs, actions, and incidents.
9. **Resilience:** Support controlled degradation, recovery, and safe service interruption.
10. **AI-specific protection:** Address prompt injection, insecure tool use, data leakage, retrieval manipulation, and model-related threats.
11. **Third-party security:** Control the risks introduced by external AI providers, APIs, data suppliers, and hosted infrastructure.
12. **Regulatory alignment:** Translate applicable legal and regulatory requirements into technical and operational controls.

### 12. AI Threat Modeling for Financial Advisory Systems

Threat modeling identifies how an attacker, compromised component, malicious insider, or accidental failure could violate security or business requirements.

For an AI financial advisor, conventional application threat modeling should be supplemented with analysis of model inputs, retrieval mechanisms, context construction, tool permissions, model outputs, and downstream financial decisions.

#### 12.1 Threat Modeling Methodology

A structured threat modeling exercise should include:

1. Define the system's intended purpose and prohibited uses.
2. Identify sensitive assets and security objectives.
3. Map components, integrations, and data flows.
4. Identify trust boundaries and privileged operations.
5. Identify threat actors and relevant capabilities.
6. Develop realistic attack scenarios.
7. Evaluate likelihood, impact, and existing controls.
8. Define mitigation requirements.
9. Assign control owners and remediation deadlines.
10. Test the mitigations and reassess residual risk.

Relevant threat actors may include external cybercriminals, fraud groups, malicious insiders, compromised third-party providers, customers attempting to bypass restrictions, and attackers seeking to manipulate retrieved content.

Threat modeling should also cover accidental failures, including incorrect configurations, faulty integrations, stale information, and unintended data disclosure.

#### 12.2 Threat Categories

The following categories provide a useful starting point.

| Threat Category | Description | Financial Advisor Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prompt injection | Malicious instructions attempt to influence model behavior | A document tells the model to disclose another customer's portfolio |
| Sensitive information disclosure | Information is revealed to an unauthorized party | The system includes private account details in an unrelated response |
| Insecure output handling | Model output is trusted or executed without proper validation | An output is inserted into a downstream workflow without schema or policy checks |
| Excessive agency | The system has more tool access or autonomy than necessary | The agent can execute transfers when it only needs to retrieve balances |
| Retrieval manipulation | Relevant content is replaced, poisoned, or ranked incorrectly | A malicious product document influences a recommendation |
| Model or configuration tampering | An unauthorized change affects system behavior | An attacker changes a system prompt or switches the approved model endpoint |
| Training-data poisoning | Training or fine-tuning data is manipulated | A compromised dataset introduces systematically misleading financial information |
| Supply-chain compromise | A dependency or external provider introduces risk | A third-party component exposes sensitive prompts or data |
| Denial of service | Resources are exhausted or critical dependencies become unavailable | Excessive requests overwhelm the AI service |
| Identity compromise | Credentials or tokens are stolen or misused | A stolen advisor session is used to access customer records |
| Unauthorized financial action | A system performs a prohibited or unapproved action | An AI agent initiates a transaction without the required customer confirmation |
| Audit evasion | Logs or records are incomplete, altered, or bypassed | A sensitive tool call is not captured in the central audit trail |

#### 12.3 Prompt Injection in Financial Advisory Workflows

Prompt injection occurs when instructions contained in user inputs, retrieved documents, or other untrusted content attempt to influence an AI system in ways that conflict with its intended behavior.

Consider a financial advisor that retrieves product documentation from external sources. A malicious document might contain instructions telling the model to ignore previous restrictions, promote a particular investment, or reveal confidential context.

The danger is not limited to the model producing a misleading answer. If the model has access to tools, manipulated instructions may influence downstream operations.

A secure design should therefore:

- Treat retrieved documents and external content as untrusted data.
- Avoid granting the model authority based solely on natural-language instructions.
- Enforce customer authorization outside the LLM.
- Restrict available tools to those required for the current task.
- Validate every sensitive tool call against an explicit policy.
- Prevent retrieved content from changing access-control rules.
- Separate financial calculations from natural-language generation.
- Validate outputs before using them in downstream systems.
- Test adversarial examples during development and after material changes.

Prompt wording alone is not a sufficient security boundary. Instructions such as "never reveal confidential data" may improve behavior, but they do not replace technical authorization and data-isolation controls.

#### 12.4 Excessive Agency and Tool Misuse

An AI agent becomes more consequential when it can invoke tools, access APIs, or trigger business workflows.

Potential tools include:

- Retrieve customer portfolio.
- Retrieve account balances.
- Search approved investment products.
- Calculate portfolio exposure.
- Generate an investment proposal.
- Create an advisory case.
- Send a customer communication.
- Submit a transaction request.

These tools should not share the same privilege level.

A tool that retrieves public product information has a different risk profile from a tool that changes a customer's portfolio or initiates a financial transaction.

The organization should apply a tool-risk classification.

| Tool Class | Example | Recommended Control Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Class 1: Public read-only | Search approved public financial education material | Source restrictions, content validation, abuse monitoring |
| Class 2: Customer-specific read-only | Retrieve an authenticated customer's portfolio | Customer-level authorization, data minimization, access logging |
| Class 3: Analytical | Calculate portfolio exposure or compare investment scenarios | Validated computation, input checks, reproducibility |
| Class 4: Workflow-changing | Create an advisory case or draft a customer communication | Scoped permissions, validation, approval where required |
| Class 5: Financially consequential | Submit a transaction or change an investment allocation | Strong authorization, explicit confirmation, transaction controls, independent validation |

The classification is illustrative. Each organization must classify tools according to their actual capabilities and consequences.

A tool should not receive a broad service credential merely because the AI application needs access to one of its functions. Credentials should be scoped to the specific resources and operations required.

For financially consequential actions, the system should use a controlled execution workflow that verifies authorization, applicable business rules, transaction limits, and required confirmation independently of the model.

5. Prevent the LLM from independently expanding data access.
6. Monitor for cross-customer retrieval anomalies.
7. Test tenant and customer isolation.
8. Preserve evidence of retrieval decisions and relevant access checks.
9. Establish a procedure to investigate and remediate suspected disclosure.

The primary preventive control is authorization at the data-access boundary. Output filtering may provide additional protection, but it should not be relied upon as the only barrier against disclosure.

#### 12.6 Example Threat Scenario: Manipulated Investment Recommendation

**Scenario:** An attacker introduces malicious instructions into an external product document. The retrieval system ranks the document highly, and the LLM uses its content to recommend a particular investment product.

**Potential consequences:**

- Misleading customer guidance.
- Unauthorized influence over recommendations.
- Conflict-of-interest concerns.
- Customer financial losses.
- Reputational damage.
- Potential legal or regulatory consequences.

**Required safeguards:**

- Restrict sources to approved financial information providers.
- Validate document origin, integrity, and freshness.
- Treat document content as untrusted.
- Separate product eligibility rules from natural-language generation.
- Require recommendation criteria to be evaluated by approved business logic.
- Test for manipulated retrieval content.
- Monitor unexpected recommendation patterns.
- Escalate material anomalies to the relevant risk and compliance owners.

A document retrieved from an approved source is not automatically trustworthy for every purpose. Its origin, integrity, relevance, and applicability must still be evaluated.

### 13. Data Protection, Privacy, and Confidential Financial Information

An AI financial advisor may process personally identifiable information, account identifiers, transaction history, investment portfolios, financial documents, income information, and other confidential records.

Data protection must therefore be designed into the system architecture and operating model rather than treated solely as a privacy notice or legal review.

The organization should establish which data is required, why it is processed, where it flows, who can access it, how long it is retained, and which providers or subprocessors receive it.

#### 13.1 Financial Data Classification

A practical classification model may include the following levels.

| Classification | Example Data | Typical Handling Requirements |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Published financial education articles and public product information | Source validation and integrity controls |
| Internal | Internal procedures and non-public operational documentation | Authorized internal access and controlled sharing |
| Confidential | Customer portfolio summaries and account-related information | Strong access controls, encryption, purpose limitation, audit logging |
| Highly Restricted | Authentication secrets, sensitive identity records, detailed transaction credentials, or particularly sensitive financial documents | Strict authorization, enhanced monitoring, minimized exposure, specialized handling |

The organization must define its own classification scheme and map it to applicable legal obligations and contractual requirements.

Not every financial record necessarily belongs in the same classification. For example, a public product brochure differs significantly from an authenticated customer's detailed transaction history.

#### 13.2 Data Minimization

Data minimization means processing only the information necessary for a specified purpose.

An AI advisor that provides a general explanation of diversification may not need the customer's name, address, full account number, or complete transaction history.

A personalized portfolio analysis may require selected holdings, asset allocations, investment objectives, and other relevant information, but not necessarily every available customer record.

The architecture should therefore avoid sending the entire customer profile to the LLM by default.

A preferred workflow is:

1. Identify the specific advisory task.
2. Determine the minimum information required.
3. Retrieve only authorized fields.
4. Perform calculations in the appropriate trusted service.
5. Provide the model with the minimum relevant context.
6. Remove unnecessary identifiers where feasible.
7. Validate that outputs do not disclose unrelated information.
8. Retain records according to defined legal, operational, and audit requirements.

Data minimization reduces exposure if the model provider, application, logs, or retrieval system is compromised.

#### 13.3 Pseudonymization and Anonymization

These terms describe different privacy outcomes.

**Pseudonymization** replaces or separates direct identifiers so that records cannot readily be attributed to a person without additional information. Re-identification may remain possible.

**Anonymization** aims to prevent identification of individuals through the processed data, taking into account reasonably available means of identification.

Replacing a customer's name with a random identifier does not automatically make a financial record anonymous. Detailed transactions, unusual portfolio characteristics, dates, and other attributes may still permit re-identification.

For an AI financial advisor, pseudonymization may reduce unnecessary exposure when a model does not require the customer's direct identity.

However, the application may still need to associate the final result with the correct authenticated customer. This mapping should remain within a controlled service and should not be exposed unnecessarily to the model.

#### 13.4 Data Flow and Privacy Boundaries

A typical privacy-aware workflow separates customer identity, financial records, analytical services, and language generation.

The architecture should also address information copied into prompts, conversation history, application logs, traces, error reports, support tickets, and provider-side telemetry.

A system can protect the main database while still exposing financial information through debugging logs or monitoring integrations.

#### 13.5 External AI Provider Data Handling

When a third-party AI service processes financial information, the organization must assess the provider's data-handling arrangements.

Relevant questions include:

- Is customer data used to train or improve the provider's models?
- Can the organization disable optional data-sharing or retention features?
- How long are prompts, outputs, and metadata retained?
- Are logs retained separately from primary service data?
- Which subprocessors can access the information?
- Where is data processed and stored?
- What international data transfers occur?
- How are customer deletion requests handled?
- What incident notification commitments apply?
- Can the provider support the organization's audit and regulatory obligations?
- What happens to data when the contract ends?
- Can the provider change relevant processing terms or service configurations?

The organization should verify the actual service configuration and contractual terms rather than rely solely on general marketing statements.

A provider's statement that it does not train on customer data does not necessarily mean that all prompts, logs, and operational metadata are never retained.

#### 13.6 Privacy Impact Assessment

Where required or appropriate, the organization should conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) before deploying a system that may create high risks to individuals.

The assessment should consider:

1. The nature and purpose of the processing.
2. The categories and volume of personal data.
3. The individuals affected.
4. The data sources and recipients.
5. The necessity and proportionality of the processing.
6. The risks to rights and freedoms.
7. The safeguards and mitigation measures.
8. The role of third-party providers.
9. The implications of automated decision-making.
10. The arrangements for transparency, correction, and challenge.
11. The retention and deletion arrangements.
12. Residual risks and any required further action.

Under the GDPR, a DPIA is required when the processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Whether a particular AI financial advisory deployment meets that threshold must be assessed against its actual processing activities and circumstances.

A DPIA does not replace the separate security risk assessment, model validation, or applicable financial-services compliance review.

### 14. Identity, Access Management, and Transaction Security

Identity and access management (IAM) is a foundational control for financial advisory systems because the application may access sensitive customer records and interact with consequential financial services.

Authorization must be enforced consistently across customer channels, AI orchestration, retrieval services, APIs, business systems, and administrative interfaces.

#### 14.1 Identity Categories

The system may contain several identity types.

| Identity Type | Example | Principal Security Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Customer identity | An authenticated retail investor | Verify identity and enforce access to the customer's own information |
| Financial advisor | A qualified employee or authorized representative | Restrict access according to role and assigned responsibilities |
| Compliance reviewer | A person investigating a recommendation or complaint | Provide appropriately scoped access to evidence and records |
| System administrator | An administrator managing the platform | Privileged access controls, monitoring, and separation of duties |
| AI application identity | A service identity used by the orchestration layer | Scoped permissions and managed credentials |
| Retrieval service identity | A service that searches approved documents | Source restrictions and access enforcement |
| Model provider identity | Credentials used to invoke an external model endpoint | Restricted endpoint access and secure credential storage |
| Automated workflow identity | A service that creates advisory cases or submits requests | Explicitly defined permitted operations and execution constraints |

Service identities should be individually identifiable. Shared credentials make it more difficult to determine which component accessed a resource or initiated an action.

#### 14.2 Role-Based and Attribute-Based Access Control

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) assigns permissions according to defined roles.

Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC) evaluates attributes such as the customer identifier, account relationship, resource classification, purpose, time, or contextual conditions.

A financial advisor may use RBAC to determine which general functions their role can perform, while ABAC may restrict the records they can access to customers assigned to their portfolio.

A secure design may combine both approaches.

For customer-facing AI services, access decisions should not be based solely on the wording of the prompt.

For example, a request stating "I am authorized to view this account" does not establish authorization. The application must verify the authenticated identity and the relevant account permissions through trusted services.

#### 14.3 Least Privilege for AI Components

The principle of least privilege requires each component to receive only the permissions necessary to perform its approved function.

The organization should:

- Use separate identities for distinct services.
- Avoid broad administrative credentials in AI orchestration.
- Restrict retrieval to approved repositories and customer scopes.
- Separate read-only capabilities from write operations.
- Apply explicit permissions to sensitive tools.
- Use managed identities or secure credential stores where supported.
- Rotate credentials according to policy and risk.
- Monitor privilege changes.
- Review permissions periodically.
- Revoke access when components or integrations are retired.

An AI model should not receive unrestricted access to core banking APIs merely because a future use case might require it.

Permissions should be added through formal change management when a new requirement is approved.

#### 14.4 Secure Transaction Workflow

If the AI advisor is connected to a transaction platform, the transaction process should be separate from natural-language generation.

```text id="aifa-p3-03"
Customer Requests Financial Action
                |
                v
AI Interprets the Request
                |
                v
Application Creates Structured Request
                |
                v
Identity and Account Authorization
                |
                v
Business Rules and Transaction Limits
                |
                v
Suitability / Eligibility Checks
                |
                v
Customer Confirmation Where Required
                |
                v
Independent Transaction Authorization
                |
                v
Execution by Authorized Financial Service
                |
                v
Transaction Status Verification
                |
                v
Audit Record and Customer Notification
```

The exact checks depend on the action and applicable requirements. Not every transaction requires an identical confirmation process, and some actions may be prohibited from AI initiation altogether.

The essential control is that the model must not independently bypass the organization's authorization, transaction, or approval mechanisms.

A natural-language response stating "Your transaction is complete" must not be treated as proof of execution. The application should obtain the transaction status from the authoritative financial service.

#### 14.5 Authentication and Session Security

Customer-facing deployments should assess:

- Multifactor authentication where appropriate.
- Secure session establishment and termination.
- Session expiration and reauthentication for sensitive operations.
- Protection against session fixation and token theft.
- Secure token storage and transmission.
- Appropriate use of OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect where applicable.
- Protection against cross-site request forgery where relevant.
- Device and risk signals where proportionate.
- Step-up authentication for defined high-risk actions.
- Revocation of compromised sessions and tokens.

The exact implementation depends on the application architecture and identity platform.

Authentication establishes who the user is, while authorization determines what that identity may access or do. Both must be enforced.

**Core Principle:** The security architecture must enforce authorization, data protection, and financial transaction controls through trusted application and enterprise services rather than relying on the LLM's instructions or judgment.

### 15. Security Monitoring, Auditability, and Operational Resilience

Security controls are effective only when the organization can detect failures, investigate incidents, and restore safe operation.

An AI financial advisor should therefore integrate with the enterprise security operations model, including security monitoring, vulnerability management, incident response, model monitoring, privacy incident handling, and business continuity.

#### 15.1 Security Monitoring Requirements

Monitoring should cover the application, infrastructure, identity services, AI interactions, retrieval system, external integrations, and financial workflows.

| Monitoring Domain | Example Events | Potential Detection Objective |
|---|---|---|
| Identity and access | Repeated authentication failures, privilege escalation, unusual account access | Detect account compromise and unauthorized access |
| API security | Abnormal request volumes, unauthorized calls, schema violations | Detect API abuse and access-control failures |
| AI behavior | Unusual refusal patterns, repeated policy bypass attempts, anomalous tool requests | Identify potential manipulation or behavioral changes |
| Retrieval security | Cross-customer retrieval anomalies, unexpected source changes | Detect data isolation failures and retrieval manipulation |
| Data protection | Unusual exports, excessive sensitive-data access, unexpected external transmission | Detect possible data leakage |
| Model performance | Increased factual errors, failed evaluations, changes in output quality | Identify deterioration in model behavior |
| Financial workflows | Rejected transactions, repeated limit violations, inconsistent transaction states | Detect misuse and workflow integrity failures |
| Third-party services | Provider outages, endpoint changes, authentication failures | Identify dependency failures and unauthorized changes |
| Infrastructure | Vulnerabilities, suspicious processes, configuration changes | Detect compromise or insecure configurations |
| Governance | Overdue risk actions, expired approvals, unreviewed changes | Identify control-management weaknesses |

Monitoring should distinguish between expected behavior, benign anomalies, and material incidents. Alert thresholds should be tested and tuned to avoid excessive false positives.

#### 15.2 Audit Logging and Evidence

The organization should preserve records sufficient to reconstruct material advisory activities and investigate incidents.

Depending on the purpose, applicable requirements, and risk, relevant records may include:

- Timestamp and interaction identifier.
- Authenticated customer or advisor reference.
- Authorization decisions.
- Relevant data-source identifiers and versions.
- Model provider and model version where available.
- Prompt or context references where appropriate and lawful.
- Retrieval results or document references.
- Financial calculation inputs and outputs.
- Recommendation rules applied.
- Output validation results.
- Tool requests and execution results.
- Human review decisions.
- Customer confirmations where required.
- Relevant system changes.
- Incident and remediation references.

The organization should not automatically retain every raw prompt, complete conversation, or customer document indefinitely.

Logging must balance auditability with data minimization, confidentiality, retention requirements, and access restrictions.

Sensitive records should be protected against unauthorized modification and deletion. Depending on the risk, the organization may use tamper-evident logging, restricted administrative permissions, and separate log-management identities.

#### 15.3 Evidence for a Material Recommendation

A defensible evidence record should enable an authorized reviewer to answer:

1. Which customer or authorized user was involved?
2. What request was made?
3. Which service and model version were used?
4. Which relevant data sources were consulted?
5. What assumptions and rules affected the result?
6. Which financial calculations were performed?
7. What validation checks were applied?
8. Was human review required?
9. Was the recommendation presented as information or as part of an advisory service?
10. What action, if any, was taken?
11. Was the customer required to confirm the action?
12. Were there warnings, exceptions, or unresolved uncertainties?

The objective is to reconstruct the relevant process without unnecessarily exposing unrelated customer information.

#### 15.4 Incident Response

AI-related incidents may combine cybersecurity, financial conduct, privacy, model risk, and operational failures.

A coordinated incident response process should include:

1. Detection and initial reporting.
2. Classification according to severity and impact.
3. Containment of the affected functionality.
4. Preservation of relevant evidence.
5. Assessment of customer and financial impact.
6. Identification of affected data, models, integrations, and users.
7. Engagement of security, privacy, compliance, legal, and business stakeholders.
8. Determination of applicable notification obligations.
9. Eradication of the underlying cause.
10. Recovery through controlled validation and approval.
11. Customer remediation where appropriate.
12. Post-incident review and control improvement.

Examples of containment actions include disabling an affected tool, restricting retrieval access, reverting a model configuration, suspending personalized recommendations, or switching to a controlled fallback service.

The response should be proportionate to the incident. A minor quality issue may require correction and monitoring, while unauthorized financial actions or significant data disclosure may require immediate containment and formal escalation.

#### 15.5 Operational Resilience

The service should be designed to fail safely when critical dependencies become unavailable or unreliable.

Potential failure conditions include:

- AI provider outage.
- Market-data feed failure.
- Unavailable customer-profile service.
- Incorrect retrieval results.
- Compromised model endpoint.
- Inconsistent transaction status.
- Excessive latency.
- Expired credentials.
- Security monitoring failure.
- Loss of access to required audit records.

A fallback process may allow the customer to access ordinary account information through established systems while temporarily disabling AI-generated recommendations.

The fallback must not silently replace unavailable authoritative data with fabricated, stale, or unverified information.

For high-impact use cases, the organization should define recovery objectives, service dependencies, escalation procedures, and conditions for restoring service.

#### 15.6 Operational Resilience Control Matrix

| Control Area | Requirement | Example Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Monitoring | Detect relevant security and operational anomalies | Alerts, monitoring dashboards, investigation records |
| Logging | Preserve required evidence with appropriate protection | Log policies, access reviews, integrity tests |
| Incident response | Define ownership, escalation, and containment | Response procedures and exercise results |
| Model rollback | Restore an approved model configuration | Version history and rollback test results |
| Provider resilience | Address external dependency failures | Provider assessments and contingency procedures |
| Data recovery | Restore required information safely | Recovery tests and integrity verification |
| Service continuity | Maintain safe alternatives for critical functions | Business continuity plans and test evidence |
| Change management | Validate material changes before deployment | Change records, approvals, regression tests |
| Vulnerability management | Identify and remediate relevant weaknesses | Scan results, remediation records, exception approvals |
| Post-incident review | Address root causes and control failures | Root-cause analysis and corrective-action tracking |

#### 15.7 Governance and Security Metrics

The organization should establish metrics that provide meaningful information about control effectiveness.

Potential indicators include:

- Number of unresolved critical security findings.
- Percentage of critical AI components with current risk assessments.
- Percentage of production model versions with approved validation evidence.
- Number of unauthorized tool-call attempts.
- Frequency of cross-customer retrieval violations.
- Percentage of critical financial calculations passing regression tests.
- Rate of materially incorrect financial outputs.
- Number of confirmed sensitive-data disclosure incidents.
- Mean time to detect and contain AI-related incidents.
- Percentage of high-risk model changes receiving required approval.
- Number of overdue remediation actions.
- Percentage of critical recovery procedures tested successfully.

Each metric should have a defined calculation method, reporting frequency, owner, threshold, and escalation path.

For example, a target of zero unauthorized financial actions is meaningful only when the organization has reliable mechanisms to detect and investigate such actions.

A low incident count should not automatically be interpreted as proof of effective controls. It may also reflect insufficient monitoring or incomplete incident reporting.

**Core Principle:** A secure AI financial advisor must be observable, auditable, recoverable, and capable of safe degradation. Governance requires evidence that controls work in production, not merely documentation that they exist.

**Key Conceptual Distinctions**

- **Authentication vs. authorization:** Authentication verifies identity; authorization determines permitted access and actions.
- **LLM instructions vs. security enforcement:** Natural-language instructions influence model behavior but do not replace enforceable access-control policies.
- **Read-only access vs. transaction authority:** Retrieving financial information does not require the same privileges as modifying accounts or initiating transactions.
- **Prompt injection vs. conventional input validation:** Prompt injection manipulates model interpretation, while conventional input validation addresses defined data and application constraints; both may require complementary controls.
- **Pseudonymization vs. anonymization:** Pseudonymized information may remain identifiable through additional information or linkage.
- **Security logging vs. unrestricted retention:** Auditability does not justify retaining all sensitive information indefinitely.
- **Model monitoring vs. security monitoring:** Model monitoring evaluates performance and behavior; security monitoring detects potential attacks, compromise, and control violations.
- **Incident containment vs. full remediation:** Containment limits immediate exposure, while remediation addresses the underlying cause and affected outcomes.
- **Availability vs. safe availability:** Keeping a service online is not always preferable to suspending a function that cannot operate safely.
- **Control implementation vs. control effectiveness:** The existence of a security control does not establish that it operates correctly or reduces risk as intended.
