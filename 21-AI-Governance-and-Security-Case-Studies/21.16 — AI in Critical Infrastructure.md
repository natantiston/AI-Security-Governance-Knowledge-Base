# 21.16 — AI in Critical Infrastructure
## Part 1: Enterprise Context, System Architecture, Critical Services, and Risk Landscape

### 1. Introduction to AI in Critical Infrastructure

Artificial intelligence is increasingly being integrated into critical infrastructure to improve operational efficiency, detect anomalies, optimize resource consumption, predict equipment failures, and support complex operational decisions.

Critical infrastructure consists of systems, assets, facilities, networks, and services whose disruption could significantly affect public safety, national security, economic stability, public health, or the continuity of essential services.

Examples include:

- Electrical power generation, transmission, and distribution.
- Water treatment and wastewater management.
- Oil and gas production, pipelines, and distribution.
- Telecommunications networks and internet infrastructure.
- Transportation systems, including railways, airports, and traffic management.
- Healthcare infrastructure and emergency medical services.
- Industrial manufacturing and chemical processing.
- Financial market infrastructure and payment systems.
- Data centers and cloud infrastructure supporting essential services.

AI can provide significant operational benefits in these environments. However, introducing AI into critical infrastructure creates risks that differ from those associated with ordinary enterprise productivity applications.

An AI assistant that generates an inaccurate internal report may create a limited operational problem. An AI system that recommends or executes an incorrect action affecting electrical grid stability, industrial pressure, water treatment, or telecommunications availability could produce widespread consequences.

The governance challenge is therefore not simply to determine whether an AI model is accurate. It is to establish whether the complete AI-enabled system can operate safely, securely, reliably, and accountably within a critical operational environment.

#### 1.1 Why Critical Infrastructure Requires a Different Governance Approach

Critical infrastructure environments have characteristics that influence AI security and risk management.

| Characteristic | Description | Governance implication |
|---|---|---|
| High operational impact | A failure may affect essential services or public safety | Risk assessments must consider consequences beyond the individual application |
| Operational technology dependency | AI may interact with industrial control systems and physical processes | Controls must account for physical effects and operational safety |
| Availability requirements | Interruptions may have immediate or cascading consequences | Security controls must preserve essential service continuity |
| Long equipment lifecycles | Industrial equipment may remain in service for decades | AI integrations must accommodate legacy systems and extended maintenance cycles |
| Complex dependencies | Electricity, telecommunications, water, transport, and cloud services depend on one another | Risk assessments must examine interdependencies and cascading failures |
| Limited maintenance windows | Some systems cannot be interrupted easily for updates or testing | Changes require coordinated operational planning |
| Safety obligations | Incorrect actions may injure people or damage equipment | Safety engineering must complement cybersecurity and AI governance |
| Mixed technology environments | Legacy operational technology may coexist with modern cloud and AI platforms | Architecture must account for different trust boundaries and security capabilities |
| Specialized operating procedures | Operators rely on validated procedures and domain expertise | AI recommendations must be evaluated against approved operational practices |
| Regulatory scrutiny | Operators may face sector-specific security, safety, and resilience obligations | Governance evidence must support applicable compliance and assurance requirements |

These characteristics mean that AI security governance cannot operate independently of operational risk management, engineering safety, business continuity, and emergency response.

#### 1.2 Distinguish Information Technology and Operational Technology

Understanding the difference between Information Technology (IT) and Operational Technology (OT) is essential when assessing AI in critical infrastructure.

**Information Technology** primarily processes, stores, transmits, and manages information.

Examples include:
- Enterprise applications.
- Corporate identity systems.
- Email and collaboration platforms.
- Business intelligence systems.
- Customer relationship management systems.
- Enterprise resource planning systems.

**Operational Technology** monitors or controls physical processes, equipment, and industrial operations.

Examples include:
- Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs).
- Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems.
- Distributed Control Systems (DCS).
- Industrial sensors and actuators.
- Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs).
- Industrial safety systems.
- Substation automation and protection systems.

The distinction is important because a cybersecurity incident affecting an IT system may primarily affect information confidentiality or business processes, while an incident involving OT may also affect equipment integrity, physical safety, or the continuity of essential services.

However, the distinction is not absolute. IT and OT are increasingly interconnected, and an incident originating in an enterprise IT environment can affect industrial operations through shared identity systems, remote-access infrastructure, data platforms, or operational integrations.

#### 1.3 AI Deployment Models in Critical Infrastructure

AI can be deployed at different levels of operational authority.

| Deployment model | Description | Example | Relative governance concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offline analytics | AI analyzes historical operational data without connecting to live control systems | Predicting transformer failure from historical maintenance records | Data quality, confidentiality, analytical reliability |
| Advisory AI | AI provides recommendations to qualified operators | Recommending inspection of an abnormal pump | Recommendation accuracy, explainability, operator reliance |
| Decision-support AI | AI evaluates operational conditions and supports defined decisions | Ranking possible causes of a network outage | Decision quality, uncertainty, human oversight |
| Supervised automation | AI proposes actions that require approval before execution | Suggesting a change to an approved industrial operating parameter | Authorization, approval integrity, validation |
| Bounded autonomous control | AI executes predefined actions within explicitly enforced limits | Adjusting an approved operating parameter within a validated range | Safety, authorization, real-time monitoring, fail-safe behavior |
| Broad autonomous operation | AI makes and executes multiple operational decisions with limited human intervention | Coordinating a complex industrial optimization workflow | Systemic risk, loss of control, cascading effects, accountability |

These categories describe different levels of authority rather than a universal maturity scale. A bounded autonomous controller may be appropriate for a narrowly defined and rigorously validated function, while a sophisticated advisory system may still be unsuitable for a particular high-consequence decision.

The appropriate deployment model depends on the operating environment, safety requirements, threat model, technical assurance, and applicable legal obligations.

**Core Principle:** AI in critical infrastructure must be governed according to the consequences of its decisions and actions, not merely according to the sophistication of its model.

### 2. Business Context and Critical Infrastructure Use Cases

Organizations adopt AI in critical infrastructure to improve reliability, reduce operating costs, identify emerging failures, optimize resource utilization, and support faster operational decisions.

These benefits are valuable, but they must be evaluated against the consequences of incorrect predictions, compromised inputs, unauthorized control actions, and failures of the supporting AI infrastructure.

#### 2.1 Electricity Generation, Transmission, and Distribution

AI can support electricity operators across generation, transmission, and distribution.

Typical applications include:

- Predictive maintenance for transformers, turbines, generators, and circuit breakers.
- Analysis of electrical load and demand patterns.
- Forecasting renewable energy generation.
- Detection of unusual grid conditions.
- Identification of equipment degradation.
- Optimization of maintenance schedules.
- Analysis of power quality and equipment telemetry.
- Decision support for fault identification and restoration.

**Example scenario**

An electricity distribution operator deploys an AI system to analyze transformer temperature, load, vibration, and historical maintenance data.

The system identifies a pattern associated with an increased probability of equipment failure and recommends an inspection.

The potential benefit is early detection of equipment degradation.

However, governance must consider whether the data is reliable, whether the recommendation is operationally appropriate, whether the system can access live control functions, and whether operators understand the limitations of its predictions.

An AI-generated recommendation should not automatically override established protection systems or validated operating procedures.

#### 2.2 Water Treatment and Distribution

AI can support water utilities through:

- Leak detection.
- Demand forecasting.
- Pump optimization.
- Equipment maintenance.
- Water-quality anomaly detection.
- Energy consumption optimization.
- Identification of abnormal pressure patterns.
- Analysis of treatment-process performance.

**Example scenario**

An AI system analyzes sensor data from a water treatment facility and identifies an unusual pattern in a water-quality measurement.

The system recommends additional sampling and an operator review.

The primary governance objective is to ensure that the AI improves detection without replacing mandatory water-quality controls, validated laboratory processes, or established safety procedures.

Risks include manipulated sensor inputs, incorrect recommendations, unauthorized changes to treatment parameters, and failures to escalate abnormal conditions.

If the AI can issue control commands, its permissions and operating boundaries require substantially greater scrutiny than those of an offline analytical system.

#### 2.3 Oil and Gas Infrastructure

AI applications in oil and gas include:

- Predictive maintenance for pumps and compressors.
- Pipeline anomaly detection.
- Equipment failure prediction.
- Analysis of vibration and pressure data.
- Monitoring of industrial processes.
- Optimization of production operations.
- Detection of unusual operating conditions.
- Support for inspection and maintenance planning.

**Example scenario**

An AI system analyzes pipeline pressure and flow measurements to identify possible leaks.

The system generates an alert when a combination of measurements deviates from the expected operating pattern.

Governance must establish how the alert is validated, who receives it, what operational action is permitted, and whether the system can influence isolation valves or other physical controls.

A false negative could delay the detection of a leak. A false positive could trigger unnecessary intervention or operational disruption.

The AI system must therefore be assessed for both analytical performance and the consequences of the operational workflow it supports.

#### 2.4 Telecommunications Infrastructure

Telecommunications operators increasingly use AI to manage complex networks and support service reliability.

Potential applications include:

- Network anomaly detection.
- Predictive maintenance of network equipment.
- Traffic forecasting.
- Radio access network optimization.
- Capacity planning.
- Detection of unusual signaling patterns.
- Fault correlation across network elements.
- Automated incident prioritization.
- Recommendations for network configuration changes.

**Example scenario**

A telecommunications operator deploys an AI system to identify possible causes of a regional service outage.

The system correlates alarms from transport, core, and access networks and recommends a remediation action.

The governance challenge is to prevent incorrect recommendations from causing a wider outage.

If the AI can execute configuration changes, the operator must establish strict boundaries around routing, access controls, network segmentation, and change authorization.

Critical actions may require human approval, while narrowly defined diagnostic operations may be automated within validated constraints.

#### 2.5 Transportation and Rail Infrastructure

AI may support:

- Predictive maintenance of railway equipment.
- Traffic-flow optimization.
- Asset inspection.
- Equipment fault detection.
- Passenger-flow analysis.
- Demand forecasting.
- Maintenance scheduling.
- Decision support for service disruption management.

**Example scenario**

An AI system analyzes sensor data from railway infrastructure and predicts a potential equipment failure.

The system prioritizes the asset for inspection and notifies the maintenance team.

The governance assessment must distinguish maintenance prioritization from safety-critical train control.

A system that recommends inspections has a different risk profile from one that can influence signaling, braking, or train movement.

Where AI affects safety-critical operations, the organization must establish the applicable safety assurance, validation, human oversight, and authorization requirements before deployment.

#### 2.6 Healthcare Infrastructure

AI can support healthcare infrastructure through:

- Predictive maintenance of medical equipment.
- Hospital demand forecasting.
- Optimization of energy and facility operations.
- Equipment utilization analysis.
- Monitoring of selected building systems.
- Support for inventory and logistics.
- Detection of operational anomalies.

**Example scenario**

An AI system predicts that a hospital's backup power equipment may require maintenance.

The system generates a maintenance recommendation based on equipment telemetry and historical failure patterns.

Governance must ensure that the recommendation does not compromise mandatory inspections, maintenance schedules, emergency preparedness, or established safety requirements.

If the AI is integrated with clinical equipment or systems supporting patient care, the assessment must also consider the specific clinical, safety, and regulatory implications of that integration.

#### 2.7 Data Centers and Cloud Infrastructure

AI is increasingly used to improve the management of data centers and digital infrastructure.

Applications include:

- Cooling optimization.
- Energy consumption forecasting.
- Predictive maintenance of power and cooling equipment.
- Capacity planning.
- Identification of abnormal infrastructure conditions.
- Workload placement recommendations.
- Support for infrastructure incident management.
- Detection of performance anomalies.

**Example scenario**

An AI system analyzes server utilization, cooling performance, and environmental measurements to recommend adjustments that reduce energy consumption.

The organization must determine whether the AI can only recommend changes or whether it can directly alter cooling, power, workload placement, or infrastructure configurations.

An apparently minor optimization may affect equipment temperature, service availability, redundancy, or the resilience of critical workloads.

The system must therefore be evaluated against the operating constraints and resilience requirements of the facility.

#### 2.8 Comparative Risk Across Critical Infrastructure Sectors

| Sector | Example AI use case | Primary risk dimensions | Key governance concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electricity | Grid anomaly detection | Availability, integrity, physical safety | Prevent unsafe operational recommendations or control actions |
| Water | Treatment-process monitoring | Public health, integrity, availability | Preserve validated treatment and water-quality safeguards |
| Oil and gas | Pipeline anomaly detection | Physical safety, environmental impact, availability | Ensure reliable detection and controlled operational response |
| Telecommunications | Network optimization | Availability, integrity, confidentiality | Prevent unauthorized configuration changes and cascading outages |
| Transportation | Predictive maintenance | Safety, availability, integrity | Preserve safety-critical operating procedures and assurance |
| Healthcare | Facility operations optimization | Patient safety, availability, privacy | Protect essential services and relevant health information |
| Data centers | Cooling optimization | Availability, integrity, energy efficiency | Maintain power, cooling, and workload resilience |

These examples illustrate common risk dimensions, but they do not establish a universal ranking of sector risk. Actual risk depends on the system's function, operational authority, environment, threat exposure, and consequences of failure.

**Core Principle:** The same AI capability can have very different risk implications depending on the critical infrastructure process it supports and the degree of operational authority it receives.

### 3. Reference Architecture for AI-Enabled Critical Infrastructure

AI systems in critical infrastructure often operate across multiple technology layers, including enterprise IT, industrial OT, edge computing, cloud platforms, data engineering, and AI services.

The architecture must preserve appropriate separation between analytical functions and systems that control physical processes.

A key design objective is to prevent an AI component from gaining unrestricted access to industrial equipment simply because it needs operational data or must communicate a recommendation.

#### 3.2 Enterprise Governance Layer

The governance layer defines the rules under which AI is developed, procured, deployed, operated, and retired.

Its responsibilities include:

- Establishing the approved business purpose.
- Defining risk appetite and acceptable operating boundaries.
- Determining the required level of human oversight.
- Approving AI use cases and material changes.
- Assigning risk and control owners.
- Establishing safety and cybersecurity assurance requirements.
- Defining monitoring and incident-reporting obligations.
- Maintaining evidence for audit and regulatory review.

Governance should establish who is authorized to approve an AI system's use in an operational process and who can suspend that use when risk becomes unacceptable.

#### 3.3 Enterprise IT Layer

The enterprise IT layer supports business applications and organizational services that may provide information to the AI system.

Examples include:

- Asset management.
- Enterprise resource planning.
- Identity and access management.
- Security information and event management.
- Maintenance management.
- Incident management.
- Enterprise data platforms.

This layer may contain sensitive operational information, including equipment configurations, asset inventories, maintenance histories, network diagrams, and incident records.

Access to these systems must be controlled according to the AI use case. An agent that only analyzes maintenance history should not automatically receive administrative permissions over the enterprise systems from which the data originates.

Compromise of enterprise IT may also affect OT when the environments share identity services, remote access, integration platforms, or administrative pathways.

#### 3.4 Controlled Integration Layer

The integration layer establishes how information and authorized actions move between AI services, enterprise IT, and operational environments.

Security responsibilities include:

- Authenticating connected systems.
- Enforcing authorization rules.
- Validating data formats and values.
- Restricting communication to approved destinations.
- Applying network segmentation.
- Logging access and control decisions.
- Enforcing rate limits where appropriate.
- Preventing unauthorized protocol translation or command execution.
- Restricting the available functions of connected APIs.

This layer is particularly important when AI services operate outside the industrial control environment.

A cloud-hosted AI model should not receive unrestricted network connectivity to PLCs, SCADA servers, or industrial safety systems merely to support an analytical use case.

#### 3.5 AI and Analytics Layer

The AI layer processes operational information and generates predictions, classifications, recommendations, or other outputs.

Potential components include:

- Data-quality validation.
- Feature processing.
- Anomaly detection models.
- Predictive maintenance models.
- Forecasting models.
- Optimization models.
- Decision-support systems.
- Output validation and confidence assessment.
- Model monitoring and evaluation.

The organization should determine whether the model is appropriate for the specific operating environment and whether its performance has been evaluated using representative data.

Particular attention should be given to:

- Missing or delayed sensor measurements.
- Incorrect timestamps.
- Calibration problems.
- Out-of-range values.
- Changes in operating conditions.
- Training data that does not represent unusual but safety-relevant conditions.
- Adversarial manipulation of input data.
- Uncertainty in predictions.
- Degraded performance following equipment or process changes.

The AI layer should not be assumed to understand physical safety constraints simply because it was trained on historical operational data.

#### 3.6 OT Control Environment

The OT control environment performs or supervises the actual industrial process.

Typical components include:

**Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs)**  
Industrial controllers that execute control logic and interact with equipment.

**Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)**  
Systems used to supervise and monitor distributed industrial processes.

**Distributed Control Systems (DCS)**  
Control systems commonly used to manage complex industrial processes.

**Human-Machine Interfaces (HMIs)**  
Interfaces through which operators observe process conditions and interact with control systems.

**Industrial sensors and actuators**  
Devices that measure physical conditions and perform physical actions.

These components may have different security capabilities, operating constraints, and maintenance requirements from conventional enterprise applications.

AI integrations must therefore account for the characteristics of the specific OT environment rather than applying IT security controls without adaptation.

#### 3.7 Safety Systems and Independent Protection

Safety systems protect people, equipment, and the environment from hazardous operating conditions.

Depending on the environment, they may include independent protection mechanisms, safety instrumented systems, protective relays, interlocks, and other engineered safeguards.

An AI system should not be permitted to bypass or disable required safety protections through an unrestricted API, privileged account, or poorly controlled integration.

Where AI is proposed for a safety-related function, the organization must determine the applicable safety engineering and assurance requirements before implementation.

The presence of an AI model does not automatically establish that the complete system is safe. Safety depends on the system design, operating environment, validation evidence, failure modes, and appropriate protective measures.

#### 3.8 Separate Read-Only Analytics from Operational Commands

One of the most important architecture decisions is whether the AI system requires permission to change operational states.

A useful default is to separate analytical data access from command execution.

| Capability | Example | Typical governance approach |
|---|---|---|
| Read operational telemetry | Retrieve historical pressure measurements | Restricted read-only access |
| Analyze alarms | Identify patterns in equipment alarms | Validate inputs and record analytical results |
| Recommend action | Suggest an inspection or operator review | Require qualified review where appropriate |
| Draft a change | Prepare a proposed configuration change | Validate against approved change procedures |
| Execute a bounded action | Perform an explicitly approved low-risk operation | Enforce action-specific authorization and limits |
| Execute a safety-critical action | Initiate an operation with significant physical consequences | Requires specialized safety assessment and authorization; may be inappropriate for AI autonomy |

The correct level of autonomy depends on the specific use case and its safety and security requirements.

**Core Principle:** The architecture must preserve the separation between AI-generated analysis and authorized operational control. Access to operational data must not automatically confer authority to change the physical process.

### 4. Critical Infrastructure Data Governance and Trust Boundaries

AI systems depend on data from sensors, industrial historians, maintenance systems, enterprise applications, external sources, and sometimes third-party platforms.

In critical infrastructure, data quality and trustworthiness are not merely analytical concerns. Incorrect data can influence operational recommendations, maintenance priorities, and decisions affecting essential services.

Data governance must therefore address provenance, integrity, availability, confidentiality, timeliness, and the authority of the source.

#### 4.1 Critical Infrastructure Data Categories

| Data category | Examples | Main governance considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Sensor and telemetry data | Temperature, pressure, vibration, voltage, flow | Integrity, accuracy, calibration, timestamps |
| Control-system data | Setpoints, operating states, control events | Authorization, integrity, change history |
| Alarm and event data | Equipment alarms, fault events, threshold violations | Completeness, time synchronization, reliable escalation |
| Asset and configuration data | Equipment models, network diagrams, configuration records | Confidentiality, integrity, controlled access |
| Maintenance data | Inspection records, repair history, work orders | Accuracy, traceability, operational relevance |
| Operational procedures | Approved operating instructions and emergency procedures | Authenticity, version control, controlled modification |
| Environmental data | Weather, temperature forecasts, external conditions | Source reliability, uncertainty, timeliness |
| Enterprise business data | Procurement, inventory, staffing, service records | Access control, privacy, business confidentiality |
| AI training data | Historical operating conditions, labeled failures, simulations | Representativeness, provenance, quality, poisoning resistance |
| AI model and configuration data | Model versions, parameters, prompts, thresholds | Integrity, change management, access control |
| Third-party data | Vendor telemetry, external threat intelligence, cloud service information | Supplier assurance, contractual controls, provenance |

The required controls depend on the data's purpose, sensitivity, operational consequence, and source.

#### 4.2 Data Provenance and Source Trust

Data provenance establishes where data originated, how it was collected, which transformations occurred, and how it reached the AI system.

For critical infrastructure, provenance should help answer:

- Which sensor, system, or external source produced the data?
- Is the source approved for the intended use?
- Has the source been authenticated where technically feasible?
- Was the data transformed or aggregated?
- Are timestamps and measurement units consistent?
- Is the data complete and sufficiently current?
- Has the data passed validation?
- Can the organization trace a model output back to the relevant inputs?
- Were any data-quality warnings or exceptions recorded?

A data source should not be considered trustworthy merely because it is internal.

An internal historian may contain erroneous measurements. A maintenance record may be outdated. A compromised sensor gateway may inject manipulated values. A third-party feed may provide inaccurate or delayed information.

Trust must be established through appropriate technical validation, source governance, and operational context.

#### 4.3 Sensor Integrity and Data Quality

Sensor measurements may be affected by:

- Equipment degradation.
- Calibration errors.
- Communication failures.
- Incorrect units.
- Clock synchronization problems.
- Missing observations.
- Duplicate events.
- Delayed transmission.
- Physical interference.
- Unauthorized modification.
- Malicious manipulation.

These conditions can affect AI performance even when the model itself has not changed.

For example, an AI model designed to identify abnormal water pressure may produce misleading results if the sensor's measurement units are incorrectly configured or if timestamps are inconsistent.

A critical infrastructure AI system should therefore validate data quality before using measurements for consequential decisions.

Where required data is unavailable or unreliable, the system should apply a defined degraded-mode policy rather than silently generating a confident recommendation from invalid inputs.

#### 4.4 Trust Boundaries in AI-Enabled Infrastructure

A trust boundary separates components or environments with different security assumptions, privileges, or control responsibilities.

Common boundaries include:

1. External data sources and enterprise systems.
2. Corporate IT and industrial OT.
3. Cloud AI services and local operational infrastructure.
4. Read-only analytics and command execution.
5. AI model outputs and independently authorized actions.
6. Vendor-managed services and enterprise-controlled systems.
7. Operational networks and safety-related control environments.
8. Training and testing environments and production environments.

Each boundary should have documented security requirements.

#### 4.5 Prevent Data Poisoning and Unauthorized Configuration Changes

Data poisoning occurs when an attacker or defective process manipulates data used to train, evaluate, or operate an AI system in a way that degrades or alters its behavior.

In critical infrastructure, possible examples include:

- Manipulating historical measurements used to train predictive-maintenance models.
- Altering failure labels in maintenance records.
- Injecting false readings into an operational analytics pipeline.
- Modifying reference data used to classify alarms.
- Tampering with model thresholds or configuration files.
- Introducing malicious instructions into an operational knowledge base.

The controls should reflect the relevant data lifecycle.

For training data, controls may include source verification, data-quality checks, controlled dataset versioning, and independent evaluation.

For operational data, controls may include authenticated ingestion, integrity checks, sensor validation, anomaly detection, and comparison with independent measurements where appropriate.

For model and configuration changes, controls may include access restrictions, version control, approval workflows, integrity verification, and documented rollback procedures.

#### 4.6 Protect Operational Data from Unauthorized Disclosure

Critical infrastructure data may reveal information that could help an attacker identify vulnerabilities, plan disruption, or target essential services.

Examples include:

- Industrial network diagrams.
- Equipment configurations.
- Asset inventories.
- Control-system versions.
- Remote-access arrangements.
- Operational procedures.
- Facility dependencies.
- Maintenance schedules.
- Security incident details.

The organization should classify this information and restrict its disclosure according to the potential consequences of exposure.

When using external AI services, the assessment should establish which data is transmitted, where it is processed, how it is retained, who can access it, and whether it may be used for purposes outside the approved business requirement.

Data minimization is especially important when an AI model can produce useful analytical results without receiving complete operational configurations or sensitive infrastructure details.

#### 4.7 Data Retention and Auditability

AI-enabled critical infrastructure may require historical records to investigate failures, demonstrate control effectiveness, and reconstruct operational decisions.

The organization should define retention requirements for:

- Operational measurements used by the AI.
- Model outputs and recommendations.
- Operator approvals and overrides.
- Executed commands.
- Model and configuration versions.
- Data-quality warnings.
- Security alerts.
- Incident evidence.
- Relevant change records.

Retention must be aligned with applicable legal obligations, sector-specific requirements, operational needs, and internal policy.

Longer retention is not automatically better. Unnecessary storage can increase confidentiality exposure and complicate data governance.

The objective is to retain sufficient trustworthy evidence for the required operational, security, safety, and audit purposes while protecting it from unauthorized access or modification.

**Core Principle:** AI governance in critical infrastructure requires verifiable data provenance, validated input quality, explicit trust boundaries, and protection against unauthorized data or configuration changes. The reliability of an AI-driven decision depends on the integrity of the complete data path, not only on the model.

### 5. Critical Infrastructure Risk Landscape and Threat Model

AI in critical infrastructure introduces risks that span cybersecurity, physical safety, operational resilience, data governance, and enterprise accountability.

The threat model must evaluate how a threat actor, defective component, unreliable data source, or unsafe design could cause the AI-enabled system to behave in a way that compromises essential services.

It must also consider accidental failures and operational conditions, not only deliberate attacks.

#### 5.1 Principal Risk Categories

| Risk category | Example scenario | Potential consequence |
|---|---|---|
| AI output manipulation | Malicious inputs cause misleading recommendations | Incorrect operational decisions |
| Sensor or data manipulation | False measurements influence the model | Unsafe or ineffective intervention |
| Unauthorized command execution | AI integration performs an unapproved control action | Equipment damage or service disruption |
| Excessive privileges | AI service account has broad OT permissions | Expanded impact following compromise |
| Prompt injection | Malicious operational text manipulates an AI agent | Unauthorized actions or information disclosure |
| Model degradation | Operating conditions differ from training data | Reduced detection or prediction reliability |
| AI service outage | External model or cloud dependency becomes unavailable | Loss of analytical capability |
| Integration compromise | Attacker abuses a gateway or API | Unauthorized access across trust boundaries |
| Data exfiltration | AI service transmits sensitive infrastructure information | Increased attack exposure |
| Unsafe automation | AI executes a recommendation without adequate validation | Physical or operational consequences |
| Monitoring failure | AI actions are not logged or detected | Delayed response and weak accountability |
| Supply-chain compromise | Vendor component or model is compromised | Loss of system integrity |
| Unauthorized change | Model, thresholds, or configuration are modified | Unexpected system behavior |
| Cascading failure | One AI-driven action affects dependent infrastructure | Wider disruption of essential services |
| Governance failure | No accountable owner or approval process exists | Uncontrolled deployment and unmanaged risk |

Risk assessments should identify the specific assets, threat scenarios, vulnerabilities, controls, owners, and consequences relevant to the deployment.

#### 5.2 Identify Critical Assets and Safety-Relevant Functions

The first step in threat modeling is to identify what must be protected.

Critical assets may include:

- Industrial control systems.
- Safety-related systems.
- Sensors and actuators.
- Operational historians.
- Network management platforms.
- Industrial gateways.
- AI inference services.
- Model repositories.
- Operational datasets.
- Identity and access systems.
- Remote-access infrastructure.
- Control configuration files.
- Essential business and operational processes.

The assessment should identify which assets are directly involved in physical control, which support decision-making, and which provide supporting information.

A compromise of an analytical reporting system may have a different consequence from a compromise of a command interface connected to industrial equipment.

The distinction should influence security requirements, monitoring intensity, testing depth, and authorization decisions.

#### 5.3 Threat Actors and Sources of Failure

Potential threat sources include:

**External attackers**

Attackers may attempt to compromise exposed services, steal credentials, manipulate data, disrupt availability, or gain access to operational networks.

**Insiders**

Employees, contractors, or privileged administrators may intentionally or accidentally misuse access, modify configurations, or disclose sensitive information.

**Compromised suppliers**

A third-party software component, AI provider, integration platform, or remote-support service may introduce vulnerabilities or provide an attacker with an access path.

**Malicious or unreliable data sources**

An external feed, sensor, maintenance record, or knowledge source may contain manipulated or incorrect information.

**Software and configuration defects**

Faulty integration logic, incorrect thresholds, weak validation, or unsafe retry behavior may cause harmful outcomes without a malicious actor.

**Model limitations**

A model may fail under unusual conditions, interpret ambiguous inputs incorrectly, or produce unreliable results outside its validated operating range.

**Environmental and operational changes**

Weather conditions, equipment degradation, process changes, and unusual demand patterns may cause the system to operate outside the conditions represented in its original evaluation.

A comprehensive threat model should include both adversarial and non-adversarial scenarios.

#### 5.4 AI-Specific Attack Scenarios

**Scenario A: Manipulated sensor measurements**

An attacker alters a measurement feed so that the AI interprets normal equipment behavior as a fault, or fails to identify an actual abnormal condition.

Potential consequences include unnecessary interventions, delayed maintenance, or failure to detect a hazardous condition.

Relevant controls include authenticated data ingestion, measurement validation, independent corroboration where feasible, anomaly detection, and clear operator escalation.

**Scenario B: Malicious instructions in operational records**

An AI assistant retrieves a support ticket, maintenance note, or operational document containing malicious instructions intended to influence its behavior.

If the agent can invoke operational tools, it may attempt an unauthorized action.

Relevant controls include treating retrieved content as untrusted data, separating instructions from retrieved content, limiting tool permissions, and enforcing independent action authorization.

**Scenario C: Compromised AI integration**

An attacker compromises an API gateway or service identity used by the AI platform.

The attacker may attempt to retrieve operational information, modify data, or invoke available tools.

Relevant controls include strong identity controls, network segmentation, scoped credentials, API authorization, monitoring, and tested credential revocation.

**Scenario D: Unsafe autonomous optimization**

An AI system changes an operating parameter to optimize energy consumption without adequately accounting for equipment limits or other operational constraints.

The result may be reduced resilience, equipment stress, or an unsafe operating condition.

Relevant controls include validated operating envelopes, independent interlocks, bounded action permissions, pre-deployment testing, and operational approval requirements.

**Scenario E: Cloud dependency failure**

An AI service becomes unavailable because of a provider outage, network failure, authentication problem, or service disruption.

If operators rely on the AI without an alternative operating process, essential workflows may be delayed.

Relevant controls include defined degraded modes, local operational procedures, dependency assessments, fallback mechanisms, and business continuity planning.

#### 5.5 Evaluate Risk Across the CIA Triad and Safety

The Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) triad remains relevant, but it does not fully describe the consequences of AI in critical infrastructure.

| Dimension | Critical infrastructure interpretation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Prevent unauthorized disclosure of sensitive infrastructure information | Exposure of industrial network diagrams |
| Integrity | Preserve the correctness of data, models, configurations, and commands | Manipulated sensor measurements |
| Availability | Maintain access to essential systems and operational services | AI platform outage affecting maintenance workflows |
| Safety | Prevent unacceptable harm to people, equipment, and the environment | Unsafe adjustment to an industrial process |
| Resilience | Maintain or restore essential functions during disruption | Continued safe operation when an AI dependency fails |
| Accountability | Establish who authorized, executed, and reviewed consequential actions | Inability to trace an AI-initiated configuration change |

These dimensions interact. An integrity failure may cause an unsafe action, while an availability incident may create pressure to bypass established controls.

The risk assessment should therefore evaluate the full chain of consequences rather than treating each dimension in isolation.

#### 5.6 Assess Cascading and Interdependency Risks

Critical infrastructure sectors depend on one another.

Examples include:

- Electricity networks depend on telecommunications for selected monitoring and coordination functions.
- Telecommunications networks depend on electrical power and backup systems.
- Water treatment facilities depend on electricity, industrial controls, and communications.
- Data centers depend on power, cooling, telecommunications, and physical security.
- Transportation systems depend on communications, energy, and digital operational services.

An AI-related incident in one environment may therefore affect other services.

For example, a faulty automated configuration change in a telecommunications network could disrupt communications used by another infrastructure operator. If the affected service supports operational monitoring or emergency coordination, the consequences may extend beyond the original system.

Threat modeling should identify critical dependencies, shared services, single points of failure, and potential cascading effects.

The organization should evaluate whether an AI service failure could affect multiple sites or essential functions and whether local controls can maintain safe operation during disruption.

#### 5.7 Risk Register Structure

A critical infrastructure AI risk register should capture the information needed to support treatment decisions and ongoing assurance.

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Risk ID | Provides a unique reference |
| Asset or process | Identifies the affected system or business function |
| Risk statement | Describes the cause, event, and consequence |
| Threat source | Identifies a plausible attacker or failure source |
| Vulnerability or weakness | Identifies the condition that enables the scenario |
| Inherent likelihood | Estimates likelihood before relevant controls |
| Inherent impact | Evaluates potential consequences before relevant controls |
| Existing controls | Documents safeguards already implemented |
| Control effectiveness | Records evidence concerning control operation |
| Residual likelihood | Estimates remaining likelihood after controls |
| Residual impact | Evaluates remaining consequences |

Risk ratings should follow the enterprise's approved methodology. The organization should not assume that a control reduces the impact of a catastrophic event merely because it lowers the likelihood of occurrence.

#### 5.8 Preliminary Risk Register Example

The following table illustrates how an enterprise might document initial risks for an AI system that analyzes operational telemetry and recommends actions to infrastructure operators.

| Risk ID | Risk scenario | Potential impact | Initial treatment direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-AI-01 | Manipulated sensor data produces misleading recommendations | Unsafe decisions or missed failures | Validate measurements and establish data provenance |
| CI-AI-02 | AI recommendation is applied without required operational review | Equipment damage or service disruption | Enforce approval requirements and operating limits |
| CI-AI-03 | AI service identity has excessive privileges | Unauthorized access or control changes | Apply least privilege and periodic access review |
| CI-AI-04 | AI integration is compromised through an exposed API | Data disclosure or unauthorized operations | Secure APIs, segment networks, and monitor access |
| CI-AI-05 | Model performance degrades after operating conditions change | Reduced prediction reliability | Monitor drift and revalidate against current conditions |

| CI-AI-07 | Sensitive operational data is sent to an unapproved external service | Confidentiality and security exposure | Restrict data transfer and assess provider controls |
| CI-AI-08 | AI-generated command bypasses established change controls | Unauthorized operational change | Enforce independent authorization and change governance |
| CI-AI-09 | Logs do not capture the complete action chain | Incomplete investigation and accountability | Implement end-to-end audit logging |
| CI-AI-10 | A failure affects interconnected infrastructure services | Cascading disruption | Assess dependencies and establish resilience measures |

These are preliminary scenarios, not completed risk ratings. The enterprise must assess likelihood, impact, control effectiveness, and residual risk using evidence from the actual deployment.

#### 5.9 Initial Governance Position

Before an AI system is authorized to influence critical infrastructure operations, the organization should establish:

- A clearly defined business purpose.
- An inventory record and accountable owner.
- A documented architecture and data-flow assessment.
- A threat model covering cybersecurity and operational consequences.
- An assessment of safety-related requirements.
- An approved data-access and integration model.
- A defined level of autonomy.
- Independent authorization for consequential actions.
- A monitoring and incident-response plan.
- A documented approach to residual-risk acceptance.

Where the organization cannot demonstrate that the system's operating boundaries are enforceable, it should restrict the deployment to a lower-risk mode or delay production use until the deficiencies are addressed.

### Core Principle

AI in critical infrastructure must be evaluated as part of a cyber-physical system whose failures can affect essential services, physical safety, and interconnected infrastructure. Effective governance combines AI risk management with cybersecurity, operational technology security, safety engineering, resilience planning, and accountable operational decision-making.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **IT vs. OT:** IT primarily processes information; OT monitors or controls physical processes. Their integration creates shared risk.
- **Advisory AI vs. autonomous control:** Providing a recommendation is fundamentally different from executing an action that changes a physical process.
- **Model accuracy vs. system safety:** A model's analytical performance does not establish that the complete system is safe to operate.
- **Data availability vs. data integrity:** Receiving data successfully does not prove that the measurements are correct or trustworthy.
- **Cybersecurity vs. operational safety:** Cybersecurity protects systems against digital threats; safety engineering addresses hazards and unacceptable physical consequences. Both may be necessary.
- **Prevention vs. detection:** Preventive controls restrict unsafe actions; detective controls identify suspicious or abnormal activity.
- **Local failure vs. cascading failure:** An incident may affect dependent services beyond the system where it originated.
- **Technical capability vs. operational authority:** The fact that an AI integration can execute a command does not mean it should be permitted to do so.
- **Initial approval vs. continuous assurance:** Deployment authorization must be supported by ongoing monitoring, reassessment, and controlled change management.

## Part 2: Threat Modeling, Cybersecurity Controls, Safety Assurance, and Risk Treatment

### 6. Threat Modeling for AI-Enabled Critical Infrastructure

Threat modeling is a structured process for identifying assets, threat actors, attack paths, vulnerabilities, and potential consequences before an AI-enabled system is deployed or materially changed.

In critical infrastructure, threat modeling must consider both digital compromise and the physical or operational consequences that may follow.

A conventional enterprise AI assessment might focus on unauthorized data disclosure, inaccurate outputs, or model misuse. A critical infrastructure assessment must also determine whether those failures could influence industrial control, disable monitoring, undermine protective mechanisms, or interrupt essential services.

The objective is to establish how an AI system could be compromised, fail, or behave outside its intended operating boundaries and to identify controls that prevent unacceptable consequences.

#### 6.1 Establish the Threat Modeling Scope

The scope should include the complete system rather than the AI model alone.

Relevant components may include:

- AI models and inference services.
- Training, validation, and operational datasets.
- Industrial sensors and telemetry sources.
- Data historians and operational data platforms.
- API gateways and integration services.
- Identity and access management systems.
- Enterprise IT and OT networks.
- SCADA, DCS, PLC, and HMI components.
- Remote administration and vendor support services.
- Model repositories and deployment pipelines.
- Monitoring, logging, and incident response systems.
- Operators, engineers, administrators, and external suppliers.
- Physical processes influenced by AI-generated recommendations or actions.

The assessment should identify which components are essential to the AI use case, which systems are merely supporting dependencies, and which assets must remain isolated from AI-related access.

**Example: Electricity Distribution Operator**

An operator deploys an AI system to analyze transformer telemetry and recommend maintenance actions.

The threat modeling scope includes the sensor network, operational historian, data ingestion pipeline, AI inference service, operator dashboard, identity provider, maintenance management platform, and relevant network boundaries.

If the AI system is advisory-only, the assessment focuses on the integrity of its recommendations, access to operational data, and the risk of operators relying on misleading outputs.

If the system can initiate switching operations, the assessment must additionally evaluate command authorization, control-system integration, protection mechanisms, operational safety, and the consequences of an unauthorized switching action.

The two deployments use similar analytical technology but require materially different assurance.

#### 6.2 Identify Security Objectives and Safety Constraints

Before enumerating threats, define what the system must protect and what it must never be permitted to do.

| Objective | Example requirement | Verification approach |
|---|---|---|
| Operational integrity | AI recommendations must use validated operational measurements | Data-quality testing and provenance review |
| Confidentiality | Only authorized users and services may access sensitive infrastructure information | Access reviews and security testing |
| Availability | Loss of the AI service must not cause an unsafe operational state | Failure injection and fallback testing |
| Authorization | AI actions must be restricted to explicitly approved functions | Permission testing and command-path review |
| Safety | AI must not bypass mandatory protective mechanisms | Safety assessment and independent control verification |
| Traceability | Consequential recommendations and actions must be reconstructable | Audit-log testing |
| Resilience | Essential operations must remain supportable during AI service disruption | Continuity exercises and degraded-mode testing |
| Accountability | Each material decision must have an identifiable owner or approval authority | Governance review and evidence inspection |

Security objectives should be converted into measurable requirements wherever practical.

For example, the requirement that an AI system must not initiate an unauthorized industrial command should be supported by technical enforcement and testing. A policy statement alone does not demonstrate that the command path is adequately protected.

#### 6.3 Identify Entry Points and Trust Boundaries

An entry point is a location through which an attacker, external system, or untrusted input can interact with the environment.

Common entry points include:

- External APIs.
- Remote-access gateways.
- Vendor maintenance connections.
- Cloud management interfaces.
- Industrial data gateways.
- Identity and authentication services.
- Software update mechanisms.
- Model deployment pipelines.
- Operational dashboards.
- Data import processes.
- Removable media and engineering workstations.
- Third-party integrations.

Each entry point should be evaluated for exposure, authentication, authorization, input validation, monitoring, and potential reach into critical assets.

The assessment should distinguish an exposed interface from a genuine attack path. A plausible attack path requires a sequence of reachable components, weaknesses, privileges, or trust relationships that could allow the attacker to achieve an objective.

#### 6.4 Construct Attack Paths

Consider an AI platform that receives operational telemetry through a gateway and can submit recommendations to an operator dashboard.

A possible attack path is:

```text
ATTACKER
   |
   v
Compromises an exposed integration service
   |
   v
Manipulates operational data
   |
   v
AI model receives misleading measurements
   |
   v
AI generates an incorrect recommendation
   |
   v
Operator receives the recommendation
   |
   v
Operational decision is influenced
   |
   v
Potential equipment or service impact
```

This is a hypothetical scenario, not an assertion that every AI deployment is vulnerable to this sequence.

The threat model must establish whether the integration service is reachable, whether its integrity controls can be bypassed, whether the AI uses the manipulated data, and whether the resulting recommendation could influence a consequential decision.

Potential interruption points include:

- Strong authentication and authorization at the integration layer.
- Data provenance checks.
- Sensor validation and independent corroboration.
- Model output validation.
- Clear uncertainty communication.
- Human review.
- Independent operational safety constraints.
- Monitoring and incident escalation.

The objective is not merely to prevent the initial compromise. It is also to prevent a compromised component from propagating its effects through the system.

#### 6.5 Use a Structured Threat Classification

A useful threat classification should cover traditional cybersecurity threats, AI-specific attacks, operational failures, and safety-relevant scenarios.

| Threat category | Example | Primary concern |
|---|---|---|
| Spoofing | Forged identity or sensor source | False trust in a user, service, or measurement |
| Tampering | Modification of data, models, or configurations | Loss of integrity |
| Repudiation | Missing or incomplete records of consequential actions | Inability to establish accountability |
| Information disclosure | Exposure of infrastructure diagrams or sensitive telemetry | Increased attack exposure |
| Denial of service | Disruption of an AI endpoint or data pipeline | Loss of analytical capability |
| Elevation of privilege | AI service account gains unauthorized administrative access | Expanded compromise impact |
| Data poisoning | Manipulated training or operational data | Degraded model behavior |
| Prompt injection | Malicious instructions embedded in retrieved operational content | Unauthorized tool use or data disclosure |
| Model evasion | Inputs designed to cause incorrect model classifications | Reduced detection reliability |
| Model or artifact tampering | Unauthorized modification of a model or deployment package | Untrusted AI behavior |
| Unsafe automation | AI initiates an action outside its validated operating limits | Physical or operational harm |
| Dependency failure | Cloud service, identity provider, or network becomes unavailable | Loss of essential supporting capability |
| Operational drift | Equipment or process changes invalidate prior assumptions | Degraded prediction or recommendation quality |
| Insider misuse | Authorized personnel misuse privileged access | Unauthorized changes or disclosure |
| Supply-chain compromise | Malicious or vulnerable third-party component enters the system | Loss of trust in a dependency |

The classification provides coverage, but it does not replace scenario-specific analysis. Each material threat must be connected to actual system components, credible attack paths, consequences, and control requirements.

#### 6.6 Prioritize Threats by Consequence and Exploitability

Threat prioritization should consider both the likelihood of a scenario and the magnitude of its consequences.

Relevant factors include:

- Accessibility of the affected component.
- Required attacker privileges.
- Complexity of exploitation.
- Availability of known weaknesses.
- Effectiveness of existing preventive controls.
- Ability to detect and contain the incident.
- Potential physical consequences.
- Number of services or facilities affected.
- Recovery time and operational dependencies.
- Regulatory and contractual obligations.

For critical infrastructure, a low-frequency event may still require strong treatment if the potential consequences are severe.

The organization should also avoid assuming that a low likelihood rating eliminates the need for treatment. Some safety and security requirements may be mandatory regardless of a calculated risk score.

#### 6.7 Threat Modeling Deliverables

A completed threat modeling exercise should produce evidence that can be used by security, engineering, operations, and governance teams.

| Deliverable | Purpose |
|---|---|
| System context diagram | Defines the system boundary and external dependencies |
| Data-flow diagram | Shows information movement and trust boundaries |
| Asset inventory | Identifies systems and information requiring protection |
| Threat scenario register | Records credible threats and their consequences |
| Attack-path analysis | Explains how a threat could affect the system |
| Safety constraint register | Defines prohibited or restricted operational behaviors |
| Control mapping | Connects threats to required safeguards |
| Risk treatment plan | Assigns remediation activities and accountable owners |
| Residual-risk assessment | Records remaining exposure after treatment |
| Test and validation plan | Defines how control effectiveness will be verified |
| Approval record | Documents the decision to proceed, restrict, defer, or reject deployment |

**Core Principle:** Threat modeling must examine the complete path from an initial threat to a possible operational consequence. Effective controls interrupt that path at multiple independent points rather than relying exclusively on the AI model to behave correctly.

### 7. Cybersecurity Control Architecture for AI in Critical Infrastructure

A critical infrastructure AI deployment requires layered security controls across the AI lifecycle, data pipeline, integration architecture, enterprise environment, and OT environment.

No single control can adequately address every threat.

For example, strong model validation cannot prevent an attacker from compromising an API gateway, while network segmentation alone cannot prevent an authorized operator from acting on a misleading recommendation.

The control architecture must combine preventive, detective, responsive, and recovery capabilities.

#### 7.1 Apply Defense in Depth

Defense in depth means establishing multiple complementary security layers so that the failure of one control does not automatically expose the entire system.

A reference control architecture may include:

```text
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| GOVERNANCE AND RISK MANAGEMENT                          |
| Policies | Risk Ownership | Approvals | Audit Evidence   |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| IDENTITY AND PRIVILEGED ACCESS                          |
| MFA | Service Identity | Least Privilege | Access Review |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| NETWORK AND INTEGRATION SECURITY                        |
| Segmentation | Firewalls | API Security | Egress Control |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| DATA AND MODEL SECURITY                                 |
| Provenance | Validation | Artifact Integrity | Testing   |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| AI APPLICATION SECURITY                                 |
| Output Validation | Tool Restrictions | Abuse Detection  |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| OPERATIONAL AUTHORIZATION AND SAFETY                     |
| Approved Commands | Independent Checks | Interlocks      |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
                           |
                           v
+---------------------------------------------------------+
| MONITORING, RESPONSE, AND RECOVERY                       |
| Logging | Alerting | Isolation | Fallback | Restoration  |
+---------------------------------------------------------+
```

The layers are complementary. Governance defines required outcomes, identity controls restrict access, integration controls limit reachability, AI security controls reduce model-specific risks, operational authorization protects consequential actions, and monitoring supports detection and recovery.

#### 7.2 Identity and Access Management

Identity and access management must cover human users, machine identities, AI services, integration components, and administrative accounts.

Recommended controls include:

- Unique identities for individual services.
- Strong authentication for administrative access.
- Multi-factor authentication where appropriate.
- Role-based or attribute-based access controls.
- Least-privilege permissions.
- Separation of administrative and operational duties.
- Time-limited privileged access where feasible.
- Periodic access recertification.
- Credential rotation and secure secret storage.
- Immediate revocation of compromised credentials.
- Monitoring of privileged activity.

An AI service should not use a shared administrator account simply because several components require access to operational data.

Permissions should be separated by function. For example, a service that reads telemetry should not automatically be permitted to modify control configurations.

Where an AI agent can invoke tools, each tool should have a defined permission boundary. The agent should not be allowed to expand its own privileges by changing its configuration, obtaining new credentials, or calling an unrestricted administrative API.

**Governance test:** Can the organization demonstrate that the AI system has only the permissions necessary for its approved function and that those permissions can be revoked promptly?

#### 7.3 Network Segmentation and OT Protection

Network segmentation restricts communication between systems according to approved operational and security requirements.

Critical infrastructure organizations should consider:

- Separation of corporate IT and OT networks.
- Controlled communication between security zones.
- Industrial firewalls and protocol-aware filtering where appropriate.
- Restricted administrative pathways.
- Controlled vendor remote access.
- Explicitly authorized data transfer between zones.
- Restrictions on unnecessary outbound connections.
- Monitoring of cross-zone traffic.
- Separate management networks where justified.
- Isolation procedures for compromised components.

The architecture should be based on the actual industrial environment and applicable OT security requirements.

AI services hosted in cloud environments should not receive direct access to industrial control networks unless that access is explicitly justified, designed, approved, and secured.

Where feasible, operational data should be exported through controlled mechanisms rather than granting broad inbound connectivity from enterprise or cloud environments.

Segmentation should also consider shared dependencies. A compromised identity provider, remote-access service, or management platform may undermine otherwise well-separated networks.

#### 7.4 Secure API and Integration Interfaces

AI-enabled infrastructure often depends on APIs and data gateways to connect operational data sources with analytics services.

Controls should include:

- Strong client and service authentication.
- Authorization for each sensitive operation.
- Input validation and schema enforcement.
- Restrictions on allowed commands and parameters.
- Rate limiting and abuse detection.
- Secure transport.
- Protection of credentials and secrets.
- Logging of requests and consequential responses.
- Restrictions on destinations and outbound communication.
- Version management and controlled API changes.
- Safe error handling.
- Testing for authorization bypass and injection weaknesses.

API authorization should be action-specific.

An integration service that can retrieve equipment telemetry should not automatically be allowed to issue control commands through the same interface.

Where commands are necessary, the organization should enforce an explicit allowlist of permitted actions and validate parameters against approved operating constraints.

#### 7.5 AI Model and Artifact Integrity

AI model integrity covers the trustworthiness of model artifacts, configurations, associated code, and deployment processes.

Recommended controls include:

- Approved model registries.
- Version control for model artifacts and configurations.
- Access restrictions on model modification.
- Integrity verification for deployed artifacts.
- Documented model provenance.
- Controlled model promotion between environments.
- Independent evaluation before production release.
- Protection of training and validation datasets.
- Recorded approval of material model changes.
- Rollback capability.
- Monitoring for unexpected model or configuration changes.

Model provenance should record the model's origin, version, intended purpose, relevant evaluation evidence, and dependencies.

Where a third-party model is used, the organization should evaluate available supplier documentation and assurance evidence. If important model details are unavailable, the resulting uncertainty should be documented and reflected in the deployment decision.

A model update must not be treated as a routine software patch when it can materially change behavior in a safety-relevant or high-consequence workflow.

#### 7.6 Secure the AI Data Pipeline

The data pipeline should protect the integrity and reliability of information used by the model.

Relevant controls include:

- Approved source inventories.
- Source authentication where supported.
- Data-quality validation.
- Timestamp and unit normalization.
- Completeness checks.
- Detection of abnormal measurements.
- Controlled data transformations.
- Versioned training and evaluation datasets.
- Protection against unauthorized dataset changes.
- Restrictions on data exports.
- Monitoring of ingestion failures.
- Defined handling of missing or invalid data.

For consequential use cases, the system should have an explicit policy for data-quality failures.

Possible responses include withholding a recommendation, displaying an uncertainty warning, requesting operator review, or switching to a validated fallback process.

The correct response depends on the operating context. The system should not automatically substitute an assumed value for a missing measurement unless that behavior has been validated and approved for the intended use.

#### 7.7 Validate AI Outputs Before Operational Use

AI output validation evaluates whether the model's result is suitable for the intended workflow.

Depending on the application, controls may include:

- Range checks.
- Type and format validation.
- Consistency checks against known constraints.
- Confidence or uncertainty thresholds.
- Comparison with independent measurements.
- Detection of contradictory recommendations.
- Restrictions on unsupported actions.
- Human review for high-consequence recommendations.
- Rejection of incomplete or malformed outputs.

For example, an AI model may recommend a maintenance priority based on equipment telemetry. The recommendation should be evaluated against approved maintenance criteria before it becomes an operational work order.

For an AI system proposing control changes, validation must be substantially more stringent and must account for the actual process, safety constraints, and authorization requirements.

A model's confidence score should not be treated as proof of correctness. Confidence estimates can be poorly calibrated, especially when the system encounters unfamiliar conditions.

#### 7.8 Tool and Action Restrictions for AI Agents

An AI agent may retrieve data, invoke APIs, create work orders, change configurations, or trigger workflows.

Its risk depends not only on what it can generate but also on what it is authorized to do.

The organization should define:

- An approved list of available tools.
- The exact functions each tool can perform.
- The resources each tool can access.
- Required approval for consequential actions.
- Parameter validation rules.
- Execution limits.
- Rate and frequency limits where appropriate.
- Prohibited actions.
- Logging requirements.
- Emergency suspension mechanisms.

A practical permission model might distinguish the following:

| Agent capability | Example | Suggested restriction |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieve data | Read approved equipment telemetry | Read-only scope limited to relevant assets |
| Analyze data | Identify unusual equipment behavior | No direct control authority |
| Create a draft | Prepare a maintenance recommendation | Draft remains unapproved until reviewed |
| Create a work order | Submit a maintenance request | Restrict to approved categories and workflow rules |
| Propose a configuration change | Prepare a change request | Require independent validation and change approval |
| Execute an operational action | Issue an approved command | Permit only when specifically justified and protected by appropriate authorization and safety mechanisms |
| Modify its own permissions | Expand available tools or credentials | Prohibit self-authorized privilege expansion |

The system should not rely on a natural-language instruction such as "do not make unsafe changes" as its primary security boundary.

Authorization must be enforced by the surrounding system.

#### 7.9 Logging and Security Monitoring

Monitoring must provide sufficient evidence to identify abnormal behavior, investigate incidents, and reconstruct consequential decisions.

Relevant events include:

- User and service authentication.
- Privileged access.
- Data ingestion failures.
- Unusual data-quality conditions.
- Model version changes.
- Configuration changes.
- AI recommendations.
- Tool invocations.
- Approval and rejection events.
- Executed commands.
- Denied actions.
- Failed authorization attempts.
- Network communication anomalies.
- Changes in model performance.
- AI service availability events.

Logs should be protected from unauthorized alteration and retained according to approved requirements.

Monitoring should also consider the relationship between AI activity and OT events. For example, a sudden increase in AI-generated recommendations combined with unusual API requests may warrant investigation.

Logging must be designed carefully to avoid unnecessarily collecting sensitive operational data or retaining information beyond legitimate requirements.

#### 7.10 Control Mapping to Security Objectives

| Security objective | Example controls | Evidence of effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Prevent unauthorized access | Strong authentication, least privilege, service identity controls | Access-test results and permission reviews |
| Protect network boundaries | Segmentation, firewall rules, restricted pathways | Network diagrams and connectivity testing |
| Protect model integrity | Artifact controls, approved registries, version management | Model provenance and deployment records |
| Protect data integrity | Source validation, schema checks, integrity monitoring | Data-quality reports and test results |
| Prevent unauthorized actions | Tool restrictions, independent authorization, command allowlists | Negative testing and approval records |
| Detect abnormal behavior | Centralized logging, alerting, behavior monitoring | Detection tests and incident records |
| Preserve availability | Redundancy, fallback procedures, dependency controls | Failover and continuity test results |
| Support investigation | Tamper-resistant audit records and traceability | Reconstruction exercises |
| Preserve safety constraints | Independent protection mechanisms and safety validation | Safety assurance evidence |
| Support controlled change | Approval workflows, regression testing, rollback | Change records and validation results |

**Core Principle:** Defense in depth must extend from data sources to AI services and operational control boundaries. Security depends on enforceable permissions, validated outputs, independent authorization, and evidence that controls work under realistic failure and attack conditions.

### 8. AI Safety Assurance and Operational Boundaries

Cybersecurity controls protect the AI-enabled environment from malicious activity and unauthorized access. Safety assurance addresses whether the system can operate without creating unacceptable hazards.

The two disciplines overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

An AI system can be free from known cybersecurity vulnerabilities and still generate unsafe recommendations. Conversely, a system with well-designed safety constraints may still expose sensitive infrastructure data or be vulnerable to cyberattack.

A complete assessment must address both.

#### 8.1 Distinguish Safety, Security, and Reliability

| Discipline | Primary question | Example concern |
|---|---|---|
| Cybersecurity | Can an unauthorized party compromise or misuse the system? | An attacker manipulates sensor data |
| Functional safety | Can the system contribute to a hazardous condition? | A control action exceeds a safe operating boundary |
| Reliability engineering | Can the system perform its intended function consistently? | A model's predictions become unreliable |
| Operational resilience | Can essential services continue or recover during disruption? | An AI service outage interrupts an important workflow |
| AI governance | Is the system used for an approved purpose with accountable oversight? | A business unit deploys an unapproved autonomous capability |

These disciplines should coordinate their assessments and share relevant evidence.

For example, a cybersecurity threat that manipulates operational measurements may also create a safety hazard. The cybersecurity assessment identifies the attack path, while the safety assessment evaluates the resulting hazardous condition and required protective measures.

#### 8.2 Define an Operational Design Domain

An Operational Design Domain (ODD) defines the conditions under which a system is intended and validated to operate.

For AI in critical infrastructure, the ODD may include:

- Equipment types and supported configurations.
- Permitted operating ranges.
- Environmental conditions.
- Expected sensor availability.
- Supported data formats.
- Network and latency assumptions.
- Maximum permitted action scope.
- Approved operating modes.
- Conditions requiring human intervention.
- Conditions under which the AI must stop making recommendations or executing actions.

The ODD should be documented and linked to validation evidence.

**Example**

An AI model is validated to identify anomalies in a specific class of industrial pumps under defined operating conditions.

The organization later proposes to use the model for a different pump type with different operating characteristics.

The model's prior validation does not automatically establish suitability for the new environment.

The organization should assess the change, evaluate relevant failure modes, conduct appropriate testing, and obtain the required approval before extending the system's use.

#### 8.3 Establish Safe Operating Boundaries

Safe operating boundaries define conditions that the AI system must not exceed or bypass.

Depending on the use case, they may include:

- Approved parameter ranges.
- Prohibited command types.
- Maximum change magnitude.
- Restricted operating modes.
- Required operator approvals.
- Conditions requiring an immediate stop.
- Maximum duration of autonomous operation.
- Restrictions on actions during abnormal conditions.
- Mandatory escalation for uncertain or contradictory results.

These boundaries should be enforced through system design, authorization mechanisms, and independent controls where appropriate.

They should not depend solely on the model's ability to interpret instructions correctly.

For example, an AI model may recommend changing a process parameter. A separate enforcement mechanism should verify that the proposed value is permitted before the action can proceed.

#### 8.4 Human Oversight Must Be Meaningful

Human oversight is effective only when the responsible person has sufficient information, authority, time, and competence to evaluate the AI's output.

A nominal approval step is inadequate if operators are expected to approve numerous complex recommendations without understanding their consequences.

A meaningful oversight process should define:

- Which decisions require human review.
- What evidence the reviewer receives.
- How uncertainty is communicated.
- Whether the reviewer can reject the recommendation.
- Whether the reviewer can stop the workflow.
- How disagreement is recorded.
- What happens when no qualified reviewer is available.
- How operator workload affects the reliability of review.

The organization should also assess automation bias: the tendency for people to rely excessively on automated recommendations, even when contradictory evidence exists.

Training should help operators understand the system's capabilities and limitations, recognize anomalous outputs, and follow established operational procedures.

#### 8.5 Define Safe Degraded Modes

A degraded mode is a defined operating state used when the AI system or one of its dependencies cannot be trusted or is unavailable.

Potential triggers include:

- AI service outage.
- Loss of required telemetry.
- Unreliable sensor data.
- Model integrity alert.
- Unexpected model behavior.
- Compromised credentials.
- Suspicious tool activity.
- Failed output validation.
- Loss of required network connectivity.
- Failure of an essential dependency.

The response depends on the function of the AI system.

For an advisory maintenance model, degraded operation may mean that recommendations are suspended while existing maintenance procedures continue.

For a system involved in real-time operational optimization, degraded operation may require returning control to a previously validated operating mode.

For a safety-related function, the required response must be established through the applicable safety engineering process.

The organization must not assume that the AI should always continue operating when its inputs, dependencies, or outputs become unreliable.

#### 8.6 Fail-Safe and Fail-Operational Design

These terms describe different design objectives.

**Fail-safe behavior** aims to transition the system to a condition that prevents unacceptable harm when a failure occurs.

**Fail-operational behavior** aims to preserve required functionality despite a failure, often through redundancy or alternative mechanisms.

The appropriate objective depends on the application.

A nonessential analytics service may be safely disabled when its integrity is uncertain. A critical control process may require continued operation through an independently validated control mechanism rather than simply shutting down.

Consequently, "turn the AI off" is not a complete safety strategy.

The organization must determine how the surrounding process behaves when AI is unavailable, untrusted, or unable to produce a valid result.

#### 8.7 Safety Assurance Evidence

Safety-related assurance should be proportionate to the system's role and consequences.

Relevant evidence may include:

- Hazard identification.
- Safety requirements.
- System architecture and safety boundaries.
- Failure-mode analysis.
- Validation and verification results.
- Testing under abnormal operating conditions.
- Evidence of independent protection mechanisms.
- Human-factors assessment.
- Degraded-mode procedures.
- Incident and recovery procedures.
- Change-control records.
- Formal approval by authorized engineering and safety personnel.

Applicable standards and assurance methods depend on the sector, system function, and jurisdiction. The organization should determine which requirements apply rather than assuming that one general AI standard is sufficient for every critical infrastructure use case.

#### 8.8 Example: AI-Assisted Industrial Pump Optimization

An industrial operator introduces AI to optimize pump performance.

The model analyzes operating measurements and recommends adjustments intended to reduce energy consumption.

A risk assessment identifies the possibility that an incorrect recommendation could cause operation outside approved equipment limits.

The organization establishes the following controls:

1. The model receives only approved telemetry.
2. Inputs are checked for completeness and validity.
3. Recommendations are compared against documented operating limits.
4. Consequential changes require authorized review.
5. Control commands are restricted to an approved set of operations.
6. Independent protection mechanisms remain in place.
7. Abnormal or uncertain conditions trigger a defined degraded mode.
8. Model performance and operating conditions are monitored.
9. Changes to the model or operating environment require reassessment.
10. Operators can suspend AI-assisted optimization and use the approved fallback process.

The organization then tests both normal operation and failure scenarios before authorizing production use.

Testing includes missing sensor measurements, inconsistent values, model-service outages, unauthorized requests, invalid recommendations, and changes to equipment configuration.

The objective is not to prove that the model never makes mistakes. It is to demonstrate that foreseeable errors and failures are managed within the approved risk tolerance and safety constraints.

**Core Principle:** Safety assurance must evaluate the complete AI-enabled process, including its inputs, outputs, human interactions, control interfaces, and failure behavior. An AI system should operate only within a documented and validated domain, with enforceable boundaries and a defined response when those boundaries cannot be trusted.

### 9. Risk Treatment and Residual Risk Acceptance

Risk treatment determines what the organization will do about identified risks and whether the remaining exposure is acceptable.

For AI in critical infrastructure, treatment decisions must account for cybersecurity, operational continuity, safety, regulatory requirements, and the potential consequences of failure.

A risk register that identifies serious weaknesses but does not assign treatment actions, owners, deadlines, and acceptance authority is not an effective risk management mechanism.

#### 9.1 Select an Appropriate Risk Treatment Strategy

Organizations commonly use four broad risk treatment strategies.

| Strategy | Meaning | Critical infrastructure example |
|---|---|---|
| Avoid | Do not undertake the activity that creates unacceptable exposure | Reject a proposed AI integration that requires an unjustified direct connection to a safety-critical control system |
| Mitigate | Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact | Introduce data validation, least privilege, independent authorization, and monitoring |
| Transfer or share | Allocate defined aspects of risk through insurance, contracts, or supplier arrangements | Establish contractual incident notification and supplier security obligations |
| Accept | Formally retain the remaining risk within approved authority and applicable requirements | Accept a documented residual risk for a low-consequence advisory use case |

Risk transfer does not eliminate the organization's responsibility for the safety and continuity of its critical services.

Similarly, risk acceptance cannot override mandatory legal obligations, safety requirements, or controls that the organization is required to implement.

#### 9.2 Distinguish Inherent and Residual Risk

**Inherent risk** is the exposure before considering the effect of the relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the exposure that remains after the controls have been implemented and their effectiveness has been assessed.

A simplified assessment might look like this:

| Risk factor | Initial assessment | Control treatment | Remaining concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manipulated sensor data | High consequence if it influences a critical recommendation | Source validation, anomaly detection, and independent checks | Some manipulation may remain difficult to detect |
| Unauthorized command | Potentially severe operational impact | Least privilege, command allowlists, independent authorization, and safety constraints | Misconfiguration or control failure remains possible |
| AI service outage | Loss of analytical capability | Fallback procedures and alternative operating methods | Reduced analytical efficiency during disruption |
| Model degradation | Reduced prediction reliability | Performance monitoring and revalidation | Rare operating conditions may remain insufficiently represented |
| Data disclosure | Exposure of sensitive operational information | Data minimization, access control, and egress restrictions | Authorized access or supplier exposure may remain |

The ratings are illustrative. Actual ratings must be determined using the organization's approved risk methodology and deployment-specific evidence.

Controls should not automatically be credited as effective simply because they appear in a design document or policy. Their implementation and operation must be verified.

#### 9.3 Establish Risk Acceptance Authority

Risk acceptance should be assigned to an individual or governance body with sufficient authority and accountability.

The appropriate authority depends on:

- The severity of the potential consequences.
- The operational function affected.
- The organization's risk appetite.
- Sector-specific requirements.
- The relevant safety responsibilities.
- The scope of the proposed AI deployment.
- The nature of the residual risk.

A business sponsor should not automatically be allowed to accept risks affecting systems outside that sponsor's operational authority.

For example, a department head responsible for an AI analytics project may be able to accept a limited business-process risk but may lack authority to approve residual risk affecting industrial safety or essential service continuity.

The organization should establish escalation thresholds for risks that require senior executive, operational, cybersecurity, or safety approval.

#### 9.4 Apply Non-Negotiable Control Requirements

Some requirements should be treated as mandatory gates rather than optional risk reductions.

Examples may include:

- Compliance with applicable legal requirements.
- Required safety protections.
- Authorized access to control systems.
- Mandatory industrial operating procedures.
- Required incident-reporting obligations.
- Protection of safety-critical functions.
- Required engineering approval for specified changes.
- Contractual or regulatory security requirements.

The precise requirements depend on the deployment and jurisdiction.

A favorable business case or high model accuracy does not justify bypassing a mandatory control.

#### 9.5 Define Risk Treatment Plans

A risk treatment plan should be specific enough to support implementation and verification.

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Risk ID | CI-AI-02 |
| Risk description | AI integration may initiate an unauthorized operational command |
| Treatment decision | Mitigate |
| Required action | Enforce an approved command allowlist and independent authorization |
| Control owner | OT Security Lead |
| Implementation owner | Industrial Integration Team |
| Supporting stakeholders | AI Engineering, Operations, Safety Engineering |
| Target date | Approved project milestone |
| Verification method | Attempt unauthorized and out-of-scope commands in a controlled test environment |
| Required evidence | Test results, configuration records, approval workflow evidence |
| Residual-risk assessment | Completed after control verification |
| Acceptance authority | Designated authority under the enterprise risk and safety governance model |
| Review trigger | Material architecture change, incident, control failure, or new threat |

Responsibilities should be explicit. A risk owner is accountable for ensuring the risk is managed, while a control owner is responsible for the design or operation of a particular control.

These roles may be held by different people.

#### 9.6 Establish Risk-Based Release Gates

A release gate determines whether an AI system is permitted to proceed to the next deployment stage.

A critical infrastructure deployment may use the following stages:

```text
USE CASE PROPOSAL
        |
        v
BUSINESS AND RISK SCREENING
        |
        v
ARCHITECTURE AND THREAT ASSESSMENT
        |
        v
CYBERSECURITY AND SAFETY REQUIREMENTS
        |
        v
CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
        |
        v
VALIDATION AND SECURITY TESTING
        |
        v
OPERATIONAL READINESS REVIEW
        |
        v
FORMAL DEPLOYMENT DECISION
        |
        v
CONTROLLED PRODUCTION OPERATION
        |
        v
CONTINUOUS MONITORING AND REASSESSMENT
```

Each gate should have documented entry criteria, evidence requirements, approval authority, and exit conditions.

A deployment should be deferred or restricted if material security or safety requirements remain unverified.

#### 9.7 Define Conditions for Suspension or Rollback

The organization should determine in advance when an AI system must be suspended, restricted, or returned to a previously validated state.

Potential triggers include:

- Evidence of model or artifact tampering.
- Unauthorized access to operational interfaces.
- Repeated invalid or contradictory outputs.
- Failure of a required protective control.
- Loss of critical data sources.
- Significant unexplained performance degradation.
- An operational incident linked to AI recommendations or actions.
- A material change outside the validated operating domain.
- Failure of required audit or monitoring mechanisms.
- A new threat that invalidates important risk assumptions.

Suspension should not itself create an unmanaged operational hazard. The response must follow the approved degraded-mode and operational continuity procedures.

Where the AI system is advisory-only, suspension may simply remove the recommendations from the operator workflow. Where it is integrated into an operational process, transition arrangements may be more complex.

#### 9.8 Document the Residual-Risk Decision

The final decision should record:

- The intended use and approved operating scope.
- The principal risks identified.
- The controls implemented.
- The evidence supporting control effectiveness.
- The remaining uncertainties.
- The residual-risk rating.
- The applicable acceptance authority.
- Any restrictions or conditions of approval.
- Monitoring and reporting requirements.
- Reassessment triggers.
- The date of approval and review requirements.

The decision should distinguish between risks that are accepted, risks that are conditionally tolerated while remediation proceeds, and risks that remain unacceptable.

Conditional approval should have clear limitations, deadlines, and escalation rules. It should not become a mechanism for indefinitely postponing mandatory controls.

**Core Principle:** Risk treatment is complete only when the organization has implemented and verified the required controls, assessed the remaining exposure, and obtained approval from the appropriate authority. For critical infrastructure, safety and mandatory obligations cannot be replaced by informal risk acceptance.

### 10. Framework Alignment and Governance Requirements

AI in critical infrastructure may be subject to several overlapping governance and security frameworks.

The purpose of framework alignment is to establish a coherent set of requirements and evidence rather than operating multiple disconnected compliance programs.

Framework selection must be based on the infrastructure sector, jurisdiction, system function, organizational obligations, and relevant contractual requirements.

#### 10.1 NIST Cybersecurity Framework

The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0** organizes cybersecurity risk management around six functions:

- Govern.
- Identify.
- Protect.
- Detect.
- Respond.
- Recover.

These functions can support the governance of AI-enabled critical infrastructure.

| CSF function | Critical infrastructure AI application |
|---|---|
| Govern | Assign accountability for AI-related cybersecurity risk and establish risk policies |
| Identify | Inventory AI components, OT assets, dependencies, and critical data |
| Protect | Implement identity controls, segmentation, secure configuration, and data protection |
| Detect | Monitor abnormal AI activity, unauthorized changes, and suspicious network behavior |
| Respond | Coordinate cybersecurity incident response with OT and operational teams |
| Recover | Restore trusted AI services and maintain essential operations through approved recovery procedures |

The CSF provides an organizing framework for cybersecurity risk management. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that a specific AI system is safe for a particular industrial process.

Organizations should map applicable CSF outcomes to actual controls, responsible owners, and evidence.

#### 10.2 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)** provides a structure for identifying and managing risks associated with AI systems.

Its four core functions are:

- Govern.
- Map.
- Measure.
- Manage.

For critical infrastructure, these functions can be applied as follows:

| AI RMF function | Example application |
|---|---|
| Govern | Establish AI oversight, accountability, risk tolerance, and lifecycle controls |
| Map | Document the use case, affected stakeholders, operating context, system boundaries, and potential harms |
| Measure | Evaluate model performance, robustness, uncertainty, security, and relevant failure scenarios |
| Manage | Prioritize risks, implement treatments, monitor performance, and reassess the system |

AI RMF activities should be coordinated with operational safety and OT cybersecurity activities.

For example, measuring model performance is important, but it must be complemented by testing whether the complete workflow behaves appropriately when the model produces an incorrect recommendation.

#### 10.3 IEC 62443 Industrial Automation and Control Systems Security

The **IEC 62443** series addresses cybersecurity for industrial automation and control systems.

It provides concepts and requirements relevant to industrial asset owners, system integrators, product suppliers, and service providers.

Potentially relevant areas include:

- Security risk assessment.
- System security requirements.
- Zones and conduits.
- Secure system design.
- Component security.
- Access control.
- System integrity.
- Restricted data flow.
- Availability and resilience.
- Security maintenance and lifecycle management.

For AI-enabled OT, IEC 62443 concepts can support the design of trust boundaries between enterprise, AI, and industrial environments.

For example, a cloud AI service may be treated as an external system whose communication with OT must be explicitly controlled. The permitted data flows, administrative pathways, and command interfaces should be defined according to the assessed risks.

The exact standards and requirements applicable to a deployment depend on its architecture and contractual or regulatory context.

#### 10.4 ISO/IEC 27001 and Information Security Management

**ISO/IEC 27001** establishes requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

Its risk-based management approach can support the governance of AI platforms, operational information, supplier relationships, access controls, incident management, and security assurance.

Relevant activities may include:

- Defining the ISMS scope.
- Assessing information security risks.
- Selecting and implementing risk treatments.
- Establishing control ownership.
- Managing suppliers.
- Monitoring control effectiveness.
- Conducting internal audits.
- Reviewing management performance.
- Maintaining continual improvement.

However, an ISO/IEC 27001-certified organization should not automatically assume that every AI or OT risk has been addressed.

The organization must determine whether the relevant AI-enabled critical infrastructure assets, processes, interfaces, and operational risks fall within the ISMS scope and whether the implemented controls adequately address them.

#### 10.5 ISO/IEC 42001 and AI Management Systems

**ISO/IEC 42001** specifies requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

It can support organizational governance of AI across its lifecycle, including responsibilities, risk management, operational controls, evaluation, monitoring, and continual improvement.

For critical infrastructure, an AI management system can help establish:

- AI use-case approval.
- Defined responsibilities.
- AI risk assessment.
- Lifecycle governance.
- Supplier and third-party oversight.
- Data and model management.
- Performance monitoring.
- Documentation and evidence.
- Corrective actions.
- Periodic management review.

ISO/IEC 42001 should be integrated with relevant cybersecurity and operational safety arrangements.

An AI management system does not independently certify that every AI application is safe for every industrial use. Application-specific validation and safety assurance remain necessary.

#### 10.6 Sector-Specific Safety and Engineering Requirements

Critical infrastructure organizations may need to apply sector-specific safety engineering, functional safety, reliability, and assurance standards.

The relevant requirements depend on the sector and the function performed by the AI-enabled system.

Examples include environments involving:

- Industrial process control.
- Electrical protection.
- Railway signaling.
- Aviation systems.
- Medical devices.
- Nuclear facilities.
- Other safety-critical industrial systems.

The organization must determine whether AI forms part of a safety-related function, supports a safety decision, or merely provides non-safety-critical analytics.

This distinction affects the required assurance, validation, independence of protective controls, and authorization process.

A general-purpose AI governance framework should not be used as a substitute for a required sector-specific safety assessment.

#### 10.7 European Union Cybersecurity and AI Regulatory Context

For organizations operating in the European Union, applicable obligations may arise from several legal instruments.

**NIS2 Directive**

The NIS2 Directive establishes cybersecurity risk-management and incident-reporting obligations for entities within its scope, including relevant sectors and essential or important entities as defined by the Directive and national implementation.

Critical infrastructure organizations should assess whether the entity, service, and relevant operations fall within scope and identify the applicable national requirements.

**Critical Entities Resilience Directive**

The Critical Entities Resilience (CER) Directive addresses the resilience of critical entities in specified sectors. Its relevance depends on the entity's designation, sector, and applicable national implementation.

**EU Artificial Intelligence Act**

The AI Act establishes obligations for AI systems and relevant actors according to the applicable legal classifications and requirements.

Not every AI system used by a critical infrastructure operator is automatically classified as high-risk. The organization must assess the system's intended purpose, role, applicable classification rules, and relevant legal provisions.

Where an AI system is subject to high-risk requirements, applicable obligations may include risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.

The applicable obligations and implementation dates should be verified against the current legal text and relevant guidance before deployment.

**General Data Protection Regulation**

Where personal data is processed, the GDPR may apply. Relevant considerations include lawful processing, purpose limitation, data minimization, security, retention, data-subject rights, and the allocation of controller and processor responsibilities.

The applicability of these requirements depends on the actual processing activities and legal context.

**Important distinction:** Regulatory scope must be assessed at the entity, system, use-case, and processing levels as applicable. The presence of AI or the label "critical infrastructure" does not automatically establish that every obligation in every instrument applies.

#### 10.8 Build a Unified Control Mapping

A unified control mapping translates framework requirements into a single set of enterprise controls and evidence.

| Governance objective | Potential framework alignment | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Establish AI accountability | NIST AI RMF; ISO/IEC 42001 | AI policy, roles, governance minutes |
| Manage cybersecurity risk | NIST CSF; ISO/IEC 27001 | Risk register, treatment plan, control evidence |
| Protect industrial networks | IEC 62443; relevant cybersecurity obligations | Network diagrams, firewall rules, segmentation tests |
| Validate AI performance | NIST AI RMF; ISO/IEC 42001 | Model evaluation, robustness tests, validation records |
| Preserve safety constraints | Applicable sector safety requirements | Hazard analysis, safety requirements, verification evidence |
| Control third parties | Applicable ISMS, AIMS, and sector requirements | Supplier assessments, contractual clauses, service reviews |
| Manage incidents | NIST CSF; applicable legal requirements | Incident procedures, exercises, notification records |
| Maintain resilience | NIST CSF; applicable resilience requirements | Continuity plans, recovery tests, dependency assessments |
| Demonstrate accountability | Applicable governance and legal requirements | Approval records, logs, risk acceptance decisions |
| Improve controls | ISO management-system approaches and applicable requirements | Audit findings, corrective actions, management reviews |

A single control may support several framework objectives, but the organization must verify that it actually satisfies the relevant requirements.

Framework mapping should not be reduced to a spreadsheet exercise in which a control is marked compliant without evidence.

#### 10.9 Evidence Required for Audit and Assurance

A mature governance program should maintain evidence appropriate to the system's risk and applicable obligations.

Potential evidence includes:

- AI system inventory.
- Business purpose and intended-use documentation.
- System architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- OT asset and dependency inventories.
- Risk assessments and threat models.
- Safety assessments where required.
- Model provenance and evaluation records.
- Data-quality and validation results.
- Access-control and network-security configurations.
- Supplier assessments.
- Security test results.
- Change approvals.
- Incident and recovery procedures.
- Monitoring records.
- Residual-risk acceptance decisions.
- Periodic review and reassessment records.

Evidence should be current, attributable, protected from unauthorized alteration, and linked to the relevant control or risk.

The organization should be able to demonstrate not only that a policy exists, but also that the control is implemented and operates as intended.

**Core Principle:** Framework alignment provides structure for AI governance, cybersecurity, and operational assurance, but compliance must be demonstrated through deployment-specific controls and evidence. A unified governance model should integrate AI risk management, OT cybersecurity, safety requirements, and applicable legal obligations without treating any single framework as a substitute for the others.

### 11. Initial Security and Safety Assessment Checklist

The following checklist can be used during early architecture review or risk screening.

It is not a substitute for a complete assessment, but it helps identify areas requiring further investigation.

| Assessment question | Expected evidence | Escalation condition |
|---|---|---|
| Is the AI use case formally approved? | Use-case record and accountable owner | No defined business purpose or ownership |
| Is the system's operating scope documented? | Intended-use and operating-domain documentation | Unclear or uncontrolled intended use |
| Are affected OT assets identified? | Asset inventory and architecture diagrams | Unknown or incomplete system boundaries |
| Are data sources approved and validated? | Source register and validation rules | Untrusted or unverified operational inputs |
| Is the data flow documented? | Data-flow diagram and trust-boundary assessment | Unknown data destinations or communication paths |
| Does the AI connect to industrial control systems? | Connectivity and permission records | Unapproved or unrestricted access |
| Can the AI execute operational commands? | Tool inventory and command authorization design | Undocumented or uncontrolled command authority |
| Are required safety constraints enforced independently? | Safety design and verification evidence | Reliance on model instructions alone |
| Are model and configuration changes controlled? | Versioning, approval, and rollback records | Unauthorized or untraceable changes |
| Is the system tested under abnormal conditions? | Test plan and results | No evidence of failure-mode testing |
| Is there a defined degraded mode? | Approved fallback and recovery procedures | Failure could leave operations without a safe alternative |
| Are relevant actions logged? | Audit records and monitoring design | Inability to reconstruct consequential actions |
| Are suppliers and dependencies assessed? | Supplier assessments and dependency inventory | Critical dependency with unknown security or continuity risks |
| Are risks assigned to accountable owners? | Risk register and treatment plans | Material risks without ownership |
| Is residual risk formally accepted? | Approval record and conditions | Unapproved high-consequence exposure |
| Are reassessment triggers defined? | Lifecycle governance procedure | Material changes can occur without review |

#### 11.1 Suggested Assessment Outcomes

The assessment should result in one of several documented outcomes:

**Proceed to detailed assessment**

The use case has a defined purpose and accountable owner, and no immediate disqualifying issue has been identified.

**Proceed with restrictions**

The use case may continue within a limited scope, such as read-only analytics or advisory-only operation, while specified controls are implemented and verified.

**Defer production deployment**

Material security, safety, governance, or resilience requirements remain unresolved.

**Reject the proposed design**

The architecture creates unacceptable exposure that cannot be reduced to an acceptable level through feasible controls.

**Suspend an existing deployment**

New evidence indicates that the system is operating outside its approved boundaries or that important controls have failed.

These outcomes should be documented, with clear conditions, approval authority, and reassessment requirements.

### Core Principle

A critical infrastructure AI system should not proceed to production solely because the model performs well in a test environment or the business case is attractive. Authorization must be supported by a documented threat model, enforceable security controls, appropriate safety assurance, verified control effectiveness, and a formally approved residual-risk decision.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Threat modeling vs. risk assessment:** Threat modeling identifies plausible attack paths and failure scenarios; risk assessment evaluates their likelihood, consequences, and treatment priority.
- **Defense in depth vs. single-point protection:** Defense in depth uses complementary safeguards so that one failed control does not automatically expose the complete system.
- **AI model security vs. system security:** Protecting the model is necessary but insufficient when data sources, APIs, identities, networks, and operational interfaces can also be compromised.
- **Human approval vs. independent authorization:** A human review process does not replace technical enforcement of permissions and operating constraints.
- **Cybersecurity assurance vs. safety assurance:** Both are necessary when digital threats or AI failures can influence physical processes.
- **Inherent risk vs. residual risk:** Residual risk must reflect the verified effectiveness of implemented controls, not merely the controls planned in a design.
- **Framework alignment vs. compliance evidence:** Mapping controls to a framework does not prove compliance unless the applicable requirements are satisfied and evidence is available.
- **Risk acceptance vs. mandatory requirements:** Risk acceptance cannot waive applicable legal obligations or required safety protections.
- **Model validation vs. operational authorization:** A validated model is not automatically authorized for every deployment, operating condition, or level of autonomy.

## Part 3: Implementation Roadmap, Operational Monitoring, Incident Response, and Resilience

### 12. Implementation Roadmap for AI in Critical Infrastructure

Implementing AI in critical infrastructure requires a controlled, risk-based approach that integrates cybersecurity, operational technology (OT), safety engineering, data governance, and business continuity.

Unlike a conventional enterprise AI deployment, an AI system supporting critical infrastructure may influence essential services, industrial equipment, physical processes, and public safety. Implementation must therefore demonstrate that the system is not only technically functional but also secure, operationally appropriate, resilient, and governed throughout its lifecycle.

The organization should not move directly from a successful proof of concept to unrestricted production deployment. Each stage should establish the evidence required to justify the next stage.

#### 12.1 Establish the Implementation Principles

Before implementation begins, the organization should define the principles governing how AI will be introduced into its operational environment.

| Principle | Implementation requirement | Governance objective |
|---|---|---|
| Safety first | Preserve mandatory safety functions and operational constraints | Prevent unacceptable physical consequences |
| Security by design | Integrate cybersecurity requirements into architecture and engineering decisions | Reduce exposure before production |
| Least privilege | Give AI services only the access required for approved functions | Limit compromise impact |
| Controlled autonomy | Introduce autonomous capabilities only when justified and validated | Prevent unauthorized or unsafe actions |
| Human accountability | Assign accountable owners for consequential decisions | Preserve decision ownership |
| Segmentation | Restrict communication between AI, enterprise IT, and OT environments | Contain compromise |
| Data integrity | Validate the origin, quality, and suitability of operational data | Reduce misleading outputs |
| Resilience | Define fallback behavior and recovery procedures | Maintain essential services |
| Lifecycle assurance | Reassess material changes to models, data, dependencies, and operating conditions | Maintain assurance over time |
| Evidence-based approval | Require documented validation and authorization | Demonstrate defensible governance |

These principles should be reflected in architecture standards, procurement requirements, project governance, and operational procedures.

#### 12.2 Phase 1 — Use-Case Identification and Initial Screening

The first phase identifies the business or operational problem that AI is intended to address.

Potential use cases include:

- Predictive maintenance.
- Equipment anomaly detection.
- Energy consumption optimization.
- Network fault prediction.
- Industrial process optimization.
- Water-quality monitoring.
- Fraud detection affecting infrastructure services.
- Asset inspection using computer vision.
- Security monitoring for industrial environments.
- Demand forecasting and resource planning.

Each proposed use case should be evaluated according to its operational significance and potential consequences.

A predictive maintenance model that provides recommendations to engineers has a different risk profile from an autonomous system that can alter industrial operating parameters.

The initial screening should establish:

1. The intended business or operational outcome.
2. The system owner and accountable executive.
3. The assets, processes, and services affected.
4. The type of data processed.
5. Whether personal, confidential, or sensitive operational information is involved.
6. Whether the AI system interacts with OT.
7. Whether the AI can initiate or influence consequential actions.
8. The potential impact of incorrect outputs or service disruption.
9. The relevant legal, regulatory, contractual, and sector-specific requirements.
10. The preliminary risk classification and required review path.

**Deliverable:** An approved AI use-case proposal containing the intended purpose, system boundaries, preliminary risk assessment, accountable owner, and proposed level of autonomy.

#### 12.3 Phase 2 — Architecture and Dependency Assessment

The second phase establishes how the AI system will interact with existing infrastructure.

The assessment should identify:

- Data sources and ingestion mechanisms.
- Model hosting and inference services.
- Enterprise applications.
- Cloud and on-premises dependencies.
- Identity providers.
- APIs and integration services.
- Network pathways.
- OT assets and industrial control systems.
- Monitoring and logging platforms.
- External suppliers and managed services.
- Backup, recovery, and fallback mechanisms.

The organization should document all important trust boundaries.

For example, an AI service hosted in a public cloud may receive telemetry from an industrial data gateway. The architecture must show how data crosses the OT boundary, which systems can initiate communication, and whether the cloud service has any ability to send information or commands back into the industrial environment.

The design should minimize unnecessary connectivity and prevent the AI platform from becoming an unrestricted bridge between enterprise IT and OT.

**Deliverables:**

- System context diagram.
- Enterprise and OT architecture diagrams.
- Data-flow diagrams.
- Asset and dependency inventory.
- Trust-boundary analysis.
- Initial cybersecurity and safety requirements.

#### 12.4 Phase 3 — Risk Assessment and Control Design

The third phase converts the initial risk assessment into implementable security, governance, and safety requirements.

The organization should evaluate:

- Cybersecurity threats.
- AI-specific attack scenarios.
- Operational failure modes.
- Data-quality risks.
- Model limitations.
- Unauthorized access and privilege escalation.
- Supplier and dependency risks.
- Service availability.
- Physical consequences.
- Human factors and automation bias.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Recovery requirements.

Each material risk should have an identified owner, a treatment decision, and a defined method for verifying the effectiveness of the proposed controls.

Control design should cover the entire AI workflow rather than the model alone.

For example, protecting an anomaly-detection model from unauthorized modification does not prevent an attacker from manipulating the sensor data supplied to it. The organization must therefore protect the model, its dependencies, the data pipeline, the integration interfaces, and the operational decision process.

**Deliverables:**

- Detailed AI risk register.
- Threat model.
- Safety and hazard assessment where applicable.
- Control requirements.
- Risk treatment plan.
- Control ownership matrix.
- Test and assurance plan.

#### 12.5 Phase 4 — Controlled Development and Integration

During development and integration, the organization implements the approved architecture and controls.

Key activities include:

- Configuring secure environments.
- Establishing service identities and permissions.
- Implementing approved data pipelines.
- Applying network segmentation.
- Configuring API security.
- Protecting model artifacts.
- Implementing output validation.
- Restricting available tools and actions.
- Establishing logging and monitoring.
- Configuring alerting and escalation.
- Implementing fallback mechanisms.
- Integrating the system with approved operational workflows.

Development and testing should be separated from production environments.

Changes to AI models, configurations, integration interfaces, or operational permissions should follow controlled change-management procedures.

Where possible, the system should initially be integrated using read-only access to operational information. Additional capabilities should be introduced only when justified by the use case and supported by an approved risk assessment.

**Deliverables:**

- Implemented security controls.
- Approved configurations.
- Integration test results.
- Model and data provenance records.
- Updated architecture documentation.
- Operational procedures.

#### 12.6 Phase 5 — Validation and Security Testing

The organization must establish that the system performs as intended and remains within its approved security and operational boundaries.

Testing should include more than model accuracy.

| Test category | Objective | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Functional testing | Verify intended system behavior | Confirm that equipment anomalies are correctly reported |
| Data validation | Verify input quality and handling | Test missing, duplicated, delayed, and inconsistent telemetry |
| Model validation | Assess performance against approved criteria | Evaluate false positives and false negatives |
| Robustness testing | Assess behavior under unusual conditions | Test operating conditions outside typical training data |
| Cybersecurity testing | Identify vulnerabilities and weaknesses | Test authentication, authorization, APIs, and network boundaries |
| AI-specific security testing | Assess AI-related attack scenarios | Test manipulated inputs or malicious retrieved content where relevant |
| Safety testing | Verify required safety constraints | Confirm that prohibited operating parameters cannot be authorized |
| Failure testing | Verify behavior when dependencies fail | Simulate model-service or telemetry outages |
| Recovery testing | Verify restoration procedures | Restore a trusted model and configuration from approved sources |
| Human-factors testing | Assess operator interaction and oversight | Test whether operators can recognize uncertainty and reject unsafe recommendations |

The organization should define acceptance criteria before testing begins.

Otherwise, teams may be tempted to reinterpret the results after discovering that the system does not meet the expected requirements.

Testing should be performed in a controlled environment that does not create an unacceptable risk to live operations. Any production testing involving operational equipment requires appropriate authorization and safeguards.

**Deliverables:**

- Approved test plan.
- Test execution records.
- Defect and remediation register.
- Model evaluation results.
- Security assessment findings.
- Safety verification evidence where applicable.
- Formal acceptance recommendation.

#### 12.7 Phase 6 — Pilot Deployment

A pilot allows the organization to evaluate the system under controlled, representative conditions before authorizing broader use.

The pilot should have a defined scope, duration, set of participants, success criteria, and rollback procedure.

Depending on the use case, the organization may choose:

- Historical-data evaluation.
- Offline analysis.
- A digital simulation.
- A test environment using representative data.
- Shadow mode.
- Advisory-only production use.

In **shadow mode**, the AI processes operational information and generates outputs, but those outputs do not directly control the live process. The organization compares the AI's results with actual outcomes or existing operational decisions.

Shadow mode can help reveal model limitations and operational integration issues without granting the system direct command authority.

However, it does not prove that an autonomous system is safe to operate. It provides evidence for a broader assurance process.

Pilot evaluation should consider both model performance and the surrounding workflow, including operator workload, alert volume, data availability, system latency, and the consequences of incorrect recommendations.

#### 12.8 Phase 7 — Production Authorization

Production authorization is a formal governance decision, not simply a technical deployment milestone.

Before approval, the organization should verify that:

- The use case remains within its approved scope.
- Material risks have been treated or appropriately escalated.
- Required controls have been implemented and tested.
- Applicable safety requirements have been satisfied.
- Required operational procedures are available.
- Operators and support personnel have received appropriate training.
- Monitoring and incident response are operational.
- Recovery and fallback procedures have been tested.
- Supplier dependencies have been assessed.
- Residual risk has been evaluated by the appropriate authority.
- Any restrictions or conditions of approval are documented.

The authorization should identify the approved system version, permitted capabilities, operating conditions, accountable owner, review requirements, and circumstances that require reassessment.

Where a system is approved only for advisory use, the approval must not be interpreted as authorization to introduce autonomous control later.

#### 12.9 Phase 8 — Operational Monitoring and Lifecycle Management

After deployment, the organization must continuously assess whether the AI system remains suitable for its operating environment.

Monitoring should cover:

- Model performance.
- Data quality.
- Security events.
- Model and configuration changes.
- System availability.
- Dependency health.
- Unauthorized access attempts.
- Operational incidents.
- Changes in the operating environment.
- Control effectiveness.
- Compliance with the approved use case.

The organization should define triggers for reassessment, such as material changes to the model, data sources, infrastructure, permissions, supplier arrangements, or operating conditions.

A model that performed acceptably at deployment may become less reliable when equipment is replaced, sensors are recalibrated, operational patterns change, or the system is extended to a new facility.

**Core Principle:** AI implementation in critical infrastructure must proceed through documented, risk-based stages. Each stage should produce evidence that justifies the next decision, and production authorization must remain limited to the capabilities and operating conditions that have actually been assessed and approved.

### 13. Operational Monitoring, Security Metrics, and Risk Indicators

Operational monitoring determines whether the AI system continues to function securely, reliably, and within its approved operating boundaries.

A critical infrastructure organization should not rely exclusively on model-performance dashboards. Effective monitoring combines AI performance, cybersecurity, OT behavior, safety-relevant conditions, service availability, and governance indicators.

The monitoring design must also distinguish between normal variation and events requiring investigation.

#### 13.1 Establish a Unified Monitoring Model

Monitoring responsibilities are commonly distributed across several teams.

| Monitoring domain | Primary responsibility | Examples of monitored events |
|---|---|---|
| AI performance | AI engineering or model owner | Prediction quality, drift, uncertainty |
| Data engineering | Data platform team | Missing measurements, ingestion failures, schema changes |
| Cybersecurity | Security operations center | Unauthorized access, suspicious API calls, compromised credentials |
| OT security | OT security team | Unexpected industrial communication, configuration changes |
| Operations | Control-room or infrastructure operations | Equipment anomalies, service degradation, operational deviations |
| Safety | Safety engineering or designated safety authority | Safety-limit violations and safety-related incidents |
| Governance and compliance | AI governance, GRC, or compliance team | Approval status, unresolved risks, overdue assessments |
| Supplier management | Vendor or third-party risk function | Supplier incidents, service-level breaches, material dependency changes |

These teams should have documented responsibilities and escalation routes.

Monitoring data should be correlated where useful, while preserving the access restrictions and information-handling requirements of each environment.

For example, a model-performance alert may become more significant when combined with a sensor-integrity alert or an unauthorized configuration change.

#### 13.2 Monitor Model Performance

Model monitoring evaluates whether the AI system continues to meet its approved performance requirements.

Relevant metrics depend on the use case.

For anomaly detection, they may include:

- Detection rate.
- False-positive rate.
- False-negative rate.
- Alert precision.
- Detection latency.
- Percentage of unclassified observations.
- Performance by equipment category.
- Performance under known operating conditions.

For predictive maintenance, monitoring may include:

- Prediction error.
- Calibration of estimated failure probabilities.
- Lead time before equipment failure.
- Missed failure events.
- Unnecessary maintenance recommendations.
- Performance by equipment type or operating condition.

For forecasting, relevant metrics may include forecast error, bias, interval coverage, and performance during unusual demand conditions.

The organization should not use a single generic accuracy score for every AI application.

The appropriate metrics must reflect the operational consequences of different types of errors.

For example, a false negative in a safety-relevant anomaly-detection workflow may be substantially more consequential than a false positive that triggers an additional inspection.

#### 13.3 Detect Data Drift and Concept Drift

**Data drift** occurs when the distribution or characteristics of incoming data change relative to the reference data used during development or validation.

Potential causes include:

- Sensor replacement.
- Equipment aging.
- Changes in measurement calibration.
- New operating conditions.
- Different environmental conditions.
- Changes to data collection.
- Changes in data formats.
- Shifts in the population or process being monitored.

**Concept drift** occurs when the relationship between the model's inputs and the outcome it predicts changes.

For example, an equipment failure model may become less reliable after a maintenance process changes the relationship between telemetry patterns and failure outcomes.

Drift does not automatically mean that the model is unsafe or unusable. It indicates that the organization should determine whether the change affects the model's suitability.

A governance process should define:

1. Which variables or outcomes are monitored.
2. What baseline is used for comparison.
3. Which thresholds trigger investigation.
4. Who evaluates the alert.
5. Whether the system may continue operating during investigation.
6. When revalidation is required.
7. Who approves a revised model.

An updated model should not be promoted to production solely because it appears to perform better on a new dataset. The organization must verify that the update does not introduce unacceptable regressions or invalidate previously established safety and security assumptions.

#### 13.4 Monitor Data Quality and Provenance

Data-quality monitoring helps determine whether the information entering the AI system remains suitable for its intended purpose.

Potential indicators include:

- Missing-data rate.
- Duplicate-record rate.
- Delayed-data frequency.
- Unexpected value ranges.
- Timestamp inconsistencies.
- Schema violations.
- Source authentication failures.
- Unexpected changes in data volume.
- Sensor disagreement.
- Failed validation checks.

Provenance monitoring should also identify unexpected changes in data origin, transformation, or routing.

A sudden change in the distribution of telemetry could be caused by a legitimate operational event, a faulty sensor, a configuration change, or deliberate manipulation. Monitoring should provide enough context for investigation rather than automatically treating every anomaly as an attack.

For critical decisions, the system should have a defined response when essential data-quality requirements are not met.

#### 13.5 Monitor Cybersecurity and OT Events

Security monitoring should cover both conventional cyber threats and activity specific to AI-enabled workflows.

Examples include:

- Repeated authentication failures.
- Unexpected privilege changes.
- Unauthorized model deployment.
- Changes to trusted model artifacts.
- Unusual data exports.
- API requests outside approved patterns.
- Attempts to invoke prohibited tools.
- Unexpected outbound network connections.
- Changes to industrial firewall rules.
- Unapproved changes to PLC or HMI configurations.
- Suspicious remote-access activity.
- Attempts to disable monitoring.
- Unusual patterns of AI-generated commands or recommendations.

Where feasible, security events should be correlated with the affected asset, model version, data source, user or service identity, and operational context.

The monitoring architecture should avoid giving an AI analytics platform unrestricted access to security-sensitive OT management functions merely to improve visibility.

Read-only monitoring and controlled telemetry export may provide sufficient visibility for many use cases.

#### 13.6 Establish KPIs, KRIs, and Control Effectiveness Indicators

These measures serve different purposes.

- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** measure operational or business performance.
- **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** identify changes in risk exposure or conditions that may lead to adverse outcomes.
- **Key Control Indicators (KCIs)** measure whether important controls are implemented and operating as intended.

The organization should define the meaning, calculation method, data source, owner, reporting frequency, and escalation threshold for each indicator.

| Indicator | Type | Example measurement | Possible escalation trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model false-negative rate | KPI/KRI | Missed anomalies divided by confirmed anomalies | Exceeds the approved limit |
| Data validation failure rate | KRI | Percentage of records rejected by validation | Sustained increase or critical-source failure |
| AI service availability | KPI | Percentage of time the service is available | Falls below the approved service objective |
| Unauthorized tool invocation attempts | KRI | Number and severity of denied attempts | Evidence of attempted abuse or a significant increase |
| Privileged access review completion | KCI | Percentage of required reviews completed | Overdue review of a critical identity |
| Model integrity verification | KCI | Percentage of required integrity checks completed successfully | Failed verification or missing evidence |
| Critical vulnerability remediation | KCI/KRI | Percentage of critical findings remediated within required timelines | Overdue finding affecting an exposed critical component |
| Fallback test completion | KCI | Completion of required fallback exercises | Failed or overdue test |
| Unresolved high-risk findings | KRI | Number and age of high-risk findings | Exceeds approved tolerance or release criteria |
| Incident response exercise performance | KCI | Completion and results of exercises | Critical response weakness remains unresolved |
| Unapproved configuration changes | KRI | Number of detected unauthorized changes | Any confirmed unauthorized change to a critical component |
| Operator override frequency | KPI/KRI | Frequency and reasons for rejecting AI recommendations | Material increase requiring model or workflow investigation |

The table provides examples, not universal thresholds. Numerical limits must be established using the system's operating requirements, risk assessment, and applicable obligations.

A metric should trigger an investigation or decision when appropriate, rather than being collected only for reporting purposes.

#### 13.7 Define Monitoring Thresholds and Escalation

Each important metric should have a defined response when its threshold is exceeded.

A practical escalation model may use the following levels:

| Level | Condition | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Expected variation with no material risk indication | Record and review through routine monitoring |
| Warning | Unusual behavior or a developing risk condition | Investigate and determine whether restrictions are needed |
| High | Material control degradation or credible security concern | Escalate to accountable technical and operational owners |
| Critical | Confirmed compromise, serious safety concern, or unacceptable operational risk | Invoke approved incident procedures and restrict or suspend affected capabilities as appropriate |

The response should be proportionate to the possible consequences.

A failed integrity check for a model used only for offline experimentation may be handled differently from a failed integrity check for a model authorized to initiate operational actions.

The organization should define escalation rules in advance rather than relying on ad hoc decisions during an incident.

#### 13.8 Monitoring Must Be Tested

The existence of a dashboard does not prove that the organization can detect a meaningful event.

Monitoring validation should test:

- Whether expected events are generated.
- Whether logs reach the monitoring platform.
- Whether alert rules trigger correctly.
- Whether critical alerts reach the correct team.
- Whether alerts include sufficient investigative context.
- Whether escalation occurs within required timeframes.
- Whether monitoring can be disabled without detection.
- Whether evidence is protected from unauthorized modification.
- Whether false positives overwhelm the response team.
- Whether monitoring remains effective during dependency failures.

Exercises should include representative cybersecurity and operational scenarios.

For example, the organization could test whether an unauthorized model replacement generates an alert, whether the alert identifies the affected deployment, and whether the responsible team can restrict the affected service.

#### 13.9 Avoid Monitoring Blind Spots

Common monitoring weaknesses include:

- Monitoring only the model, not its dependencies.
- Collecting logs without reviewing them.
- Alerting on technical events without operational context.
- Failing to monitor model and configuration changes.
- Ignoring unusual data-source behavior.
- Assuming that a low error rate proves safety.
- Failing to monitor authorized users and service identities.
- Not testing escalation procedures.
- Retaining logs that cannot be reliably attributed to a user, service, or action.
- Allowing the monitoring system to depend entirely on the component it is supposed to monitor.

A resilient design should preserve independent visibility into important events, particularly when the AI platform itself could be compromised.

**Core Principle:** Operational monitoring must connect AI performance, data integrity, cybersecurity, OT behavior, and governance indicators to defined actions. Metrics become valuable when they reveal changes in risk and lead to timely, accountable decisions.

### 14. Incident Response for AI-Enabled Critical Infrastructure

Incident response is the coordinated process of detecting, assessing, containing, eradicating, and recovering from events that threaten the confidentiality, integrity, availability, or safe operation of a system.

For AI-enabled critical infrastructure, incident response must account for the possibility that a cybersecurity incident could influence physical processes or interrupt essential services.

The response plan must coordinate cybersecurity teams with OT operators, safety personnel, engineering teams, suppliers, and relevant management authorities.

#### 14.1 Identify AI-Specific Incident Scenarios

Potential incidents include:

- Unauthorized modification of a model.
- Compromise of an AI service account.
- Manipulation of operational data.
- Prompt injection that causes unauthorized tool use.
- Unauthorized access to industrial integration APIs.
- AI-generated recommendations outside approved operating limits.
- Model degradation affecting operational decisions.
- Loss of an AI service supporting essential operations.
- Exposure of sensitive infrastructure information.
- Compromise of a third-party AI service.
- Unauthorized changes to an AI deployment pipeline.
- Failure of an AI-related control that supports an important operational process.

The incident classification should distinguish a model-quality issue from a security incident, while recognizing that the two can overlap.

For example, an increase in false positives may indicate ordinary model degradation. If the increase follows a confirmed unauthorized data-source change, the event may also require a cybersecurity investigation.

#### 14.2 Establish Incident Severity Criteria

Severity should be determined by the actual and potential consequences of the incident, not merely by the technical component affected.

Relevant factors include:

- Potential impact on public safety.
- Effect on essential services.
- Number and criticality of affected assets.
- Loss of operational control or visibility.
- Integrity of operational measurements.
- Ability to continue safely without AI.
- Scope of unauthorized access.
- Potential data exposure.
- Recovery complexity.
- Regulatory and contractual notification obligations.

An incident involving a low-impact analytical dashboard may be managed differently from an incident affecting AI-supported industrial control.

The severity model should identify the conditions that require immediate escalation to OT operations, safety leadership, executive management, or external authorities.

#### 14.3 Phase 1 — Preparation

Preparation activities establish the resources and procedures required to respond effectively.

They include:

- Maintaining an incident response plan.
- Defining AI and OT incident classifications.
- Identifying critical AI systems and dependencies.
- Establishing contact lists and escalation paths.
- Assigning decision-making authority.
- Preparing evidence collection procedures.
- Defining model suspension and isolation procedures.
- Establishing fallback and continuity arrangements.
- Maintaining approved model and configuration backups.
- Preparing supplier escalation procedures.
- Conducting tabletop and technical exercises.
- Defining relevant legal and regulatory notification processes.

The response plan should identify who has authority to suspend an AI capability and who determines the appropriate operational state during that suspension.

For example, cybersecurity personnel may have authority to request the isolation of a compromised AI service, while the OT operations lead determines how the affected process transitions to its approved fallback mode.

The exact authority structure should be documented before an incident occurs.

#### 14.4 Phase 2 — Detection and Triage

Detection may originate from a security monitoring platform, AI performance dashboard, industrial control alarm, operator report, supplier notification, or external threat intelligence.

During triage, the response team should establish:

1. What happened?
2. Which system and assets are affected?
3. When did the event begin?
4. Is the event ongoing?
5. Is there evidence of unauthorized access or modification?
6. Could the event affect operational safety?
7. Could essential services be interrupted?
8. Is the AI system still trustworthy?
9. Are related systems or dependencies affected?
10. Which teams and authorities must be notified internally?

The team should distinguish confirmed facts from hypotheses.

For example, an anomalous model output does not by itself prove that the model has been compromised. The response team should investigate the input data, model version, configuration, recent changes, and relevant security events.

However, uncertainty should not prevent proportionate protective action when the potential consequences are severe.

#### 14.5 Phase 3 — Containment

Containment limits the spread or impact of an incident.

Depending on the scenario, measures may include:

- Revoking compromised credentials.
- Restricting affected API permissions.
- Blocking unauthorized communication paths.
- Suspending AI-generated actions.
- Disabling affected tools or integrations.
- Isolating a compromised AI service.
- Switching to an approved advisory-only mode.
- Restricting access to compromised data sources.
- Preventing further model deployment.
- Invoking an approved fallback procedure.

Containment decisions must consider the operational consequences of the action.

Simply disconnecting an AI service may be appropriate for a nonessential analytics application but may require carefully coordinated transition arrangements for a system supporting real-time operations.

The organization should prioritize safe operational continuity while preventing further compromise.

#### 14.6 Phase 4 — Evidence Preservation and Investigation

Evidence preservation supports root-cause analysis, accountability, recovery, and any required reporting or legal proceedings.

Potential evidence includes:

- Authentication logs.
- Privileged access records.
- API requests and responses.
- Network security events.
- Model and configuration versions.
- Data ingestion records.
- Model deployment history.
- Tool invocation logs.
- Approval records.
- Operator actions.
- Industrial system alarms.
- Relevant system snapshots.
- Supplier notifications.
- Change-management records.

The response team should preserve timestamps, record provenance, and maintain appropriate access controls.

Evidence collection must not create additional operational risk. Investigative actions that could affect live industrial processes should be coordinated with authorized OT personnel.

The team should also assess whether the incident affected the integrity of historical data, because corrupted data may compromise later model evaluation or incident reconstruction.

#### 14.7 Phase 5 — Eradication and Remediation

Eradication removes the cause of the incident and addresses the weaknesses that allowed it to occur.

Potential actions include:

- Removing unauthorized access.
- Replacing compromised credentials.
- Correcting configuration weaknesses.
- Patching vulnerable components.
- Removing malicious integrations.
- Restoring approved model artifacts.
- Validating affected datasets.
- Correcting API permissions.
- Strengthening network restrictions.
- Revising monitoring rules.
- Addressing supplier-related weaknesses.
- Updating incident response procedures.

Restoring the original model alone may be insufficient.

If an attacker compromised the identity provider or deployment pipeline, the organization must address that underlying weakness before trusting the restored model.

The remediation process should identify the root cause and any contributing factors, including weaknesses in governance, architecture, change management, monitoring, or operational procedures.

#### 14.8 Phase 6 — Recovery and Controlled Reintroduction

Recovery returns the system to an approved operational state.

Before reintroducing an AI capability, the organization should verify:

- The source of the restored model is trusted.
- Model and configuration integrity checks pass.
- Required data sources are trustworthy.
- Compromised credentials have been addressed.
- Relevant vulnerabilities have been remediated.
- Required security controls are functioning.
- Operational dependencies are healthy.
- Safety constraints remain effective.
- Monitoring and alerting are operational.
- The fallback arrangement remains available.
- Required authorization has been obtained.

Depending on the incident, recovery may involve staged reintroduction.

For example, an AI system might initially return in read-only or advisory mode while its outputs are monitored and compared against independent operational evidence.

Any restoration to higher levels of autonomy should require appropriate reassessment and authorization.

#### 14.9 Phase 7 — Lessons Learned and Governance Improvement

After the incident, the organization should assess both the technical cause and the governance weaknesses that contributed to the event.

Questions include:

- Was the risk identified before deployment?
- Were the required controls implemented?
- Did monitoring detect the event promptly?
- Were responsibilities clearly assigned?
- Did the response team have sufficient authority?
- Did containment create avoidable operational disruption?
- Were fallback procedures effective?
- Were suppliers able to provide the required support?
- Were reporting obligations identified and fulfilled?
- Did the incident expose a weakness affecting other AI deployments?

The organization should update the risk register, control requirements, architecture standards, incident procedures, supplier assessments, and training where necessary.

Lessons learned should be translated into assigned corrective actions with deadlines and verification requirements.

#### 14.10 Example Incident: Manipulated Operational Telemetry

Consider an AI system that identifies abnormal equipment behavior using telemetry exported from an industrial data gateway.

The security operations center detects an unusual pattern in incoming measurements. At approximately the same time, the AI begins producing recommendations inconsistent with independent operational indicators.

The organization activates its response process.

| Stage | Response action | Governance objective |
|---|---|---|
| Detection | Correlate data anomalies, model outputs, and security events | Establish whether the event may be security-related |
| Triage | Identify affected data sources, models, and operational processes | Determine scope and potential consequences |
| Containment | Restrict the affected data path and suspend reliance on questionable recommendations | Prevent further operational influence |
| Safety coordination | Have authorized operations personnel assess the actual process state | Preserve safe operation |
| Investigation | Review gateway logs, source integrity, model versions, and configuration changes | Identify the cause and preserve evidence |
| Remediation | Correct the compromised component or data path and address underlying weaknesses | Remove the cause |
| Recovery | Validate trusted data, restore approved components, and test model behavior | Establish system trust |
| Reauthorization | Obtain approval before returning to the previously authorized operating mode | Prevent premature restoration |
| Improvement | Update controls, threat models, and monitoring rules | Reduce recurrence risk |

The organization should not assume that the model itself was compromised simply because it produced misleading recommendations. The incident investigation must establish whether the cause was manipulated data, sensor failure, model degradation, a software defect, or another factor.

**Core Principle:** Incident response for AI-enabled critical infrastructure must protect both the digital environment and the physical or operational process. Containment, investigation, and recovery must be coordinated with authorized OT and safety personnel, and an AI system should not return to its previous operating mode until its trustworthiness and required controls have been re-established.

### 15. Operational Resilience, Business Continuity, and Disaster Recovery

Operational resilience is the organization's ability to maintain or restore important services when systems fail, become compromised, or encounter unexpected conditions.

For critical infrastructure, resilience must be designed around the service being delivered rather than the availability of the AI application alone.

An AI service can be unavailable while the infrastructure continues operating safely. Conversely, an AI service may remain technically available while providing misleading outputs that undermine operational reliability.

Resilience planning must address both conditions.

#### 15.1 Distinguish Availability, Continuity, and Recovery

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| System availability | Whether a system can be accessed and perform its intended function | AI inference service is responding |
| Business continuity | Ability to maintain important organizational activities during disruption | Maintenance teams continue operating using established procedures |
| Operational resilience | Ability to withstand, adapt to, respond to, and recover from disruption | A utility maintains essential service despite an AI platform outage |
| Disaster recovery | Restoration of systems, data, and supporting infrastructure after a major disruption | Restore the AI platform and its approved configuration |
| Safety fallback | Transition to an operating state that manages hazards when normal functionality is unavailable | Suspend AI-assisted optimization and use an approved operating procedure |

These concepts overlap but should not be treated as interchangeable.

A recovery plan that restores the AI service does not necessarily demonstrate that essential infrastructure services can continue safely during the outage.

#### 15.2 Identify Critical Business and Operational Services

Resilience planning should begin by identifying the services that must be maintained.

Examples include:

- Electricity distribution.
- Water treatment and distribution.
- Telecom connectivity.
- Industrial process control.
- Emergency communications.
- Transportation operations.
- Healthcare infrastructure.
- Essential monitoring and alarm functions.

The organization should determine whether the AI system is essential to delivering the service or whether it provides an enhancement that can be temporarily removed.

This distinction affects recovery priorities and the required fallback arrangements.

For example, an AI model that predicts equipment maintenance requirements may improve efficiency but may not be essential to maintaining the immediate safe operation of a facility. A system supporting a time-critical operational function may require more stringent availability and recovery arrangements.

#### 15.3 Conduct a Business Impact Analysis

A Business Impact Analysis (BIA) identifies the consequences of disruption and supports recovery prioritization.

For an AI-enabled critical infrastructure service, the analysis should consider:

- Operational consequences of AI unavailability.
- Consequences of incorrect AI outputs.
- Impact on essential services.
- Dependencies on identity, networking, cloud services, and data platforms.
- Manual operating capability.
- Required staff and specialist expertise.
- Availability of alternative data sources.
- Maximum tolerable disruption.
- Recovery dependencies.
- Safety implications.
- Supplier recovery commitments.

The BIA should distinguish between losing the AI service and losing the operational process supported by that service.

These may have different consequences and different recovery requirements.

#### 15.4 Define Recovery Time and Recovery Point Objectives

Two commonly used recovery measures are:

**Recovery Time Objective (RTO):** The target time within which a system or service should be restored after disruption.

**Recovery Point Objective (RPO):** The target maximum amount of data loss measured in time that the organization can tolerate.

Illustrative example:

A nonessential AI analytics service may have an RTO of several hours because operational staff can continue using established procedures.

A system supporting a time-sensitive operational workflow may require a much shorter recovery time or an alternative mechanism that remains available during the outage.

RPO depends on the data and recovery requirements. Some AI services can reconstruct analytical outputs from retained source data, while others may depend on state or records that cannot easily be recreated.

These objectives must be established through business impact analysis and operational requirements. They should not be assigned generically to every AI system.

#### 15.5 Design an AI Fallback Strategy

The fallback strategy defines how operations continue when the AI system is unavailable or untrusted.

Potential strategies include:

**Manual operation**

Qualified personnel perform the relevant task using approved procedures.

**Conventional automation**

The system returns to a validated non-AI control mechanism.

**Read-only mode**

The AI can continue analyzing information, but its outputs cannot trigger changes to operational systems.

**Advisory-only mode**

The AI produces recommendations that require additional review before use.

**Redundant service**

An alternative service provides the required functionality when the primary AI service is unavailable.

**Controlled suspension**

The AI capability is disabled while the surrounding process follows an approved operating procedure.

The appropriate strategy depends on the operational function and associated hazards.

A fallback procedure must be realistic. It should account for staffing, training, response time, equipment availability, and the possibility that multiple supporting services may fail together.

#### 15.6 Avoid Common-Mode Failure

Common-mode failure occurs when multiple components that appear independent are affected by the same underlying event.

For example, an organization may deploy two AI inference services but have both depend on:

- The same identity provider.
- The same cloud region.
- The same network pathway.
- The same operational data source.
- The same model repository.
- The same deployment pipeline.
- The same compromised administrator account.

In such cases, the apparent redundancy may provide less resilience than expected.

The organization should identify shared dependencies and assess whether the recovery architecture remains effective when those dependencies fail or are compromised.

Redundancy should be evaluated through failure scenarios, not merely through the number of servers or model instances deployed.

#### 15.7 Protect Backups and Recovery Artifacts

Recovery depends on the availability of trusted data, software, models, configurations, and operational documentation.

The organization should protect:

- Approved model artifacts.
- Model configurations.
- Required application components.
- Relevant datasets and metadata.
- Integration configurations.
- Infrastructure-as-code or equivalent configuration records.
- Security policies and access configurations.
- Recovery procedures.
- Audit records needed for investigation.
- Documentation of approved versions and dependencies.

Backups should be protected from unauthorized modification and deletion.

Where appropriate, the organization should maintain isolated or otherwise protected copies that are not exposed to the same administrative and operational risks as production systems.

Restoration testing should verify both recoverability and integrity. A backup that can be restored but contains compromised software or an untrusted model is not an acceptable recovery source.

#### 15.8 Test Resilience Under Realistic Failure Conditions

Resilience testing should examine the complete service, including its dependencies and operational fallback arrangements.

Potential scenarios include:

- AI service outage.
- Cloud-region disruption.
- Loss of the identity provider.
- Loss of operational telemetry.
- Corruption of a model artifact.
- Compromise of the deployment pipeline.
- Failure of the primary data gateway.
- Network isolation between enterprise and OT environments.
- Loss of a key supplier.
- Failure of the primary monitoring platform.
- Simultaneous failure of several dependencies.

Testing should establish whether the organization can detect the failure, activate the fallback, maintain the required operational state, restore trusted components, and resume normal operation.

Testing must be planned and controlled to avoid creating unacceptable risk to live infrastructure.

#### 15.9 Coordinate Cyber Recovery and Operational Recovery

Cyber recovery and operational recovery should be coordinated but may have different objectives.

Cyber recovery focuses on restoring trusted systems and removing the causes of compromise.

Operational recovery focuses on restoring the required service safely and reliably.

A system may be technically restored before it is safe to return to full operational use.

For example, an AI platform may be available after a cyber incident, but the organization may still need to verify the integrity of its data sources, model artifacts, permissions, and integration pathways.

The recovery plan should therefore include distinct authorization steps for technical restoration and return to the approved operational mode.

#### 15.10 Example: Regional Cloud Outage

A utility operates an AI-based asset analytics service in a cloud region. The system analyzes telemetry and recommends maintenance priorities.

The cloud region becomes unavailable.

The organization has designed the service as an advisory capability rather than an essential control mechanism.

Its continuity procedure requires:

1. Confirming that the outage affects the approved AI service.
2. Suspending reliance on unavailable recommendations.
3. Continuing maintenance activities through existing procedures and approved records.
4. Preserving operational telemetry locally where feasible.
5. Monitoring the outage and supplier communications.
6. Restoring the AI service in an approved environment.
7. Verifying model, configuration, data, and access integrity.
8. Reconciling any work completed during the outage.
9. Validating normal operation before returning to the standard workflow.
10. Reviewing whether the outage exposed unacceptable dependency risk.

The organization does not need to preserve uninterrupted AI availability if the essential service can continue safely without it. It must, however, ensure that the fallback is operationally viable and that recovery meets the approved requirements.

**Core Principle:** Resilience is measured by the ability to maintain or restore essential services safely, not simply by keeping the AI application online. Effective continuity and disaster recovery plans address AI failure, cyber compromise, shared dependencies, trusted restoration, and the operational consequences of switching to fallback procedures.

### 16. Governance Operating Model and Accountability

AI in critical infrastructure requires coordination across business leadership, cybersecurity, OT engineering, safety, AI engineering, operations, legal, procurement, and risk management.

Without clear accountability, important decisions may fall between teams. AI engineering may focus on model performance, cybersecurity may focus on unauthorized access, and operations may focus on service continuity without a single governance mechanism integrating all three perspectives.

A governance operating model defines who makes decisions, who implements controls, who verifies effectiveness, and who accepts the remaining risk.

#### 16.3 Establish Decision Rights

Governance should specify who can approve:

- New AI use cases.
- Material architecture changes.
- Connections to OT environments.
- Introduction of additional autonomy.
- Changes to operational permissions.
- Production deployment.
- Material model updates.
- Exceptions to security requirements.
- Residual-risk acceptance.
- Suspension or restriction of AI capabilities.
- Restoration after a serious incident.

Approval authority should be proportionate to the potential consequences.

A low-risk analytics application may be approved through a standard technology governance process. A system capable of affecting safety-critical industrial operations may require formal technical, operational, cybersecurity, and safety authorization.

#### 16.4 Separate Risk Ownership from Control Ownership

**Risk ownership** means accountability for ensuring that a risk is assessed, treated, monitored, and escalated.

**Control ownership** means responsibility for designing, implementing, operating, or maintaining a specific control.

For example, an operations manager may own the risk that unreliable AI recommendations disrupt an operational workflow. The AI engineering team may own model validation controls, while the OT security team owns controls governing access to industrial interfaces.

The risk owner must ensure that the combined treatment is sufficient. Individual control owners are responsible for their assigned safeguards.

#### 16.5 Integrate Governance with Change Management

Material changes to an AI system should trigger reassessment when they affect the system's risk profile or approved operating boundaries.

Examples include:

- A new model or materially changed model version.
- New data sources.
- Changes to sensor configuration.
- A new cloud region or hosting provider.
- Additional API integrations.
- Expanded tool permissions.
- Increased autonomy.
- New equipment types.
- A new operational site.
- Changes to the fallback procedure.
- New legal or regulatory obligations.
- Significant incidents or newly identified threats.

The change process should determine whether the existing approval remains valid or whether additional assessment and authorization are required.

A change should not be considered low-risk merely because it is technically small. A minor permission change may substantially increase the system's ability to affect critical assets.

#### 16.6 Manage Exceptions and Risk Acceptance

Exceptions should be controlled, time-bound where appropriate, and supported by a documented rationale.

An exception record should include:

- The requirement that cannot currently be met.
- The reason for the exception.
- The affected systems and processes.
- The associated risk.
- Existing compensating controls.
- The accountable risk owner.
- The approving authority.
- The expiry or review date.
- The remediation plan, if applicable.
- Conditions that trigger immediate reassessment.

Exceptions should not silently become permanent design decisions.

If an exception affects mandatory legal or safety requirements, the organization must determine whether it is legally and operationally permissible before considering approval.

#### 16.7 Maintain a Governance Evidence Repository

A governance evidence repository should provide controlled access to the records needed to demonstrate that the AI system is managed appropriately.

It may contain:

- Use-case approvals.
- Architecture and data-flow diagrams.
- Risk assessments.
- Threat models.
- Safety assessments.
- Model documentation.
- Data provenance records.
- Control test results.
- Security findings.
- Change approvals.
- Supplier assessments.
- Incident reports.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Training records.
- Monitoring reports.
- Audit findings and corrective actions.

Evidence should be version-controlled, attributable, protected, and retrievable.

The repository should also preserve the relationship between requirements, controls, tests, findings, and approvals. This makes it possible to determine whether a material risk has been addressed and what evidence supports the decision.

**Core Principle:** Effective AI governance depends on explicit decision rights, accountable risk ownership, verifiable control ownership, and independent assurance where required. The organization must maintain governance throughout the system lifecycle rather than treating approval as a one-time activity.

### 17. Implementation Acceptance Criteria and Operational Readiness Review

The Operational Readiness Review (ORR) is a formal assessment that determines whether an AI-enabled critical infrastructure system is ready to enter or continue production operation.

The review brings together evidence from architecture, cybersecurity, AI validation, operational engineering, safety, resilience, supplier management, and governance.

It should be conducted against predefined criteria rather than relying on a general statement that the project is complete.

#### 17.1 Define Readiness Domains

A practical ORR can evaluate the following domains.

| Domain | Required assurance question |
|---|---|
| Business purpose | Is the intended use approved and clearly defined? |
| System architecture | Are system boundaries, dependencies, and communication pathways documented? |
| Data governance | Are data sources approved, suitable, and appropriately protected? |
| AI validation | Does the model meet its defined acceptance criteria? |
| Cybersecurity | Are required security controls implemented and tested? |
| OT integration | Are industrial interfaces restricted and authorized? |
| Safety | Are applicable safety requirements and protective measures verified? |
| Human oversight | Can responsible personnel understand, challenge, and override recommendations where required? |
| Monitoring | Are critical events and performance changes detectable? |
| Incident response | Are responsibilities, escalation paths, and containment procedures established? |
| Resilience | Are fallback and recovery arrangements tested? |
| Supplier assurance | Have material third-party dependencies been assessed? |
| Compliance | Have applicable legal and contractual obligations been evaluated? |
| Risk treatment | Are material risks assigned, treated, and appropriately escalated? |
| Operational support | Are staffing, documentation, training, and maintenance arrangements in place? |
| Change management | Are model, configuration, and integration changes controlled? |
| Auditability | Can consequential actions and approvals be reconstructed? |

#### 17.2 Define Evidence-Based Acceptance Criteria

Acceptance criteria should be specific and testable.

Weak criterion:

"AI security is adequate."

Stronger criterion:

"All production AI service identities have documented owners, approved permissions, and successful access-control test results. No unresolved authorization weakness permits the AI service to perform an unapproved action against a critical operational interface."

Another weak criterion:

"The system has a fallback plan."

Stronger criterion:

"The approved fallback procedure has been exercised under a representative AI-service failure scenario, and the test record demonstrates that authorized personnel can transition to the required operating mode within the approved recovery target."

The exact requirements depend on the use case, architecture, and applicable obligations.

#### 17.3 Classify Findings by Release Impact

Findings should be classified according to their consequences and their effect on the authorization decision.

| Finding category | Example | Typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| Critical blocker | Uncontrolled AI access to a safety-critical command interface | Do not authorize the affected capability |
| High-risk blocker | Required security or safety control is missing or unverified | Defer deployment or restrict scope |
| Conditional issue | A bounded weakness has an approved compensating control and authorized treatment | Proceed only if formal criteria permit |
| Moderate issue | A weakness requires remediation but does not violate a release requirement | Assign an owner and deadline under approved governance |
| Low-priority improvement | A nonmaterial enhancement to documentation or efficiency | Track through normal improvement processes |

These categories should align with the organization's risk methodology.

A numeric risk score alone should not determine whether a safety-critical release can proceed. Mandatory requirements and unacceptable consequences may create blockers regardless of the overall score.

#### 17.4 Conduct a Formal Go/No-Go Review

The review should consider:

1. Whether the system satisfies the approved intended use.
2. Whether material risks have been addressed.
3. Whether required controls have been tested.
4. Whether safety-related requirements have been verified.
5. Whether residual risk has been assessed.
6. Whether the correct authority has approved the remaining exposure.
7. Whether operational personnel are prepared.
8. Whether fallback and recovery procedures are available.
9. Whether monitoring and incident response are functioning.
10. Whether release restrictions are documented.

The decision should be recorded as one of the following:

- **Go:** The system meets the required release criteria.
- **Conditional go:** Deployment is permitted only within explicit restrictions and approved conditions.
- **No-go:** Material requirements remain unsatisfied.
- **Restricted deployment:** Only a limited capability or operating mode is authorized.
- **Reassessment required:** A material change or new evidence invalidates the previous approval basis.

A conditional go must not be used to bypass mandatory controls or unresolved unacceptable risk.

#### 17.5 Define Production Restrictions Explicitly

Where deployment is restricted, the restrictions should be enforceable and documented.

Examples include:

- Read-only access to operational telemetry.
- Advisory-only recommendations.
- No direct control-system commands.
- Access limited to specified equipment.
- Operation restricted to defined facilities.
- Mandatory human approval for consequential actions.
- Prohibition on processing certain data classes.
- Prohibition on unapproved external integrations.
- Restrictions on model updates.
- Additional monitoring during an initial operating period.

Restrictions should be implemented through technical and operational controls where feasible.

A restriction documented only in a project report may be insufficient if the system architecture still permits the prohibited action.

#### 17.6 Establish Post-Deployment Review

Production approval should establish a review schedule and clear triggers for earlier reassessment.

Review triggers may include:

- Significant performance degradation.
- New security threats.
- A cybersecurity incident.
- A safety-related event.
- Material architecture changes.
- Changes to the intended use.
- New data sources or equipment.
- Expanded autonomy.
- Supplier changes.
- Regulatory changes.
- Failure of an important control.
- A change in the criticality of the supported service.

The review should verify whether the original assumptions remain valid and whether the current control environment continues to support the approved use.

#### 17.7 Example: Release Decision for an AI-Powered Grid Analytics Platform

An electricity operator proposes an AI platform to identify abnormal grid conditions and recommend investigation priorities.

The system does not directly execute switching commands.

The ORR finds that:

- The business purpose is approved.
- The architecture and data flows are documented.
- Operational data sources have been assessed.
- The model has completed its defined validation.
- Access controls and network restrictions have been tested.
- Recommendations are subject to established operational review.
- Monitoring and escalation procedures are available.
- The fallback procedure has been exercised.
- Remaining risks have been assessed.
- The designated authorities have approved the deployment within the advisory-only scope.

The organization authorizes production use with explicit restrictions preventing direct control-system command execution.

Several months later, the project team proposes to add autonomous switching functionality.

This change materially alters the system's potential consequences and operating authority. The existing approval cannot automatically be reused. The proposed capability requires additional architecture analysis, threat modeling, safety assurance, control validation, operational testing, and formal authorization.

The example illustrates an essential governance rule: approval is tied to the assessed use case, architecture, permissions, and operating conditions—not merely to the name of the AI platform.

**Core Principle:** Operational readiness must be demonstrated through specific, testable evidence. The final deployment decision should reflect the actual capabilities and restrictions of the system, the effectiveness of required controls, and the authority to accept the remaining risk.

This process supports continual improvement and helps ensure that the governance model remains aligned with the actual operating environment.

### Core Principle

AI governance in critical infrastructure should be evidence-driven, traceable, and continuously maintained. Each material risk should connect to an accountable owner, a defined treatment, verified controls, an authorized residual-risk decision, and ongoing monitoring.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Implementation vs. authorization:** Building and testing a system does not automatically authorize it for production.
- **Pilot success vs. production readiness:** A successful pilot provides evidence but does not replace full operational, cybersecurity, and safety assurance.
- **Model performance vs. system assurance:** Model metrics do not demonstrate that the surrounding architecture, interfaces, and operational procedures are secure.
- **KPI vs. KRI vs. KCI:** KPIs measure performance, KRIs signal changing exposure, and KCIs assess control implementation or effectiveness.
- **Data drift vs. concept drift:** Data drift concerns changes in input distributions; concept drift concerns changes in the relationship between inputs and outcomes.
- **Detection vs. response:** Detecting an event is not sufficient unless accountable personnel can investigate and take appropriate action.
- **Technical recovery vs. operational recovery:** Restoring an application does not prove that the supported service can resume safely.
- **Risk owner vs. control owner:** The risk owner is accountable for managing exposure; the control owner is responsible for an assigned safeguard.
- **Conditional approval vs. unrestricted approval:** A conditional approval permits only the documented scope and restrictions.
- **Documented control vs. effective control:** A policy or procedure must be supported by implementation evidence and, where appropriate, effectiveness testing.
- **System availability vs. operational resilience:** A system can be unavailable while the essential service continues, or available while delivering unreliable outputs.
- **Approval vs. lifecycle assurance:** Authorization is based on defined assumptions and must be reassessed when material changes invalidate those assumptions.

### 19. Integrated Enterprise Case Study: AI-Powered Electricity Grid Monitoring and Optimization

This case study demonstrates how an organization can apply AI governance, cybersecurity risk management, operational technology (OT) security, safety assurance, and resilience principles to a realistic critical infrastructure deployment.

The scenario concerns an electricity distribution operator implementing an AI platform to improve grid monitoring, predict equipment failures, and recommend operational adjustments.

The objective is to improve operational efficiency without introducing unacceptable cybersecurity, safety, or service-continuity risks.

This is a hypothetical enterprise case study. The architecture, risks, controls, and decisions are illustrative and must be adapted to the actual infrastructure, jurisdiction, engineering requirements, and operating environment.

#### 19.1 Business Context

The electricity operator manages substations, transformers, distribution feeders, remote monitoring systems, industrial control infrastructure, and supporting enterprise applications.

The organization experiences several operational challenges:

- Unexpected equipment failures.
- Increasing maintenance costs.
- Limited visibility into equipment degradation.
- Large volumes of telemetry.
- Difficulty identifying abnormal operating patterns.
- Pressure to improve service reliability.
- Increasing cybersecurity threats against operational infrastructure.
- Need to demonstrate governance and regulatory compliance.

Management proposes an AI-enabled platform that analyzes operational telemetry and generates maintenance recommendations.

The proposed system will use historical equipment data, sensor measurements, maintenance records, and operational information to identify abnormal behavior and estimate the likelihood of equipment failure.

The initial deployment will operate in an advisory capacity. It will not directly issue switching commands or modify protective settings.

This restriction is important because it limits the system's operational authority while the organization establishes evidence of reliability and control effectiveness.

#### 19.2 Proposed Business Objectives

The project establishes measurable objectives before development begins.

| Business objective | Proposed measurement | Governance consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Improve maintenance planning | Reduction in avoidable emergency maintenance events | Confirm that the measurement reflects actual operational improvement |
| Improve anomaly detection | Detection performance against verified historical events | Evaluate false negatives and false positives separately |
| Improve equipment visibility | Percentage of in-scope assets with usable telemetry | Validate data coverage and quality |
| Reduce investigation effort | Change in time required to identify relevant anomalies | Confirm that efficiency gains do not reduce investigation quality |
| Maintain operational continuity | Compliance with approved service and recovery objectives | Test fallback procedures and supporting dependencies |
| Preserve security | No unresolved release-blocking security findings | Verify control effectiveness rather than relying on documentation |
| Maintain safety | Compliance with applicable safety requirements | Obtain the required engineering and safety approvals |
| Demonstrate governance | Complete approval and evidence records | Maintain traceability from risks to controls and decisions |

The proposed measurements are illustrative. Numerical targets should be defined using the operator's baseline performance, operational requirements, and approved risk methodology.

The project must not treat improved prediction accuracy as sufficient evidence that the system is secure or safe.

#### 19.3 System Architecture

The proposed architecture contains six principal layers.

**Layer 1 — Operational Assets**

The physical environment contains transformers, substations, circuit breakers, distribution equipment, sensors, and associated industrial systems.

These assets generate measurements and operational events.

**Layer 2 — OT Monitoring and Control**

The operator uses supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA), human-machine interfaces (HMIs), programmable logic controllers (PLCs), protection systems, and other industrial components as appropriate to the installation.

The AI platform must not bypass approved industrial security boundaries or independent protection mechanisms.

**Layer 3 — Industrial Data Collection**

An approved industrial data gateway exports selected telemetry to the analytics environment.

The gateway validates data formats, enforces approved communication rules, and restricts unnecessary connectivity.

**Layer 4 — AI and Analytics Platform**

The AI platform processes approved telemetry and historical maintenance data.

It generates anomaly alerts, equipment health indicators, and maintenance recommendations.

**Layer 5 — Enterprise Applications**

The platform integrates with authorized maintenance management, asset management, and reporting applications.

Recommendations may be converted into draft work orders for review.

**Layer 6 — Governance and Security Oversight**

The organization applies identity management, access controls, logging, monitoring, risk management, model lifecycle governance, and operational approval procedures across the architecture.

#### 19.5 Proposed Deployment Strategy

**Stage 3 — Advisory production**

The AI generates recommendations for authorized personnel.

**Stage 4 — Controlled expansion**

The organization may extend the model to additional equipment categories or facilities after reassessing the operating conditions, data quality, model performance, and associated risks.

Direct operational command authority is not included in the approved scope.

- Data quality and provenance controls are implemented.
- Model validation meets approved acceptance criteria.
- Security testing is completed.
- Operational fallback procedures are tested.
- Monitoring and incident response are operational.
- Applicable safety and regulatory requirements are assessed.
- Material residual risks are formally reviewed.
- Production approval is documented.
- Expansion of the system's capabilities requires reassessment.

The committee authorizes deployment only when the required evidence has been provided and the designated authorities have approved the remaining risk.

**Core Principle:** The business case for AI in critical infrastructure must be supported by an architecture that preserves operational boundaries, limits system authority, and demonstrates measurable control effectiveness. Deployment should progress through controlled stages, with every expansion of capability subject to risk-based reassessment.

### 20. Critical Infrastructure AI Risk Register

A risk register provides a structured record of identified risks, their consequences, treatment decisions, accountable owners, and remaining exposure.

The following example register applies to the hypothetical electricity distribution operator described in Section 19.

The ratings are illustrative rather than assessments of a real utility. Organizations should use their approved risk methodology and validate each rating against the actual architecture, threat environment, operational context, and control evidence.

#### 20.1 Risk Scoring Methodology

A simple five-by-five model can be used for preliminary prioritization.

**Likelihood**

| Score | Rating | General interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rare | Unlikely under the assessed conditions |
| 2 | Unlikely | Possible but not expected in normal circumstances |
| 3 | Possible | Credible under some conditions |
| 4 | Likely | Expected to occur with meaningful frequency |
| 5 | Almost certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

**Impact**

| Score | Rating | General interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Insignificant | Minimal operational or business effect |
| 2 | Minor | Limited disruption with straightforward recovery |
| 3 | Moderate | Material disruption requiring coordinated response |
| 4 | Major | Significant service, financial, regulatory, or operational impact |
| 5 | Severe | Potential for catastrophic consequences, major essential-service disruption, or serious harm |

A preliminary score can be calculated as:

**Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact**

For example, likelihood 4 multiplied by impact 5 produces a score of 20 out of 25.

The organization must define score bands and treatment rules before using the model for formal decisions.

Importantly, a numerical score must not override mandatory safety requirements, applicable legal obligations, or specific release-blocking conditions.

| Risk ID | Risk description | Primary consequence | Inherent score | Key treatment | Risk owner | Residual assessment |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|---|
| CI-01 | Manipulated operational telemetry influences AI recommendations | Incorrect maintenance or operational decisions | 20 | Validate data sources, monitor anomalies, corroborate critical measurements | OT Operations Manager | Reassess after control testing |
| CI-02 | Unauthorized access to the AI service | Data exposure or unauthorized use of integrations | 16 | Strong authentication, least privilege, access monitoring | AI Platform Owner | Reassess after access testing |

The scores above are illustrative starting points. They should not be interpreted as validated risk ratings for the hypothetical operator.

#### 20.3 Risk Treatment Priorities

The operator should prioritize treatment according to consequences, mandatory requirements, attack paths, and control dependencies.

**Priority 1 — Critical boundary and safety risks**

Examples:

- CI-06: Uncontrolled path into OT.
- CI-18: Missing independent enforcement of required safety constraints.

These risks require resolution of the underlying architecture or safety weakness before the affected capability can be authorized.

**Priority 2 — Integrity and privilege risks**

Examples:

- CI-01: Manipulated telemetry.
- CI-03: Excessive service permissions.
- CI-04: Unauthorized model changes.
- CI-13: Uncontrolled deployment changes.
- CI-15: Restoration of untrusted artifacts.

These risks require effective preventive and detective controls supported by verification evidence.

**Priority 3 — Operational and governance risks**

Examples:

- CI-07: AI service outage.
- CI-08: Model degradation.
- CI-12: Operator over-reliance.
- CI-16: Changes without reassessment.
- CI-17: Incomplete dependency mapping.

These risks require lifecycle governance, monitoring, continuity planning, and accountable ownership.

#### 20.4 Risk Treatment Plan

Each material risk should have a corresponding treatment record.

| Risk ID | Treatment action | Verification evidence | Approval requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| CI-01 | Validate telemetry and establish independent checks for critical measurements | Data-quality test results and source-integrity evidence | OT and AI system owners |
| CI-03 | Remove unnecessary permissions from AI service identities | Permission review and negative authorization tests | IAM and security owners |
| CI-04 | Protect model artifacts and enforce controlled promotion | Integrity test results and deployment records | AI engineering and security assurance |
| CI-06 | Restrict communication to approved data flows | Firewall review, connectivity tests, and architecture approval | OT security and authorized OT engineering |
| CI-08 | Establish model-performance thresholds and revalidation triggers | Monitoring specification and test results | AI system owner |
| CI-10 | Assess critical suppliers and define incident and recovery obligations | Supplier assessment and contractual evidence | Vendor risk and procurement |
| CI-12 | Introduce operator training and defined review procedures | Training records and human-factors evaluation | Operations manager |
| CI-15 | Verify trusted backups and controlled restoration | Recovery exercise and artifact integrity results | Disaster recovery and system owners |
| CI-16 | Require reassessment of material changes | Change-control records and approval evidence | AI governance and system owner |
| CI-18 | Verify independent enforcement of safety constraints | Applicable safety verification evidence | Designated safety authority |

#### 20.5 Residual Risk Assessment

Residual risk must be determined after the relevant controls have been implemented and tested.

The organization should evaluate:

- Whether the control addresses the intended threat.
- Whether the control is correctly configured.
- Whether the control operates consistently.
- Whether the control can be bypassed.
- Whether dependencies weaken the control.
- Whether independent verification is required.
- Whether the remaining exposure is acceptable.
- Whether any mandatory requirements remain unsatisfied.

A risk should not automatically be reduced from high to low merely because several controls have been documented.

For example, network segmentation may reduce exposure, but the organization must verify that unauthorized paths are blocked and that relevant administrative pathways are also controlled.

Likewise, model integrity controls may reduce the likelihood of unauthorized modification but do not independently protect against manipulated input data or model limitations.

**Core Principle:** A risk register must drive accountable action and verified risk reduction. In critical infrastructure, residual-risk decisions must reflect actual control effectiveness, operational consequences, and applicable mandatory requirements—not merely the number of controls listed.

### 21. AI Governance Audit and Assurance Framework

An AI governance audit evaluates whether the organization has established and maintained the governance arrangements, technical controls, operational procedures, and evidence required to manage AI-related risks.

The objective is to determine whether controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

For AI-enabled critical infrastructure, the audit must consider the interaction between AI governance, cybersecurity, OT security, operational safety, resilience, and regulatory compliance.

An audit should not be limited to reviewing policy documents or interviewing the project team. It should examine evidence of actual implementation and, where appropriate, test control operation.

#### 21.1 Define the Audit Scope

The audit scope should identify:

- AI systems and use cases under review.
- Relevant operational services.
- Supporting IT and OT environments.
- Model and data lifecycle activities.
- Supplier and third-party dependencies.
- Applicable governance frameworks.
- Relevant legal and regulatory obligations.
- Safety-related functions, where applicable.
- Relevant control owners.
- Audit period and evidence requirements.

The auditor should establish whether the AI system is advisory-only, partially automated, or authorized to perform actions.

This distinction materially affects the scope of testing.

For example, an advisory model may require evidence of data integrity, recommendation validation, and human oversight. An AI system with operational command authority may require additional testing of command authorization, safety constraints, failure behavior, and control-system integration.

#### 21.2 Audit Governance and Accountability

The audit should assess whether the organization has defined the governance arrangements necessary to control the AI lifecycle.

| Audit question | Evidence to inspect | Potential finding |
|---|---|---|
| Is the AI use case formally approved? | Use-case approval and intended-use documentation | Deployment lacks documented authorization |
| Is an accountable system owner assigned? | Governance records and responsibility matrix | Ownership is unclear |
| Are material risks recorded? | Risk register and assessment records | Important risks are omitted |
| Are decision rights documented? | Approval matrix and governance procedures | Unclear authority for deployment or suspension |
| Are risk exceptions controlled? | Exception register and approval evidence | Exceptions remain open without appropriate approval |
| Are material changes reviewed? | Change records and reassessment evidence | Changes occur without risk review |
| Is residual risk formally accepted where required? | Risk acceptance records | High-consequence exposure lacks authorized acceptance |
| Is independent assurance performed where required? | Audit plans and assurance reports | Control effectiveness is not independently evaluated |

The auditor should determine whether governance operates in practice, not merely whether documents exist.

#### 21.3 Audit Architecture and OT Security

Architecture assurance should examine whether the system's documented design matches its deployed configuration.

Potential audit procedures include:

- Reviewing system context diagrams.
- Comparing architecture documentation with actual interfaces.
- Inspecting OT connectivity rules.
- Reviewing approved data flows.
- Examining remote-access pathways.
- Reviewing API permissions.
- Assessing service identities.
- Verifying network segmentation.
- Inspecting management interfaces.
- Reviewing administrative access.
- Evaluating dependencies on shared identity and cloud services.
- Confirming that prohibited communication paths are blocked.

Testing that affects live OT systems must be appropriately authorized and conducted in a controlled manner.

The auditor should not assume that a diagram proves segmentation. Where necessary, the organization should provide configuration evidence and authorized test results.

#### 21.4 Audit AI Data and Model Governance

The audit should examine whether data and model lifecycle activities are controlled.

Key questions include:

- Are approved data sources documented?
- Are data-quality requirements defined?
- Is data provenance maintained where required?
- Are training and evaluation datasets protected?
- Are model versions recorded?
- Are model changes subject to approval?
- Are evaluation criteria established before testing?
- Are model limitations documented?
- Is performance monitored after deployment?
- Are drift triggers defined?
- Is revalidation required when assumptions change?
- Are untrusted or out-of-scope inputs handled appropriately?
- Can the organization identify which model version produced a consequential output?

Potential evidence includes model documentation, dataset records, evaluation results, deployment history, monitoring records, and change approvals.

The auditor should also consider whether validation evidence corresponds to the model and configuration currently operating in production.

#### 21.5 Audit Cybersecurity Controls

Cybersecurity assurance should evaluate the design and effectiveness of controls that protect the AI platform and its dependencies.

Relevant areas include:

- Identity and access management.
- Privileged access management.
- Service-account permissions.
- Network segmentation.
- API security.
- Vulnerability management.
- Secure configuration.
- Model artifact integrity.
- Secrets management.
- Security logging.
- Monitoring and alerting.
- Supplier security.
- Change management.
- Incident response.
- Backup and recovery.

Where appropriate, the auditor should inspect evidence of access-control testing, vulnerability remediation, configuration reviews, security exercises, and incident investigations.

A control should not be considered effective solely because the responsible team states that it is in place.

#### 21.6 Audit Safety and Operational Readiness

Where the AI system may affect safety-related or consequential operational decisions, the audit should determine whether the required assurance process has been followed.

Questions may include:

- Has the system's operating domain been defined?
- Are applicable hazards documented?
- Are safety constraints established?
- Are required independent protections maintained?
- Has the system been tested under abnormal conditions?
- Are human oversight responsibilities clear?
- Are fallback procedures approved?
- Have operational personnel been trained?
- Are release criteria documented?
- Has the appropriate authority approved the deployment?
- Are changes to the operating scope reassessed?

The auditor should evaluate the evidence against the applicable sector and safety requirements.

A generic AI governance policy is not sufficient evidence that a safety-related system has been appropriately engineered and verified.

#### 21.7 Audit Monitoring and Incident Response

The audit should assess whether monitoring and response capabilities can identify and manage relevant events.

Potential audit tests include:

- Confirming that required events are logged.
- Reviewing log integrity and access restrictions.
- Testing whether representative alerts trigger.
- Verifying escalation to accountable teams.
- Reviewing incident classifications.
- Examining incident response exercises.
- Assessing evidence preservation procedures.
- Reviewing containment and recovery decisions.
- Confirming that lessons learned generate corrective actions.
- Verifying that material incidents trigger risk reassessment.

For critical infrastructure, the audit should assess whether cybersecurity response procedures are coordinated with OT operations and safety responsibilities.

#### 21.8 Audit Business Continuity and Recovery

Resilience assurance should evaluate whether the organization can continue essential operations and restore trusted AI functionality.

Relevant evidence includes:

- Business impact analysis.
- Recovery objectives.
- Dependency assessments.
- Backup protection arrangements.
- Recovery procedures.
- Fallback instructions.
- Recovery exercise results.
- Supplier continuity commitments.
- Evidence of restoration integrity.
- Post-recovery approval records.

The auditor should distinguish between a successful application restart and demonstrated restoration of a trusted, operationally acceptable service.

#### 21.9 Audit Findings and Remediation

Audit findings should identify the requirement or expected control, the observed condition, the risk or consequence, and the corrective action required.

A useful finding structure is:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Finding ID | Unique reference |
| Audit criterion | Applicable requirement, control, or approved procedure |
| Condition | What the auditor observed |
| Evidence | Records, configurations, tests, or interviews supporting the finding |
| Cause | Why the weakness occurred, where established |
| Risk or consequence | Potential impact on security, safety, resilience, or compliance |
| Severity | Rating under the organization's audit methodology |
| Corrective action | Specific remediation required |
| Action owner | Accountable remediation owner |
| Target date | Approved completion date |
| Verification | Method used to confirm remediation |
| Closure approval | Authority responsible for closing the finding |

The auditor should distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions. Where the evidence is insufficient to reach a conclusion, the finding should state the limitation rather than asserting an unsupported failure.

#### 21.10 Audit Reporting

The final report should communicate:

- Scope and objectives.
- Audit criteria.
- Systems and processes reviewed.
- Procedures performed.
- Important limitations.
- Findings and severity.
- Evidence supporting conclusions.
- Root causes where established.
- Corrective actions.
- Responsible owners and target dates.
- Matters requiring executive attention.
- Overall assurance conclusion.

The report should distinguish between control design weaknesses, implementation failures, operating-effectiveness failures, and evidence limitations.

This distinction matters because different weaknesses require different remediation.

**Core Principle:** AI governance audit must verify that controls are designed appropriately, implemented as intended, and effective in practice. For critical infrastructure, assurance must cover the complete AI-enabled operational environment, including OT boundaries, safety requirements, data and model integrity, monitoring, incident response, and recovery.

### 22. Executive Reporting, Risk Appetite, and Strategic Decision-Making

Executive reporting translates technical and operational evidence into information that supports decisions about investment, risk acceptance, deployment restrictions, and business priorities.

Executives do not need every model metric or firewall configuration detail. They need a reliable view of whether the AI system is delivering its intended outcome, whether material risks remain, whether controls are effective, and whether the organization is meeting its obligations.

Reporting should not conceal severe risks behind a single average score or a general statement that the project is progressing well.

#### 22.1 Establish Executive Reporting Objectives

Executive reporting should answer six questions:

1. What business or operational value is the AI system delivering?
2. What essential services or critical assets does it affect?
3. What are the most significant current risks?
4. Are required security and safety controls operating effectively?
5. Can the organization maintain essential services if the AI system fails?
6. What decisions or interventions are required from leadership?

The reporting frequency should reflect the criticality of the system, the rate of change, the threat environment, and the organization's governance requirements.

Significant incidents or unacceptable risk conditions should be escalated immediately through established procedures rather than waiting for a scheduled report.

#### 22.2 Executive AI Risk Dashboard

A dashboard may include the following indicators.

| Reporting category | Example measure | Executive interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Business value | Improvement in maintenance planning or anomaly identification | Is the AI system achieving its intended purpose? |
| Model performance | Performance against approved operational thresholds | Is the model still suitable for its approved use? |
| Critical security findings | Number and age of unresolved high-risk findings | Is material exposure remaining untreated? |
| OT boundary assurance | Status of required connectivity and segmentation tests | Are industrial boundaries protected? |
| Access governance | Status of privileged access reviews | Are identities and permissions appropriately controlled? |
| Model integrity | Status of integrity checks and approved model versions | Can the organization trust the deployed artifacts? |
| Data quality | Critical-source validation failures | Are model inputs suitable for consequential use? |
| Operational resilience | Status of fallback and recovery tests | Can essential services continue during disruption? |
| Incident response | Significant incidents and overdue corrective actions | Is the organization responding effectively? |
| Compliance | Status of applicable obligations and open findings | Are legal and contractual requirements being addressed? |
| Change governance | Material changes awaiting approval | Is the deployed system within its authorized scope? |
| Residual risk | High-consequence risks requiring executive attention | Are remaining risks understood and properly governed? |

Each indicator should have a defined calculation method, reporting owner, data source, threshold, and escalation rule.

#### 22.3 Define Risk Appetite and Tolerance

Risk appetite describes the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain in achieving its objectives.

Risk tolerance defines more specific limits or conditions for acceptable variation or exposure.

For AI in critical infrastructure, the organization should not define appetite solely in financial terms.

Relevant dimensions include:

- Public safety.
- Service availability.
- Operational integrity.
- Cybersecurity.
- Data confidentiality.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Supplier dependency.
- Environmental consequences.
- Recovery capability.
- Model uncertainty.

The organization may tolerate temporary loss of an analytical enhancement while maintaining a very low tolerance for unauthorized access to a safety-critical command interface.

Risk appetite should therefore be translated into specific technical and operational requirements.

For example, a statement that "AI risk should be low" is insufficient. The organization should identify which risks must be eliminated, which controls are mandatory, which residual risks may be accepted, and who has authority to make the decision.

#### 22.4 Distinguish Business Value from Risk Acceptability

A business case may demonstrate that an AI system improves efficiency, reduces maintenance costs, or enhances operational visibility.

Those benefits do not automatically make the system acceptable from a security or safety perspective.

The executive decision should consider both the value of the use case and the feasibility of managing its risks.

A useful decision matrix is:

| Business value | Risk condition | Potential decision |
|---|---|---|
| High | Risks adequately treated and requirements satisfied | Approve within the assessed scope |
| High | Material risks remain unresolved | Defer, restrict, or redesign |
| Moderate | Risks adequately controlled | Approve if proportionate to cost and operational need |
| Low | Significant implementation or residual risk | Reject or discontinue |
| Uncertain | Insufficient evidence | Continue controlled evaluation without expanding operational authority |

This approach avoids allowing expected financial benefits to override unacceptable exposure.

#### 22.5 Escalate Material Risk Decisions

Executives and designated authorities should receive timely escalation when:

- A mandatory control is not implemented.
- A serious safety concern emerges.
- A critical trust boundary is compromised.
- A high-consequence residual risk requires authorized decision-making.
- A material incident affects essential services.
- A supplier dependency creates unacceptable exposure.
- Recovery objectives cannot be met.
- A proposed change materially increases autonomy.
- A significant compliance gap remains unresolved.
- The system no longer operates within its approved scope.

The escalation should identify the issue, potential consequences, immediate protective measures, available options, accountable owners, and the decision required.

#### 22.6 Strategic Decision: Expand, Restrict, or Retire

Over time, leadership may need to decide whether to expand, maintain, restrict, or retire an AI capability.

Expansion should be supported by evidence that the system remains suitable and that the organization can manage the increased scope.

Restriction may be appropriate when certain functions create disproportionate risk but lower-risk capabilities remain useful.

Retirement may be necessary when the system no longer provides sufficient value, its risks cannot be adequately managed, its dependencies become unsustainable, or its intended purpose is no longer valid.

Retirement itself requires governance. The organization should manage data retention, credential revocation, integration removal, model artifact disposition, supplier termination, and continuity of the supported operational process.

**Core Principle:** Executive oversight should connect business value with verified control effectiveness and explicit risk decisions. A critical infrastructure AI deployment should be expanded, restricted, or retired based on evidence, operational consequences, and the organization's approved risk appetite—not enthusiasm for AI or model performance alone.

### 23. Final Enterprise Governance Recommendations

AI in critical infrastructure should be treated as an enterprise risk and operational governance concern, not merely an AI engineering initiative.

The organization must coordinate technology, cybersecurity, safety, operations, legal compliance, supplier management, and executive accountability throughout the system lifecycle.

The following recommendations consolidate the principal governance requirements into an actionable enterprise program.

#### 23.1 Establish a Formal AI Governance Policy for Critical Infrastructure

The policy should define:

- Permitted AI use cases.
- Prohibited or restricted applications.
- Risk classification criteria.
- Required architecture and security assessments.
- Approval requirements.
- Human oversight expectations.
- Model and data lifecycle controls.
- Operational autonomy boundaries.
- Supplier governance requirements.
- Incident response obligations.
- Monitoring and reassessment requirements.
- Residual-risk acceptance authority.
- Retirement and decommissioning requirements.

The policy should be supported by standards, procedures, and technical requirements that can be implemented and tested.

#### 23.2 Maintain a Complete AI and Dependency Inventory

The organization should maintain an inventory of AI systems, models, data sources, integrations, suppliers, hosting environments, operational dependencies, and accountable owners.

The inventory should identify which systems affect critical services and whether they have access to OT or other high-consequence environments.

Unknown AI deployments and undocumented integrations create governance blind spots and should be addressed through an established discovery and approval process.

#### 23.3 Adopt a Risk-Based Autonomy Model

The organization should define what levels of autonomy are permitted for different categories of AI use cases.

A useful internal model may distinguish:

- Offline analytical use.
- Advisory recommendations.
- Human-approved workflow execution.
- Limited autonomous actions within predefined constraints.
- Higher-consequence autonomous operation requiring specialized assurance.

These categories are a governance tool, not a universal legal classification.

Each proposed level should have specific authorization, testing, monitoring, fallback, and review requirements.

Increasing autonomy should require additional evidence because the consequences of an incorrect or manipulated output may increase when human review or independent authorization is removed.

#### 23.4 Enforce Security Boundaries Between AI and OT

The organization should explicitly define the communication paths and permissions required between AI platforms, enterprise IT, industrial data gateways, and OT systems.

Broad connectivity should not be granted simply to simplify integration.

Controls should include:

- Approved data flows.
- Network segmentation.
- Least-privilege service identities.
- Restricted administrative access.
- Secure integration interfaces.
- Controlled outbound communication.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Independent operational authorization for consequential actions.
- Periodic validation of network boundaries.

The architecture should ensure that compromise of the AI platform does not automatically provide unrestricted access to industrial assets.

#### 23.5 Establish Lifecycle Assurance for Models and Data

The organization should maintain controls over data sourcing, preparation, validation, model development, evaluation, deployment, monitoring, updates, and retirement.

Model and data changes should be assessed according to their effect on the approved use case.

Lifecycle assurance should include:

- Documented intended use.
- Data provenance and quality requirements.
- Model version management.
- Validation and robustness testing.
- Security testing.
- Drift monitoring.
- Change control.
- Rollback capability.
- Reassessment triggers.
- Documented approval decisions.

The organization should be able to identify which model version was deployed, what evidence supported its approval, and whether it remains suitable for its operating environment.

#### 23.6 Integrate Cybersecurity, Safety, and Operational Risk Management

Cybersecurity and safety teams should work together where attacks or AI failures can affect physical processes.

The organization should maintain clear boundaries between the responsibilities of the CISO, OT security leadership, engineering, operations, and the designated safety authority.

Required safety functions should not depend solely on the AI model's correct behavior.

Independent protective mechanisms should be maintained where required by the applicable engineering and safety requirements.

#### 23.7 Implement Continuous Monitoring and Evidence-Based Assurance

The organization should define indicators for AI performance, data quality, cybersecurity, OT security, service availability, control effectiveness, and governance.

Each important indicator should have:

- A documented definition.
- An accountable owner.
- An approved data source.
- A threshold or evaluation criterion.
- An escalation path.
- A required response.
- A review or reporting frequency.

Monitoring should be tested, and material findings should lead to corrective action.

#### 23.8 Test Incident Response and Recovery

The organization should conduct exercises covering both technical compromise and operational consequences.

Relevant scenarios include:

- Manipulated telemetry.
- Unauthorized model modification.
- Compromised service identities.
- AI-service outage.
- Supplier compromise.
- Loss of critical data sources.
- Failure of an industrial integration gateway.
- Unauthorized tool invocation.
- Failed recovery from trusted backups.
- An AI recommendation that conflicts with operational evidence.

Exercises should evaluate detection, escalation, containment, safe fallback, evidence preservation, restoration, and return-to-service authorization.

#### 23.9 Maintain a Unified Governance and Audit Evidence Repository

Evidence should link requirements, risks, controls, test results, findings, corrective actions, and approvals.

The organization should avoid fragmented evidence that cannot establish whether an important risk has been addressed.

The repository should support internal audit, external assurance, management review, regulatory requests, incident investigation, and lifecycle reassessment.

#### 23.10 Review Governance Maturity Periodically

The organization should periodically evaluate whether its AI governance arrangements remain appropriate as its technology, operating environment, and threat landscape evolve.

A maturity review may consider:

- Policy and governance effectiveness.
- Risk identification and treatment.
- Architecture and OT security.
- AI and data lifecycle assurance.
- Supplier oversight.
- Monitoring and incident response.
- Operational resilience.
- Auditability.
- Management accountability.
- Corrective action effectiveness.

Maturity should be measured by actual control capability and evidence rather than by the number of policies, dashboards, or governance committees established.

### 24. Final Lessons from the Case Study

The electricity distribution scenario illustrates several principles that apply across critical infrastructure sectors.

**Lesson 1 — AI risk is system risk.**

The model is only one component of the overall system. Data sources, integration interfaces, identity systems, networks, suppliers, operators, and operational dependencies can all contribute to risk.

**Lesson 2 — Data integrity can be as important as model integrity.**

A properly protected model can still generate misleading recommendations if its input data is manipulated, incomplete, stale, or unsuitable.

**Lesson 3 — Autonomy must be explicitly governed.**

A system authorized to provide recommendations is not automatically authorized to execute operational actions. Changes in autonomy require reassessment and approval.

**Lesson 4 — Human oversight must be meaningful.**

Operators must have the information, competence, time, and authority required to challenge AI recommendations and follow established procedures.

**Lesson 5 — Safety constraints require appropriate independent enforcement.**

Where required, protective mechanisms must remain effective even when an AI model produces incorrect outputs or the AI platform is compromised.

**Lesson 6 — Monitoring must lead to action.**

Dashboards and alerts are useful only when they identify material conditions and trigger defined responses by accountable teams.

**Lesson 7 — Resilience is about essential services.**

An AI application may be unavailable without causing unacceptable disruption if a validated fallback exists. Conversely, an available application may be untrustworthy after compromise.

**Lesson 8 — Supplier risk extends beyond the model provider.**

The complete dependency chain may include cloud services, identity providers, data platforms, integration vendors, managed service providers, and software suppliers.

**Lesson 9 — Compliance must be demonstrated through evidence.**

Policies and framework mappings are necessary governance artifacts, but effective assurance requires implementation records, test results, monitoring evidence, and documented decisions.

**Lesson 10 — Approval is conditional on the assessed scope.**

A change in model, data, permissions, architecture, equipment, or autonomy may invalidate assumptions that supported the original approval.

**Lesson 11 — Residual risk requires accountable decisions.**

Risk scores do not replace professional judgment, mandatory requirements, or formal acceptance by the appropriate authority.

**Lesson 12 — Governance must continue throughout the lifecycle.**

The organization must monitor the system, reassess material changes, investigate incidents, verify corrective actions, and retire the capability responsibly when necessary.

### 25. Chapter 21.16 Knowledge Consolidation and Practical Application

The governance approach developed in this case study can be applied to other critical infrastructure environments, including water utilities, telecommunications networks, transportation systems, industrial facilities, healthcare infrastructure, and data centers.

The specific engineering requirements and legal obligations will vary, but the underlying governance questions remain consistent.

#### 25.1 Enterprise Assessment Questions

A practitioner assessing an AI-enabled critical infrastructure system should be able to answer the following questions.

**Business and governance**

1. What operational problem is the AI system intended to solve?
2. Who owns the system and its associated risks?
3. What capabilities are formally authorized?
4. Which decisions require human or independent approval?
5. Who can suspend the system or accept residual risk?

**Architecture and cybersecurity**

6. What assets, services, and dependencies form the complete system?
7. Where are the trust boundaries?
8. How does data move between IT, AI, and OT environments?
9. Which identities and permissions can access critical interfaces?
10. Can a compromised AI component reach systems outside its approved scope?

**Data and model assurance**

11. Are the data sources approved and validated?
12. Is the model's intended use documented?
13. Has the model been evaluated under representative and abnormal conditions?
14. Are model versions and configurations controlled?
15. What happens when data quality or model performance deteriorates?

**Safety and operations**

16. Can the AI influence a physical process or critical operational decision?
17. Which safety constraints apply?
18. Are required protective mechanisms independent of AI behavior?
19. What is the approved fallback mode?
20. Can the organization maintain essential operations during an AI outage?

**Monitoring and response**

21. Which events and indicators are monitored?
22. Who receives critical alerts?
23. How does the organization respond to suspected model or data compromise?
24. How are evidence and affected artifacts preserved?
25. What criteria must be met before normal operation resumes?

**Risk and assurance**

26. What are the most consequential identified risks?
27. Which controls address each risk?
28. How has control effectiveness been verified?
29. Which residual risks require formal acceptance?
30. What evidence demonstrates that the system remains within its approved operating scope?

These questions can form the basis of an initial assessment, technical workshop, internal audit, or governance review.

#### 25.2 Recommended Practitioner Deliverables

For a real enterprise engagement, the practitioner should aim to produce the following outputs:

1. AI use-case assessment.
2. AI and dependency inventory.
3. System architecture diagram.
4. Data-flow and trust-boundary diagrams.
5. OT connectivity assessment.
6. AI threat model.
7. Risk and control register.
8. Safety assessment coordination record, where applicable.
9. Control requirements and framework mapping.
10. Model and data validation assessment.
11. Security test plan and results.
12. Operational monitoring specification.
13. Incident response and recovery procedures.
14. Supplier assurance assessment.
15. Operational readiness review.
16. Residual-risk acceptance record.
17. Executive risk dashboard.
18. Lifecycle review and reassessment plan.

The practitioner should ensure that these outputs are tailored to the actual environment. Reusing a template without validating the architecture, operating conditions, and applicable requirements can create a false sense of assurance.

#### 25.3 Final Governance Decision Framework

A final decision should integrate five dimensions.

| Dimension | Decision question | Minimum expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Business value | Does the system provide a justified operational benefit? | Approved purpose and measurable objectives |
| Cybersecurity | Are relevant threats and attack paths adequately controlled? | Verified controls and documented residual risks |
| Safety and operations | Can the system operate within its approved boundaries? | Applicable safety assurance and validated operational procedures |
| Resilience | Can essential services continue or recover during failure? | Tested fallback and recovery arrangements |
| Governance and compliance | Is the system authorized, accountable, and auditable? | Documented approval, ownership, evidence, and review requirements |

The final authorization must reflect the combined outcome of these dimensions.

A system should not receive unrestricted approval merely because it satisfies one dimension while leaving unacceptable weaknesses in another.

### Final Core Principle

AI in critical infrastructure should be governed as a high-consequence enterprise capability whose security, safety, reliability, and accountability depend on the complete operational environment—not on the AI model alone.

Successful governance requires a traceable relationship between business objectives, architecture, threat modeling, data and model assurance, enforceable security controls, operational safety, resilience, risk acceptance, and continuous monitoring.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **AI capability vs. authorized use:** A system's technical capabilities do not define the permissions granted to it.
- **Model integrity vs. data integrity:** Both must be protected because trustworthy models can still produce misleading outputs from untrustworthy data.
- **Cybersecurity vs. operational safety:** Cybersecurity reduces malicious compromise; safety assurance addresses hazards and unacceptable operational outcomes.
- **Risk score vs. release decision:** A numerical rating supports prioritization but cannot override mandatory requirements or unacceptable consequences.
- **Control implementation vs. control effectiveness:** A configured safeguard must be verified under appropriate conditions.
- **AI availability vs. essential-service continuity:** Maintaining the AI service is not always necessary if a safe, validated fallback preserves the essential function.
- **Supplier assurance vs. supplier dependence:** Assessing a supplier's security is not enough unless the organization also understands the consequences of supplier failure.
- **Audit documentation vs. operational evidence:** Documents describe intended controls; implementation and test evidence demonstrate whether those controls operate as expected.
- **Risk acceptance vs. risk elimination:** Acceptance retains remaining exposure and requires appropriate authority; it does not mean that the risk has disappeared.
- **Initial approval vs. lifecycle authorization:** Authorization remains valid only while its underlying assumptions, operating scope, and control requirements remain satisfied.
