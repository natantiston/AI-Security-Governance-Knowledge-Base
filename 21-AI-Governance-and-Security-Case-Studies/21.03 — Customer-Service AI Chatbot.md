# 22.03 — Generative AI Terminology
## Part 1 — Foundations and Core Concepts

## 1. Introduction to Generative Artificial Intelligence

**Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI or GenAI)** refers to a category of AI systems designed to generate new content based on patterns learned from data. Generated outputs may include text, images, audio, video, software code, synthetic data, and other forms of digital content.

Traditional AI systems often classify, predict, detect, or recommend. Generative AI can perform these functions as well, but it is distinguished by its ability to produce new outputs that resemble or extend the patterns represented in its training data.

Examples include:
- Generating business reports from structured instructions.
- Producing images from natural-language descriptions.
- Summarizing lengthy corporate documents.
- Creating software code from functional requirements.
- Generating synthetic datasets for testing.
- Producing audio or video from text prompts.
- Answering questions using retrieved enterprise knowledge.
- Assisting analysts with risk assessments and compliance documentation.

Generative AI is not a single technology. It is an umbrella category encompassing multiple model architectures, training methods, data types, and application patterns.

### 1.1 Generative AI from an enterprise perspective

An enterprise should not evaluate a GenAI solution solely by examining the model. The operational system may also include user interfaces, enterprise data sources, retrieval components, APIs, plugins, identity services, external vendors, and downstream business applications.

Consider an enterprise assistant that helps employees interpret internal cybersecurity policies.

```text
Employee
   |
   v
Enterprise AI Assistant
   |
   v
Application and Access Controls
   |
   +--------------------+
   |                    |
   v                    v
Generative AI Model   Enterprise Knowledge Sources
   |                    |
   +---------+----------+
             |
             v
       Generated Answer
             |
             v
       Employee Review
             |
             v
       Business Decision
```

The model may generate a technically convincing answer, but the enterprise must also determine whether the employee was authorized to access the underlying documents, whether the answer is accurate, whether confidential information was exposed, and whether the output is appropriate for the intended business decision.

### 1.2 Why Generative AI terminology matters for governance

Generative AI introduces concepts that do not always map neatly to conventional software governance.

For example:

| Traditional IT concept | Related GenAI consideration |
|---|---|
| Application input | Natural-language prompt, uploaded document, image, audio, or other input |
| Application output | Generated text, code, image, audio, or other content |
| Business logic | Model behavior, instructions, orchestration logic, and application rules |
| Database query | May involve retrieval-augmented generation or other information-retrieval mechanisms |
| Software defect | May involve deterministic software defects, model limitations, or unpredictable output behavior |
| Access control | Must cover application access, model capabilities, data retrieval, and connected tools |
| Change management | May need to account for model updates, prompts, datasets, retrieval indexes, and configuration changes |
| Quality assurance | May require statistical evaluation, adversarial testing, and repeated testing across representative scenarios |
| Security incident | May involve data disclosure, prompt injection, unsafe output, or unauthorized tool execution |

These are conceptual comparisons, not direct equivalences. A prompt is not the same as a database query, and a model hallucination is not necessarily the same as a conventional software defect.

### 1.3 Governance implications

The terminology used in GenAI projects influences how organizations define controls, assign responsibilities, assess risks, and establish acceptance criteria.

A GRC professional should be able to distinguish among:
- The model and the complete AI application.
- Training data and information supplied at runtime.
- Generated content and verified information.
- Model behavior and deterministic application logic.
- A security control and a model capability.
- A technical limitation and an accepted business risk.
- A model evaluation result and an assurance conclusion.

**Core Principle:** Generative AI governance must address the model, the surrounding application, the information it processes, the people who use it, and the business decisions influenced by its outputs.

**Key conceptual distinction:** A generative model is a component of an AI-enabled solution, not necessarily the complete solution.

## 2. Generative AI (GenAI)

**Definition:** Generative AI is a class of artificial intelligence that produces new content or representations based on patterns learned from data or other forms of model conditioning.

Generative AI systems may generate outputs from text, images, audio, video, structured data, or combinations of these modalities.

### 2.1 Common generative AI capabilities

| Capability | Description | Enterprise example | Governance consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text generation | Produces natural-language content | Drafting a policy document | Accuracy, confidentiality, and human review |
| Summarization | Produces a shorter representation of source material | Summarizing audit findings | Omission of critical facts and misleading summaries |
| Image generation | Creates or modifies visual content | Generating marketing concepts | Intellectual property, impersonation, and misuse |
| Audio generation | Produces speech or other audio | Creating synthetic voice announcements | Voice impersonation and consent |
| Video generation | Produces or modifies video | Creating training material | Deepfakes and misleading representations |
| Code generation | Produces software code | Drafting a security automation script | Vulnerabilities, insecure dependencies, and licensing |
| Synthetic-data generation | Produces artificial data resembling characteristics of real data | Creating test datasets | Re-identification risk and statistical validity |
| Multimodal generation | Produces or combines multiple content types | Analyzing an image and generating a written report | Cross-modal attacks and inconsistent interpretation |

### 2.2 Generative AI versus traditional predictive AI

Traditional predictive AI commonly estimates a value, assigns a category, or calculates a probability. Generative AI produces content or representations. The distinction is useful, but it is not absolute: modern systems may combine predictive and generative capabilities.

| Dimension | Predictive AI | Generative AI |
|---|---|---|
| Typical objective | Predict, classify, rank, or detect | Generate or transform content |
| Typical output | Label, score, probability, forecast, or ranking | Text, image, audio, code, or another generated representation |
| Example | Detecting potentially fraudulent transactions | Generating an explanation of a flagged transaction |
| Common evaluation | Precision, recall, false-positive rate, calibration | Task quality, factual accuracy, relevance, safety, robustness |
| Security concern | Manipulated inputs, model evasion, data poisoning | Prompt injection, unsafe outputs, information disclosure, model abuse |
| Governance concern | Decision validity and discriminatory impact | Output reliability, content provenance, appropriate reliance, and misuse |

These categories can overlap. A generative model may classify information, while a predictive system may generate explanations or recommendations around its predictions.

### 2.3 Generative AI does not imply autonomous decision-making

A system that generates text is not necessarily an autonomous agent.

For example, a system may draft a recommendation for a human analyst without being permitted to execute any action. Another system may use a model to select tools, retrieve records, and initiate approved workflows.

The governance requirements depend on the complete system's capabilities, permissions, and operational context—not merely on whether it uses a generative model.

### 2.4 Security and GRC considerations

Organizations should determine:

1. What content the system can generate.
2. Which users and business processes can access it.
3. What information is supplied to the model.
4. Whether generated content can trigger automated actions.
5. Whether outputs require human approval.
6. How output quality and harmful behavior are evaluated.
7. Whether usage complies with contractual, legal, privacy, and intellectual-property obligations.
8. How model and application changes are controlled.

**Core Principle:** Generative AI describes a content-generation capability. It does not, by itself, define the system's autonomy, reliability, security posture, or permitted business use.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Content generation and autonomous action are different capabilities and must be assessed separately.

## 3. Generative Model

**Definition:** A generative model is a model that learns patterns or a probability distribution from data and uses that learned representation to generate new samples or content.

The exact mechanism depends on the model architecture. Different generative models may learn to predict tokens, denoise data, model probability distributions, or transform inputs into generated outputs.

### 3.1 How generative models work conceptually

A simplified conceptual process is:

```text
Training Data
     |
     v
Data Preparation and Processing
     |
     v
Model Training or Adaptation
     |
     v
Trained Model
     |
     v
Runtime Input or Conditioning
     |
     v
Generation Process
     |
     v
Generated Output
     |
     v
Application Validation and Use
```

This diagram represents a simplified lifecycle. Real systems may use multiple training stages, external tools, retrieval mechanisms, safety components, or post-processing services.

### 3.2 Important characteristics

**Learned patterns:** The model learns statistical or structural relationships represented in its training process. It does not necessarily store or understand information in the same way a human does.

**Conditional generation:** Many models generate content based on supplied inputs, instructions, context, or other conditioning signals.

**Sampling and decoding:** Some generation processes select among possible outputs according to model scores and decoding settings. Different settings can affect output variability.

**Model limitations:** A model may produce plausible but incorrect, incomplete, biased, or inappropriate content.

**Dependence on context:** Output quality can depend on the clarity of instructions, the relevance of the supplied information, and the model's learned capabilities.

### 3.3 Security implications

A generative model's behavior can be influenced by several factors:

- The quality and characteristics of training data.
- The model architecture and training methodology.
- Runtime prompts and contextual inputs.
- Model configuration and decoding parameters.
- Fine-tuning or other adaptation procedures.
- Retrieved information and external tool results.
- Application-level restrictions and validation controls.
- Changes introduced by the model provider.

These factors create different risk categories. A weakness in training data governance is not the same as a prompt-injection vulnerability, and a flawed application permission model is not necessarily a defect in the underlying model.

### 3.4 Governance implications

A model inventory should identify, where applicable:

| Inventory attribute | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Model name and version | Identifies the model in use |
| Model provider or developer | Establishes accountability and supply-chain context |
| Model type and architecture category | Supports technical classification |
| Intended purpose | Defines approved use |
| Prohibited or restricted uses | Establishes boundaries |
| Training or adaptation information | Records available information about how the model was developed or modified |
| Runtime data categories | Identifies information supplied during use |
| Known limitations | Documents material constraints and failure modes |
| Evaluation evidence | Supports performance, safety, and security assessments |
| Update mechanism | Identifies how model changes are introduced |
| Business owner | Establishes accountable ownership |

Some information, especially proprietary training details, may not be available from an external model provider. In such cases, the organization should record the limitation and assess whether the available transparency is sufficient for the intended use.

**Core Principle:** A generative model's learned behavior is only one part of the enterprise risk picture. Governance must also account for runtime inputs, application controls, deployment configuration, and intended use.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Model risk concerns the model's capabilities and limitations; application risk also includes how the model is integrated, configured, accessed, and used.

## 4. Foundation Model

**Definition:** A foundation model is a model trained on broad data at scale and designed to support a range of downstream tasks or applications, often through prompting, adaptation, fine-tuning, or integration with other components.

Foundation models can support language, vision, audio, multimodal processing, and other capabilities.

The term is commonly associated with large pretrained models, but the defining idea is their broad applicability as a foundation for multiple downstream uses—not simply their size.

### 4.1 Foundation models and downstream applications

A single foundation model may support multiple enterprise applications.

```text
                 Foundation Model
                       |
          +------------+------------+
          |            |            |
          v            v            v
     HR Assistant  Legal Assistant  Security Assistant
          |            |            |
          v            v            v
     HR Knowledge  Legal Records  Security Policies
          |            |            |
          v            v            v
      HR Controls  Legal Controls  Security Controls
```

The applications may share the same underlying model while having different data sources, users, permissions, business purposes, and risk profiles.

Consequently, approving the foundation model does not automatically approve every application built on it.

### 4.2 Foundation model versus application

| Dimension | Foundation model | AI application |
|---|---|---|
| Primary function | Provides general learned capabilities | Delivers a defined user or business function |
| Scope | Potentially supports multiple tasks | Usually designed for a specific workflow or set of workflows |
| Data access | Depends on its deployment and integration | May access databases, documents, APIs, and business systems |
| User permissions | May be provided by a platform or integration | Must be enforced across the complete application and connected resources |
| Business rules | May be limited or absent at the model level | Can include workflow logic, approval requirements, and business constraints |
| Risk assessment | Evaluates model-level capabilities and limitations | Evaluates the model plus integration, data, access, workflow, and use |
| Approval | May be approved for defined deployment conditions | Requires assessment against its specific intended purpose and operating environment |

### 4.3 Foundation models and third-party risk

Organizations consuming a foundation model through an external provider should consider:

- Provider security and privacy practices.
- Contractual restrictions on data use.
- Whether submitted data may be used for training or service improvement.
- Data retention and deletion arrangements.
- Geographic processing and storage considerations.
- Model versioning and change notifications.
- Service availability and resilience.
- Security testing and transparency evidence.
- Subprocessors and other supply-chain dependencies.
- Exit arrangements and alternative service options.

These considerations vary according to the deployment model and contract. An externally hosted model does not necessarily use customer inputs for training, and a model deployed within an organization's own infrastructure does not automatically eliminate all security or privacy risks.

### 4.4 Foundation model governance

A practical governance approach distinguishes three assessment layers:

1. **Model-level assessment:** Evaluates the model's capabilities, known limitations, security characteristics, provider information, and available evaluation evidence.
2. **Application-level assessment:** Evaluates the implementation, data sources, user access, integrations, workflow controls, and intended use.
3. **Use-case-level assessment:** Evaluates the specific business purpose, affected stakeholders, decision consequences, and acceptable level of risk.

A foundation model may be suitable for one use case but unsuitable for another. For example, a model that performs adequately when drafting internal meeting summaries may not be sufficiently reliable for independently making high-impact employment decisions.

**Core Principle:** Foundation models enable reuse, but model approval must not become blanket authorization for every downstream application.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Reusing an approved model does not eliminate the need to assess each application's architecture, data access, business purpose, and risk exposure.

## 5. Pretrained Model

**Definition:** A pretrained model is a model that has already undergone a training stage before being used for a subsequent task, adapted for a particular purpose, or deployed in an application.

Pretraining can establish general capabilities that are subsequently refined through additional training, fine-tuning, or other adaptation methods.

### 5.1 Why pretraining matters

Training a capable model from scratch can require substantial data, computing resources, specialist expertise, and operational investment. Organizations may therefore use pretrained models supplied by commercial providers, research organizations, or open-model developers.

A pretrained model may be used directly or adapted to improve performance on a particular domain or task.

Examples include:
- Using a pretrained language model to summarize enterprise documents.
- Adapting a model to recognize specialized terminology.
- Fine-tuning a model on curated examples of customer-service interactions.
- Deploying a pretrained vision model for image analysis.

### 5.2 Pretrained does not mean production-ready

A pretrained model may have strong general capabilities while still being unsuitable for a particular enterprise environment.

Potential concerns include:

- Insufficient performance on domain-specific tasks.
- Inadequate handling of sensitive information.
- Biases inherited from training data.
- Unknown or poorly documented data provenance.
- Weaknesses under adversarial inputs.
- Inadequate security evaluation.
- Licensing or usage restrictions.
- Incompatibility with enterprise operational requirements.

A pretrained model should therefore be assessed against the intended use rather than accepted solely on the basis of its reputation, benchmark results, or provider claims.

### 5.3 Pretraining and data governance

Pretraining may involve large and diverse datasets. The enterprise consuming a pretrained model may not control those datasets or have access to complete information about their origins.

This creates a distinction between **data governance for model development** and **data governance for model deployment**.

| Governance area | Model-development perspective | Enterprise-deployment perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Data provenance | What sources contributed to training? | What data is supplied to the deployed application? |
| Data rights | Were training data and associated rights appropriately managed? | Are the enterprise's uses permitted by applicable terms and law? |
| Personal information | How was personal information handled during training? | How is personal information processed during inference and storage? |
| Security | How were training pipelines and datasets protected? | How are prompts, retrieved records, outputs, and integrations protected? |
| Quality | How representative and reliable were the training datasets? | Is the model suitable for the enterprise's specific users and tasks? |
| Transparency | What information is available about development? | Is the available evidence sufficient for the enterprise's risk decision? |

The deploying organization may not be able to independently verify all development-stage controls. It should identify evidence gaps and determine whether contractual protections, independent assurance, technical testing, or use restrictions are necessary.

### 5.4 GRC assessment questions

A reviewer should ask:

1. Who developed and supplied the pretrained model?
2. What information is available about its training and evaluation?
3. Are the model's licensing and usage terms acceptable?
4. What known limitations are relevant to the intended use?
5. Has the model been evaluated using representative enterprise scenarios?
6. Does the application introduce additional security risks?
7. Are there restrictions on model modification, redistribution, or deployment?
8. What happens when the provider changes the model or discontinues support?

**Core Principle:** Pretraining provides an initial set of learned capabilities; it does not establish fitness for a particular business purpose.

**Key conceptual distinction:** A model's general training history and its suitability for a specific enterprise use case are separate assessment questions.

## 6. Inference

**Definition:** Inference is the process of using a trained model to produce a prediction, classification, representation, or generated output from an input.

In generative AI, inference is the runtime process through which a model processes a prompt or other input and produces generated content.

### 6.1 The inference lifecycle

A simplified text-generation workflow is:

```text
User Request
     |
     v
Input Validation
     |
     v
Prompt and Context Construction
     |
     v
Model Inference
     |
     v
Generated Tokens or Other Output
     |
     v
Output Validation and Safety Checks
     |
     v
Application Response
```

Not every implementation follows these steps in precisely this order. Some systems apply additional filters before or after model inference, while others use retrieval or tools as part of a larger orchestration workflow.

### 6.2 Training versus inference

| Dimension | Training | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Adjusts model parameters or otherwise learns from data | Uses an existing model to produce outputs |
| Typical timing | During model development or an adaptation process | During runtime use |
| Input | Training examples, labels where applicable, and other learning signals | Prompts, user inputs, context, or other runtime data |
| Primary output | A trained or adapted model | A prediction or generated result |
| Main governance concerns | Data provenance, training integrity, data quality, and development security | Input confidentiality, access control, output reliability, and runtime security |
| Typical changes | Model parameters or training artifacts may change | Outputs are produced using the deployed model and current configuration |

Inference does not normally mean that the model is being retrained. A model can produce many outputs during inference without changing its learned parameters.

However, an application may separately log interactions, store feedback, update a retrieval index, or use collected information in a later training process. Those activities should be governed independently.

### 6.3 Inference-time risks

Inference introduces risks that may differ from training-stage risks.

**Sensitive-data exposure:** Confidential information may be submitted to a model or inadvertently included in its output.

**Prompt injection:** Malicious instructions in user input or external content may attempt to influence model behavior.

**Unsafe generation:** The model may produce harmful, inaccurate, discriminatory, or policy-violating content.

**Excessive resource consumption:** Repeated or unusually large requests may increase costs or exhaust computational resources.

**Unauthorized access through integrations:** An application may use model-generated instructions or outputs to interact with systems in ways that exceed permitted authority if authorization is poorly designed.

**Inconsistent outputs:** Similar inputs may produce different results, depending on the model, settings, context, and generation process.

### 6.4 Inference cost and operational control

Inference can consume computing resources each time the model is invoked. Depending on the service and architecture, cost may depend on input volume, output volume, model size, processing time, hardware utilization, or other provider-specific measures.

Operational controls may include:
- Rate limits and quotas.
- Maximum input and output lengths.
- User and application authentication.
- Usage monitoring and anomaly detection.
- Budget thresholds and cost alerts.
- Timeout and retry controls.
- Service availability monitoring.
- Logging designed to avoid unnecessary sensitive-data retention.

The correct controls depend on the deployment architecture and business criticality.

**Core Principle:** Inference is the runtime use of a trained model. Runtime security must address both the information entering the model and the outputs and actions resulting from its use.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Processing a prompt during inference does not automatically mean the model is learning from that prompt or retaining it for future training.

## 7. Prompt

**Definition:** A prompt is an input supplied to a generative AI model to influence or guide its response. A prompt may contain a question, instruction, task description, contextual information, examples, formatting requirements, or a combination of these elements.

Prompts may be supplied directly by users or constructed programmatically by an application.

### 7.1 Common prompt components

| Component | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Instruction | Defines the task the model should perform | “Summarize the audit findings.” |
| Context | Provides relevant background information | “The findings relate to an ISO 27001 internal audit.” |
| Input data | Supplies material to process | A pasted audit observation |
| Constraints | Specifies boundaries or requirements | “Do not include personal identifiers.” |
| Output format | Defines the expected response structure | “Return a table with risk, impact, and recommendation.” |
| Examples | Demonstrates a desired response pattern | A sample risk-register entry |

Not every prompt contains every component. The design depends on the task, the application, and the model's capabilities.

### 7.2 User prompts and system instructions

A generative AI application may combine several kinds of instructions and contextual inputs.

- **System instructions:** High-level instructions configured by the model platform or application to define behavior and boundaries.
- **Developer instructions:** Instructions supplied by the application developer to guide how the application uses the model.
- **User prompts:** Requests and information supplied by the user.
- **Retrieved context:** Documents or other information retrieved from external sources and provided to the model.
- **Tool results:** Information returned by connected tools or services and supplied to the model.

The exact terminology and precedence rules vary by model platform. These categories should not be assumed to have identical implementation semantics across providers.

A simplified example:

```text
Application Instructions
        |
        v
User Request
        |
        v
Relevant Retrieved Context
        |
        v
Constructed Model Input
        |
        v
Model Response
        |
        v
Application-Level Validation
```

A critical security consideration is that retrieved documents and user-supplied content may contain instructions that should be treated as untrusted data rather than trusted control instructions.

### 7.3 Prompt engineering

**Prompt engineering** is the practice of designing and refining prompts to improve the relevance, clarity, consistency, or usefulness of model outputs.

Common techniques include:
- Giving explicit task instructions.
- Providing relevant context.
- Defining output structure.
- Specifying constraints.
- Providing examples.
- Breaking complex tasks into manageable steps.
- Asking the model to identify uncertainty or missing information.

Prompt engineering can improve performance, but it is not a substitute for security architecture, access control, testing, or independent verification.

For example, adding “Do not reveal confidential information” to a prompt does not reliably enforce confidentiality. The application must implement appropriate authorization and data-handling controls.

### 7.4 Prompt security and governance

Prompts can contain:
- Confidential business information.
- Personal information.
- Credentials or authentication tokens.
- Proprietary source code.
- Customer records.
- Security architecture details.
- Untrusted instructions embedded in supplied documents.

Organizations should establish rules governing what employees may submit to approved AI systems and how prompts are processed, logged, retained, and protected.

A prompt may also expose a governance issue if the task itself is prohibited, such as requesting unauthorized processing of personal data or asking the model to make a consequential decision without required human oversight.

### 7.5 Prompt-related assessment questions

A GRC or security reviewer should determine:

1. Who can submit prompts?
2. What categories of information may be included?
3. Are prompts stored, logged, or shared with third parties?
4. Are sensitive inputs minimized or appropriately protected?
5. Can prompts influence access to tools or external systems?
6. How are malicious instructions in user input or retrieved content handled?
7. Are prompts and their templates subject to change control?
8. Can prompt changes alter the system's risk profile?
9. How are prompts tested against representative and adversarial scenarios?
10. What evidence demonstrates that prompt-related controls operate effectively?

**Core Principle:** Prompts influence model behavior, but instructions alone are not dependable security boundaries. Enforceable controls must be implemented in the application, infrastructure, and surrounding governance processes.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Prompt engineering seeks to improve model behavior; security engineering establishes and enforces the boundaries within which that behavior is allowed.

## 8. Generated Output

**Definition:** Generated output is the content produced by a generative AI model in response to an input, prompt, or other conditioning information.

Generated outputs may include prose, summaries, recommendations, code, images, audio, video, structured records, or intermediate content used by another system.

### 8.1 Types of generated output

| Output type | Example | Principal governance concern |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive content | A summary of a policy | Factual accuracy and omission |
| Analytical content | A risk assessment narrative | Unsupported conclusions and incorrect reasoning |
| Recommendations | Suggested remediation actions | Inappropriate reliance and decision accountability |
| Structured data | A JSON object containing risk fields | Schema validity and semantic correctness |
| Software code | A proposed automation script | Security vulnerabilities and unsafe execution |
| Synthetic media | A generated voice or image | Deception, impersonation, and content provenance |
| Automated-action instructions | A proposed API call or workflow command | Authorization, validation, and unintended effects |

The output format does not determine whether the content is trustworthy. A correctly formatted response may still contain false information or unsafe recommendations.

### 8.2 Output validation

Organizations should determine what validation is required before an output is used.

Possible validation controls include:

- Factual verification against authoritative sources.
- Schema and format validation.
- Policy and content checks.
- Sensitive-data detection.
- Code analysis and testing.
- Human review for consequential decisions.
- Authorization checks before actions are executed.
- Reconciliation with authoritative business records.
- Monitoring for anomalous or unsafe outputs.

The required validation level should be proportionate to the potential impact of an error.

For example, an AI-generated brainstorming list may need limited review. A recommendation affecting employee termination, credit eligibility, medical treatment, or critical infrastructure requires substantially stronger controls and may be subject to specific legal obligations.

### 8.3 Output quality dimensions

Generated output should be assessed against multiple dimensions rather than a single general quality score.

| Dimension | Assessment question |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | Are factual claims correct? |
| Relevance | Does the response address the intended task? |
| Completeness | Are material facts or considerations missing? |
| Consistency | Does the response contradict itself or authoritative information? |
| Safety | Does it contain harmful or prohibited content? |
| Privacy | Does it disclose personal or confidential information improperly? |
| Fairness | Could it create unjustified or discriminatory outcomes? |
| Traceability | Can important claims be linked to evidence or authoritative sources where required? |
| Format validity | Does the output conform to the required structure? |
| Action safety | Could downstream use produce unauthorized or harmful effects? |

These dimensions are related but distinct. A response may be relevant and well formatted while being factually incorrect.

### 8.4 Human review and accountability

Human review is an important control for many enterprise GenAI applications, particularly when outputs influence material decisions.

However, human review must be meaningful. A reviewer needs appropriate competence, sufficient time, access to supporting evidence, and authority to reject or correct the output.

Simply requiring a person to click an approval button does not establish effective oversight.

A practical control model is:

```text
AI-Generated Output
        |
        v
Automated Validation
        |
        v
Risk-Based Review Requirement
        |
        +------------------------+
        |                        |
        v                        v
Low-Risk Permitted Use     High-Impact or Sensitive Use
        |                        |
        v                        v
Controlled Use             Qualified Human Review
                                 |
                                 v
                         Approve / Correct / Reject
                                 |
                                 v
                         Authorized Business Action
```

This is an illustrative governance pattern. Some outputs may be prohibited entirely, while others may be used automatically if appropriate testing, authorization, and monitoring controls have been established.

### 8.5 Output ownership and records management

Organizations should define who is responsible for reviewing, approving, publishing, or acting on AI-generated content.

Relevant questions include:
- Is the output a draft or an approved business record?
- Who is accountable for the final decision?
- Must the output be retained for audit or legal purposes?
- Does the output contain confidential or personal information?
- Is disclosure of AI assistance required?
- Must the output be linked to source evidence?
- Can the output be distributed externally?
- What correction or escalation process applies when an error is discovered?

Generated content may become a business record, a contractual deliverable, a customer communication, or evidence supporting a decision. Its status should be determined by the organization's policies and applicable obligations.

**Core Principle:** Generated output should be treated according to its purpose, risk, and evidential status—not presumed correct simply because it is fluent, detailed, or confidently presented.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Output generation is not output validation. A model's ability to produce an answer does not establish that the answer is accurate, safe, authorized, or suitable for operational use.

## 9. Conditional Generation

**Definition:** Conditional generation is a generation process in which a model's output is influenced by specified input information, constraints, context, or other conditioning signals.

In GenAI, the model may generate content based on a text prompt, an image, audio input, structured information, retrieved documents, or a combination of inputs.

### 9.1 Examples of conditional generation

| Conditioning input | Generated output | Enterprise example |
|---|---|---|
| Natural-language prompt | Text | Drafting a supplier assessment |
| Image | Text or another image | Describing a product image |
| Audio recording | Text or audio | Producing a meeting transcript or summary |
| Text instructions and examples | Structured text | Generating a risk-register entry |
| Retrieved enterprise documents | Grounded response | Answering questions about internal policies |
| Structured business data | Narrative explanation | Explaining a financial variance |

The model's output depends on both the conditioning information and its learned behavior. Providing relevant context can improve results, but it does not guarantee factual accuracy.

### 9.2 Conditional generation and enterprise context

Consider an AI assistant asked to assess a third-party vendor using an internal questionnaire and the vendor's security documentation.

The application may provide:
- The user's request.
- The vendor's questionnaire responses.
- Security certifications and supporting evidence.
- Internal assessment criteria.
- A required output format.

The model may generate a preliminary assessment based on these inputs. However, it may misinterpret evidence, overlook contradictions, or treat unsupported claims as established facts.

A responsible implementation should require the reviewer to distinguish among:
1. Information explicitly supported by evidence.
2. Information inferred from available evidence.
3. Information that remains unknown.
4. Information that requires further validation.

### 9.3 Governance considerations

Conditional generation raises questions about:
- Input provenance and trustworthiness.
- Authorization to access the conditioning data.
- The possibility of malicious instructions embedded in supplied content.
- Whether the input is sufficient for the requested task.
- Whether output claims can be traced to supporting evidence.
- Whether missing information is communicated clearly.
- Whether the model is permitted to use particular data categories.

**Core Principle:** Conditioning information can guide generation, but its presence does not make the information complete, authoritative, or trustworthy.

**Key conceptual distinction:** A model can be conditioned on a document without having verified that the document is accurate or that the user is authorized to access it.

## 10. Model Parameters and Generation Parameters

These terms describe different concepts and should not be used interchangeably.

### 10.1 Model parameters

**Definition:** Model parameters are learned numerical values, such as weights and biases, that influence the model's behavior.

During training, optimization procedures adjust model parameters according to the training objective. During ordinary inference, the deployed model typically uses its existing parameters to process inputs and produce outputs.

Model parameters are central to the model's learned capabilities. They are distinct from the prompts, user inputs, and runtime settings used to invoke the model.

### 10.2 Generation parameters

**Definition:** Generation parameters are runtime settings that influence how a generative model produces an output.

Depending on the model and platform, these may include temperature, top-p, maximum output length, and other decoding or generation controls.

Generation parameters generally influence the selection or form of outputs without constituting a new training process.

### 10.3 Common generation parameters

| Term | General meaning | Potential effect | Governance consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Controls the degree to which token selection is influenced by the model's output distribution | Higher values often increase variation; lower values often produce more predictable selections | Test whether the setting is appropriate for the use case |
| Top-p | Restricts sampling to a set of candidate tokens whose cumulative probability reaches a specified threshold | Can affect diversity and variation | Document and validate configuration changes |
| Maximum output tokens | Sets a limit on the amount of generated token content | May constrain response length or truncate output | Ensure critical information is not silently omitted |
| Random seed | Where supported, helps control randomness in generation | May improve repeatability under particular conditions | Do not assume identical outputs across model versions or environments |
| Stop sequences | Where supported, specifies content patterns that terminate generation | Can limit or truncate output | Verify that output is not cut off in a way that creates unsafe or misleading results |

Availability, semantics, and exact effects vary by model and provider. These settings do not provide universal guarantees about accuracy, security, or repeatability.

### 10.4 Model parameters versus generation parameters

| Dimension | Model parameters | Generation parameters |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Learned numerical values within the model | Runtime configuration values |
| Established through | Training or model adaptation | Application or inference configuration |
| Primary influence | Learned behavior and capabilities | How outputs are generated from the model's available choices |
| Typical change process | Training, fine-tuning, or model replacement | Configuration update |
| Governance implication | Model version control and change assessment | Configuration management and regression testing |

### 10.5 Configuration management

Organizations should identify which model and generation settings are used in production, who can change them, and how changes are evaluated.

A configuration change may affect:
- Output variability.
- Response length.
- Task performance.
- Cost and latency.
- Safety behavior.
- The reproducibility of test results.

Changing a generation parameter is not automatically a high-risk change, but the impact should be evaluated in the context of the application. For example, a change that increases output length may affect cost, while a change that alters generation behavior may affect an application that relies on consistent structured responses.

**Core Principle:** Learned model parameters and runtime generation settings influence outputs in different ways and should be governed as distinct configuration categories.

**Key conceptual distinction:** Changing a generation setting is not the same as retraining or fine-tuning the model, although either type of change may require testing and approval.

## 11. Generative AI Governance Vocabulary: Summary

The terms in this lesson establish a foundation for discussing GenAI systems in a technically precise and governance-oriented manner.

| Term | Concise definition | Governance significance |
|---|---|---|
| Generative AI | AI that generates new content or representations | Establishes the broad technology category |
| Generative model | A model capable of generating samples or content based on learned patterns | Supports model-level risk assessment |
| Foundation model | A broadly trained model that can support multiple downstream tasks | Requires separation of model approval from application approval |
| Pretrained model | A model trained before a subsequent use or adaptation stage | Requires fitness-for-purpose assessment |
| Inference | Runtime use of a trained model to produce an output | Focuses attention on runtime security and data handling |
| Prompt | Input used to guide a model's response | Raises confidentiality, input-validation, and prompt-security concerns |
| Generated output | Content produced by the model | Requires risk-based validation and appropriate accountability |
| Conditional generation | Generation influenced by specified inputs or context | Requires assessment of input quality, provenance, and trust |
| Model parameters | Learned numerical values that influence model behavior | Relevant to model versions, training, and adaptation |
| Generation parameters | Runtime settings that influence output generation | Relevant to configuration control and regression testing |

### Practical application for GRC professionals

When reviewing a GenAI solution, use these terms to structure the initial assessment:

1. **Identify the model:** Determine whether the system uses a foundation model, a specialized model, or a combination of models.
2. **Identify the operating mode:** Establish whether the model is used for inference only, adapted through fine-tuning, or incorporated into a broader learning pipeline.
3. **Identify the inputs:** Document prompts, uploaded files, retrieved data, and other information supplied during runtime.
4. **Identify the outputs:** Determine what content is generated and how it is consumed.
5. **Identify the conditioning context:** Establish which documents, instructions, and other sources influence responses.
6. **Identify configuration controls:** Record relevant model versions, generation settings, and change-management requirements.
7. **Identify validation controls:** Determine how the organization detects inaccurate, unsafe, unauthorized, or incomplete outputs.
8. **Identify accountability:** Assign responsibility for approving the use case, reviewing outputs where necessary, and accepting residual risk.

A technically accurate vocabulary improves the quality of risk registers, control matrices, assessment questionnaires, architecture reviews, and management decisions.

**Core Principle:** Precise terminology is a governance control in its own right. It reduces ambiguity between model capabilities, application behavior, runtime configuration, data handling, and business accountability.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**
- Generative AI is a capability category; a foundation model is one type of model used to deliver that capability.
- Pretraining establishes learned capabilities; inference uses an existing model to produce outputs.
- A prompt guides model behavior; it does not reliably enforce security boundaries.
- Generated output is a result, not proof of correctness.
- Model parameters represent learned values; generation parameters configure aspects of runtime output production.
- Model-level approval does not replace application-level and use-case-level risk assessment.
