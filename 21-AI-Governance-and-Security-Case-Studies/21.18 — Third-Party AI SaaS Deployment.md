# Chapter 21.18 — Third-Party AI SaaS Deployment
## Part 1: Enterprise Context, Architecture, Supplier Dependencies, and Risk Landscape

## 1. Introduction to Third-Party AI SaaS Deployment

### 1.1 Definition and Scope

A **Third-Party AI Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) deployment** occurs when an organization adopts an artificial intelligence capability operated wholly or partly by an external provider rather than building and operating the complete AI solution within its own environment.

The provider may supply an AI-enabled business application, a generative AI assistant, a machine-learning platform, an AI-powered customer service solution, or an API that allows the organization to integrate AI capabilities into existing business systems.

Examples include:

- An enterprise deploying an externally hosted generative AI assistant for employees.
- A financial institution integrating a third-party AI API into its customer service platform.
- A telecommunications company using a supplier-hosted AI system to analyze customer complaints.
- A human resources department adopting an AI recruitment platform.
- A cybersecurity team using a vendor-provided AI service to analyze security alerts.
- A multinational organization integrating an AI-enabled customer relationship management platform into its business processes.

In these deployments, the enterprise does not necessarily control the AI model, the underlying infrastructure, the training process, the model-update schedule, or the provider's internal security operations.

The enterprise may control user access, business configuration, data submission, integration permissions, and certain monitoring capabilities. However, these controls do not eliminate the risks associated with the provider's internal operations or subcontractors.

**The central governance challenge is that the enterprise remains accountable for its business decisions, data protection obligations, and risk acceptance even when another organization operates the AI service.**

### 1.2 What Makes Third-Party AI SaaS Different from Conventional SaaS?

Traditional SaaS governance already addresses identity management, data protection, service availability, supplier assurance, contractual obligations, and incident response.

AI introduces additional concerns because the service may generate outputs, make predictions, classify people or transactions, retrieve information, or recommend actions using probabilistic models.

The enterprise must therefore assess both conventional cloud-service risks and risks arising from AI behavior, model dependencies, and the provider's AI lifecycle.

| Governance dimension | Conventional SaaS concern | Additional AI SaaS concern |
|---|---|---|
| Data protection | Unauthorized access to stored data | Sensitive information may be submitted through prompts, retrieved through connected sources, or retained in provider-controlled systems |
| Confidentiality | Unauthorized disclosure of application data | The service may expose information through generated outputs, retrieval behavior, or insecure integrations |
| Integrity | Unauthorized modification of records | AI-generated content, classifications, recommendations, or actions may be inaccurate or manipulated |
| Availability | Service outage or degraded performance | Model unavailability, inference limits, provider-side model changes, or dependency failures may disrupt AI-supported processes |
| Access control | Users access functions according to assigned permissions | AI components may inherit, misuse, or exceed permissions granted to connected tools and data sources |
| Change management | Application releases alter functionality | Model updates may change output quality, behavior, safety characteristics, or business suitability |
| Supplier management | Vendor financial, operational, and security risk | Additional uncertainty concerning model provenance, training-data practices, subprocessors, evaluation methods, and AI-specific incidents |
| Compliance | Application processes regulated information | AI outputs may influence regulated decisions or introduce additional obligations concerning transparency, human oversight, and accountability |
| Auditability | Application logs record user and system activity | Logs may need to capture model versions, prompts, retrieved context, tool calls, output decisions, and human approvals |
| Intellectual property | Ownership and permitted use of application data | Rights concerning submitted content, generated outputs, model training, and provider reuse may require explicit contractual treatment |

The distinction is important: **a supplier's general security certification does not establish that its AI service is suitable for every enterprise use case.**

A provider may have strong infrastructure security but inadequate controls for sensitive prompt data, model updates, output validation, or autonomous actions.

### 1.3 The Shared-Responsibility Principle

Third-party AI SaaS governance must distinguish between responsibilities retained by the enterprise and responsibilities performed by the provider.

The provider may operate the infrastructure, host the model, maintain the application, and manage platform-level security. The enterprise remains responsible for determining whether the service is appropriate for its intended purpose and whether its own implementation is adequately controlled.

Typical enterprise responsibilities include:

- Classifying information before it is submitted to the service.
- Defining approved use cases and prohibited activities.
- Selecting an appropriate service and deployment configuration.
- Establishing identity and access controls.
- Securing integrations and connected data sources.
- Validating AI outputs before consequential use.
- Monitoring business outcomes and security events.
- Assessing regulatory and contractual requirements.
- Managing user training and acceptable-use policies.
- Maintaining incident response and exit arrangements.
- Approving residual risks through the appropriate authority.

Typical provider responsibilities may include:

- Securing the hosted infrastructure and service components within its control.
- Managing platform-level access and privileged administration.
- Protecting service data according to contractual commitments.
- Operating the model-serving environment.
- Maintaining service availability and resilience.
- Providing relevant security documentation and incident notifications.
- Managing subprocessors and dependencies within the contractual scope.
- Implementing agreed retention, deletion, and service-security controls.

The exact allocation depends on the service architecture, contract, deployment model, and available configuration options.

A responsibility matrix should identify not only who performs each activity but also who remains accountable for the resulting risk.

| Activity | Enterprise | AI SaaS provider | Shared governance requirement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business use-case approval | Accountable | Consulted | Define approved purposes and boundaries |
| Enterprise data classification | Accountable | Supports through service documentation | Establish permitted data categories |
| Infrastructure security | Verifies supplier assurance | Accountable for provider-controlled infrastructure | Validate the responsibility boundary |
| Identity and access management | Accountable for enterprise identities and permissions | Accountable for provider-side controls | Integrate authentication and authorization securely |
| Model development and updates | Evaluates suitability and monitors impact | Responsible for provider-controlled model lifecycle | Define change notification and revalidation requirements |
| AI output validation | Accountable for business use | Provides available technical safeguards and documentation | Define testing and human-review requirements |
| Incident response | Accountable for enterprise response | Accountable for provider-side response | Coordinate notification, evidence preservation, and containment |
| Data deletion | Verifies enterprise-side deletion and contractual commitments | Executes provider-side deletion within the agreed scope | Validate deletion, backups, retention exceptions, and evidence |
| Regulatory compliance | Accountable for applicable enterprise obligations | Fulfills contractual and legally applicable provider obligations | Map obligations to specific responsibilities |
| Service exit | Accountable for enterprise continuity and migration | Provides agreed export, deletion, and transition support | Test portability and termination procedures |

**Core distinction:** Outsourcing service operation is not equivalent to outsourcing enterprise accountability.

## 2. Business Context and Strategic Drivers

### 2.1 Why Enterprises Adopt Third-Party AI SaaS

Organizations frequently adopt third-party AI SaaS because building a comparable capability internally may require substantial investment in infrastructure, specialized personnel, model development, security engineering, and ongoing maintenance.

A managed AI service can reduce implementation time and provide access to capabilities that would otherwise be difficult to develop.

Common business drivers include:

1. **Faster implementation:** Deploy AI capabilities without building an entire model-serving platform.
2. **Lower initial capital expenditure:** Use a subscription or consumption-based commercial model.
3. **Access to specialized capabilities:** Benefit from commercially available language models, classification systems, analytics, or document-processing services.
4. **Scalability:** Increase or decrease service consumption according to business demand.
5. **Integration with existing applications:** Connect AI capabilities to established CRM, ERP, collaboration, HR, or security platforms.
6. **Reduced operational burden:** Transfer certain infrastructure and platform-maintenance activities to the provider.
7. **Innovation acceleration:** Test use cases without establishing a complete internal AI engineering organization.
8. **Global service availability:** Support distributed employees, customers, and business operations.

These benefits are real, but they should be evaluated against the total risk and cost of operating the service.

A subscription fee does not necessarily represent the complete cost. Integration engineering, compliance reviews, security monitoring, user training, usage controls, independent testing, and eventual migration must also be considered.

### 2.2 Typical Enterprise Use Cases

| Business function | Example AI SaaS capability | Primary business value | Principal governance concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human resources | Recruitment assistance and employee self-service | Faster administrative processing | Employee data protection, bias, and inappropriate decision automation |
| Customer service | Conversational chatbot | Reduced response times and service costs | Incorrect customer guidance, data leakage, and unauthorized actions |
| Finance | Invoice extraction and transaction analysis | Improved processing efficiency | Financial inaccuracies, fraud exposure, and financial-data confidentiality |
| Legal | Contract analysis and document summarization | Faster document review | Privilege, confidentiality, accuracy, and unauthorized disclosure |
| Cybersecurity | AI-assisted alert analysis | Faster investigation and triage | Incorrect prioritization, sensitive-log exposure, and unsafe response actions |
| Sales and marketing | Content generation and customer segmentation | Increased productivity and personalization | Personal-data use, intellectual property, and misleading content |
| IT operations | Incident summarization and troubleshooting | Reduced investigation effort | Exposure of infrastructure details and harmful remediation advice |
| Executive management | Enterprise knowledge assistant | Faster access to business information | Unauthorized retrieval and misleading strategic recommendations |
| Software development | AI coding assistant | Faster development and code review | Proprietary source-code exposure and insecure generated code |

The governance requirements must reflect the business impact of each use case rather than merely the product category.

For example, an AI assistant used to summarize public marketing material presents a different risk profile from the same service connected to confidential customer records and authorized to update financial transactions.

### 2.3 Use-Case Risk Classification

An enterprise should classify proposed AI SaaS use cases before approving deployment.

A practical classification model considers:

- Data sensitivity.
- Business-process criticality.
- Impact on individuals.
- Degree of AI autonomy.
- Integration with enterprise systems.
- Potential financial or operational consequences.
- Applicable legal and contractual requirements.
- Availability of effective human oversight.
- Reversibility of AI-supported actions.

A possible four-tier model is shown below.

| Tier | Classification | Typical example | Minimum governance expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Low impact | Summarizing publicly available information | Approved service, acceptable-use rules, basic output validation |
| Tier 2 | Moderate impact | Drafting internal procedures using approved internal documents | Data classification, access controls, security assessment, output review |
| Tier 3 | High impact | Analyzing customer records or recommending consequential business decisions | Formal risk assessment, privacy and legal review, documented testing, enhanced monitoring, accountable human oversight |
| Tier 4 | Critical or highly consequential | An AI service with privileged access to production systems or authority to execute material transactions | Executive-level risk review, strict authorization boundaries, independent validation, tested containment and recovery, explicit approval of residual risk |

This is an internal governance model, not a universal legal classification. An organization should adapt it to its risk appetite, sector, applicable law, and established AI risk-management framework.

The tier should be determined by the highest material risk characteristic, not by an average score that allows one severe exposure to disappear among lower-risk attributes.

For example, a service handling highly sensitive data should not be classified as low risk simply because its user interface is read-only.

### 2.4 Business Case Versus Risk Case

An AI SaaS business case should be evaluated through two complementary assessments.

**Business case**

- What problem will the service solve?
- What measurable benefit is expected?
- What process improvement is anticipated?
- How will productivity, quality, or cost reduction be measured?
- What implementation and operating costs will be incurred?
- What dependencies will be introduced?

**Risk case**

- What data will leave the enterprise's direct control?
- What security and privacy exposures will arise?
- What happens if the provider changes the model?
- Can the service produce harmful or materially incorrect outputs?
- What happens if the provider becomes unavailable?
- Can the enterprise demonstrate compliance?
- Can the organization exit without unacceptable disruption?

A deployment should not be approved solely because projected productivity gains exceed the subscription cost.

The business case must remain viable after including governance, control implementation, assurance, monitoring, incident response, and exit costs.

### 2.5 Example: Enterprise Deployment Decision

A multinational telecommunications company proposes using a third-party generative AI SaaS service to assist its customer-care teams.

The intended service will:

- Summarize customer complaints.
- Retrieve information from approved support procedures.
- Draft responses for customer-service representatives.
- Recommend troubleshooting steps.
- Integrate with the enterprise CRM.
- Potentially access customer account information.

The expected benefits include faster case handling, more consistent responses, and reduced administrative workload.

However, the proposed deployment introduces several material risks:

| Risk scenario | Business consequence | Required decision |
|---|---|---|
| Customer personal data is retained or reused outside approved terms | Privacy breach and regulatory exposure | Verify provider data-use and retention commitments |
| The AI retrieves information belonging to another customer | Confidentiality breach | Validate authorization enforcement across retrieval and CRM integrations |
| The model recommends an incorrect billing adjustment | Financial loss and customer complaints | Require authorized human approval for adjustments |
| The provider changes model behavior without adequate notice | Reduced service quality or increased risk | Establish change-notification and regression-testing requirements |
| The service becomes unavailable | Customer-service disruption | Define fallback procedures and service-continuity arrangements |
| The provider cannot supply required audit evidence | Assurance and compliance gaps | Establish evidence obligations and audit rights before approval |

The correct decision is not automatically to reject the service. It is to determine whether the business benefits justify the residual risk after the proposed controls are implemented and tested.

**Core Principle:** Business value establishes why the organization wants the service. Risk assessment determines whether the service can be deployed under acceptable conditions.

## 3. Reference Architecture for Third-Party AI SaaS

### 3.1 Enterprise AI SaaS Architecture

A third-party AI SaaS deployment usually contains several interacting components:

1. Enterprise users and business processes.
2. Enterprise identity and access management.
3. User-facing applications.
4. Integration services and API gateways.
5. Enterprise data sources.
6. External AI SaaS endpoints.
7. Provider-controlled model and infrastructure components.
8. Logging, monitoring, and security operations.
9. Governance, policy, and assurance functions.

The architecture must distinguish between components controlled by the enterprise and components operated by the provider.

This distinction is fundamental to risk assessment because the enterprise cannot directly inspect or control every internal provider component.

### 3.3 Trust Boundaries

A **trust boundary** separates components, identities, or organizations with different security assumptions and levels of control.

Third-party AI SaaS deployments commonly contain the following boundaries.

| Boundary | Example | Primary security question |
|---|---|---|
| User-to-enterprise application | Employee signs in to the AI interface | Is the user authenticated and authorized? |
| Enterprise application-to-integration layer | CRM submits a prompt through an API gateway | Are requests validated, authorized, and logged? |
| Enterprise-to-provider boundary | Internal data is sent to an external AI endpoint | What information leaves the enterprise environment, and under which terms? |
| Application-to-data-source boundary | AI service retrieves internal documents | Are source permissions preserved during retrieval? |
| Provider-to-subprocessor boundary | Provider uses another company for hosting or processing | Are downstream dependencies disclosed and controlled? |
| Model-to-output boundary | Model generates a response | Can the output contain sensitive information, unsafe instructions, or unsupported claims? |
| AI-to-business-action boundary | AI recommendation triggers a workflow | Are actions authorized, validated, and reversible? |
| Provider-to-enterprise monitoring boundary | Provider supplies logs and incident notifications | Is the available evidence sufficient for detection and investigation? |

Trust boundaries must be evaluated in both directions.

For example, the enterprise must consider what information it sends to the provider and what information the provider returns. The outbound risk includes disclosure of confidential data; the inbound risk includes malicious content, misleading recommendations, unsafe tool instructions, or manipulated outputs.

### 3.4 Enterprise-to-Provider Data Flow

A typical request follows this sequence:

1. An authenticated employee submits a question through an enterprise application.
2. The enterprise application identifies the user's permissions and business context.
3. The integration layer validates the request and applies applicable security policies.
4. Approved contextual data may be retrieved from enterprise repositories.
5. The enterprise sends the permitted prompt and context to the external AI SaaS service.
6. The provider processes the request using its service and model infrastructure.
7. The provider returns an AI-generated response.
8. The enterprise applies available validation, policy checks, and human review.
9. The response is presented to the user or passed to an authorized workflow.
10. Relevant events are logged and monitored according to the enterprise's retention and privacy requirements.

Not every service follows this sequence exactly. Some providers process prompts directly through their interfaces, while others use APIs, retrieval services, or connected enterprise applications.

The architecture assessment must document the actual data flow, not merely the vendor's marketing description.

### 3.5 Data-Flow Risk Analysis

| Data-flow stage | Potential exposure | Required governance consideration |
|---|---|---|
| User input | Personal information, credentials, source code, confidential business data | Input restrictions, user guidance, data-loss prevention |
| Context retrieval | Documents beyond the user's authorization | Permission-aware retrieval and access-control testing |
| API transmission | Data intercepted or exposed through insecure integrations | Encryption, endpoint validation, secure authentication |
| Provider processing | Data handled under provider-controlled conditions | Contractual data-use restrictions, retention terms, processing locations |
| Model response | Incorrect, manipulated, or sensitive output | Output validation, sensitive-data controls, human review |
| Tool invocation | Unauthorized changes to enterprise systems | Least privilege, action-specific authorization, transaction controls |
| Logging | Prompts and responses retained in logs | Log minimization, access restrictions, retention policies |
| Incident investigation | Insufficient provider evidence | Contractual cooperation, event access, evidence-preservation procedures |

### 3.6 Architectural Decision: Direct Access or Enterprise Gateway?

An enterprise may allow users to access an AI SaaS service directly or require access through an enterprise-controlled gateway.

| Consideration | Direct access | Enterprise gateway |
|---|---|---|
| Deployment speed | Often faster | Requires integration and configuration |
| Centralized policy enforcement | May be limited to provider controls | Can enforce enterprise-specific policies |
| Data-loss prevention | Depends on endpoint and provider capabilities | Can apply additional inspection where technically feasible |
| Identity integration | Depends on available enterprise integration | Can centralize identity and authorization checks |
| Logging | May be limited to provider and endpoint logs | Can provide centralized request metadata and security events |
| API security | Depends on the provider's native mechanisms | Can apply gateway-level validation, authentication, and rate limits |
| Operational complexity | Potentially lower | Higher integration and maintenance burden |
| Visibility | May be fragmented | Can improve visibility if logs are complete and appropriately configured |

A gateway is not automatically safer. It can become a concentration point for sensitive information, introduce latency, create new privileged infrastructure, or provide a false sense of security if requests are not inspected effectively.

The decision should consider the sensitivity of the data, the required control capabilities, the available provider features, the deployment cost, and the operational team's ability to maintain the architecture.

**Core Principle:** Security architecture must make trust boundaries, data flows, control ownership, and enforcement points explicit. An enterprise cannot manage risks that it has not identified in the actual service architecture.

## 4. AI SaaS Data Governance, Privacy, and Information Handling

### 4.1 Data Classification Before Deployment

The enterprise must identify which data categories may be submitted to the AI SaaS service and which must be prohibited or subject to additional approval.

Data classification should be based on the organization's existing information-classification scheme, supplemented by AI-specific handling rules.

Typical categories include:

- Public information.
- Internal business information.
- Confidential commercial information.
- Personal data.
- Sensitive personal data or other specially protected information.
- Customer records.
- Financial and payment-related information.
- Authentication secrets and cryptographic material.
- Source code and intellectual property.
- Security logs and incident investigation records.
- Critical infrastructure information.
- Legally privileged or contractually restricted information.

The exact categories depend on the enterprise's industry, jurisdiction, contractual obligations, and risk appetite.

A provider's ability to encrypt information does not automatically make every data category suitable for submission. Encryption protects data under particular conditions; it does not determine whether the provider is authorized to process that data in the first place.

### 4.2 Data-Handling Decision Matrix

| Data category | Example | Initial governance position | Additional controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public | Published product documentation | Generally suitable for approved use cases | Validate output accuracy and service terms |
| Internal | Non-sensitive process descriptions | Potentially permitted | Approved service, access control, acceptable-use policy |
| Confidential | Internal strategy and commercial plans | Restricted pending assessment | Contractual data-use review, retention assessment, access controls |
| Personal data | Customer names and account details | Requires purpose-specific review | Privacy assessment, minimization, access restrictions, lawful processing analysis |
| Sensitive personal data | Health or other specially protected records | High scrutiny; may be prohibited in some use cases | Legal review, strict necessity assessment, enhanced safeguards |
| Credentials and secrets | Passwords, API keys, private keys | Prohibited from ordinary prompt submission | Secret-management controls and detection mechanisms |
| Source code | Proprietary applications | Restricted or approved only under defined terms | Intellectual property review, source-code protection, output security testing |
| Security telemetry | Alerts, logs, threat intelligence | Restricted according to sensitivity | Redaction, data minimization, tenant isolation review |
| Regulated financial data | Payment or financial records | Subject to regulatory and contractual requirements | Data-flow analysis, access restrictions, processing and retention review |
| Legally privileged information | Confidential legal advice | Requires explicit legal authorization | Privilege protection, restricted access, provider-term review |

These positions are illustrative. An enterprise must define its own approved, restricted, and prohibited categories.

### 4.3 Data Minimization and Purpose Limitation

**Data minimization** means sending only the information necessary to achieve the approved business purpose.

If a support agent asks the AI system to summarize a technical complaint, the prompt may not need the customer's full name, home address, complete account history, or payment information.

The enterprise should determine whether information can be:

- Removed entirely.
- Redacted before submission.
- Replaced with pseudonymous identifiers.
- Aggregated or summarized.
- Retrieved only when needed.
- Restricted to a particular user or case.
- Deleted after a defined retention period.

However, redaction and pseudonymization must not be treated as guaranteed anonymization.

A combination of seemingly harmless attributes may allow re-identification. Free-text content can also contain identifying details that automated filters fail to detect.

Purpose limitation is equally important. Data approved for generating an internal summary should not automatically be approved for model training, product improvement, unrelated analytics, or a different business purpose.

The enterprise must distinguish between the purpose of its own processing and the purposes for which the provider may process submitted information.

### 4.4 Provider Data-Use and Retention Questions

Before approving a third-party AI SaaS service, the enterprise should obtain clear answers to the following questions.

**Data use**

- Does the provider use prompts or uploaded documents to train or improve models?
- Does the answer differ between consumer, business, enterprise, and API offerings?
- Are separate opt-in or opt-out settings available?
- Do support personnel or subcontractors have access to submitted data?
- Are prompts used for abuse detection, service troubleshooting, or other operational purposes?
- Can the provider use customer data for purposes beyond those explicitly authorized by the enterprise?

**Data retention**

- How long are prompts, responses, uploaded documents, and derived data retained?
- Are retention periods configurable?
- Are logs retained separately from primary application data?
- What happens to information in backups?
- Does deletion cover cached data, derived indexes, and connected retrieval stores?
- Are there legal, security, or operational exceptions to deletion?

**Data location and processing**

- Where are data stored and processed?
- Can processing occur outside the enterprise's expected region?
- Which subprocessors may access the data?
- Can the provider change processing locations or subprocessors?
- What contractual safeguards apply to international data transfers?

**Data isolation**

- How does the provider isolate customer tenants?
- What prevents one customer's information from appearing in another customer's responses?
- How are customer-specific indexes, embeddings, and caches protected?
- What testing or assurance evidence is available for tenant isolation?

**Important distinction:** A statement that customer data is not used to train a foundation model does not, by itself, establish that the provider retains no data or that no other processing occurs.

Training, service operation, safety monitoring, abuse detection, troubleshooting, and legal retention are different activities. They must be assessed separately.

### 4.5 Personal Data and Privacy Governance

When an AI SaaS deployment processes personal data, the enterprise must determine the applicable legal roles, obligations, and safeguards.

For deployments involving the European Union, relevant considerations may include the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), applicable national data-protection law, and the circumstances in which a data protection impact assessment is required.

The assessment should consider:

- The lawful basis for processing.
- The defined purpose of processing.
- Necessity and proportionality.
- Data minimization.
- Accuracy and correction mechanisms.
- Storage limitation.
- Security of processing.
- Data-subject rights.
- Processor and subprocessor arrangements.
- International data transfers.
- Transparency and accountability.
- The potential impact on individuals.

The enterprise should not assume that the AI SaaS provider's standard privacy documentation resolves all of these issues. The customer's own use case, configuration, data sources, and decision processes may create additional obligations.

Where the service supports consequential decisions about individuals, the organization should also evaluate whether automated decision-making rules, transparency requirements, human intervention, or other sector-specific obligations apply.

A privacy assessment must reflect the actual use case rather than the general capabilities of the product.

### 4.6 AI Model Training and Customer Data

One of the most important contractual distinctions is whether the provider can use enterprise-submitted information to train, fine-tune, evaluate, or otherwise improve AI models.

The enterprise should distinguish among at least five processing categories:

| Processing category | Description | Governance question |
|---|---|---|
| Inference | Processing input to generate an output | What data is processed, where, and for how long? |
| Service operation | Processing required to operate and support the service | What operational data is retained or accessed? |
| Abuse and safety monitoring | Processing intended to detect misuse or unsafe activity | What information is collected, who can access it, and how long is it retained? |
| Model evaluation and improvement | Using information to evaluate or improve model behavior | Is customer data used, under what authorization, and with what restrictions? |
| Model training or fine-tuning | Incorporating information into model parameters or a specialized model | What consent, contract terms, isolation, and deletion limitations apply? |

These categories may overlap in a particular provider's architecture. The table is an assessment framework, not a claim that every service implements them as separate systems.

The organization should document its approved position, obtain contractual confirmation where necessary, and verify that the selected product configuration actually implements the intended restrictions.

A marketing statement or informal sales assurance is not a substitute for binding terms and technically applicable configuration.

### 4.7 Data Governance Control Checklist

Before production deployment, the enterprise should confirm that:

- [ ] Approved and prohibited data categories are documented.
- [ ] The business purpose for processing is defined.
- [ ] Data owners have approved the intended use.
- [ ] Privacy and legal assessments have been completed where required.
- [ ] Provider data-use terms have been reviewed.
- [ ] Model-training and service-improvement terms are understood.
- [ ] Retention and deletion requirements are documented.
- [ ] Data residency and transfer requirements have been assessed.
- [ ] Subprocessors and relevant downstream dependencies have been reviewed.
- [ ] Tenant isolation and access controls have been assessed.
- [ ] Input filtering and redaction controls have been tested where required.
- [ ] Logging does not create unnecessary copies of sensitive prompts and outputs.
- [ ] User training explains what information must not be submitted.
- [ ] A process exists for handling data-subject requests and relevant incidents.
- [ ] The enterprise has documented the limitations of provider assurances and available evidence.

The checklist should be supported by evidence. A checked box without a named owner, verification method, or supporting record does not demonstrate that the control is operating effectively.

**Core Principle:** Data governance for AI SaaS must cover the entire processing lifecycle, including collection, transmission, inference, retention, secondary use, logging, deletion, and incident investigation.

## 5. Third-Party AI SaaS Risk Landscape and Initial Risk Register

### 5.1 Principal Risk Categories

A third-party AI SaaS deployment creates interconnected risks across technology, supplier management, business operations, privacy, legal compliance, and enterprise governance.

The principal categories are:

**1. Supplier and concentration risk**

The enterprise becomes dependent on an external provider's service availability, financial stability, security capabilities, roadmap, and contractual commitments.

**2. Data confidentiality and privacy risk**

Sensitive information may be disclosed, retained, processed in unexpected locations, or used for purposes that the enterprise has not authorized.

**3. Identity and access risk**

Weak identity integration, excessive permissions, compromised credentials, or insufficient segregation of duties may expose the AI service or connected enterprise systems.

**4. AI output risk**

The model may produce incorrect, misleading, biased, unsafe, or unsupported outputs that influence business decisions.

**5. Model and service change risk**

Provider-side updates may alter model behavior, output quality, security characteristics, or the availability of capabilities relied upon by the enterprise.

**6. Integration and API risk**

Poorly secured interfaces may expose sensitive information, permit unauthorized requests, or allow AI outputs to trigger inappropriate actions.

**7. Regulatory and contractual risk**

The deployment may conflict with data-protection obligations, sector rules, intellectual property requirements, records-management policies, or contractual restrictions.

**8. Monitoring and auditability risk**

The enterprise may lack sufficient visibility into prompts, outputs, model versions, provider incidents, or the handling of customer data.

**9. Business continuity and exit risk**

Provider outages, commercial disputes, service discontinuation, or excessive switching costs may disrupt business processes.

**10. AI-specific adversarial risk**

Prompt injection, malicious content, data poisoning, model manipulation, and retrieval abuse may compromise the integrity or security of AI-supported workflows.

### 5.4 Inherent Risk, Residual Risk, and Risk Acceptance

The enterprise should distinguish between inherent risk and residual risk.

**Inherent risk** is the exposure before considering the effectiveness of relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the exposure remaining after existing controls and their demonstrated effectiveness are considered.

For example:

A third-party AI service can access customer records through an enterprise CRM integration. The inherent risk of unauthorized disclosure may be high because the service processes personal data and has access to connected information.

The enterprise implements:

- Role-based authorization.
- Permission-aware data retrieval.
- Data minimization.
- API restrictions.
- Prompt and output security testing.
- Monitoring for anomalous access.
- Human approval for consequential actions.
- Contractual restrictions on provider data use.

These controls may reduce the likelihood or impact of the original scenario. However, the risk is not automatically eliminated. Their effectiveness must be tested, and remaining limitations must be documented.

A risk owner should determine whether the residual risk is within the organization's approved risk appetite. Risks exceeding delegated authority must be escalated to the appropriate executive or risk committee.

Risk acceptance should document the scenario, business justification, applicable controls, residual exposure, accountable approver, expiry or review date, and any conditions attached to the approval.

### 5.5 Initial Governance Questions for the AI SaaS Provider

The enterprise should use structured questions to establish the provider's security, privacy, operational, and AI-governance posture.

| Assessment domain | Key question | Evidence to request |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate security | What security management framework and controls govern the service? | Relevant independent assurance reports, certifications, and control descriptions |
| AI governance | How are model risks, safety issues, and material behavior changes governed? | AI governance documentation, evaluation summaries, and change-management procedures |
| Data use | Is customer data used for training, model improvement, or other secondary purposes? | Binding contractual terms and applicable product configuration documentation |
| Data retention | What are the retention and deletion rules for prompts, responses, logs, and backups? | Retention schedules, deletion procedures, and documented exceptions |
| Privacy | What processing roles, subprocessors, and transfer mechanisms apply? | Data-processing agreement, subprocessor information, and relevant transfer documentation |
| Access control | How are privileged provider accounts and tenant boundaries protected? | Access-control descriptions, assurance evidence, and relevant test summaries |
| Encryption | How is data protected in transit and at rest? | Technical documentation and configuration options |

| Resilience | What service-continuity and disaster-recovery arrangements exist? | Service commitments, resilience documentation, and recovery-test summaries |
| Change management | How are material model and service changes communicated? | Release policies, notification procedures, and version-management capabilities |
| Audit rights | What assurance and audit evidence can customers obtain? | Contractual audit provisions and independent assurance reports |
| Exit | How can enterprise data be exported, deleted, and verified at termination? | Exit procedures, export formats, deletion terms, and transition commitments |

The provider's answers should be evaluated against the actual service, selected product tier, deployment configuration, and intended business use.

Evidence from one product or service offering should not automatically be assumed to apply to another. Likewise, a corporate-level certification may not cover every service, region, feature, or subprocessor used in the deployment.

### 5.6 Initial Go/No-Go Criteria

Before production approval, the organization should establish minimum acceptance criteria.

A proposed deployment should normally be blocked or escalated when any of the following conditions exists:

- The provider's data-use terms conflict with enterprise confidentiality requirements.
- Required legal or privacy assessments have not been completed.
- The service cannot enforce necessary access restrictions.
- The proposed use case requires security capabilities that the provider cannot demonstrate.
- High-impact AI outputs will be used without adequate validation or accountability.
- Connected-system permissions exceed the minimum required for the approved purpose.
- Material supplier risks have no viable treatment plan.
- Required audit evidence or incident cooperation cannot be obtained.
- Business continuity and exit arrangements are inadequate for the service's criticality.
- Residual risk exceeds the authority of the proposed approver.

A conditional approval may be appropriate when remaining issues are explicitly documented, temporary restrictions are enforceable, and an authorized risk owner approves the conditions.

For example, an enterprise may approve an AI service for public information and low-risk internal drafting while prohibiting confidential customer data until the provider's retention terms and data-processing arrangements are validated.

The approval must be specific to the defined service, configuration, data categories, users, integrations, and use cases. Approval of a vendor does not constitute unrestricted approval of every capability that vendor offers.

### 5.7 Initial Governance Position

Third-party AI SaaS adoption should be treated as a combined **technology, supplier, data-governance, and business-risk decision**.

The enterprise should not assume that a mature provider automatically offers a suitable service for every business purpose. Nor should it assume that retaining all data inside the enterprise automatically makes an internally hosted AI system safer in every respect.

The correct decision depends on the actual architecture, sensitivity of information, provider commitments, control effectiveness, business consequences, applicable obligations, and available alternatives.

**Core Principle:** Approve third-party AI SaaS only when the organization understands the service boundary, has validated the provider's commitments, has implemented effective enterprise-side controls, and has formally accepted the remaining risk at the appropriate authority level.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Vendor approval vs. use-case approval:** Approving a supplier does not automatically authorize every AI capability or business process it offers.
- **Security certification vs. service suitability:** General assurance evidence does not prove that the specific AI service satisfies every deployment requirement.
- **Data encryption vs. authorized processing:** Encryption does not establish that the provider is permitted to process the information.
- **Model training vs. service operation:** A prohibition on model training does not necessarily prohibit retention or other processing.
- **AI recommendation vs. authorized action:** A generated recommendation should not acquire business authority merely because it came from an approved service.
- **Inherent risk vs. residual risk:** Controls reduce risk only to the extent that they are appropriately designed, implemented, and demonstrably effective.
- **Supplier responsibility vs. enterprise accountability:** The provider may operate the service, but the enterprise must govern its use and accept its own residual business risk.

## Part 2: Supplier Due Diligence, Regulatory Compliance, Threat Modeling, and Security Controls

## 6. Third-Party AI Supplier Due Diligence and Risk Assessment

### 6.1 Why Traditional Supplier Assessments Are Insufficient

Traditional third-party risk management (TPRM) evaluates suppliers according to their financial stability, information security, privacy practices, business continuity, contractual obligations, and operational resilience.

These controls remain necessary when procuring AI SaaS. However, an AI supplier assessment must also evaluate the risks arising from the model, its development and operation, the service's data-processing behavior, and its interaction with enterprise systems.

A supplier may hold an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate, maintain a mature security operations center, and provide independent assurance reports while still presenting material AI-specific risks.

Examples include:

- The provider does not disclose material limitations of its AI model.
- Customer prompts are retained longer than the enterprise permits.
- The provider changes model behavior without sufficient notification.
- The service cannot provide evidence of testing against prompt injection.
- The AI system retrieves information without reliably enforcing source permissions.
- The provider's incident notification process does not meet enterprise requirements.
- The provider uses subprocessors that have not been assessed for the intended data categories.
- The enterprise cannot obtain sufficient logs to investigate an AI-related security incident.

The objective is not to replace conventional supplier assessments. It is to extend them to cover AI-specific risks.

### 6.2 AI SaaS Supplier Assessment Domains

A comprehensive assessment should cover the following domains.

| Assessment domain | Primary objective | Key questions |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate governance | Understand the provider's accountability structure | Who owns AI risk, security, privacy, and incident response? |
| Information security | Evaluate the provider's security management system | What security controls protect the service and supporting infrastructure? |
| AI governance | Assess the provider's management of AI-related risks | How are model risks, evaluations, limitations, and changes governed? |
| Data governance | Understand the lifecycle of enterprise information | What data is collected, processed, retained, shared, or reused? |
| Privacy | Determine how personal data is protected | What processing roles, purposes, retention rules, and transfer arrangements apply? |
| Model security | Assess threats affecting the AI system | How does the provider evaluate prompt injection, information disclosure, and model abuse? |
| Identity and access | Verify authentication and authorization capabilities | Are SSO, MFA, role-based access, privileged access controls, and access reviews supported? |
| Application security | Assess the service's software security | How are vulnerabilities identified, prioritized, remediated, and verified? |
| API and integration security | Evaluate interfaces with enterprise systems | How are API credentials, authorization, rate limits, and integration permissions protected? |
| Logging and monitoring | Determine whether security events are observable | What events can customers access, and how long are they retained? |
| Resilience | Evaluate service continuity | What availability commitments, recovery arrangements, and contingency options exist? |
| Subprocessor management | Understand downstream dependencies | Which subprocessors handle enterprise data, and how are changes communicated? |
| Contractual assurance | Establish enforceable obligations | Are data-use restrictions, notification obligations, audit provisions, and exit requirements documented? |
| AI lifecycle management | Evaluate model and service changes | How are new models, features, and material behavioral changes introduced? |
| Exit and portability | Reduce excessive supplier dependency | Can the enterprise export data, transition workflows, and verify deletion? |

The assessment should be proportionate to the use case. A low-risk application using public information may not require the same depth of evidence as an AI service connected to customer records, financial transactions, or production infrastructure.

However, proportionality must not be used to bypass mandatory legal, contractual, or security requirements.

### 6.3 Risk-Based Supplier Tiering

The enterprise should classify AI suppliers according to the risk created by the specific service and its intended use.

Supplier tiering should consider:

1. **Data sensitivity:** What information will the supplier process?
2. **Business criticality:** What happens if the service becomes unavailable?
3. **System access:** What enterprise systems and permissions can the service use?
4. **Decision influence:** Can outputs affect customers, employees, finances, or regulated decisions?
5. **Autonomy:** Can the AI service initiate actions without human approval?
6. **Concentration:** How dependent is the organization on this supplier?
7. **Regulatory exposure:** Which legal and contractual requirements apply?
8. **Assurance limitations:** How much visibility does the enterprise have into the provider's controls?

An illustrative tiering model is shown below.

| Supplier tier | Risk profile | Example | Assessment requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Low | Limited business impact, public data, no privileged integration | AI tool used to summarize public information | Basic security and privacy review, acceptable-use assessment, service-term review |
| Tier 2 — Moderate | Internal information and ordinary business workflows | Internal document-drafting assistant | Standard TPRM assessment, access-control review, data-use and retention review |
| Tier 3 — High | Confidential data, personal data, important integrations, or consequential recommendations | Customer-service AI connected to CRM | Enhanced due diligence, privacy review, AI-specific security testing, contractual assurance, continuity planning |
| Tier 4 — Critical | Highly consequential processing, privileged system access, or significant operational dependency | AI service authorized to initiate production changes or financial transactions | Executive risk review, independent assurance where appropriate, stringent authorization controls, tested recovery, enhanced monitoring, explicit residual-risk approval |

These tiers are an internal governance model, not a statutory classification. The organization should define its own thresholds and escalation requirements.

The same supplier may occupy different tiers for different deployments. A provider used for public-content generation may be low risk in one context but high risk when integrated with sensitive customer data.

### 6.4 Evidence-Based Due Diligence

A supplier questionnaire is useful for collecting information, but questionnaire responses alone provide limited assurance.

The enterprise should seek evidence proportionate to the risks being assessed.

Examples include:

- Independent security assurance reports.
- Relevant certification statements and their scope.
- Data-processing agreements.
- Subprocessor lists.
- Data-retention and deletion documentation.
- Security architecture descriptions.
- Penetration-testing summaries.
- Vulnerability-management procedures.
- Incident-response commitments.
- Business continuity and disaster-recovery documentation.
- AI model evaluation summaries.
- Model-change notification policies.
- AI security testing documentation.
- Available logging and monitoring specifications.
- Contractual audit provisions.
- Service-level agreements.
- Exit and data-portability procedures.

Evidence must be examined for scope, currency, applicability, and limitations.

For example, a certification may cover the provider's corporate information security management system but not every product or service feature. A penetration test may cover the web application but exclude model behavior, connected-system authorization, or prompt-injection scenarios.

The correct question is not simply, "Does the provider have a certification?"

It is:

**Does the available evidence demonstrate that the specific service and deployment can meet the enterprise's security, privacy, resilience, and AI-governance requirements?**

### 6.6 Supplier Risk Findings and Remediation

- Remediation deadline.
- Verification method.
- Residual risk.
- Approval authority.
- Review date.

## 7. Regulatory and Framework Alignment

### 7.1 Why Regulatory Mapping Matters

Third-party AI SaaS deployments may involve multiple regulatory and contractual obligations simultaneously.

A single service could process employee information, customer records, confidential commercial documents, and cybersecurity telemetry. Each category may introduce different requirements.

Regulatory mapping should therefore start with the actual use case, data flows, affected individuals, business process, and jurisdiction.

The organization must distinguish between:

- Legally binding requirements.
- Contractual obligations.
- Regulatory guidance.
- Voluntary management-system standards.
- Technical security frameworks.
- Internal policies and risk-appetite requirements.

These sources may complement one another, but they are not interchangeable.

A security framework can help structure risk management without independently establishing legal compliance.

### 7.2 GDPR and Personal Data Processing

For an enterprise operating in the European Union, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) may apply when the AI SaaS deployment processes personal data within its scope.

Relevant considerations include:

**Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency**

The enterprise must determine the lawful basis for processing and provide appropriate information to affected individuals.

**Purpose limitation**

Personal data should be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not processed incompatibly with those purposes.

**Data minimization**

The enterprise should limit processing to information necessary for the approved purpose.

**Accuracy**

Where personal data is used to inform business decisions, the organization should address inaccurate or misleading information and provide appropriate correction mechanisms.

**Storage limitation**

Retention periods should be defined and justified, subject to applicable legal requirements.

**Integrity and confidentiality**

Appropriate technical and organizational measures should protect personal data against unauthorized access, disclosure, alteration, and loss.

**Accountability**

The enterprise should maintain evidence supporting its compliance decisions and the effectiveness of relevant controls.

**Processor governance**

Where a provider acts as a processor, the parties should establish the required contractual arrangements and address applicable obligations concerning processing instructions, confidentiality, security, subprocessors, assistance, and deletion or return of personal data.

The enterprise must determine the parties' actual roles based on their activities and applicable law. Contract labels alone do not conclusively establish whether a party acts as a controller or processor.

A data protection impact assessment may be required when processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals. The organization should assess this requirement against the actual processing context rather than assuming that every AI deployment automatically requires one.

### 7.3 EU AI Act Considerations

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems. Its application depends on factors such as the system's intended purpose, role in the value chain, classification, and the obligations applicable to the relevant organization.

For third-party AI SaaS deployments, the enterprise should determine:

- Whether the service qualifies as an AI system under the applicable legal definitions.
- The system's intended purpose and actual use.
- Whether the use case falls within a prohibited practice or a regulated category.
- Whether high-risk AI requirements apply.
- Which obligations apply to the provider, deployer, or other relevant actor.
- Whether transparency requirements apply.
- Whether human oversight is necessary.
- Whether technical documentation, instructions, or other information must be obtained from the provider.
- Whether the enterprise's configuration or modifications affect its legal role.
- Whether applicable implementation dates or transitional provisions affect the deployment.

An enterprise should not automatically classify an AI service as high risk simply because it uses advanced models. Nor should it assume that a general-purpose AI model makes every downstream application high risk.

Classification depends on the applicable provisions and the actual system and use case.

Where the service supports recruitment, employee management, access to essential services, or other potentially regulated activities, legal review should determine the specific requirements.

### 7.4 ISO/IEC 27001 and Information Security Management

ISO/IEC 27001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an information security management system (ISMS).

For AI SaaS procurement and deployment, the enterprise can use its ISMS to govern:

- Supplier security assessments.
- Risk assessment and treatment.
- Access control.
- Information classification.
- Incident management.
- Business continuity.
- Security requirements in supplier agreements.
- Monitoring and assurance.
- Documented evidence and management review.

The enterprise should assess the applicable control set and statement of applicability rather than assuming that every control applies identically to every deployment.

The provider's certification can contribute to supplier assurance, but the enterprise must still assess the service's scope, residual risks, and its own responsibilities.

### 7.5 ISO/IEC 42001 and AI Management Systems

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for an AI management system (AIMS).

An enterprise can use an AI management system to establish governance for AI use cases, including third-party AI services.

Relevant management activities may include:

- AI policy and objectives.
- Defined roles and responsibilities.
- AI risk assessment and treatment.
- Impact assessment where applicable to the organization's approach.
- Operational controls.
- Supplier and third-party relationship management.
- Monitoring and performance evaluation.
- Internal audit.
- Management review.
- Corrective action and continual improvement.

The organization should determine how its AI management system interfaces with the existing ISMS, privacy management processes, enterprise risk management, and procurement controls.

A provider's ISO/IEC 42001 certification, where held, can support assurance. It does not establish that the provider's service is automatically appropriate for the enterprise's intended purpose.

### 7.6 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides a voluntary structure for managing AI risks.

Its four core functions are:

| Function | Purpose | Application to third-party AI SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| Govern | Establish policies, accountability, and organizational risk-management practices | Define approval authority, supplier requirements, AI policy, and ongoing accountability |
| Map | Establish context and identify risks | Document the use case, affected parties, data flows, provider dependencies, and foreseeable harms |
| Measure | Analyze and assess risks | Evaluate model behavior, security controls, privacy impacts, reliability, and performance |
| Manage | Prioritize and respond to risks | Implement controls, treat findings, monitor the service, and review residual risk |

The framework helps structure risk management but does not replace applicable law or contractual obligations.

For third-party AI SaaS, the enterprise should use the framework to connect business context, provider assurance, technical testing, and ongoing monitoring into a coherent lifecycle.

### 7.7 Regulatory and Framework Mapping Matrix

The mapping must be adapted to the organization, jurisdiction, industry, and actual service. It should identify specific requirements rather than merely listing standards in a policy document.

### 7.8 Compliance Ownership and Accountability

The enterprise should define clear ownership for regulatory and framework-related activities.

| Stakeholder | Principal responsibility |
|---|---|
| Business owner | Defines the use case, expected benefits, business impact, and acceptable operating boundaries |
| AI system owner | Coordinates AI lifecycle governance, performance monitoring, and relevant technical assessments |
| Information Security | Evaluates security architecture, threat scenarios, control effectiveness, and incident readiness |
| Privacy or Data Protection Officer, where appointed | Advises on applicable data-protection requirements and relevant impact assessments |
| Legal | Reviews legal obligations, contractual commitments, intellectual property, and regulatory applicability |
| Procurement or TPRM | Coordinates supplier due diligence and contractual risk management |
| Enterprise Architecture | Validates system integration, trust boundaries, dependencies, and architectural standards |
| Compliance | Maps applicable obligations to controls and evidence |
| Internal Audit | Independently assesses governance and control effectiveness within its mandate |
| Risk owner | Owns the business risk and ensures appropriate treatment and escalation |
| Authorized executive or committee | Approves residual risks that exceed delegated management authority |

These responsibilities may be distributed differently depending on organizational structure. The important requirement is that accountability is explicit and conflicts of responsibility are resolved.

**Core Principle:** Regulatory compliance must be mapped to the actual use case, legal role, processing activity, and deployment configuration. Naming a standard or obtaining a supplier certificate does not establish compliance by itself.

## 8. Threat Modeling for Third-Party AI SaaS

### 8.1 Threat Modeling Objectives

Threat modeling identifies how a service may be attacked, misused, misconfigured, or fail in ways that compromise enterprise objectives.

For third-party AI SaaS, the assessment must consider both traditional application threats and AI-specific failure or attack paths.

The objective is to answer five questions:

1. What assets and business processes require protection?
2. Who or what could threaten them?
3. Through which interfaces, identities, data flows, or dependencies could harm occur?
4. What controls prevent, detect, or limit the threat?
5. What residual risk remains after those controls are implemented?

Threat modeling should cover the entire deployment, including enterprise-managed components, provider-controlled components, connected systems, and downstream suppliers.

### 8.2 Assets Requiring Protection

Typical assets include:

- Customer and employee personal data.
- Confidential business information.
- Intellectual property and source code.
- Authentication credentials and API keys.
- Enterprise documents and knowledge repositories.
- AI prompts, outputs, and contextual data.
- Model configurations and system instructions.
- API integrations and connected business systems.
- Audit logs and incident evidence.
- AI-supported business decisions.
- Service availability and business continuity.
- Provider contracts, assurance reports, and risk records.

The organization should identify each asset's owner, classification, permitted use, and business impact if compromised.

An AI SaaS deployment can expose assets that are not stored directly within the AI service. For example, an AI assistant connected to a CRM may create a pathway to customer records even when the CRM remains hosted in the enterprise's existing environment.

### 8.3 Threat Actors and Threat Sources

| Threat source | Motivation or failure mechanism | Potential attack or impact |
|---|---|---|
| External attacker | Financial gain, espionage, disruption | Account compromise, data exfiltration, API abuse |
| Malicious authenticated user | Intentional misuse of authorized access | Sensitive-data extraction, policy bypass, unauthorized actions |
| Compromised employee account | Credential theft or session compromise | Unauthorized AI access and connected-system activity |
| Malicious insider at the provider | Abuse of privileged access | Unauthorized access to provider-controlled data or systems |
| Compromised subprocessor | Downstream supplier compromise | Exposure of data or disruption through provider dependencies |
| Malicious document author | Embedding instructions in content likely to be processed by AI | Indirect prompt injection or unauthorized information retrieval |
| Careless employee | Accidental submission of restricted information | Confidentiality or privacy breach |
| Defective provider update | Unintended behavior change | Output degradation, broken controls, or workflow disruption |
| Misconfigured enterprise integration | Excessive privileges or weak authorization | Unauthorized data access or business actions |
| Operational failure | Capacity, connectivity, or service outage | Loss of service availability and business interruption |

Threat modeling should not assume that all threats originate outside the organization. Employees, suppliers, configuration errors, and provider-side failures can create equally important exposures.

### 8.4 AI SaaS Attack Scenarios

**Scenario A: Sensitive information disclosure through prompts**

An employee pastes a confidential customer export into an AI service that is not approved for that data category.

Potential consequences include unauthorized external processing, contractual violations, or exposure through provider-side retention.

Relevant controls include approved-service restrictions, user training, data-loss prevention, input redaction, and provider data-use controls.

**Scenario B: Indirect prompt injection**

An employee asks an AI assistant to summarize a document retrieved from an external website. The document contains instructions designed to influence the assistant's behavior.

If the assistant treats untrusted document content as authoritative instructions, it may generate misleading outputs or attempt unauthorized actions through connected tools.

Relevant controls include treating retrieved content as untrusted, separating instructions from data, limiting tool permissions, validating outputs, and testing realistic attack scenarios.

**Scenario C: Excessive integration privileges**

An AI assistant is connected to an enterprise CRM using a service account with permission to read and modify every customer record.

A defect, compromised account, or malicious instruction may cause the system to retrieve or modify information beyond the approved use case.

Relevant controls include least privilege, scoped service identities, action-specific authorization, transaction limits, and human approval for consequential changes.

**Scenario D: Provider-side model change**

The provider updates the model used by the service. The new model produces more inaccurate recommendations or changes how it follows enterprise instructions.

If the enterprise has no change-notification process or regression tests, the change may introduce risk without being detected promptly.

Relevant controls include change monitoring, documented acceptance criteria, regression testing, rollback or fallback arrangements where available, and periodic reassessment.

**Scenario E: Insufficient incident evidence**

A data leak is suspected, but the enterprise has no access to relevant prompt logs, model-version records, or provider incident details.

The organization may be unable to establish the scope of the incident, determine affected data, or complete required notifications within applicable deadlines.

Relevant controls include contractual cooperation requirements, available audit logs, evidence-preservation procedures, and a defined provider incident-escalation process.

### 8.5 Threat Modeling Using STRIDE and AI-Specific Analysis

STRIDE is a traditional threat-modeling method that groups threats into six categories:

- Spoofing.
- Tampering.
- Repudiation.
- Information disclosure.
- Denial of service.
- Elevation of privilege.

It can help identify conventional threats affecting AI SaaS components, but it should be supplemented with AI-specific analysis.

| Threat category | Third-party AI SaaS example | Additional AI consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Spoofing | Attacker impersonates an employee or integration | Can compromised identity be used to submit malicious prompts or invoke AI-connected tools? |
| Tampering | Attacker modifies API requests or configuration | Can untrusted content manipulate model behavior or alter retrieved context? |
| Repudiation | User denies initiating a sensitive action | Do logs link the user, request, model response, approval, and resulting action? |
| Information disclosure | Unauthorized user retrieves confidential records | Can prompts, retrieval results, outputs, or provider logs disclose sensitive information? |
| Denial of service | API abuse exhausts service capacity or quotas | Can excessive inference requests or provider limits disrupt critical workflows? |
| Elevation of privilege | Compromised service identity gains broader access | Can AI-generated instructions trigger tools operating with excessive permissions? |

AI-specific scenarios should also include hallucinations, output manipulation, model or data poisoning where applicable, unsafe tool use, and failures of human oversight.

Not every AI SaaS service is equally vulnerable to every AI attack category. The assessment should be based on the service's actual architecture, exposed capabilities, and available evidence.

### 8.6 Threat-to-Control Traceability

Threat modeling is useful only when identified threats are connected to implemented controls and testing.

| Threat scenario | Preventive control | Detective control | Assurance activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized data submission | Approved-use policy, data classification, input controls | DLP alerts and access monitoring | Test representative restricted-data scenarios |
| Unauthorized retrieval | Source-system authorization and permission-aware retrieval | Retrieval and access-event monitoring | Test cross-user and cross-tenant access boundaries |
| Prompt injection | Untrusted-input handling and restricted tool permissions | Detection of suspicious requests or actions where feasible | Red-team testing using representative injection scenarios |
| Excessive tool privileges | Least privilege and action-specific authorization | Monitoring of tool invocations and sensitive operations | Test unauthorized-action attempts |
| Incorrect high-impact output | Output validation and human review | Quality monitoring and error reporting | Evaluate outputs against defined acceptance criteria |
| Provider model change | Change-management requirements and configuration controls | Provider notifications and output-quality monitoring | Regression testing after material changes |
| Provider outage | Fallback procedures and alternative workflows | Availability and latency monitoring | Exercise continuity and recovery procedures |
| Inadequate incident evidence | Contractual logging and cooperation requirements | Monitoring for missing or delayed evidence | Tabletop exercise and evidence-access validation |

A control should be considered effective only when its design is appropriate and implementation has been verified.

For example, a written requirement that all high-impact outputs receive human review is insufficient if the application allows those outputs to trigger consequential actions without an approval checkpoint.

### 8.7 Threat Modeling Limitations

Threat modeling cannot establish that an AI system is completely secure.

The provider may not expose its full internal architecture, training data, model weights, or detailed evaluation results. The enterprise must therefore document assurance limitations and avoid making unsupported assumptions about provider internals.

Where direct verification is unavailable, the organization may rely on a combination of contractual commitments, independent assurance, service documentation, controlled testing, observed behavior, and restrictions on permitted use.

If a material threat cannot be adequately assessed or treated, the organization should reduce the deployment scope, restrict sensitive integrations, require additional evidence, or reject the proposed use case.

**Core Principle:** Threat modeling must connect realistic attack paths to specific assets, enforceable controls, observable evidence, and documented residual risk.

## 9. Security Control Architecture and Risk Treatment

### 9.1 Defense-in-Depth for AI SaaS

No single control can adequately secure every aspect of a third-party AI SaaS deployment.

For example:

- Encryption does not prevent an authorized user from submitting confidential information.
- SSO does not prevent an authenticated user from misusing the service.
- A provider's security certification does not validate every AI output.
- Prompt filtering does not eliminate all prompt-injection attacks.
- Human review is ineffective if reviewers lack sufficient information or routinely approve outputs without scrutiny.
- Logging does not prevent an incident, although it can support detection and investigation.

The enterprise should implement defense in depth across identity, data, application integration, AI behavior, supplier governance, and operational monitoring.

### 9.2 Control Layer 1: Identity and Access Management

Identity and access management controls should govern who can use the service, what data they can access, and which actions they can perform.

Recommended controls include:

- Enterprise single sign-on where supported.
- Multi-factor authentication.
- Role-based access control.
- Least-privilege permissions.
- Privileged access management for administrative accounts.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Prompt removal of access when users change roles or leave.
- Restrictions on unmanaged accounts where appropriate.
- Separate identities for users, applications, and automated integrations.
- Secure management and rotation of API credentials.
- Conditional access or device restrictions where justified by risk.

The enterprise should evaluate whether the provider supports the required controls in the selected product tier.

It should also distinguish between permission to access the AI service and permission to access data through the service.

A user may be authorized to use the assistant but not authorized to retrieve every document indexed by its retrieval system.

### 9.3 Control Layer 2: Data Protection

Data controls should prevent unnecessary or unauthorized disclosure.

Relevant measures include:

- Data classification before submission.
- Approved and prohibited data categories.
- Data minimization.
- Redaction or pseudonymization where appropriate.
- Data-loss prevention controls.
- Encryption in transit and at rest, as applicable.
- Restriction of sensitive uploads.
- Provider data-use and retention restrictions.
- Appropriate deletion and records-management procedures.
- Segregation of customer or business-unit information.
- Protection of prompts, responses, and logs.
- Restrictions on secrets and credentials.
- Documented handling of data-subject requests.

The enterprise should not rely exclusively on user awareness when technical enforcement is feasible and proportionate to the risk.

For example, an organization handling sensitive customer data should consider whether data-loss prevention, approved connectors, and controlled integration paths are more reliable than a policy that simply tells employees not to paste confidential information into an AI tool.

### 9.4 Control Layer 3: Application and API Security

Where the AI SaaS service integrates with enterprise applications, the integration boundary becomes a critical security control point.

Controls should include:

**Authentication**

Verify the identity of users, applications, and services.

**Authorization**

Enforce permissions for each data request and business action.

**Input validation**

Validate request structure, allowed parameters, data categories, and expected formats.

**Output validation**

Validate AI outputs before they are used by downstream systems.

**Rate limiting**

Prevent excessive requests and reduce the impact of abuse or uncontrolled consumption.

**Secrets management**

Store API keys and other credentials in approved secret-management systems rather than embedding them in source code, prompts, or configuration files without appropriate protection.

**Network and egress controls**

Restrict outbound connections to approved endpoints where technically feasible.

**Error handling**

Prevent sensitive information from being exposed through error messages or diagnostic responses.

**Transaction controls**

Require appropriate authorization for financial, administrative, or otherwise consequential actions.

**Dependency management**

Assess and monitor relevant libraries, integration services, and other technical dependencies.

### 9.5 Control Layer 4: AI-Specific Security

AI-specific controls should address threats arising from the model's behavior and the way it processes instructions, context, and outputs.

Relevant measures include:

- Explicitly defining the system's intended purpose and permitted tasks.
- Separating trusted system instructions from untrusted user and retrieved content.
- Treating retrieved documents and external content as potentially malicious.
- Restricting the data available to each request.
- Enforcing authorization outside the model rather than relying on the model to enforce permissions.
- Constraining tool access to approved operations.
- Validating model outputs before execution.
- Applying safety and policy checks where appropriate.
- Testing for prompt injection and sensitive-information disclosure.
- Evaluating hallucination rates and task-specific accuracy.
- Monitoring behavior after model updates.
- Providing mechanisms to disable or restrict unsafe functionality.
- Maintaining human oversight for consequential decisions.

An important design rule is that **the model should not be the sole enforcement mechanism for security-critical decisions**.

For example, a model may be instructed not to delete customer records. That instruction is not a sufficient security boundary if the connected service account has unrestricted deletion permissions.

The application should independently enforce authorization, transaction limits, and approval requirements.

### 9.6 Control Layer 5: Supplier and Contractual Governance

Technical controls must be supported by appropriate supplier obligations.

Contractual and governance requirements should address, where applicable:

- Permitted data-processing purposes.
- Restrictions on training and secondary data use.
- Confidentiality obligations.
- Data-retention and deletion requirements.
- Subprocessor governance.
- Security incident notification and cooperation.
- Vulnerability management.
- Service availability and recovery.
- Material service and model changes.
- Security assurance and audit evidence.
- Data export and portability.
- Termination assistance.
- Allocation of responsibilities.
- Applicable legal and regulatory obligations.

Contract language should be specific enough to verify compliance.

For example, a clause requiring the provider to "maintain appropriate security" may be insufficient for a critical service unless it is supported by defined obligations, evidence rights, incident requirements, and appropriate remedies.

The enterprise should also evaluate whether the provider can practically meet the obligations being negotiated. Contractual language without operational capability does not create effective security.

### 9.7 Control Layer 6: Monitoring and Incident Response

AI SaaS monitoring should combine enterprise-side telemetry with provider-side evidence where available.

Relevant events include:

- User authentication and access changes.
- Administrative activity.
- API requests and authorization failures.
- Sensitive-data policy violations.
- Unusual request volumes.
- Suspicious retrieval patterns.
- Tool invocation and consequential actions.
- Output-validation failures.
- Provider availability and latency.
- Material model or service changes.
- Security incidents and provider notifications.
- Unusual cost or usage patterns.

The enterprise should determine which events can be collected, which must be obtained from the provider, and which cannot be observed.

Where prompt or response logging would create additional privacy or confidentiality risks, the organization should apply appropriate minimization, access restrictions, and retention controls.

Incident response procedures should cover both conventional security incidents and AI-specific events, including unsafe outputs, prompt injection, unauthorized tool execution, unexpected data disclosure, and material changes in system behavior.

### 9.8 Control Mapping Matrix

| Risk area | Primary controls | Responsible function | Example assurance evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unauthorized access | SSO, MFA, RBAC, access reviews | IAM / Information Security | Access configuration, review records, authentication logs |
| Data leakage | Classification, DLP, minimization, provider restrictions | Data Governance / Security | Policy configuration, test results, contractual terms |
| Privacy noncompliance | Privacy assessment, processing agreements, retention controls | Privacy / Legal | Assessment records, processing terms, retention evidence |
| Prompt injection | Untrusted-input handling, restricted tools, validation | AI Security / Application Security | Red-team reports, test cases, remediation records |
| Unauthorized actions | Least privilege, external authorization, approval gates | Application Owner / Security | Permission tests, workflow approvals, audit logs |
| Inaccurate outputs | Evaluation, output validation, human review | Business Owner / AI System Owner | Evaluation reports, quality metrics, review records |
| Supplier compromise | TPRM, independent assurance, incident cooperation | Procurement / Security | Supplier assessment, assurance reports, contract |
| Provider outage | Fallback processes, continuity planning, recovery testing | Business Continuity / IT Operations | Recovery tests, continuity plans, service reports |
| Model changes | Change notification, regression testing, release controls | AI System Owner / Change Management | Change records, test results, release approvals |
| Audit evidence gaps | Logging requirements, evidence retention, contractual access | Compliance / Security / Legal | Evidence register, log catalogue, contract clauses |
| Cost and usage abuse | Quotas, budgets, rate limits, anomaly alerts | FinOps / Service Owner | Usage reports, alert configuration, budget records |
| Exit and lock-in | Portability assessment, export testing, termination plan | Enterprise Architecture / Procurement | Export test results, exit plan, deletion confirmation |

The responsible function should be refined into named accountable and operational roles within the organization's governance model.

### 9.9 Risk Treatment Options

Risk treatment should follow the enterprise's established risk-management methodology.

Four common treatment options are:

**1. Mitigate**

Implement controls that reduce likelihood, impact, or both.

Example: Restrict a CRM-connected AI assistant to read-only access and require human approval for customer-record changes.

**2. Avoid**

Do not undertake the activity that creates unacceptable exposure.

Example: Prohibit submission of highly sensitive customer records to a provider whose data-use terms do not meet mandatory enterprise requirements.

**3. Transfer or share**

Use contractual arrangements, insurance, or other mechanisms to allocate certain financial or operational consequences.

This does not eliminate the underlying risk or automatically transfer legal accountability.

Example: Establish contractual incident notification, remediation, and liability provisions with the provider.

**4. Accept**

Retain the residual risk through a documented decision by an authorized risk owner.

Acceptance should be explicit, time-bounded where appropriate, and consistent with organizational risk appetite.

Example: Accept a documented, limited availability risk for a non-critical AI drafting service because a manual fallback remains available.

The organization should not treat acceptance as a substitute for implementing mandatory controls.

### 9.10 Risk Treatment Example

Consider a third-party AI assistant connected to an enterprise knowledge repository containing confidential commercial information.

The initial assessment identifies a risk that the assistant may retrieve information outside the requesting employee's authorization.

**Initial risk statement**

Because the retrieval integration may not consistently enforce source permissions, an employee could receive confidential information belonging to another department, resulting in unauthorized disclosure and possible contractual or reputational damage.

**Proposed treatment**

1. Enforce source-system authorization before retrieval.
2. Associate each request with the authenticated user's identity and permitted access scope.
3. Restrict the retrieval service to approved repositories.
4. Prevent the model from independently expanding its data-access privileges.
5. Log relevant retrieval decisions and access denials.
6. Test cross-user, cross-department, and unauthorized-document scenarios.
7. Monitor for suspicious retrieval behavior.
8. Establish an incident procedure for suspected unauthorized disclosure.

**Verification**

The security team tests whether a user can retrieve documents outside the user's authorized scope, including through indirect requests and adversarial prompts.

The team records the results, verifies remediation, and documents any remaining limitations.

**Residual-risk decision**

The risk owner reviews the evidence, confirms that the remaining risk is within the approved tolerance, and obtains additional approval if required.

This example illustrates the complete treatment cycle: define the risk, implement controls, test effectiveness, assess residual risk, and obtain authorized acceptance.

**Core Principle:** Security controls must operate across the enterprise, the integration layer, the AI service, and the supplier relationship. Risk treatment is complete only when the controls have been verified and the remaining exposure has been formally addressed.

## 10. AI SaaS Governance Gate and Initial Deployment Decision

### 10.1 Purpose of the Governance Gate

The AI SaaS governance gate is a formal decision point used to determine whether a proposed service may proceed to production.

It consolidates the outputs of supplier due diligence, legal and privacy review, security architecture, AI evaluation, business validation, and residual-risk assessment.

The purpose is to prevent an AI service from entering production merely because:

- Procurement has completed a purchase.
- A business unit has started a pilot.
- The provider holds security certifications.
- The service has demonstrated useful outputs.
- The integration is technically functional.
- The project deadline is approaching.

Technical functionality and business usefulness do not establish that a service is safe, compliant, or operationally ready.

### 10.2 Mandatory Governance Gate Criteria

| Gate | Approval question | Required evidence | Failure outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business justification | Is the use case defined and justified? | Business case, scope, success criteria, accountable owner | Reject or return for clarification |
| Data governance | Are permitted data categories and processing purposes defined? | Data-flow map, classification, retention requirements | Block affected data use |
| Supplier due diligence | Is the provider sufficiently assessed for the risk tier? | Supplier assessment, assurance evidence, findings register | Escalate, restrict, or reject |
| Legal and privacy | Are applicable obligations identified and addressed? | Legal review, privacy assessment, contractual terms | Block until mandatory requirements are met |
| Security architecture | Are trust boundaries and integrations adequately protected? | Architecture review, threat model, control design | Block unresolved material exposures |
| AI evaluation | Is the service suitable for its intended purpose? | Test results, performance criteria, known limitations | Restrict use or require remediation |
| Access control | Are user and service permissions appropriately limited? | Access configuration, permission tests, service-account design | Block excessive privileges |
| Incident readiness | Can relevant incidents be detected, investigated, and escalated? | Logging design, response procedures, provider contacts | Block high-risk production use until addressed |
| Business continuity | Is there a viable fallback for service disruption? | Continuity assessment, recovery procedures, test evidence | Restrict critical dependency |
| Residual-risk approval | Is the remaining exposure within authorized tolerance? | Risk register, treatment evidence, signed decision | Escalate or reject |

Some gates may be satisfied through equivalent evidence appropriate to the architecture. However, mandatory requirements must not be waived merely because a provider does not expose a desired capability.

### 10.3 Governance Decision Outcomes

The gate should produce one of four explicit outcomes.

**Approved**

The service satisfies the applicable requirements, required controls are implemented, and residual risks have been accepted by authorized stakeholders.

**Conditionally approved**

The service may proceed within a defined scope subject to enforceable restrictions, specified remediation actions, deadlines, and accountable owners.

Conditional approval should not permit activities that violate mandatory legal, contractual, or security requirements.

**Deferred**

The decision is postponed because essential evidence, testing, or remediation is incomplete.

**Rejected**

The proposed deployment is unacceptable because mandatory requirements cannot be met, material risks cannot be adequately treated, or the business justification does not support the remaining exposure.

### 10.4 Example: Conditional Approval

A company proposes an AI SaaS assistant for internal document summarization.

The provider has completed the enterprise's standard security assessment. However, the enterprise has not yet validated the provider's retention behavior for prompts submitted through a particular integration.

The organization may consider a restricted pilot if:

- Only approved, low-sensitivity documents are permitted.
- Confidential and personal data are blocked from the pilot.
- The integration uses least-privilege access.
- The pilot has a defined scope and end date.
- Monitoring and incident escalation are enabled.
- The outstanding retention issue has a named owner and deadline.
- Production expansion requires a new approval decision.

The organization should not describe this as unrestricted approval of the provider or service.

It is approval of a specific deployment configuration under defined conditions.

### 10.5 Governance Gate Decision Record

Every material deployment should maintain a decision record containing:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Service identity | Provider, product, service tier, and relevant configuration |
| Business purpose | Approved use cases and excluded activities |
| Business owner | Accountable owner of the business process |
| AI system owner | Accountable owner for AI lifecycle and operational governance |
| Data scope | Approved data categories, sources, and processing purposes |
| Architecture | Integrations, trust boundaries, and system dependencies |
| Supplier assessment | Risk tier, due diligence results, and outstanding findings |
| Legal and privacy position | Applicable requirements and completed assessments |
| Security assessment | Threat model, control findings, and test results |
| AI evaluation | Performance criteria, limitations, and validation evidence |
| Residual risks | Remaining exposures and treatment plans |
| Risk acceptance | Authorized approver and acceptance rationale |
| Conditions | Restrictions, remediation actions, deadlines, and owners |
| Monitoring | Required metrics, alerts, and review frequency |
| Continuity and exit | Fallback arrangements, portability, and termination considerations |
| Approval status | Approved, conditionally approved, deferred, or rejected |
| Review trigger | Scheduled reassessment and material-change triggers |

This record provides traceability between the original business request, the assessed risk, the implemented controls, and the final decision.

### 10.6 Final Deployment Principle

The enterprise should approve third-party AI SaaS based on a defensible combination of business value, supplier assurance, legal applicability, technical controls, AI evaluation, operational readiness, and residual-risk acceptance.

The decision should be specific to the actual deployment and remain valid only while the relevant assumptions and controls continue to hold.

Material changes in provider behavior, data scope, integration permissions, business purpose, legal requirements, or threat conditions should trigger reassessment.

**Core Principle:** Production approval is a documented risk decision, not a procurement milestone. The organization must demonstrate that the proposed AI SaaS deployment is suitable for its intended purpose, adequately controlled, operationally supportable, and approved by the appropriate authority.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Supplier due diligence vs. continuous assurance:** Initial assessment establishes a baseline; ongoing monitoring determines whether the provider and deployment remain acceptable.
- **Framework alignment vs. legal compliance:** Frameworks support structured governance but do not replace applicable legal analysis.
- **Threat identification vs. risk treatment:** Identifying an attack scenario is only the first step; effective controls and residual-risk decisions are also required.
- **Control design vs. control effectiveness:** A documented control is not sufficient without evidence that it operates as intended.
- **Conditional approval vs. unrestricted approval:** A restricted pilot must remain within its explicitly authorized boundaries.
- **Technical success vs. production readiness:** A functioning integration does not establish that the service meets security, privacy, governance, and resilience requirements.
