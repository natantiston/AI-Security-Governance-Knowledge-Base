# 21.18 — Third-Party AI SaaS Deployment
## Part 1: Business Context, AI SaaS Architecture, Data Flows, and Risk Landscape

### 1. Introduction to Third-Party AI SaaS Governance

Organizations increasingly adopt third-party Artificial Intelligence Software-as-a-Service (AI SaaS) platforms to improve productivity, automate business processes, analyze information, support decision-making, and deliver new digital services.

Instead of developing and operating AI infrastructure internally, an organization can subscribe to an externally hosted service that provides access to foundation models, generative AI capabilities, intelligent automation, enterprise search, conversational assistants, predictive analytics, or AI-enabled business applications.

Examples include enterprise AI assistants, AI-powered customer-service platforms, intelligent document-processing systems, AI-enabled Customer Relationship Management (CRM) applications, and specialized AI solutions integrated into enterprise workflows.

These services can deliver significant business value, but they also introduce dependencies and risks that traditional SaaS governance may not fully address.

The organization must evaluate not only the security of the software and infrastructure, but also how AI models process information, generate outputs, interact with connected systems, respond to untrusted instructions, and influence business decisions.

**The central governance challenge is determining whether the organization can use an externally operated AI service while maintaining appropriate control over its data, identities, business processes, security obligations, and residual risks.**

#### 1.1 What Is Third-Party AI SaaS?

Third-party AI SaaS is a software service delivered and operated by an external provider that incorporates AI capabilities and is made available to customers through a subscription, consumption-based pricing model, or another commercial arrangement.

The service may include several layers:

| Layer | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| SaaS application | The application through which users interact with the AI service. | Enterprise AI assistant or AI-powered CRM application. |
| AI model | The model generating, classifying, summarizing, predicting, or transforming information. | Large language model or specialized predictive model. |
| Model orchestration | Components coordinating prompts, model calls, retrieval, tools, and output processing. | Workflow engine or model gateway. |
| Data processing | Components handling user inputs, retrieved documents, conversation history, and outputs. | Document processing, embeddings, or retrieval pipelines. |
| Integration layer | Connections between the AI service and enterprise applications. | CRM, ERP, collaboration platforms, and enterprise APIs. |
| Security and administration | Capabilities controlling identity, permissions, policies, logging, and administration. | Single sign-on, role-based access, audit logs, and administrative policies. |
| Provider infrastructure | Underlying computing, storage, networking, and supporting services. | Cloud infrastructure operated by the provider or its subcontractors. |

Not every AI SaaS product exposes these layers separately. Some providers offer a fully integrated application, while others allow customers to configure models, retrieval sources, tools, and workflows.

From a GRC perspective, the objective is to understand which components exist, who controls them, and which risks can be managed by the customer.

#### 1.2 Traditional SaaS Versus AI SaaS

Traditional SaaS and AI SaaS share many security requirements, but AI introduces additional sources of uncertainty.

| Dimension | Traditional SaaS | AI SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| Primary functionality | Executes predefined application logic and business workflows. | May generate content, interpret natural-language instructions, infer patterns, or produce probabilistic outputs. |
| Input handling | Often relies on structured forms, predefined fields, and application interfaces. | May accept natural-language prompts, uploaded files, images, audio, and retrieved content. |
| Output behavior | Usually follows predefined application logic and data rules. | May produce variable outputs that are inaccurate, inconsistent, or influenced by context. |
| Security boundaries | Commonly centered on identity, application permissions, APIs, and infrastructure. | Must additionally consider model behavior, prompt injection, retrieval security, and generated content. |
| Data exposure | Data may be exposed through application vulnerabilities, misconfiguration, or excessive access. | Data may also be disclosed through prompts, retrieval results, conversation history, or generated outputs. |
| Change management | Software releases and configuration changes are key concerns. | Model changes, altered safety behavior, retrieval changes, and AI-specific regressions may also matter. |
| Testing | Functional testing, penetration testing, configuration review, and control validation. | These remain necessary, supplemented by AI-specific adversarial testing and output evaluation. |
| Human oversight | Depends on business process and application criticality. | May require additional review because users can over-trust generated content or automated recommendations. |
| Risk assessment | Often focuses on application security, data protection, and availability. | Must also address AI-specific failure modes and the consequences of probabilistic behavior. |

The distinction does not mean every AI SaaS service is inherently more dangerous than traditional SaaS. Risk depends on the intended use, data sensitivity, integration scope, autonomy, and potential consequences of failure.

An AI tool that drafts non-sensitive internal communications may present limited business risk. The same underlying model connected to customer records, financial transactions, or privileged administrative tools may require substantially stronger controls.

#### 1.3 Common Enterprise Use Cases

| Use case | Business objective | Typical data processed | Main governance concerns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise AI assistant | Improve employee productivity and knowledge access. | Internal documents, prompts, emails, and business records. | Confidentiality, access control, provider data use, and inaccurate output. |
| AI customer-service chatbot | Automate customer inquiries and support. | Customer identifiers, conversation history, product information, and support records. | Privacy, customer harm, identity verification, and unauthorized disclosure. |
| AI-powered contact center | Support agents, summarize calls, and automate selected interactions. | Call recordings, transcripts, customer records, and interaction metadata. | Personal-data processing, retention, transcription accuracy, and human oversight. |
| AI-powered CRM | Improve customer insights, sales workflows, and campaign personalization. | Customer profiles, sales records, preferences, and communications. | Data minimization, profiling, permissions, and downstream decision impact. |
| AI document processing | Extract information from contracts, invoices, and business documents. | Commercial agreements, financial records, and potentially personal data. | Extraction accuracy, data leakage, document integrity, and retention. |
| AI coding assistant | Accelerate software development. | Source code, technical documentation, and developer prompts. | Intellectual property, secret exposure, insecure code, and software supply-chain risk. |
| AI security operations | Support alert triage, investigation, and security analysis. | Security logs, incident records, indicators, and investigation notes. | Sensitive telemetry, false conclusions, privileged access, and response automation. |
| AI financial analysis | Support financial research, forecasting, or decision preparation. | Financial information, customer data, and market information. | Accuracy, confidentiality, model limitations, and regulated decision-making. |
| AI recruitment assistant | Support candidate screening and recruitment workflows. | CVs, employment history, interview records, and assessment data. | Discrimination, privacy, explainability, and employment-related obligations. |
| AI knowledge-management platform | Search and summarize enterprise knowledge. | Documents, policies, project records, and internal communications. | Retrieval authorization, stale information, and cross-department data exposure. |

The GRC team should assess the actual use case rather than classifying risk solely according to the product's marketing category.

A product marketed as a general-purpose assistant can become a high-impact business dependency when integrated into consequential workflows.

#### 1.4 Governance Objectives

The organization should establish the following objectives before deployment:

1. **Business legitimacy:** Confirm that the AI service addresses a defined business need and has an accountable owner.
2. **Approved use:** Define permitted and prohibited activities, users, data categories, and decisions.
3. **Data protection:** Ensure that data submitted to or generated by the service is handled according to applicable requirements.
4. **Supplier assurance:** Establish whether the provider can meet the organization's security, privacy, contractual, and operational requirements.
5. **Security by design:** Integrate identity, authorization, encryption, logging, and secure integration requirements into the architecture.
6. **AI-specific risk management:** Assess prompt injection, output reliability, retrieval security, and other relevant AI failure modes.
7. **Human accountability:** Define where human review, approval, or intervention is required.
8. **Operational resilience:** Establish how the organization will detect incidents, manage service disruptions, and maintain critical operations.
9. **Continuous assurance:** Reassess risks when the service, provider, model, data, or business use changes.
10. **Controlled exit:** Maintain the ability to terminate the service and manage data, identities, integrations, and business dependencies securely.

These objectives should be reflected in the organization's AI governance policy, third-party risk management process, security requirements, and approval workflow.

### 2. Business Context, Ownership, and Shared Responsibility

Third-party AI SaaS governance begins with understanding the business context.

A technically secure service can still be unsuitable if it is used for an unauthorized purpose, processes data without an appropriate legal basis, produces outputs that users treat as authoritative without verification, or introduces unacceptable dependency on a supplier.

The organization must therefore connect the technology assessment to the business process and the consequences of failure.

#### 2.1 Define the Business Use Case

A use case should be documented before procurement or implementation proceeds too far.

| Business context element | Required description |
|---|---|
| Business problem | What problem is the organization trying to solve? |
| Intended purpose | What specific tasks will the AI service perform? |
| Business owner | Who is accountable for the business outcome and appropriate use? |
| User population | Which employees, contractors, customers, or external parties will use the service? |
| Data categories | What information will be submitted, retrieved, generated, or stored? |
| Decision impact | Will the output influence customer, employee, financial, legal, security, or operational decisions? |
| Autonomy level | Does the service provide suggestions, prepare actions, or execute actions? |
| Integration scope | Which enterprise applications and data sources will be connected? |
| Business criticality | What happens if the service becomes unavailable or produces incorrect output? |
| Success criteria | How will business value, accuracy, and acceptable performance be measured? |
| Limitations | What should the service not be used for? |
| Exit conditions | Under what circumstances would the organization suspend or terminate the service? |

A vague objective such as “improve productivity using AI” is insufficient for a defensible risk assessment.

A stronger statement would be:

> The organization intends to deploy an enterprise AI assistant for internal document summarization and policy discovery. The initial deployment will exclude restricted customer records, regulated decision-making, and autonomous execution of business transactions. The business owner will evaluate productivity improvements, output quality, and security-control effectiveness during a controlled pilot.

This statement defines an intended purpose, a restricted scope, and measurable governance considerations.

#### 2.2 Distinguish AI Assistance from Autonomous Execution

One of the most important decisions is determining what the AI is permitted to do.

| Autonomy level | Description | Example | Governance implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1: Information retrieval | Retrieves or presents information from approved sources. | Finds a company policy or summarizes an internal document. | Strong access controls, source authorization, and information accuracy checks. |
| Level 2: Content generation | Produces drafts, summaries, explanations, or recommendations. | Drafts an email or summarizes a customer interaction. | Output validation, confidentiality protection, and user guidance. |
| Level 3: Decision support | Recommends an action that a person evaluates. | Suggests a support-ticket priority or identifies a potential fraud case. | Decision criteria, human oversight, performance monitoring, and appropriate challenge mechanisms. |
| Level 4: Human-approved execution | Prepares or initiates an action that requires explicit human approval before completion. | Prepares a refund or proposes a customer-account change for approval. | Separation of duties, approval controls, transaction validation, and audit logging. |
| Level 5: Bounded autonomous execution | Executes predefined actions within constrained permissions and approved limits. | Automatically categorizes tickets or performs a low-impact workflow action. | Strict authorization, action limits, monitoring, rollback or recovery, and defined suspension criteria. |

These levels are a practical governance model, not a universal regulatory classification.

The appropriate level depends on the use case. Even information retrieval can create significant risk if the system exposes sensitive information to unauthorized users.

A useful rule is:

**The greater the AI service's authority to access information, influence decisions, or execute actions, the stronger the controls required around authorization, validation, monitoring, and accountability.**

#### 2.4 Customer Responsibilities

The organization should normally expect to manage or oversee the following areas:

- Define the intended use and permitted data categories.
- Determine which employees or systems may access the service.
- Configure identity, permissions, and customer-controlled security settings.
- Protect API keys, integration credentials, and service identities.
- Review data flows and authorize data transfers.
- Validate AI outputs where accuracy or safety matters.
- Assess supplier security and contractual commitments.
- Maintain appropriate customer-side logs and monitoring.
- Manage incidents affecting the organization's users, data, or business processes.
- Ensure that applicable regulatory and contractual obligations are addressed.
- Review changes to the service and the intended use.
- Maintain a secure exit and continuity plan.

The organization cannot delegate accountability for its own business decisions merely by using an external AI provider.

#### 2.5 Provider Responsibilities

Depending on the contract and service architecture, the provider may be responsible for:

- Operating the contracted service and supporting infrastructure.
- Protecting provider-managed components.
- Maintaining service availability within agreed commitments.
- Implementing provider-side security controls.
- Managing provider personnel and privileged access.
- Operating the model service and associated infrastructure.
- Managing subprocessors under applicable contractual obligations.
- Providing relevant security documentation and assurance evidence.
- Notifying customers of specified incidents or material changes.
- Implementing agreed retention, export, and deletion processes.
- Supporting contractual incident response and service continuity arrangements.

These responsibilities must be verified rather than assumed. A provider may offer security features that are optional, restricted to particular subscription tiers, or dependent on customer configuration.

#### 2.6 Accountability and Governance Boundaries

The organization should identify accountable owners for:

| Governance area | Typical accountable role |
|---|---|
| Business need and intended purpose | Business owner |
| AI service lifecycle | AI system or service owner |
| Data classification and permitted use | Data owner |
| Cybersecurity risk assessment | Designated cybersecurity risk owner or governance function, according to policy |
| Privacy compliance | Relevant controller and privacy governance functions |
| Supplier relationship | Supplier or contract owner |
| Architecture and technical integration | Enterprise architect or designated technology owner |
| Security-control operation | Relevant control owner |
| Business-process decisions | Authorized business decision-maker |
| Residual risk acceptance | Person or committee with appropriate delegated authority |
| Independent assurance | Internal audit or another appropriately independent assurance function |

The person coordinating a risk assessment is not automatically the person authorized to accept the risk.

Similarly, a provider's security team cannot approve the customer's business use of the service on the customer's behalf.

### 3. Reference Architecture for Third-Party AI SaaS

Architecture analysis establishes how users, data, models, enterprise systems, and external providers interact.

The GRC team does not need to develop the AI model or write application code to assess the architecture. It does need to understand the system boundaries, information flows, trust relationships, and security responsibilities.

The goal is to identify where information can enter, where it is processed, which systems can access it, and where unauthorized actions or disclosure could occur.

#### 3.1 High-Level Reference Architecture

Consider an enterprise deploying a third-party AI assistant connected to an internal knowledge repository and selected business applications.

Actual deployments may process data in the provider's environment, the customer's environment, or both. Some services may use external model providers, while others operate their own models. The architecture must be validated against the selected service.

#### 3.2 Architecture Components and Security Considerations

| Component | Function | Security and governance concerns |
|---|---|---|
| User interface | Accepts prompts and presents outputs. | User authentication, data submission, session security, and inappropriate use. |
| Enterprise identity provider | Establishes user identity and authentication context. | MFA, account lifecycle, session controls, and identity federation. |
| AI access layer | Enforces customer-side policies and routes approved requests. | Policy bypass, insufficient inspection, and incomplete visibility. |
| SaaS application | Coordinates the user experience and AI features. | Tenant configuration, administrative privileges, and data exposure. |
| Model orchestration | Coordinates prompts, context, model calls, and workflows. | Prompt injection, unsafe tool invocation, and incorrect trust assumptions. |
| AI model service | Generates or transforms content. | Model limitations, provider data handling, output quality, and model changes. |
| Knowledge connector | Retrieves information from enterprise sources. | Excessive permissions, stale indexes, insecure credentials, and authorization failures. |
| Enterprise repository | Stores documents and business records. | Classification, access control, retention, integrity, and source-data protection. |
| Enterprise API | Allows access to other applications or workflows. | Excessive API scopes, credential exposure, transaction misuse, and missing authorization checks. |
| Logging and monitoring | Records relevant activity and supports detection. | Incomplete telemetry, retention gaps, tampering, and insufficient investigation context. |
| Provider infrastructure | Hosts service components and data-processing resources. | Provider security, availability, isolation, and subcontractor dependency. |

#### 3.3 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary separates components or entities with different security assumptions, permissions, or administrative control.

Third-party AI SaaS deployments commonly contain several important trust boundaries.

**Boundary 1: User to enterprise environment**

The organization must establish that the user is authenticated and authorized to use the AI service.

**Boundary 2: Enterprise environment to provider**

Data leaves the organization's direct administrative boundary when it is transmitted to an external service. The organization must understand what is transmitted, under which contractual terms, and how the provider processes it.

**Boundary 3: Model to connected data sources**

The model may request or receive information from enterprise repositories. The organization must verify that the requesting user is authorized to access the retrieved content.

**Boundary 4: Model to external tools**

If the service can invoke APIs or business workflows, a model-generated instruction may lead to an action. Tool authorization must be enforced independently of the model's decision.

**Boundary 5: Provider to subprocessors**

Provider-managed information may be processed by cloud infrastructure providers, model suppliers, analytics services, or other subcontractors.

**Boundary 6: AI output to business decision**

Generated information may be consumed by a person or downstream application. The organization must establish whether the output requires validation before it influences a consequential decision.

#### 3.4 Architecture Review Questions

A GRC consultant should ask the architecture and technology teams:

1. Where is the AI application hosted?
2. Which provider operates the model?
3. Does the service use additional subprocessors?
4. What data leaves the enterprise environment?
5. Where are prompts, files, outputs, and logs processed and retained?
6. Are data transfers encrypted?
7. How are user identities propagated into connected data sources?
8. Are source-system permissions enforced during retrieval?
9. Can the AI access or modify business records?
10. Which operations require explicit human approval?
11. How are service identities and API credentials managed?
12. Which security events are visible to the customer?
13. Can the organization disable individual integrations or features?
14. What happens when the provider or model becomes unavailable?
15. How are model changes and material service changes communicated?
16. Can the organization export required data and evidence at termination?

These questions should be supported by architecture diagrams, provider documentation, configuration evidence, and targeted testing.

#### 3.5 Architecture and Data-Flow Documentation

At minimum, the organization should maintain:

- A system context diagram.
- A component architecture diagram.
- A data-flow diagram.
- A trust-boundary diagram.
- An identity and access model.
- An integration inventory.
- A supplier and subprocessor dependency inventory.
- A security-control responsibility matrix.
- A record of material assumptions and known limitations.

The diagrams should distinguish the enterprise environment from the provider environment and show the direction of data movement.

A diagram that merely displays boxes labeled “User,” “AI,” and “Database” is insufficient for a detailed risk assessment if it omits trust boundaries, permissions, data categories, and external dependencies.

### 4. Data Flows, Data Classification, and Information Protection

Data governance is a central part of third-party AI SaaS risk management because the organization may disclose information to a provider outside its direct administrative control.

The organization must understand what information enters the AI service, why it is processed, who can access it, how it is retained, and whether it can be used for additional purposes.

A service may be technically secure and still be unsuitable for a particular data category if the contractual or legal conditions for processing that data are not satisfied.

#### 4.1 AI SaaS Data Lifecycle

A typical AI SaaS data lifecycle includes the following stages:

```text id="aissaas18-01-03"
[Data Created or Collected]
            |
            v
[Classification and Authorization]
            |
            v
[Data Submitted to AI SaaS]
            |
            v
[Provider-Side Processing]
            |
            v
[Retrieval / Context Assembly]
            |
            v
[Model Processing]
            |
            v
[AI-Generated Output]
            |
            v
[Human Review or Application Use]
            |
            v
[Storage, Retention, or Deletion]
```

This is a simplified lifecycle. Some services also maintain conversation history, embeddings, cached content, audit logs, evaluation records, or other derived information.

The organization should determine which data artifacts exist and whether each artifact is subject to different access, retention, and deletion requirements.

#### 4.2 Data Categories

| Data category | Examples | Main concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Public information | Public product descriptions, published policies, public documentation. | Accuracy, integrity, and unauthorized use of external content. |
| Internal business information | Internal procedures, project plans, meeting notes. | Unauthorized disclosure and inappropriate sharing. |
| Confidential business information | Commercial strategy, financial forecasts, contracts, intellectual property. | Competitive harm, contractual exposure, and loss of confidentiality. |
| Personal data | Customer details, employee records, contact information. | Lawful processing, transparency, data minimization, access rights, and retention. |
| Sensitive or specially protected personal data | Health information or other data receiving special legal protection where applicable. | Heightened legal restrictions, access controls, and potential harm to individuals. |
| Authentication secrets | Passwords, API keys, access tokens, private keys. | Credential compromise and unauthorized access. |
| Security-sensitive information | Vulnerability reports, incident records, detection rules, architecture diagrams. | Facilitation of attacks and exposure of defensive capabilities. |
| Regulated business information | Payment-card data, financial records, or other regulated records. | Applicable legal, contractual, and sector-specific requirements. |

The exact classification labels should align with the organization's data-classification policy.

The organization must not assume that a provider's default security settings make every data category appropriate for processing.

#### 4.3 Data-Flow Assessment

For every material data flow, document:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Data-flow ID | Unique identifier for the flow. |
| Source system | System or person from which the data originates. |
| Destination | AI SaaS service, provider component, connected application, or downstream recipient. |
| Data categories | Types of information transmitted. |
| Business purpose | Reason for the transfer. |
| Data owner | Person or role authorizing the use. |
| Processing party | Organization or supplier processing the data. |
| Transfer mechanism | API, browser session, connector, file upload, or other mechanism. |
| Security controls | Encryption, authentication, authorization, DLP, and other applicable safeguards. |
| Retention | Expected storage duration and deletion requirements. |
| Legal or contractual basis | Applicable authorization and processing requirements. |
| Logging | Evidence of relevant transfers and access events. |
| Risk assessment | Identified risks and treatment decisions. |

This inventory supports privacy assessments, security architecture reviews, supplier assurance, incident response, and audit readiness.

#### 4.4 Example: Enterprise Knowledge Assistant

Assume an organization connects an AI assistant to an internal document repository.

An employee asks:

> Summarize the latest customer contract and identify its renewal obligations.

The system may process:

1. The employee's identity and request.
2. Search terms or retrieval instructions.
3. Document metadata.
4. Relevant contract content.
5. Context assembled for the model.
6. The model-generated summary.
7. Relevant audit and usage events.

Potential risks include:

- The employee is not authorized to access the contract.
- The retrieval system ignores source-document permissions.
- The provider retains the document content beyond the approved period.
- The generated summary omits an important contractual condition.
- A malicious instruction embedded in the document influences the output.
- The output is stored in a location with broader access than the source document.

The risk assessment must therefore cover both the original document and every subsequent representation of its information.

#### 4.5 Prompt, Output, and Derived-Data Governance

AI SaaS data governance should not focus exclusively on uploaded files.

| Data artifact | Governance question |
|---|---|
| User prompt | Does it contain personal, confidential, or regulated information? |
| Uploaded document | Who owns it, who may submit it, and which retention rules apply? |
| Retrieved content | Were source permissions enforced before the content entered the model context? |
| Conversation history | Who can access previous interactions, and how long are they retained? |
| Generated output | Does it reproduce confidential information or contain inaccurate claims? |
| Embeddings | Can the derived representation expose or reveal information, and how is access controlled? |
| Cached content | Where is it stored, and when is it invalidated or deleted? |
| Audit logs | Do they contain prompts, personal data, or other sensitive details? |
| Evaluation datasets | Are they authorized for testing, and do they contain sensitive information? |
| Feedback and ratings | Can they contain confidential content or be used for additional model improvement? |

Embeddings are numerical representations used in some AI systems to support similarity search and retrieval. They should not automatically be treated as harmless or anonymous. Their risk depends on the implementation, associated metadata, accessibility, and potential for information inference or reconstruction.

#### 4.6 Data Retention, Model Training, and Deletion

The organization should obtain clear answers to three separate questions.

**First: Is the data retained?**

Determine whether prompts, files, outputs, conversation histories, and logs are stored, and identify the applicable retention periods.

**Second: Is the data used for model training or service improvement?**

Determine whether customer content may be used for training, fine-tuning, evaluation, human review, product improvement, or other purposes. Do not assume that a service's enterprise label guarantees that every data-use pathway is disabled.

**Third: Can the data be deleted?**

Determine how deletion works across primary storage, derived artifacts, indexes, caches, backups, and subprocessors, subject to applicable legal and contractual retention requirements.

These questions should be resolved through the provider's actual service terms, contractual commitments, technical documentation, and configuration settings.

#### 4.7 Data-Minimization Controls

Practical controls include:

- Restricting the service to approved data categories.
- Removing unnecessary personal identifiers before submission where appropriate.
- Blocking prohibited secrets and sensitive information through available controls.
- Limiting the documents and repositories accessible to the AI.
- Applying source-system permissions to retrieval operations.
- Restricting conversation-history retention.
- Limiting administrative access to stored interactions.
- Defining permitted uses of generated output.
- Reviewing data-sharing and model-training settings.
- Establishing procedures for deletion, export, and incident-related evidence preservation.

Anonymization and pseudonymization are not interchangeable.

Pseudonymized data may remain personal data under applicable law when reidentification is reasonably possible using additional information. Anonymization requires a stronger assessment that individuals are no longer identifiable by means reasonably likely to be used.

Removing a person's name alone does not establish that a dataset is anonymous.

### 5. Risk Landscape and Initial Risk Register

The risk landscape identifies the main ways a third-party AI SaaS deployment could harm the organization, its customers, employees, or other affected parties.

A comprehensive assessment should combine conventional cybersecurity risks with AI-specific, privacy, supplier, operational, and business risks.

The purpose of an initial risk register is to establish a baseline for investigation and treatment. It is not a final risk rating: likelihood, impact, control effectiveness, and residual risk must be validated against the actual service and deployment.

#### 5.1 Primary Risk Categories

| Risk category | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality risk | Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. | The AI retrieves confidential documents for an unauthorized user. |
| Integrity risk | Information or business records are altered incorrectly or maliciously. | An AI-enabled workflow submits an incorrect change to a customer record. |
| Availability risk | The service or a critical dependency becomes unavailable. | Provider downtime interrupts customer support. |
| Identity and access risk | Accounts, roles, credentials, or permissions are compromised or misconfigured. | An overprivileged service identity accesses unnecessary business records. |
| AI-specific security risk | Model behavior or AI workflows are exploited or manipulated. | Indirect prompt injection influences a connected workflow. |
| Output reliability risk | AI-generated information is inaccurate, misleading, incomplete, or unsuitable. | A contract summary omits a critical renewal condition. |
| Privacy risk | Personal data is processed inappropriately or disclosed without proper authorization. | Employee information is retained or used beyond the approved purpose. |
| Supplier risk | The provider fails to meet security, contractual, or operational requirements. | The supplier cannot provide adequate incident information or control evidence. |
| Fourth-party risk | A provider's subcontractor or dependency introduces additional exposure. | A model-hosting dependency experiences a security incident. |
| Compliance risk | The deployment fails to satisfy an applicable legal or contractual obligation. | The service is used for a purpose requiring controls that have not been implemented. |
| Operational resilience risk | The organization cannot maintain or recover critical business operations. | A provider outage has no workable fallback process. |
| Concentration risk | Too many critical functions depend on one provider or service. | Multiple business processes fail following a single provider disruption. |
| Intellectual-property risk | Proprietary information is disclosed or reused in an unauthorized manner. | Confidential source code or product designs are submitted to an unapproved service. |
| Human-factor risk | Users misunderstand AI limitations or bypass required safeguards. | Employees rely on generated answers without checking critical facts. |
| Governance risk | Ownership, approval authority, or control responsibilities are unclear. | A service is deployed without an accountable business owner or risk approval. |
| Exit risk | The organization cannot terminate the service securely or maintain continuity. | Data and integration dependencies cannot be transferred or removed promptly. |

A risk should not be marked “accepted” merely because the initial rating is low or because a provider claims that the risk is addressed.

#### 5.3 Inherent Risk Versus Residual Risk

These concepts must be distinguished from the beginning.

**Inherent risk** is the exposure before considering the effectiveness of relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the exposure that remains after considering the controls that are actually implemented and effective.

For example:

- Inherent risk: The AI assistant can access a repository containing confidential business information.
- Existing control: Enterprise identity integration and document permissions are configured.
- Control gap: The retrieval component does not consistently enforce the requesting user's document permissions.
- Residual-risk conclusion: The confidentiality risk remains material until authorization is corrected and validated.

The presence of SSO or repository permissions does not justify reducing the risk rating if the AI retrieval path bypasses those permissions.

#### 5.4 Initial Risk Treatment Priorities

During initial assessment, the GRC team should prioritize:

1. **Data exposure:** Determine which sensitive information can reach the provider and whether its use is authorized.
2. **Access control:** Verify that users and connected systems cannot retrieve or modify information beyond their permissions.
3. **Supplier assurance:** Confirm the provider's security posture, data-use practices, incident commitments, and subcontractor dependencies.
4. **AI-specific attack paths:** Evaluate prompt injection, unauthorized retrieval, unsafe tool invocation, and relevant output-handling failures.
5. **Privacy and compliance:** Determine which obligations apply to the actual use case.
6. **Operational resilience:** Establish the consequences of provider failure and whether a viable fallback exists.
7. **Human oversight:** Identify where users must validate outputs or approve actions.
8. **Evidence and accountability:** Assign owners and establish the evidence required to support approval.

These priorities are a starting point. The final sequence should reflect actual risk severity, legal requirements, and business criticality.

#### 5.5 Initial Governance Decision

Before the organization commits to full production deployment, it should be able to answer:

- Is the business purpose clearly defined?
- Is the proposed use authorized by the organization?
- Which data categories will be processed?
- Has the provider's role and service architecture been established?
- Are material data flows and trust boundaries documented?
- Are the customer and provider responsibilities understood?
- Have the major cybersecurity, AI-specific, privacy, supplier, and resilience risks been identified?
- Are accountable owners assigned?
- Are there any immediately disqualifying conditions, such as prohibited data use or an inability to satisfy mandatory requirements?
- Is there sufficient information to proceed to detailed supplier, regulatory, and security assessment?

If these questions cannot be answered, the organization should not treat the service as ready for production approval. It may continue discovery or a suitably restricted assessment, but uncertainty must be documented rather than mistaken for low risk.

### Core Principle

**Third-party AI SaaS governance begins with understanding the business purpose, architecture, data flows, responsibility boundaries, and credible risk scenarios.** The organization must know what the AI service is permitted to do, what information it can access, which external parties process that information, and what could happen if the service or its controls fail.

A defensible initial assessment does not depend on whether the provider markets its service as enterprise-grade or secure. It depends on the actual use case, verified architecture, data sensitivity, supplier commitments, enforceable controls, and accountable risk ownership.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **AI SaaS versus traditional SaaS:** AI SaaS retains conventional application-security requirements while introducing additional risks from probabilistic outputs, prompt injection, retrieval, and model behavior.
- **Business purpose versus technical capability:** A feature being available does not mean the organization is authorized or justified in using it.
- **AI assistance versus autonomous execution:** Generating a recommendation and executing a business action have different control and accountability requirements.
- **Provider operation versus customer accountability:** Outsourcing service operation does not automatically transfer the customer's business, data, or regulatory responsibilities.
- **Trust boundary versus network boundary:** A trust boundary reflects differences in control, permissions, or security assumptions; it may exist even within a single network or application.
- **Data submission versus data processing:** Sending a prompt is only one stage; retrieval, retention, output generation, logging, and derived-data handling may introduce additional exposure.
- **Inherent risk versus residual risk:** Existing controls should reduce the assessed risk only to the extent that their effectiveness is supported by evidence.
- **Risk identification versus risk acceptance:** Identifying a risk does not authorize it, and an initial risk register does not replace a formal assessment and approval process.

## Part 2: Regulatory Frameworks, Supplier Due Diligence, Risk Assessment, and Security Requirements

### 6. Regulatory Frameworks and Governance Requirements

Before approving a third-party AI SaaS deployment, the organization must determine which legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal governance requirements apply.

The assessment should consider both the organization’s general obligations as a business and any additional obligations arising from the AI system’s intended purpose, data processing activities, industry, and deployment environment.

A common governance mistake is to assume that purchasing an enterprise AI subscription automatically establishes regulatory compliance. A subscription may provide useful security capabilities, but the customer remains responsible for determining whether the deployment satisfies its own obligations.

#### 6.1 Establish the Applicable Regulatory Landscape

The following frameworks and regulations may be relevant to third-party AI SaaS deployments. Their applicability depends on the organization, jurisdiction, processing activities, and intended use.

| Framework or regulation | Primary focus | Relevance to third-party AI SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) | Personal-data processing and protection. | Lawfulness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, security, data-subject rights, international transfers, and controller–processor obligations. |
| EU Artificial Intelligence Act | Regulation of AI systems according to their characteristics, roles, and intended uses. | AI literacy, prohibited practices, high-risk AI obligations, transparency requirements, provider and deployer responsibilities, and other applicable requirements. |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information Security Management System (ISMS). | Supplier security, risk management, access control, incident management, and information-security governance. |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI Management System (AIMS). | Governance of AI-related risks, responsibilities, policies, lifecycle processes, and continual improvement. |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) | AI risk management. | Structured identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring of AI-related risks. |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) | Cybersecurity risk management. | Govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover activities. |
| SOC 2 | Independent examination of controls against applicable Trust Services Criteria. | Evidence about the service provider’s controls, depending on the examination scope and report. |
| Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) | Protection of payment-card account data within applicable scope. | Relevant when the AI deployment interacts with systems or processes that affect payment-card data or the security of the cardholder data environment. |
| Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) | Digital operational resilience in the EU financial sector. | Relevant to in-scope financial entities and their ICT third-party arrangements, including applicable AI SaaS dependencies. |
| NIS2 Directive and applicable national implementing law | Cybersecurity and risk-management obligations for in-scope entities. | Relevant to covered organizations and applicable supplier and cybersecurity risk-management obligations. |
| Internal security policies | Organization-specific minimum security requirements. | Approved services, permitted data, access restrictions, logging, acceptable use, and mandatory approval processes. |
| Contractual obligations | Commitments made to customers, partners, and suppliers. | Confidentiality, data handling, security controls, audit rights, incident notification, and service commitments. |

This table is an applicability checklist, not a declaration that every listed framework applies to every deployment.

For example, a business using an AI assistant for internal document summarization may face a different set of requirements from a financial institution using AI in a regulated customer decision process.

#### 6.2 GDPR: Personal-Data Processing

When personal data is processed through an AI SaaS service, the organization must determine its role and the provider’s role under applicable data-protection law.

In a common arrangement, the organization determines the purposes and essential means of processing and acts as the data controller, while the AI SaaS provider processes personal data on the controller’s behalf as a processor.

However, roles must be determined from the actual processing arrangement. A provider may independently determine certain purposes for particular processing activities and could have a different role for those activities.

Key assessment areas include:

**Purpose limitation**

The organization should establish why personal data is being submitted to the AI service and whether the processing is compatible with the stated purpose.

**Data minimization**

Only personal data necessary for the authorized use case should be processed.

**Lawfulness and transparency**

The organization should identify an appropriate legal basis and address applicable transparency obligations.

**Processor governance**

Where the provider acts as a processor, the organization should verify the applicable contractual requirements, including those under Article 28 of the GDPR.

**Security of processing**

Technical and organizational measures should be appropriate to the risk, considering the nature of the data and processing.

**International data transfers**

The organization should determine whether personal data is transferred to or accessed from countries outside the European Economic Area and assess the applicable transfer requirements.

**Data-subject rights**

The organization should establish how requests involving personal data held in prompts, conversation histories, documents, outputs, or other relevant records will be handled.

**Data protection impact assessment**

Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals’ rights and freedoms, the organization must assess whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is required under Article 35.

A DPIA is not automatically mandatory for every AI SaaS deployment. Its necessity depends on the processing and applicable criteria.

#### 6.3 EU AI Act: Provider and Deployer Responsibilities

The EU AI Act introduces obligations that depend on the AI system, its intended purpose, risk classification, the role of the organization, and the applicable implementation timeline.

For a third-party AI SaaS deployment, the organization should establish:

1. Whether the service qualifies as an AI system under the applicable legal definition.
2. The intended purpose and relevant system capabilities.
3. Whether the organization acts as a provider, deployer, or another relevant operator.
4. Whether the use case falls within a prohibited practice, a high-risk category, or another regulated category.
5. Whether transparency, AI literacy, human oversight, or other requirements apply.
6. Whether the provider has supplied sufficient information for the organization to meet its own obligations.
7. Whether modifications or changes in intended purpose affect the organization’s legal role or obligations.
8. Whether the provider’s contractual and technical arrangements support compliance.

**Important distinction:** Using a third-party AI model does not automatically make the customer a provider of the AI system. However, certain modifications, changes in intended purpose, or other circumstances may alter the customer’s legal responsibilities under the applicable rules.

Similarly, not every general-purpose AI assistant is automatically a high-risk AI system. Classification depends on the relevant legal criteria and actual use.

The organization should obtain legal or specialist compliance advice when the intended use may fall within a regulated category.

#### 6.4 ISO/IEC 27001 and Supplier Security

ISO/IEC 27001 provides an information-security management framework for identifying, treating, monitoring, and continually improving information-security risks.

For third-party AI SaaS, the organization should evaluate supplier security within its own ISMS and risk-management process.

Relevant areas include:

- Supplier relationships and contractual security requirements.
- Information classification and handling.
- Identity and access management.
- Secure configuration.
- Cryptographic protection.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability management.
- Incident response.
- Business continuity.
- Change management.
- Compliance and independent assurance.

The organization should not interpret an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate as proof that every AI-specific risk is adequately controlled.

The certificate’s scope, validity, exclusions, and relationship to the contracted service must be examined.

#### 6.5 ISO/IEC 42001 and AI Governance

ISO/IEC 42001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI Management System.

Its relevance to third-party AI SaaS includes governance of:

- AI policies and organizational responsibilities.
- AI-related risks and opportunities.
- Intended purpose and context of use.
- Supplier and third-party relationships.
- AI lifecycle processes.
- Monitoring and evaluation.
- Incident management and corrective action.
- Documented information and continual improvement.

The organization should integrate AI governance with existing information-security, privacy, enterprise-risk, and supplier-management processes rather than operating separate, disconnected governance structures.

An AI Management System certification is not a substitute for assessing the specific AI service, data flows, intended purpose, and contractual conditions.

#### 6.6 Evidence-Based Regulatory Mapping

The organization should maintain a regulatory applicability register.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Requirement ID | Unique identifier for the obligation. |
| Source | Regulation, standard, contract, or internal policy. |
| Requirement | Specific obligation or control objective. |
| Applicability rationale | Why the requirement applies or does not apply. |
| Accountable owner | Role responsible for the obligation. |
| Implementation status | Implemented, partially implemented, planned, not applicable, or not assessed. |
| Evidence | Policy, configuration, contract, assessment, test, or other supporting record. |
| Identified gap | Difference between the requirement and the current state. |
| Remediation action | Action needed to address the gap. |
| Due date | Agreed completion date. |
| Approval | Relevant risk or compliance decision. |

A regulatory register should distinguish an obligation that has been legally assessed as not applicable from one that has simply not been investigated.

The objective is not to collect the largest possible number of certificates and questionnaires. It is to obtain enough reliable evidence to make a defensible decision about the provider’s ability to meet the organization’s requirements.

#### 7.2 Supplier Inherent-Risk Classification

The organization should classify the supplier relationship before deciding how much due diligence is required.

Relevant factors include:

| Factor | Lower-risk example | Higher-risk example |
|---|---|---|
| Data sensitivity | Public information only. | Restricted customer records or sensitive business information. |

The organization should define its own risk-rating methodology and escalation thresholds.

A useful principle is to scale due diligence according to the potential impact of failure, not merely the purchase price or number of users.

#### 7.3 Supplier Due-Diligence Questionnaire

The following questions provide a baseline for assessing an AI SaaS provider.

**A. Company and Service Governance**

1. What legal entity provides the contracted service?
2. Which service components are included in the proposed subscription?
3. Which components are operated by the provider, and which are supplied by other organizations?
4. Does the provider maintain documented information-security and AI governance policies?
5. How are material changes to the service communicated to customers?
6. Does the provider have a formal risk-management and security-assurance process?
7. Which business continuity and disaster recovery arrangements support the service?

**B. Information Security**

8. Does the provider maintain a current information-security certification or independent assurance report?
9. What is the scope of the certification or report?
10. How are privileged accounts protected?
11. How are vulnerabilities identified, prioritized, and remediated?
12. What security testing is performed, and how frequently?
13. How are security incidents detected and investigated?
14. What incident notification commitments apply to the customer?
15. Which customer-visible security logs and administrative audit events are available?
16. How are encryption and key-management responsibilities allocated?

**C. AI Architecture and Model Governance**

17. Which AI models and model providers support the service?
18. Can the model provider change without customer approval or notification?
19. Are customer prompts, files, outputs, or feedback used to train or improve models?
20. Which data-processing and retention settings can the customer configure?
21. How are model updates tested before deployment?
22. What mechanisms exist to detect or mitigate prompt injection and unsafe tool use?
23. How does the service handle inaccurate, unsafe, or potentially harmful outputs?
24. Can the customer restrict the model’s access to data sources and external tools?
25. What testing evidence is available for the relevant AI capabilities?

**D. Data Protection and Privacy**

26. What categories of customer data are processed?
27. Where is customer data processed and stored?
28. Which subprocessors can access customer data?
29. What retention periods apply to prompts, outputs, logs, and derived data?
30. How does deletion work across relevant storage systems and subprocessors?
31. Does the provider support applicable data-subject rights requests?
32. What contractual arrangements support international data transfers where applicable?
33. Can the customer disable optional data-sharing or model-improvement features?

**E. Business Continuity and Exit**

34. What availability commitments apply?
35. How are service disruptions communicated?
36. What recovery objectives are relevant to the contracted service?
37. Can customer data and required audit records be exported in a usable format?
38. What happens to customer data after termination?
39. Can the customer revoke integrations, credentials, and access promptly?
40. Does the provider support an orderly transition to an alternative service?

The questionnaire is a starting point. Responses should be validated against the proposed service, applicable contracts, independent assurance evidence, and the provider’s actual configuration options.

#### 7.4 Independent Assurance Evidence

Common evidence sources include:

| Evidence | What it can support | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC 27001 certificate | Evidence that an ISMS within the certified scope has been assessed. | Does not prove that every relevant control or AI capability is effective. |
| SOC 2 Type I report | Evidence about control design at a specified point in time. | Does not demonstrate operating effectiveness over an extended period. |
| SOC 2 Type II report | Evidence about control design and operation over a defined examination period. | Scope, exceptions, complementary user-entity controls, and report age matter. |
| Penetration-test executive summary | Evidence about the scope and high-level results of selected security testing. | May exclude critical components and does not replace the customer’s own testing. |

| Data-processing agreement | Evidence of contractual processing arrangements. | Does not prove that the provider has implemented every required control. |

| Security architecture documentation | Information about system boundaries and control design. | Describes intended design; implementation still requires validation. |

### 8. AI-Specific Threat Modeling and Security Requirements

Traditional security assessments remain essential, but they do not fully address threats arising from the way AI systems interpret instructions, retrieve context, generate outputs, and interact with external tools.

Threat modeling provides a structured method for identifying relevant attack paths and translating them into testable security requirements.

#### 8.1 Establish the Threat Model

The organization should identify:

1. The assets requiring protection.
2. The trust boundaries through which data or instructions move.
3. The actors who may intentionally or unintentionally create risk.
4. The system components and integrations that can be attacked.
5. The threat scenarios relevant to the intended use.
6. The controls that prevent, detect, or limit each scenario.
7. The tests and evidence needed to demonstrate control effectiveness.

Relevant actors may include malicious external users, compromised employees, careless users, malicious document authors, compromised suppliers, and attackers targeting connected enterprise applications.

Not every threat is equally relevant to every AI SaaS deployment. Threat modeling should reflect the actual architecture and capabilities.

#### 8.2 AI-Specific Threat Scenarios

| Threat | Description | Potential consequence | Typical governance response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct prompt injection | A user submits instructions designed to override the intended behavior or safeguards of the AI system. | Policy bypass, inappropriate output, or unsafe actions. | Define usage restrictions, evaluate model behavior, constrain permissions, and test relevant attack scenarios. |
| Indirect prompt injection | Instructions are embedded in external content, such as documents, web pages, or retrieved records, and influence the AI when processed. | Data exposure or manipulation of downstream actions. | Treat retrieved content as untrusted, enforce authorization, constrain tool access, and test indirect-injection scenarios. |
| Sensitive-information disclosure | The system reveals information that should not be disclosed to the requesting user. | Confidentiality breach or privacy harm. | Enforce access control at retrieval and application layers; test cross-user and cross-role isolation. |
| Insecure output handling | An application treats AI-generated content as trusted input without suitable validation. | Injection vulnerabilities, unsafe execution, or corrupted business records. | Validate and constrain outputs before downstream processing or execution. |
| Excessive agency | The AI has permissions or tools beyond what the business use requires. | Unauthorized transactions, changes, or data access. | Apply least privilege, explicit authorization, action limits, and human approval for consequential actions. |
| Retrieval poisoning | Malicious or misleading content is introduced into a knowledge source or retrieval corpus. | Incorrect answers, manipulated recommendations, or unsafe behavior. | Govern source ingestion, restrict write access, validate content, and monitor changes. |
| Model or service dependency compromise | A provider or supporting component is compromised. | Data exposure, service manipulation, or operational disruption. | Assess supplier security, dependencies, incident commitments, and recovery arrangements. |
| Unreliable output | The model produces false, unsupported, or misleading information. | Poor decisions, financial loss, or customer harm. | Establish use-case-specific quality criteria, human review, and escalation procedures. |
| Denial of service or resource exhaustion | Excessive requests or other attacks degrade service availability or create unexpected costs. | Service disruption or financial exposure. | Apply usage limits, quotas, monitoring, and appropriate resilience arrangements. |
| Cross-tenant exposure | Data is exposed across customer boundaries due to a service or configuration failure. | Unauthorized disclosure of customer information. | Review provider assurance, isolation commitments, incident history where available, and relevant testing evidence. |

Prompt injection is not simply a conventional input-validation problem. An AI model may interpret malicious instructions in context, and there may be no universal filtering mechanism that reliably prevents every attack.

Therefore, the architecture must not rely exclusively on the model correctly ignoring malicious instructions. Access control, tool authorization, output validation, and restrictions on consequential actions must be enforced independently wherever possible.

#### 8.3 Security Requirements Matrix

Threats should be translated into requirements that can be assigned, implemented, and tested.

| Requirement ID | Security requirement | Responsible party | Verification evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AISEC-01 | The service must authenticate users through an approved identity mechanism appropriate to the deployment. | Customer and provider, according to architecture. | Identity configuration and authentication tests. |
| AISEC-02 | Access to AI features and connected information must follow approved authorization rules. | Primarily customer, with relevant provider capabilities. | Role matrix, configuration review, and access-control testing. |
| AISEC-03 | The AI must not gain unrestricted access to enterprise data or tools merely because a user submits a prompt. | Customer and provider, according to implementation. | Permission tests, integration review, and adversarial testing. |
| AISEC-04 | Retrieved content must be authorized for the requesting user and intended purpose. | Customer and integration owner. | Retrieval tests across users, roles, and document classifications. |
| AISEC-05 | Data submitted to the service must comply with approved classification and handling requirements. | Customer. | Data-flow review, DLP configuration, and controlled test cases. |
| AISEC-06 | Customer data must be protected in transit and at rest where applicable to the architecture. | Customer and provider according to control ownership. | Configuration evidence, provider assurance, and technical validation. |
| AISEC-07 | AI-generated content must be validated before it is used in security-sensitive or consequential operations. | Business and application owners. | Workflow design, approval evidence, and output-validation tests. |
| AISEC-08 | Tool invocation must be constrained by explicit permissions and business rules. | Customer and provider, according to architecture. | Tool-permission inventory and negative authorization tests. |
| AISEC-09 | Relevant security events must be logged and available for investigation. | Customer and provider, according to the service. | Logging configuration, sample events, and detection tests. |
| AISEC-10 | The organization must have a documented method to disable the service or relevant integrations during an incident. | Customer and service owner. | Incident procedures and an observed or simulated shutdown test. |
| AISEC-11 | Material model or service changes must be evaluated for relevant security and business impact. | Provider and customer, according to contract and control ownership. | Change-notification records, reassessment evidence, and regression tests. |
| AISEC-12 | The service must meet defined retention, export, and deletion requirements for relevant data. | Customer and provider. | Contractual terms, configuration evidence, and deletion verification where available. |

The responsible party must be confirmed for the actual deployment. A requirement assigned jointly to the customer and provider still needs a clearly identified owner for implementation and evidence collection.

#### 8.4 Security Requirements Must Be Testable

Requirements should be expressed in terms of observable outcomes.

Weak requirement:

> The AI system must be secure against prompt injection.

This statement is too broad to demonstrate conclusively.

Stronger requirements include:

- Retrieved documents must be authorized according to the requesting user's permissions.
- Instructions found in retrieved content must not independently grant access to restricted data.
- The AI must not invoke a privileged action without satisfying the application's authorization and approval rules.
- Generated content must be validated before being interpreted as an executable command or trusted application input.
- The organization must be able to suspend affected integrations if an attack or control failure is detected.

These requirements are more useful because they describe controls that can be implemented and tested.

Testing should include both successful authorization scenarios and negative cases in which unauthorized access or action must be denied.

#### 8.5 AI Security Testing Scope

Depending on the service and use case, testing may include:

| Test category | Objective |
|---|---|
| Authentication testing | Confirm that unauthorized users cannot access the service. |
| Authorization testing | Confirm that access is limited to approved roles, data, and actions. |
| Cross-user isolation testing | Confirm that one user cannot retrieve another user's restricted information. |
| Prompt-injection testing | Evaluate whether malicious instructions influence behavior in unacceptable ways. |
| Retrieval-security testing | Confirm that retrieved content respects source permissions and approved scope. |
| Output-handling testing | Verify that generated content cannot bypass downstream validation or authorization. |
| Tool-use testing | Confirm that the AI cannot execute unauthorized or out-of-scope actions. |
| Data-leakage testing | Evaluate whether sensitive information can be disclosed through relevant prompts or outputs. |
| Logging testing | Confirm that required security events are captured and available. |
| Resilience testing | Evaluate service disruption, fallback procedures, and recovery arrangements. |
| Regression testing | Check whether relevant controls remain effective after material changes. |

Testing should be proportionate to risk and coordinated with the provider's terms, approved testing scope, and applicable authorization requirements.

The organization should not perform intrusive testing against a provider-managed service without confirming that the activity is authorized.

#### 8.6 Control Effectiveness and Evidence

A security requirement is not satisfied merely because it appears in a policy or supplier questionnaire.

Evidence should demonstrate that the relevant control exists and works as intended.

For example:

| Control claim | Weak evidence | Stronger evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Only authorized users can access the service. | A statement that SSO is supported. | Approved identity configuration, role assignments, and access-control test results. |
| Retrieval respects document permissions. | A supplier statement that enterprise search is secure. | Tests showing that users with different permissions receive only authorized results. |
| The AI cannot perform unauthorized transactions. | A policy instructing the AI not to execute certain actions. | Independent application authorization, constrained service permissions, and negative transaction tests. |
| Sensitive prompts are protected. | A generic statement that the provider uses encryption. | Evidence covering the relevant data flows, applicable encryption controls, retention, and access restrictions. |
| Incidents can be investigated. | A statement that logging is available. | Sample logs, event coverage, retention settings, and a demonstrated investigation workflow. |

The strength of evidence should be considered alongside its scope, recency, independence, and applicability to the actual service.

### Core Principle

**Third-party AI SaaS approval requires both supplier assurance and deployment-specific security validation.** A provider may operate a well-secured service, yet the customer can still create unacceptable risk through excessive permissions, inappropriate data use, weak integrations, inadequate output validation, or unclear accountability.

The organization should translate applicable obligations and identified threats into explicit security requirements, assign ownership, and collect evidence demonstrating that controls operate effectively.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Regulatory applicability versus certification:** A certificate can support assurance but does not establish compliance with every applicable legal obligation.
- **Supplier assurance versus deployment assurance:** Evidence about the provider's general controls does not prove that the customer's configuration and integrations are secure.
- **Provider responsibility versus customer responsibility:** Contractual allocation matters, but each party must implement the controls within its actual sphere of responsibility.
- **AI-specific testing versus conventional security testing:** AI testing supplements, rather than replaces, authentication, authorization, penetration testing, vulnerability management, and other established security practices.
- **Security policy versus enforceable control:** A written instruction does not prevent unauthorized access unless it is supported by appropriate technical or procedural controls.
- **Risk identification versus verified mitigation:** A risk is not adequately treated until the selected controls are implemented, validated, and supported by evidence.
- **Supplier transparency versus supplier trustworthiness:** Limited visibility may prevent adequate assurance even when there is insufficient evidence to conclude that the provider is insecure.
- **Risk-based approval versus blanket approval:** Approval should apply to a defined service, configuration, data scope, and intended purpose—not automatically to every capability the provider offers.
