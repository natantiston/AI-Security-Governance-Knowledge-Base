# 21.18 — Third-Party AI SaaS Deployment
## Part 1: Business Context, AI SaaS Architecture, Data Flows, and Risk Landscape

### 1. Introduction to Third-Party AI SaaS Governance

Organizations increasingly adopt third-party Artificial Intelligence Software-as-a-Service (AI SaaS) platforms to improve productivity, automate business processes, analyze information, support decision-making, and deliver new digital services.

Instead of developing and operating AI infrastructure internally, an organization can subscribe to an externally hosted service that provides access to foundation models, generative AI capabilities, intelligent automation, enterprise search, conversational assistants, predictive analytics, or AI-enabled business applications.

Examples include enterprise AI assistants, AI-powered customer-service platforms, intelligent document-processing systems, AI-enabled Customer Relationship Management (CRM) applications, and specialized AI solutions integrated into enterprise workflows.

These services can deliver significant business value, but they also introduce dependencies and risks that traditional SaaS governance may not fully address.

The organization must evaluate not only the security of the software and infrastructure, but also how AI models process information, generate outputs, interact with connected systems, respond to untrusted instructions, and influence business decisions.

**The central governance challenge is determining whether the organization can use an externally operated AI service while maintaining appropriate control over its data, identities, business processes, security obligations, and residual risks.**

#### 1.1 What Is Third-Party AI SaaS?

Third-party AI SaaS is a software service delivered and operated by an external provider that incorporates AI capabilities and is made available to customers through a subscription, consumption-based pricing model, or another commercial arrangement.

The service may include several layers:

| Layer | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| SaaS application | The application through which users interact with the AI service. | Enterprise AI assistant or AI-powered CRM application. |
| AI model | The model generating, classifying, summarizing, predicting, or transforming information. | Large language model or specialized predictive model. |
| Model orchestration | Components coordinating prompts, model calls, retrieval, tools, and output processing. | Workflow engine or model gateway. |
| Data processing | Components handling user inputs, retrieved documents, conversation history, and outputs. | Document processing, embeddings, or retrieval pipelines. |
| Integration layer | Connections between the AI service and enterprise applications. | CRM, ERP, collaboration platforms, and enterprise APIs. |
| Security and administration | Capabilities controlling identity, permissions, policies, logging, and administration. | Single sign-on, role-based access, audit logs, and administrative policies. |
| Provider infrastructure | Underlying computing, storage, networking, and supporting services. | Cloud infrastructure operated by the provider or its subcontractors. |

Not every AI SaaS product exposes these layers separately. Some providers offer a fully integrated application, while others allow customers to configure models, retrieval sources, tools, and workflows.

From a GRC perspective, the objective is to understand which components exist, who controls them, and which risks can be managed by the customer.

#### 1.2 Traditional SaaS Versus AI SaaS

Traditional SaaS and AI SaaS share many security requirements, but AI introduces additional sources of uncertainty.

| Dimension | Traditional SaaS | AI SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| Primary functionality | Executes predefined application logic and business workflows. | May generate content, interpret natural-language instructions, infer patterns, or produce probabilistic outputs. |
| Input handling | Often relies on structured forms, predefined fields, and application interfaces. | May accept natural-language prompts, uploaded files, images, audio, and retrieved content. |
| Output behavior | Usually follows predefined application logic and data rules. | May produce variable outputs that are inaccurate, inconsistent, or influenced by context. |
| Security boundaries | Commonly centered on identity, application permissions, APIs, and infrastructure. | Must additionally consider model behavior, prompt injection, retrieval security, and generated content. |
| Data exposure | Data may be exposed through application vulnerabilities, misconfiguration, or excessive access. | Data may also be disclosed through prompts, retrieval results, conversation history, or generated outputs. |
| Change management | Software releases and configuration changes are key concerns. | Model changes, altered safety behavior, retrieval changes, and AI-specific regressions may also matter. |
| Testing | Functional testing, penetration testing, configuration review, and control validation. | These remain necessary, supplemented by AI-specific adversarial testing and output evaluation. |
| Human oversight | Depends on business process and application criticality. | May require additional review because users can over-trust generated content or automated recommendations. |
| Risk assessment | Often focuses on application security, data protection, and availability. | Must also address AI-specific failure modes and the consequences of probabilistic behavior. |

The distinction does not mean every AI SaaS service is inherently more dangerous than traditional SaaS. Risk depends on the intended use, data sensitivity, integration scope, autonomy, and potential consequences of failure.

An AI tool that drafts non-sensitive internal communications may present limited business risk. The same underlying model connected to customer records, financial transactions, or privileged administrative tools may require substantially stronger controls.

#### 1.3 Common Enterprise Use Cases

| Use case | Business objective | Typical data processed | Main governance concerns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enterprise AI assistant | Improve employee productivity and knowledge access. | Internal documents, prompts, emails, and business records. | Confidentiality, access control, provider data use, and inaccurate output. |
| AI customer-service chatbot | Automate customer inquiries and support. | Customer identifiers, conversation history, product information, and support records. | Privacy, customer harm, identity verification, and unauthorized disclosure. |
| AI-powered contact center | Support agents, summarize calls, and automate selected interactions. | Call recordings, transcripts, customer records, and interaction metadata. | Personal-data processing, retention, transcription accuracy, and human oversight. |
| AI-powered CRM | Improve customer insights, sales workflows, and campaign personalization. | Customer profiles, sales records, preferences, and communications. | Data minimization, profiling, permissions, and downstream decision impact. |
| AI document processing | Extract information from contracts, invoices, and business documents. | Commercial agreements, financial records, and potentially personal data. | Extraction accuracy, data leakage, document integrity, and retention. |
| AI coding assistant | Accelerate software development. | Source code, technical documentation, and developer prompts. | Intellectual property, secret exposure, insecure code, and software supply-chain risk. |
| AI security operations | Support alert triage, investigation, and security analysis. | Security logs, incident records, indicators, and investigation notes. | Sensitive telemetry, false conclusions, privileged access, and response automation. |
| AI financial analysis | Support financial research, forecasting, or decision preparation. | Financial information, customer data, and market information. | Accuracy, confidentiality, model limitations, and regulated decision-making. |
| AI recruitment assistant | Support candidate screening and recruitment workflows. | CVs, employment history, interview records, and assessment data. | Discrimination, privacy, explainability, and employment-related obligations. |
| AI knowledge-management platform | Search and summarize enterprise knowledge. | Documents, policies, project records, and internal communications. | Retrieval authorization, stale information, and cross-department data exposure. |

The GRC team should assess the actual use case rather than classifying risk solely according to the product's marketing category.

A product marketed as a general-purpose assistant can become a high-impact business dependency when integrated into consequential workflows.

#### 1.4 Governance Objectives

The organization should establish the following objectives before deployment:

1. **Business legitimacy:** Confirm that the AI service addresses a defined business need and has an accountable owner.
2. **Approved use:** Define permitted and prohibited activities, users, data categories, and decisions.
3. **Data protection:** Ensure that data submitted to or generated by the service is handled according to applicable requirements.
4. **Supplier assurance:** Establish whether the provider can meet the organization's security, privacy, contractual, and operational requirements.
5. **Security by design:** Integrate identity, authorization, encryption, logging, and secure integration requirements into the architecture.
6. **AI-specific risk management:** Assess prompt injection, output reliability, retrieval security, and other relevant AI failure modes.
7. **Human accountability:** Define where human review, approval, or intervention is required.
8. **Operational resilience:** Establish how the organization will detect incidents, manage service disruptions, and maintain critical operations.
9. **Continuous assurance:** Reassess risks when the service, provider, model, data, or business use changes.
10. **Controlled exit:** Maintain the ability to terminate the service and manage data, identities, integrations, and business dependencies securely.

These objectives should be reflected in the organization's AI governance policy, third-party risk management process, security requirements, and approval workflow.

### 2. Business Context, Ownership, and Shared Responsibility

Third-party AI SaaS governance begins with understanding the business context.

A technically secure service can still be unsuitable if it is used for an unauthorized purpose, processes data without an appropriate legal basis, produces outputs that users treat as authoritative without verification, or introduces unacceptable dependency on a supplier.

The organization must therefore connect the technology assessment to the business process and the consequences of failure.

#### 2.1 Define the Business Use Case

A use case should be documented before procurement or implementation proceeds too far.

| Business context element | Required description |
|---|---|
| Business problem | What problem is the organization trying to solve? |
| Intended purpose | What specific tasks will the AI service perform? |
| Business owner | Who is accountable for the business outcome and appropriate use? |
| User population | Which employees, contractors, customers, or external parties will use the service? |
| Data categories | What information will be submitted, retrieved, generated, or stored? |
| Decision impact | Will the output influence customer, employee, financial, legal, security, or operational decisions? |
| Autonomy level | Does the service provide suggestions, prepare actions, or execute actions? |
| Integration scope | Which enterprise applications and data sources will be connected? |
| Business criticality | What happens if the service becomes unavailable or produces incorrect output? |
| Success criteria | How will business value, accuracy, and acceptable performance be measured? |
| Limitations | What should the service not be used for? |
| Exit conditions | Under what circumstances would the organization suspend or terminate the service? |

A vague objective such as “improve productivity using AI” is insufficient for a defensible risk assessment.

A stronger statement would be:

> The organization intends to deploy an enterprise AI assistant for internal document summarization and policy discovery. The initial deployment will exclude restricted customer records, regulated decision-making, and autonomous execution of business transactions. The business owner will evaluate productivity improvements, output quality, and security-control effectiveness during a controlled pilot.

This statement defines an intended purpose, a restricted scope, and measurable governance considerations.

#### 2.2 Distinguish AI Assistance from Autonomous Execution

One of the most important decisions is determining what the AI is permitted to do.

| Autonomy level | Description | Example | Governance implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1: Information retrieval | Retrieves or presents information from approved sources. | Finds a company policy or summarizes an internal document. | Strong access controls, source authorization, and information accuracy checks. |
| Level 2: Content generation | Produces drafts, summaries, explanations, or recommendations. | Drafts an email or summarizes a customer interaction. | Output validation, confidentiality protection, and user guidance. |
| Level 3: Decision support | Recommends an action that a person evaluates. | Suggests a support-ticket priority or identifies a potential fraud case. | Decision criteria, human oversight, performance monitoring, and appropriate challenge mechanisms. |
| Level 4: Human-approved execution | Prepares or initiates an action that requires explicit human approval before completion. | Prepares a refund or proposes a customer-account change for approval. | Separation of duties, approval controls, transaction validation, and audit logging. |
| Level 5: Bounded autonomous execution | Executes predefined actions within constrained permissions and approved limits. | Automatically categorizes tickets or performs a low-impact workflow action. | Strict authorization, action limits, monitoring, rollback or recovery, and defined suspension criteria. |

These levels are a practical governance model, not a universal regulatory classification.

The appropriate level depends on the use case. Even information retrieval can create significant risk if the system exposes sensitive information to unauthorized users.

A useful rule is:

**The greater the AI service's authority to access information, influence decisions, or execute actions, the stronger the controls required around authorization, validation, monitoring, and accountability.**

#### 2.4 Customer Responsibilities

The organization should normally expect to manage or oversee the following areas:

- Define the intended use and permitted data categories.
- Determine which employees or systems may access the service.
- Configure identity, permissions, and customer-controlled security settings.
- Protect API keys, integration credentials, and service identities.
- Review data flows and authorize data transfers.
- Validate AI outputs where accuracy or safety matters.
- Assess supplier security and contractual commitments.
- Maintain appropriate customer-side logs and monitoring.
- Manage incidents affecting the organization's users, data, or business processes.
- Ensure that applicable regulatory and contractual obligations are addressed.
- Review changes to the service and the intended use.
- Maintain a secure exit and continuity plan.

The organization cannot delegate accountability for its own business decisions merely by using an external AI provider.

#### 2.5 Provider Responsibilities

Depending on the contract and service architecture, the provider may be responsible for:

- Operating the contracted service and supporting infrastructure.
- Protecting provider-managed components.
- Maintaining service availability within agreed commitments.
- Implementing provider-side security controls.
- Managing provider personnel and privileged access.
- Operating the model service and associated infrastructure.
- Managing subprocessors under applicable contractual obligations.
- Providing relevant security documentation and assurance evidence.
- Notifying customers of specified incidents or material changes.
- Implementing agreed retention, export, and deletion processes.
- Supporting contractual incident response and service continuity arrangements.

These responsibilities must be verified rather than assumed. A provider may offer security features that are optional, restricted to particular subscription tiers, or dependent on customer configuration.

#### 2.6 Accountability and Governance Boundaries

The organization should identify accountable owners for:

| Governance area | Typical accountable role |
|---|---|
| Business need and intended purpose | Business owner |
| AI service lifecycle | AI system or service owner |
| Data classification and permitted use | Data owner |
| Cybersecurity risk assessment | Designated cybersecurity risk owner or governance function, according to policy |
| Privacy compliance | Relevant controller and privacy governance functions |
| Supplier relationship | Supplier or contract owner |
| Architecture and technical integration | Enterprise architect or designated technology owner |
| Security-control operation | Relevant control owner |
| Business-process decisions | Authorized business decision-maker |
| Residual risk acceptance | Person or committee with appropriate delegated authority |
| Independent assurance | Internal audit or another appropriately independent assurance function |

The person coordinating a risk assessment is not automatically the person authorized to accept the risk.

Similarly, a provider's security team cannot approve the customer's business use of the service on the customer's behalf.

### 3. Reference Architecture for Third-Party AI SaaS

Architecture analysis establishes how users, data, models, enterprise systems, and external providers interact.

The GRC team does not need to develop the AI model or write application code to assess the architecture. It does need to understand the system boundaries, information flows, trust relationships, and security responsibilities.

The goal is to identify where information can enter, where it is processed, which systems can access it, and where unauthorized actions or disclosure could occur.

#### 3.1 High-Level Reference Architecture

Consider an enterprise deploying a third-party AI assistant connected to an internal knowledge repository and selected business applications.

Actual deployments may process data in the provider's environment, the customer's environment, or both. Some services may use external model providers, while others operate their own models. The architecture must be validated against the selected service.

#### 3.2 Architecture Components and Security Considerations

| Component | Function | Security and governance concerns |
|---|---|---|
| User interface | Accepts prompts and presents outputs. | User authentication, data submission, session security, and inappropriate use. |
| Enterprise identity provider | Establishes user identity and authentication context. | MFA, account lifecycle, session controls, and identity federation. |
| AI access layer | Enforces customer-side policies and routes approved requests. | Policy bypass, insufficient inspection, and incomplete visibility. |
| SaaS application | Coordinates the user experience and AI features. | Tenant configuration, administrative privileges, and data exposure. |
| Model orchestration | Coordinates prompts, context, model calls, and workflows. | Prompt injection, unsafe tool invocation, and incorrect trust assumptions. |
| AI model service | Generates or transforms content. | Model limitations, provider data handling, output quality, and model changes. |
| Knowledge connector | Retrieves information from enterprise sources. | Excessive permissions, stale indexes, insecure credentials, and authorization failures. |
| Enterprise repository | Stores documents and business records. | Classification, access control, retention, integrity, and source-data protection. |
| Enterprise API | Allows access to other applications or workflows. | Excessive API scopes, credential exposure, transaction misuse, and missing authorization checks. |
| Logging and monitoring | Records relevant activity and supports detection. | Incomplete telemetry, retention gaps, tampering, and insufficient investigation context. |
| Provider infrastructure | Hosts service components and data-processing resources. | Provider security, availability, isolation, and subcontractor dependency. |

#### 3.3 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary separates components or entities with different security assumptions, permissions, or administrative control.

Third-party AI SaaS deployments commonly contain several important trust boundaries.

**Boundary 1: User to enterprise environment**

The organization must establish that the user is authenticated and authorized to use the AI service.

**Boundary 2: Enterprise environment to provider**

Data leaves the organization's direct administrative boundary when it is transmitted to an external service. The organization must understand what is transmitted, under which contractual terms, and how the provider processes it.

**Boundary 3: Model to connected data sources**

The model may request or receive information from enterprise repositories. The organization must verify that the requesting user is authorized to access the retrieved content.

**Boundary 4: Model to external tools**

If the service can invoke APIs or business workflows, a model-generated instruction may lead to an action. Tool authorization must be enforced independently of the model's decision.

**Boundary 5: Provider to subprocessors**

Provider-managed information may be processed by cloud infrastructure providers, model suppliers, analytics services, or other subcontractors.

**Boundary 6: AI output to business decision**

Generated information may be consumed by a person or downstream application. The organization must establish whether the output requires validation before it influences a consequential decision.

#### 3.4 Architecture Review Questions

A GRC consultant should ask the architecture and technology teams:

1. Where is the AI application hosted?
2. Which provider operates the model?
3. Does the service use additional subprocessors?
4. What data leaves the enterprise environment?
5. Where are prompts, files, outputs, and logs processed and retained?
6. Are data transfers encrypted?
7. How are user identities propagated into connected data sources?
8. Are source-system permissions enforced during retrieval?
9. Can the AI access or modify business records?
10. Which operations require explicit human approval?
11. How are service identities and API credentials managed?
12. Which security events are visible to the customer?
13. Can the organization disable individual integrations or features?
14. What happens when the provider or model becomes unavailable?
15. How are model changes and material service changes communicated?
16. Can the organization export required data and evidence at termination?

These questions should be supported by architecture diagrams, provider documentation, configuration evidence, and targeted testing.

#### 3.5 Architecture and Data-Flow Documentation

At minimum, the organization should maintain:

- A system context diagram.
- A component architecture diagram.
- A data-flow diagram.
- A trust-boundary diagram.
- An identity and access model.
- An integration inventory.
- A supplier and subprocessor dependency inventory.
- A security-control responsibility matrix.
- A record of material assumptions and known limitations.

The diagrams should distinguish the enterprise environment from the provider environment and show the direction of data movement.

A diagram that merely displays boxes labeled “User,” “AI,” and “Database” is insufficient for a detailed risk assessment if it omits trust boundaries, permissions, data categories, and external dependencies.

### 4. Data Flows, Data Classification, and Information Protection

Data governance is a central part of third-party AI SaaS risk management because the organization may disclose information to a provider outside its direct administrative control.

The organization must understand what information enters the AI service, why it is processed, who can access it, how it is retained, and whether it can be used for additional purposes.

A service may be technically secure and still be unsuitable for a particular data category if the contractual or legal conditions for processing that data are not satisfied.

#### 4.1 AI SaaS Data Lifecycle

A typical AI SaaS data lifecycle includes the following stages:

```text id="aissaas18-01-03"
[Data Created or Collected]
            |
            v
[Classification and Authorization]
            |
            v
[Data Submitted to AI SaaS]
            |
            v
[Provider-Side Processing]
            |
            v
[Retrieval / Context Assembly]
            |
            v
[Model Processing]
            |
            v
[AI-Generated Output]
            |
            v
[Human Review or Application Use]
            |
            v
[Storage, Retention, or Deletion]
```

This is a simplified lifecycle. Some services also maintain conversation history, embeddings, cached content, audit logs, evaluation records, or other derived information.

The organization should determine which data artifacts exist and whether each artifact is subject to different access, retention, and deletion requirements.

#### 4.2 Data Categories

| Data category | Examples | Main concerns |
|---|---|---|
| Public information | Public product descriptions, published policies, public documentation. | Accuracy, integrity, and unauthorized use of external content. |
| Internal business information | Internal procedures, project plans, meeting notes. | Unauthorized disclosure and inappropriate sharing. |
| Confidential business information | Commercial strategy, financial forecasts, contracts, intellectual property. | Competitive harm, contractual exposure, and loss of confidentiality. |
| Personal data | Customer details, employee records, contact information. | Lawful processing, transparency, data minimization, access rights, and retention. |
| Sensitive or specially protected personal data | Health information or other data receiving special legal protection where applicable. | Heightened legal restrictions, access controls, and potential harm to individuals. |
| Authentication secrets | Passwords, API keys, access tokens, private keys. | Credential compromise and unauthorized access. |
| Security-sensitive information | Vulnerability reports, incident records, detection rules, architecture diagrams. | Facilitation of attacks and exposure of defensive capabilities. |
| Regulated business information | Payment-card data, financial records, or other regulated records. | Applicable legal, contractual, and sector-specific requirements. |

The exact classification labels should align with the organization's data-classification policy.

The organization must not assume that a provider's default security settings make every data category appropriate for processing.

#### 4.3 Data-Flow Assessment

For every material data flow, document:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Data-flow ID | Unique identifier for the flow. |
| Source system | System or person from which the data originates. |
| Destination | AI SaaS service, provider component, connected application, or downstream recipient. |
| Data categories | Types of information transmitted. |
| Business purpose | Reason for the transfer. |
| Data owner | Person or role authorizing the use. |
| Processing party | Organization or supplier processing the data. |
| Transfer mechanism | API, browser session, connector, file upload, or other mechanism. |
| Security controls | Encryption, authentication, authorization, DLP, and other applicable safeguards. |
| Retention | Expected storage duration and deletion requirements. |
| Legal or contractual basis | Applicable authorization and processing requirements. |
| Logging | Evidence of relevant transfers and access events. |
| Risk assessment | Identified risks and treatment decisions. |

This inventory supports privacy assessments, security architecture reviews, supplier assurance, incident response, and audit readiness.

#### 4.4 Example: Enterprise Knowledge Assistant

Assume an organization connects an AI assistant to an internal document repository.

An employee asks:

> Summarize the latest customer contract and identify its renewal obligations.

The system may process:

1. The employee's identity and request.
2. Search terms or retrieval instructions.
3. Document metadata.
4. Relevant contract content.
5. Context assembled for the model.
6. The model-generated summary.
7. Relevant audit and usage events.

Potential risks include:

- The employee is not authorized to access the contract.
- The retrieval system ignores source-document permissions.
- The provider retains the document content beyond the approved period.
- The generated summary omits an important contractual condition.
- A malicious instruction embedded in the document influences the output.
- The output is stored in a location with broader access than the source document.

The risk assessment must therefore cover both the original document and every subsequent representation of its information.

#### 4.5 Prompt, Output, and Derived-Data Governance

AI SaaS data governance should not focus exclusively on uploaded files.

| Data artifact | Governance question |
|---|---|
| User prompt | Does it contain personal, confidential, or regulated information? |
| Uploaded document | Who owns it, who may submit it, and which retention rules apply? |
| Retrieved content | Were source permissions enforced before the content entered the model context? |
| Conversation history | Who can access previous interactions, and how long are they retained? |
| Generated output | Does it reproduce confidential information or contain inaccurate claims? |
| Embeddings | Can the derived representation expose or reveal information, and how is access controlled? |
| Cached content | Where is it stored, and when is it invalidated or deleted? |
| Audit logs | Do they contain prompts, personal data, or other sensitive details? |
| Evaluation datasets | Are they authorized for testing, and do they contain sensitive information? |
| Feedback and ratings | Can they contain confidential content or be used for additional model improvement? |

Embeddings are numerical representations used in some AI systems to support similarity search and retrieval. They should not automatically be treated as harmless or anonymous. Their risk depends on the implementation, associated metadata, accessibility, and potential for information inference or reconstruction.

#### 4.6 Data Retention, Model Training, and Deletion

The organization should obtain clear answers to three separate questions.

**First: Is the data retained?**

Determine whether prompts, files, outputs, conversation histories, and logs are stored, and identify the applicable retention periods.

**Second: Is the data used for model training or service improvement?**

Determine whether customer content may be used for training, fine-tuning, evaluation, human review, product improvement, or other purposes. Do not assume that a service's enterprise label guarantees that every data-use pathway is disabled.

**Third: Can the data be deleted?**

Determine how deletion works across primary storage, derived artifacts, indexes, caches, backups, and subprocessors, subject to applicable legal and contractual retention requirements.

These questions should be resolved through the provider's actual service terms, contractual commitments, technical documentation, and configuration settings.

#### 4.7 Data-Minimization Controls

Practical controls include:

- Restricting the service to approved data categories.
- Removing unnecessary personal identifiers before submission where appropriate.
- Blocking prohibited secrets and sensitive information through available controls.
- Limiting the documents and repositories accessible to the AI.
- Applying source-system permissions to retrieval operations.
- Restricting conversation-history retention.
- Limiting administrative access to stored interactions.
- Defining permitted uses of generated output.
- Reviewing data-sharing and model-training settings.
- Establishing procedures for deletion, export, and incident-related evidence preservation.

Anonymization and pseudonymization are not interchangeable.

Pseudonymized data may remain personal data under applicable law when reidentification is reasonably possible using additional information. Anonymization requires a stronger assessment that individuals are no longer identifiable by means reasonably likely to be used.

Removing a person's name alone does not establish that a dataset is anonymous.

### 5. Risk Landscape and Initial Risk Register

The risk landscape identifies the main ways a third-party AI SaaS deployment could harm the organization, its customers, employees, or other affected parties.

A comprehensive assessment should combine conventional cybersecurity risks with AI-specific, privacy, supplier, operational, and business risks.

The purpose of an initial risk register is to establish a baseline for investigation and treatment. It is not a final risk rating: likelihood, impact, control effectiveness, and residual risk must be validated against the actual service and deployment.

#### 5.1 Primary Risk Categories

| Risk category | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality risk | Unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. | The AI retrieves confidential documents for an unauthorized user. |
| Integrity risk | Information or business records are altered incorrectly or maliciously. | An AI-enabled workflow submits an incorrect change to a customer record. |
| Availability risk | The service or a critical dependency becomes unavailable. | Provider downtime interrupts customer support. |
| Identity and access risk | Accounts, roles, credentials, or permissions are compromised or misconfigured. | An overprivileged service identity accesses unnecessary business records. |
| AI-specific security risk | Model behavior or AI workflows are exploited or manipulated. | Indirect prompt injection influences a connected workflow. |
| Output reliability risk | AI-generated information is inaccurate, misleading, incomplete, or unsuitable. | A contract summary omits a critical renewal condition. |
| Privacy risk | Personal data is processed inappropriately or disclosed without proper authorization. | Employee information is retained or used beyond the approved purpose. |
| Supplier risk | The provider fails to meet security, contractual, or operational requirements. | The supplier cannot provide adequate incident information or control evidence. |
| Fourth-party risk | A provider's subcontractor or dependency introduces additional exposure. | A model-hosting dependency experiences a security incident. |
| Compliance risk | The deployment fails to satisfy an applicable legal or contractual obligation. | The service is used for a purpose requiring controls that have not been implemented. |
| Operational resilience risk | The organization cannot maintain or recover critical business operations. | A provider outage has no workable fallback process. |
| Concentration risk | Too many critical functions depend on one provider or service. | Multiple business processes fail following a single provider disruption. |
| Intellectual-property risk | Proprietary information is disclosed or reused in an unauthorized manner. | Confidential source code or product designs are submitted to an unapproved service. |
| Human-factor risk | Users misunderstand AI limitations or bypass required safeguards. | Employees rely on generated answers without checking critical facts. |
| Governance risk | Ownership, approval authority, or control responsibilities are unclear. | A service is deployed without an accountable business owner or risk approval. |
| Exit risk | The organization cannot terminate the service securely or maintain continuity. | Data and integration dependencies cannot be transferred or removed promptly. |

A risk should not be marked “accepted” merely because the initial rating is low or because a provider claims that the risk is addressed.

#### 5.3 Inherent Risk Versus Residual Risk

These concepts must be distinguished from the beginning.

**Inherent risk** is the exposure before considering the effectiveness of relevant controls.

**Residual risk** is the exposure that remains after considering the controls that are actually implemented and effective.

For example:

- Inherent risk: The AI assistant can access a repository containing confidential business information.
- Existing control: Enterprise identity integration and document permissions are configured.
- Control gap: The retrieval component does not consistently enforce the requesting user's document permissions.
- Residual-risk conclusion: The confidentiality risk remains material until authorization is corrected and validated.

The presence of SSO or repository permissions does not justify reducing the risk rating if the AI retrieval path bypasses those permissions.

#### 5.4 Initial Risk Treatment Priorities

During initial assessment, the GRC team should prioritize:

1. **Data exposure:** Determine which sensitive information can reach the provider and whether its use is authorized.
2. **Access control:** Verify that users and connected systems cannot retrieve or modify information beyond their permissions.
3. **Supplier assurance:** Confirm the provider's security posture, data-use practices, incident commitments, and subcontractor dependencies.
4. **AI-specific attack paths:** Evaluate prompt injection, unauthorized retrieval, unsafe tool invocation, and relevant output-handling failures.
5. **Privacy and compliance:** Determine which obligations apply to the actual use case.
6. **Operational resilience:** Establish the consequences of provider failure and whether a viable fallback exists.
7. **Human oversight:** Identify where users must validate outputs or approve actions.
8. **Evidence and accountability:** Assign owners and establish the evidence required to support approval.

These priorities are a starting point. The final sequence should reflect actual risk severity, legal requirements, and business criticality.

#### 5.5 Initial Governance Decision

Before the organization commits to full production deployment, it should be able to answer:

- Is the business purpose clearly defined?
- Is the proposed use authorized by the organization?
- Which data categories will be processed?
- Has the provider's role and service architecture been established?
- Are material data flows and trust boundaries documented?
- Are the customer and provider responsibilities understood?
- Have the major cybersecurity, AI-specific, privacy, supplier, and resilience risks been identified?
- Are accountable owners assigned?
- Are there any immediately disqualifying conditions, such as prohibited data use or an inability to satisfy mandatory requirements?
- Is there sufficient information to proceed to detailed supplier, regulatory, and security assessment?

If these questions cannot be answered, the organization should not treat the service as ready for production approval. It may continue discovery or a suitably restricted assessment, but uncertainty must be documented rather than mistaken for low risk.

### Core Principle

**Third-party AI SaaS governance begins with understanding the business purpose, architecture, data flows, responsibility boundaries, and credible risk scenarios.** The organization must know what the AI service is permitted to do, what information it can access, which external parties process that information, and what could happen if the service or its controls fail.

A defensible initial assessment does not depend on whether the provider markets its service as enterprise-grade or secure. It depends on the actual use case, verified architecture, data sensitivity, supplier commitments, enforceable controls, and accountable risk ownership.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **AI SaaS versus traditional SaaS:** AI SaaS retains conventional application-security requirements while introducing additional risks from probabilistic outputs, prompt injection, retrieval, and model behavior.
- **Business purpose versus technical capability:** A feature being available does not mean the organization is authorized or justified in using it.
- **AI assistance versus autonomous execution:** Generating a recommendation and executing a business action have different control and accountability requirements.
- **Provider operation versus customer accountability:** Outsourcing service operation does not automatically transfer the customer's business, data, or regulatory responsibilities.
- **Trust boundary versus network boundary:** A trust boundary reflects differences in control, permissions, or security assumptions; it may exist even within a single network or application.
- **Data submission versus data processing:** Sending a prompt is only one stage; retrieval, retention, output generation, logging, and derived-data handling may introduce additional exposure.
- **Inherent risk versus residual risk:** Existing controls should reduce the assessed risk only to the extent that their effectiveness is supported by evidence.
- **Risk identification versus risk acceptance:** Identifying a risk does not authorize it, and an initial risk register does not replace a formal assessment and approval process.

## Part 2: Regulatory Frameworks, Supplier Due Diligence, Risk Assessment, and Security Requirements

### 6. Regulatory Frameworks and Governance Requirements

Before approving a third-party AI SaaS deployment, the organization must determine which legal, regulatory, contractual, and internal governance requirements apply.

The assessment should consider both the organization’s general obligations as a business and any additional obligations arising from the AI system’s intended purpose, data processing activities, industry, and deployment environment.

A common governance mistake is to assume that purchasing an enterprise AI subscription automatically establishes regulatory compliance. A subscription may provide useful security capabilities, but the customer remains responsible for determining whether the deployment satisfies its own obligations.

#### 6.1 Establish the Applicable Regulatory Landscape

The following frameworks and regulations may be relevant to third-party AI SaaS deployments. Their applicability depends on the organization, jurisdiction, processing activities, and intended use.

| Framework or regulation | Primary focus | Relevance to third-party AI SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) | Personal-data processing and protection. | Lawfulness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimization, security, data-subject rights, international transfers, and controller–processor obligations. |
| EU Artificial Intelligence Act | Regulation of AI systems according to their characteristics, roles, and intended uses. | AI literacy, prohibited practices, high-risk AI obligations, transparency requirements, provider and deployer responsibilities, and other applicable requirements. |
| ISO/IEC 27001 | Information Security Management System (ISMS). | Supplier security, risk management, access control, incident management, and information-security governance. |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | AI Management System (AIMS). | Governance of AI-related risks, responsibilities, policies, lifecycle processes, and continual improvement. |
| NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) | AI risk management. | Structured identification, assessment, treatment, and monitoring of AI-related risks. |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) | Cybersecurity risk management. | Govern, identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover activities. |
| SOC 2 | Independent examination of controls against applicable Trust Services Criteria. | Evidence about the service provider’s controls, depending on the examination scope and report. |
| Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) | Protection of payment-card account data within applicable scope. | Relevant when the AI deployment interacts with systems or processes that affect payment-card data or the security of the cardholder data environment. |
| Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) | Digital operational resilience in the EU financial sector. | Relevant to in-scope financial entities and their ICT third-party arrangements, including applicable AI SaaS dependencies. |
| NIS2 Directive and applicable national implementing law | Cybersecurity and risk-management obligations for in-scope entities. | Relevant to covered organizations and applicable supplier and cybersecurity risk-management obligations. |
| Internal security policies | Organization-specific minimum security requirements. | Approved services, permitted data, access restrictions, logging, acceptable use, and mandatory approval processes. |
| Contractual obligations | Commitments made to customers, partners, and suppliers. | Confidentiality, data handling, security controls, audit rights, incident notification, and service commitments. |

This table is an applicability checklist, not a declaration that every listed framework applies to every deployment.

For example, a business using an AI assistant for internal document summarization may face a different set of requirements from a financial institution using AI in a regulated customer decision process.

#### 6.2 GDPR: Personal-Data Processing

When personal data is processed through an AI SaaS service, the organization must determine its role and the provider’s role under applicable data-protection law.

In a common arrangement, the organization determines the purposes and essential means of processing and acts as the data controller, while the AI SaaS provider processes personal data on the controller’s behalf as a processor.

However, roles must be determined from the actual processing arrangement. A provider may independently determine certain purposes for particular processing activities and could have a different role for those activities.

Key assessment areas include:

**Purpose limitation**

The organization should establish why personal data is being submitted to the AI service and whether the processing is compatible with the stated purpose.

**Data minimization**

Only personal data necessary for the authorized use case should be processed.

**Lawfulness and transparency**

The organization should identify an appropriate legal basis and address applicable transparency obligations.

**Processor governance**

Where the provider acts as a processor, the organization should verify the applicable contractual requirements, including those under Article 28 of the GDPR.

**Security of processing**

Technical and organizational measures should be appropriate to the risk, considering the nature of the data and processing.

**International data transfers**

The organization should determine whether personal data is transferred to or accessed from countries outside the European Economic Area and assess the applicable transfer requirements.

**Data-subject rights**

The organization should establish how requests involving personal data held in prompts, conversation histories, documents, outputs, or other relevant records will be handled.

**Data protection impact assessment**

Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals’ rights and freedoms, the organization must assess whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is required under Article 35.

A DPIA is not automatically mandatory for every AI SaaS deployment. Its necessity depends on the processing and applicable criteria.

#### 6.3 EU AI Act: Provider and Deployer Responsibilities

The EU AI Act introduces obligations that depend on the AI system, its intended purpose, risk classification, the role of the organization, and the applicable implementation timeline.

For a third-party AI SaaS deployment, the organization should establish:

1. Whether the service qualifies as an AI system under the applicable legal definition.
2. The intended purpose and relevant system capabilities.
3. Whether the organization acts as a provider, deployer, or another relevant operator.
4. Whether the use case falls within a prohibited practice, a high-risk category, or another regulated category.
5. Whether transparency, AI literacy, human oversight, or other requirements apply.
6. Whether the provider has supplied sufficient information for the organization to meet its own obligations.
7. Whether modifications or changes in intended purpose affect the organization’s legal role or obligations.
8. Whether the provider’s contractual and technical arrangements support compliance.

**Important distinction:** Using a third-party AI model does not automatically make the customer a provider of the AI system. However, certain modifications, changes in intended purpose, or other circumstances may alter the customer’s legal responsibilities under the applicable rules.

Similarly, not every general-purpose AI assistant is automatically a high-risk AI system. Classification depends on the relevant legal criteria and actual use.

The organization should obtain legal or specialist compliance advice when the intended use may fall within a regulated category.

#### 6.4 ISO/IEC 27001 and Supplier Security

ISO/IEC 27001 provides an information-security management framework for identifying, treating, monitoring, and continually improving information-security risks.

For third-party AI SaaS, the organization should evaluate supplier security within its own ISMS and risk-management process.

Relevant areas include:

- Supplier relationships and contractual security requirements.
- Information classification and handling.
- Identity and access management.
- Secure configuration.
- Cryptographic protection.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Vulnerability management.
- Incident response.
- Business continuity.
- Change management.
- Compliance and independent assurance.

The organization should not interpret an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate as proof that every AI-specific risk is adequately controlled.

The certificate’s scope, validity, exclusions, and relationship to the contracted service must be examined.

#### 6.5 ISO/IEC 42001 and AI Governance

ISO/IEC 42001 provides requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI Management System.

Its relevance to third-party AI SaaS includes governance of:

- AI policies and organizational responsibilities.
- AI-related risks and opportunities.
- Intended purpose and context of use.
- Supplier and third-party relationships.
- AI lifecycle processes.
- Monitoring and evaluation.
- Incident management and corrective action.
- Documented information and continual improvement.

The organization should integrate AI governance with existing information-security, privacy, enterprise-risk, and supplier-management processes rather than operating separate, disconnected governance structures.

An AI Management System certification is not a substitute for assessing the specific AI service, data flows, intended purpose, and contractual conditions.

#### 6.6 Evidence-Based Regulatory Mapping

The organization should maintain a regulatory applicability register.

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Requirement ID | Unique identifier for the obligation. |
| Source | Regulation, standard, contract, or internal policy. |
| Requirement | Specific obligation or control objective. |
| Applicability rationale | Why the requirement applies or does not apply. |
| Accountable owner | Role responsible for the obligation. |
| Implementation status | Implemented, partially implemented, planned, not applicable, or not assessed. |
| Evidence | Policy, configuration, contract, assessment, test, or other supporting record. |
| Identified gap | Difference between the requirement and the current state. |
| Remediation action | Action needed to address the gap. |
| Due date | Agreed completion date. |
| Approval | Relevant risk or compliance decision. |

A regulatory register should distinguish an obligation that has been legally assessed as not applicable from one that has simply not been investigated.

The objective is not to collect the largest possible number of certificates and questionnaires. It is to obtain enough reliable evidence to make a defensible decision about the provider’s ability to meet the organization’s requirements.

#### 7.2 Supplier Inherent-Risk Classification

The organization should classify the supplier relationship before deciding how much due diligence is required.

Relevant factors include:

| Factor | Lower-risk example | Higher-risk example |
|---|---|---|
| Data sensitivity | Public information only. | Restricted customer records or sensitive business information. |

A useful principle is to scale due diligence according to the potential impact of failure, not merely the purchase price or number of users.

#### 7.3 Supplier Due-Diligence Questionnaire

The following questions provide a baseline for assessing an AI SaaS provider.

**A. Company and Service Governance**

1. What legal entity provides the contracted service?
2. Which service components are included in the proposed subscription?
3. Which components are operated by the provider, and which are supplied by other organizations?
4. Does the provider maintain documented information-security and AI governance policies?
5. How are material changes to the service communicated to customers?
6. Does the provider have a formal risk-management and security-assurance process?
7. Which business continuity and disaster recovery arrangements support the service?

**B. Information Security**

8. Does the provider maintain a current information-security certification or independent assurance report?
9. What is the scope of the certification or report?
10. How are privileged accounts protected?
11. How are vulnerabilities identified, prioritized, and remediated?
12. What security testing is performed, and how frequently?
13. How are security incidents detected and investigated?
14. What incident notification commitments apply to the customer?
15. Which customer-visible security logs and administrative audit events are available?
16. How are encryption and key-management responsibilities allocated?

**C. AI Architecture and Model Governance**

17. Which AI models and model providers support the service?
18. Can the model provider change without customer approval or notification?
19. Are customer prompts, files, outputs, or feedback used to train or improve models?
20. Which data-processing and retention settings can the customer configure?
21. How are model updates tested before deployment?
22. What mechanisms exist to detect or mitigate prompt injection and unsafe tool use?
23. How does the service handle inaccurate, unsafe, or potentially harmful outputs?
24. Can the customer restrict the model’s access to data sources and external tools?
25. What testing evidence is available for the relevant AI capabilities?

**D. Data Protection and Privacy**

26. What categories of customer data are processed?
27. Where is customer data processed and stored?
28. Which subprocessors can access customer data?
29. What retention periods apply to prompts, outputs, logs, and derived data?
30. How does deletion work across relevant storage systems and subprocessors?
31. Does the provider support applicable data-subject rights requests?
32. What contractual arrangements support international data transfers where applicable?
33. Can the customer disable optional data-sharing or model-improvement features?

**E. Business Continuity and Exit**

34. What availability commitments apply?
35. How are service disruptions communicated?
36. What recovery objectives are relevant to the contracted service?
37. Can customer data and required audit records be exported in a usable format?
38. What happens to customer data after termination?
39. Can the customer revoke integrations, credentials, and access promptly?
40. Does the provider support an orderly transition to an alternative service?

The questionnaire is a starting point. Responses should be validated against the proposed service, applicable contracts, independent assurance evidence, and the provider’s actual configuration options.

#### 7.4 Independent Assurance Evidence

Common evidence sources include:

| Evidence | What it can support | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| ISO/IEC 27001 certificate | Evidence that an ISMS within the certified scope has been assessed. | Does not prove that every relevant control or AI capability is effective. |
| SOC 2 Type I report | Evidence about control design at a specified point in time. | Does not demonstrate operating effectiveness over an extended period. |
| SOC 2 Type II report | Evidence about control design and operation over a defined examination period. | Scope, exceptions, complementary user-entity controls, and report age matter. |

### 8. AI-Specific Threat Modeling and Security Requirements

Traditional security assessments remain essential, but they do not fully address threats arising from the way AI systems interpret instructions, retrieve context, generate outputs, and interact with external tools.

Threat modeling provides a structured method for identifying relevant attack paths and translating them into testable security requirements.

#### 8.1 Establish the Threat Model

The organization should identify:

1. The assets requiring protection.
2. The trust boundaries through which data or instructions move.
3. The actors who may intentionally or unintentionally create risk.
4. The system components and integrations that can be attacked.
5. The threat scenarios relevant to the intended use.
6. The controls that prevent, detect, or limit each scenario.
7. The tests and evidence needed to demonstrate control effectiveness.

Relevant actors may include malicious external users, compromised employees, careless users, malicious document authors, compromised suppliers, and attackers targeting connected enterprise applications.

Not every threat is equally relevant to every AI SaaS deployment. Threat modeling should reflect the actual architecture and capabilities.

#### 8.2 AI-Specific Threat Scenarios

| Threat | Description | Potential consequence | Typical governance response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct prompt injection | A user submits instructions designed to override the intended behavior or safeguards of the AI system. | Policy bypass, inappropriate output, or unsafe actions. | Define usage restrictions, evaluate model behavior, constrain permissions, and test relevant attack scenarios. |
| Indirect prompt injection | Instructions are embedded in external content, such as documents, web pages, or retrieved records, and influence the AI when processed. | Data exposure or manipulation of downstream actions. | Treat retrieved content as untrusted, enforce authorization, constrain tool access, and test indirect-injection scenarios. |
| Sensitive-information disclosure | The system reveals information that should not be disclosed to the requesting user. | Confidentiality breach or privacy harm. | Enforce access control at retrieval and application layers; test cross-user and cross-role isolation. |
| Insecure output handling | An application treats AI-generated content as trusted input without suitable validation. | Injection vulnerabilities, unsafe execution, or corrupted business records. | Validate and constrain outputs before downstream processing or execution. |
| Excessive agency | The AI has permissions or tools beyond what the business use requires. | Unauthorized transactions, changes, or data access. | Apply least privilege, explicit authorization, action limits, and human approval for consequential actions. |
| Retrieval poisoning | Malicious or misleading content is introduced into a knowledge source or retrieval corpus. | Incorrect answers, manipulated recommendations, or unsafe behavior. | Govern source ingestion, restrict write access, validate content, and monitor changes. |
| Model or service dependency compromise | A provider or supporting component is compromised. | Data exposure, service manipulation, or operational disruption. | Assess supplier security, dependencies, incident commitments, and recovery arrangements. |
| Unreliable output | The model produces false, unsupported, or misleading information. | Poor decisions, financial loss, or customer harm. | Establish use-case-specific quality criteria, human review, and escalation procedures. |
| Denial of service or resource exhaustion | Excessive requests or other attacks degrade service availability or create unexpected costs. | Service disruption or financial exposure. | Apply usage limits, quotas, monitoring, and appropriate resilience arrangements. |
| Cross-tenant exposure | Data is exposed across customer boundaries due to a service or configuration failure. | Unauthorized disclosure of customer information. | Review provider assurance, isolation commitments, incident history where available, and relevant testing evidence. |

Prompt injection is not simply a conventional input-validation problem. An AI model may interpret malicious instructions in context, and there may be no universal filtering mechanism that reliably prevents every attack.

Therefore, the architecture must not rely exclusively on the model correctly ignoring malicious instructions. Access control, tool authorization, output validation, and restrictions on consequential actions must be enforced independently wherever possible.

#### 8.3 Security Requirements Matrix

Threats should be translated into requirements that can be assigned, implemented, and tested.

| Requirement ID | Security requirement | Responsible party | Verification evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| AISEC-01 | The service must authenticate users through an approved identity mechanism appropriate to the deployment. | Customer and provider, according to architecture. | Identity configuration and authentication tests. |
| AISEC-02 | Access to AI features and connected information must follow approved authorization rules. | Primarily customer, with relevant provider capabilities. | Role matrix, configuration review, and access-control testing. |
| AISEC-03 | The AI must not gain unrestricted access to enterprise data or tools merely because a user submits a prompt. | Customer and provider, according to implementation. | Permission tests, integration review, and adversarial testing. |
| AISEC-04 | Retrieved content must be authorized for the requesting user and intended purpose. | Customer and integration owner. | Retrieval tests across users, roles, and document classifications. |
| AISEC-05 | Data submitted to the service must comply with approved classification and handling requirements. | Customer. | Data-flow review, DLP configuration, and controlled test cases. |
| AISEC-06 | Customer data must be protected in transit and at rest where applicable to the architecture. | Customer and provider according to control ownership. | Configuration evidence, provider assurance, and technical validation. |
| AISEC-07 | AI-generated content must be validated before it is used in security-sensitive or consequential operations. | Business and application owners. | Workflow design, approval evidence, and output-validation tests. |
| AISEC-08 | Tool invocation must be constrained by explicit permissions and business rules. | Customer and provider, according to architecture. | Tool-permission inventory and negative authorization tests. |
| AISEC-09 | Relevant security events must be logged and available for investigation. | Customer and provider, according to the service. | Logging configuration, sample events, and detection tests. |
| AISEC-10 | The organization must have a documented method to disable the service or relevant integrations during an incident. | Customer and service owner. | Incident procedures and an observed or simulated shutdown test. |
| AISEC-11 | Material model or service changes must be evaluated for relevant security and business impact. | Provider and customer, according to contract and control ownership. | Change-notification records, reassessment evidence, and regression tests. |
| AISEC-12 | The service must meet defined retention, export, and deletion requirements for relevant data. | Customer and provider. | Contractual terms, configuration evidence, and deletion verification where available. |

The responsible party must be confirmed for the actual deployment. A requirement assigned jointly to the customer and provider still needs a clearly identified owner for implementation and evidence collection.

#### 8.4 Security Requirements Must Be Testable

Requirements should be expressed in terms of observable outcomes.

Weak requirement:

> The AI system must be secure against prompt injection.

This statement is too broad to demonstrate conclusively.

Stronger requirements include:

- Retrieved documents must be authorized according to the requesting user's permissions.
- Instructions found in retrieved content must not independently grant access to restricted data.
- The AI must not invoke a privileged action without satisfying the application's authorization and approval rules.
- Generated content must be validated before being interpreted as an executable command or trusted application input.
- The organization must be able to suspend affected integrations if an attack or control failure is detected.

These requirements are more useful because they describe controls that can be implemented and tested.

Testing should include both successful authorization scenarios and negative cases in which unauthorized access or action must be denied.

#### 8.5 AI Security Testing Scope

Depending on the service and use case, testing may include:

| Test category | Objective |
|---|---|
| Authentication testing | Confirm that unauthorized users cannot access the service. |
| Authorization testing | Confirm that access is limited to approved roles, data, and actions. |
| Cross-user isolation testing | Confirm that one user cannot retrieve another user's restricted information. |
| Prompt-injection testing | Evaluate whether malicious instructions influence behavior in unacceptable ways. |
| Retrieval-security testing | Confirm that retrieved content respects source permissions and approved scope. |
| Output-handling testing | Verify that generated content cannot bypass downstream validation or authorization. |
| Tool-use testing | Confirm that the AI cannot execute unauthorized or out-of-scope actions. |
| Data-leakage testing | Evaluate whether sensitive information can be disclosed through relevant prompts or outputs. |
| Logging testing | Confirm that required security events are captured and available. |
| Resilience testing | Evaluate service disruption, fallback procedures, and recovery arrangements. |
| Regression testing | Check whether relevant controls remain effective after material changes. |

Testing should be proportionate to risk and coordinated with the provider's terms, approved testing scope, and applicable authorization requirements.

The organization should not perform intrusive testing against a provider-managed service without confirming that the activity is authorized.

#### 8.6 Control Effectiveness and Evidence

A security requirement is not satisfied merely because it appears in a policy or supplier questionnaire.

Evidence should demonstrate that the relevant control exists and works as intended.

For example:

| Control claim | Weak evidence | Stronger evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Only authorized users can access the service. | A statement that SSO is supported. | Approved identity configuration, role assignments, and access-control test results. |
| Retrieval respects document permissions. | A supplier statement that enterprise search is secure. | Tests showing that users with different permissions receive only authorized results. |
| The AI cannot perform unauthorized transactions. | A policy instructing the AI not to execute certain actions. | Independent application authorization, constrained service permissions, and negative transaction tests. |
| Sensitive prompts are protected. | A generic statement that the provider uses encryption. | Evidence covering the relevant data flows, applicable encryption controls, retention, and access restrictions. |
| Incidents can be investigated. | A statement that logging is available. | Sample logs, event coverage, retention settings, and a demonstrated investigation workflow. |

The strength of evidence should be considered alongside its scope, recency, independence, and applicability to the actual service.

### Core Principle

**Third-party AI SaaS approval requires both supplier assurance and deployment-specific security validation.** A provider may operate a well-secured service, yet the customer can still create unacceptable risk through excessive permissions, inappropriate data use, weak integrations, inadequate output validation, or unclear accountability.

The organization should translate applicable obligations and identified threats into explicit security requirements, assign ownership, and collect evidence demonstrating that controls operate effectively.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Regulatory applicability versus certification:** A certificate can support assurance but does not establish compliance with every applicable legal obligation.
- **Supplier assurance versus deployment assurance:** Evidence about the provider's general controls does not prove that the customer's configuration and integrations are secure.
- **Provider responsibility versus customer responsibility:** Contractual allocation matters, but each party must implement the controls within its actual sphere of responsibility.
- **AI-specific testing versus conventional security testing:** AI testing supplements, rather than replaces, authentication, authorization, penetration testing, vulnerability management, and other established security practices.
- **Security policy versus enforceable control:** A written instruction does not prevent unauthorized access unless it is supported by appropriate technical or procedural controls.
- **Risk identification versus verified mitigation:** A risk is not adequately treated until the selected controls are implemented, validated, and supported by evidence.
- **Supplier transparency versus supplier trustworthiness:** Limited visibility may prevent adequate assurance even when there is insufficient evidence to conclude that the provider is insecure.
- **Risk-based approval versus blanket approval:** Approval should apply to a defined service, configuration, data scope, and intended purpose—not automatically to every capability the provider offers.

### 9. Technical Security Implementation and Control Deployment

After defining the business use case, documenting the architecture, assessing the supplier, and establishing security requirements, the organization must translate those requirements into operational controls.

This stage addresses a fundamental governance question:

**How can the organization demonstrate that the approved AI SaaS deployment is configured securely, operates within its authorized boundaries, and remains controllable throughout its lifecycle?**

The organization should not assume that a provider's security features are enabled by default or that a control described in a supplier questionnaire is effective in the customer's environment.

Security implementation must be validated against the approved architecture, risk assessment, data-classification requirements, and security-control ownership model.

#### 9.2 Establish a Security Baseline

A baseline should be specific enough to verify and should distinguish mandatory requirements from recommendations.

| Control domain | Baseline requirement | Verification evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Integrate with the approved enterprise identity provider where supported and appropriate. | Identity configuration and authentication tests. |
| Authentication | Enforce MFA and applicable authentication policies for relevant users and administrators. | Identity-provider configuration and test results. |
| Authorization | Assign least-privilege roles to users, administrators, integrations, and service identities. | Role matrix and access review. |

| Incident response | Establish escalation, containment, and provider-contact procedures. | Incident response plan and exercise results. |
| Change management | Define reassessment triggers for material service, model, and configuration changes. | Change-management records. |

The baseline must account for provider limitations. If the service cannot meet a mandatory requirement, the organization should determine whether an effective alternative control exists or whether the deployment must be restricted or rejected.

#### 9.3 Configuration Management

AI SaaS configurations can change as administrators enable features, connect additional repositories, create integrations, or grant new permissions.

The organization should maintain a controlled configuration record containing:

- Approved service and subscription tier.
- Enabled AI features and models, where configurable.
- Authorized user groups.
- Administrative roles.
- Connected enterprise applications.
- Approved data sources.
- Available model-training and data-sharing settings.
- Retention and deletion configurations.
- Logging and monitoring settings.
- API integrations and service identities.
- Approved geographic or processing restrictions, where supported.
- Exceptions and compensating controls.
- Configuration owner and review date.

Changes that affect the service's risk profile should be assessed before implementation where practicable.

For example, connecting an AI assistant to a restricted customer database is not merely an additional configuration task. It expands the service's data access and may introduce new privacy, confidentiality, and authorization risks.

#### 9.4 Control Ownership and Implementation Evidence

Every security requirement should have an accountable owner, an implementation status, and a defined method of verification.

| Requirement | Implementation owner | Validation owner | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

| Logging and alerting | Security operations | Security monitoring owner | Event samples and alert-test records. |
| Retention and deletion | Service owner and relevant data owner | Privacy or compliance reviewer, as appropriate | Configuration evidence and deletion-process documentation. |
| AI output validation | Business process owner | Relevant control owner | Workflow documentation and validation-test results. |

### 10. Identity, Access Management, and Integration Security

Identity and access management become particularly important when an AI SaaS service can retrieve enterprise information or invoke business applications.

A service may authenticate the user correctly while still exposing information the user is not authorized to access. Authentication establishes identity; authorization determines what that identity may do.

AI integrations must preserve this distinction.

#### 10.1 Enterprise Identity Integration

Where supported and appropriate, the organization should integrate the AI SaaS service with its enterprise identity provider.

Relevant controls include:

- Single sign-on (SSO).
- Multi-factor authentication (MFA).
- Conditional access.
- Role-based access control (RBAC).
- Privileged access management.
- Automated provisioning and deprovisioning.
- Session management.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Administrative activity logging.

The organization should also verify that local accounts, emergency accounts, service accounts, and other alternative access paths are appropriately controlled.

SSO alone does not establish that the service is securely configured. Excessive permissions, weak application roles, compromised sessions, and insecure integrations can still create significant exposure.

#### 10.2 Least Privilege for Users and Service Identities

The principle of least privilege requires each identity to receive only the permissions necessary for its approved purpose.

This applies to:

1. Human users.
2. Service accounts.
3. API clients.
4. Knowledge connectors.
5. Administrative identities.
6. AI agents and tool integrations.
7. Automated workflows.

Consider an AI assistant connected to a CRM system.

A read-only assistant that summarizes approved customer records may not need permission to modify customer accounts, export all customer data, or administer CRM users.

Granting these additional permissions creates unnecessary risk.

A useful permission model is:

| Identity | Intended function | Appropriate permission approach |
|---|---|---|
| Standard employee | Use approved AI features. | Access only to permitted features and data. |
| AI service administrator | Configure the service. | Administrative privileges limited to designated administrators. |
| Knowledge connector | Retrieve authorized documents. | Read access to approved repositories and scopes. |
| CRM integration identity | Retrieve selected CRM information. | Minimum required API permissions and data scope. |
| Workflow execution identity | Perform an approved business action. | Restricted action permissions, transaction limits, and independent authorization. |
| Emergency administrator | Recover service access during an incident. | Strong protection, restricted use, and monitored activation. |

Service identities should have identifiable owners, managed credentials, appropriate rotation or expiration controls, and a defined process for revocation.

#### 10.3 Retrieval Authorization

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) allows an AI application to retrieve relevant information from external sources and provide it to a model as context.

A common implementation indexes enterprise documents and uses semantic search to identify relevant passages.

The critical governance requirement is that retrieval must respect the organization's authorization model.

```text id="aissaas18-03-02"
[Authenticated User]
        |
        v
[User Identity and Permissions]
        |
        v
[AI Application Request]
        |
        v
[Retrieval Request]
        |
        v
[Authorization Enforcement]
        |
        +---------------------------+
        |                           |
        v                           v
[Authorized Documents]      [Unauthorized Documents]
        |                           |
        v                           v
[Approved Context]            [Access Denied]
        |
        v
[Model Processing]
        |
        v
[Generated Response]
```

This is a conceptual control flow. Implementations differ, and the authorization mechanism may operate within the source system, connector, retrieval service, or a combination of components.

The important requirement is that unauthorized content must not enter the model context simply because it is relevant to the user's question.

For example, an employee who cannot access executive compensation documents should not obtain their contents through an AI assistant that indexes the same repository.

Controls should address:

- Source-system permissions.
- Document-level and, where relevant, field-level authorization.
- Identity propagation into retrieval systems.
- Permission changes after documents have been indexed.
- Removal of access following employee termination or role changes.
- Cache invalidation and stale authorization information.
- Retrieval logs and access-denial events.
- Cross-user and cross-role testing.

**A secure document repository does not guarantee secure AI retrieval.** The complete retrieval path must be assessed.

#### 10.4 API and Connector Security

AI SaaS platforms may integrate with CRM, ERP, IT service management, collaboration, identity, and document-management systems.

Each integration introduces a trust relationship.

The organization should maintain an integration inventory documenting:

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Integration ID | Unique identifier. |
| Source and destination | Systems exchanging information or commands. |
| Business purpose | Why the integration exists. |
| Data exchanged | Data categories transmitted in each direction. |
| Authentication method | OAuth, managed identity, certificates, API keys, or another mechanism. |
| Authorization scope | Specific permissions granted to the integration. |
| Credential owner | Role accountable for credential lifecycle management. |
| Network restrictions | Applicable network or endpoint restrictions. |
| Logging | Available authentication, access, and action events. |
| Rate limits | Relevant quotas and abuse protections. |
| Business criticality | Consequences of integration failure. |
| Revocation method | How access can be disabled during an incident or at termination. |

API keys and other credentials should not be embedded in prompts, exposed in generated output, or stored in unapproved repositories.

Where supported, the organization should prefer managed identity or appropriately scoped token-based authentication over long-lived, broadly privileged credentials.

API security testing should verify both permitted and prohibited operations. A successful authentication test does not demonstrate that the integration's authorization scope is appropriate.

#### 10.5 AI Tool Invocation and Excessive Agency

Some AI services can invoke tools, call APIs, execute workflows, or perform actions on behalf of users.

This capability can be valuable, but it changes the security model.

The AI may interpret a request, select a tool, prepare parameters, and initiate an action. The application must not treat the model's decision as sufficient authorization.

A safer design separates reasoning from permission enforcement.

```text id="aissaas18-03-03"

      |

                             |
                             v
                    [Controlled Execution]
                             |
                             v
                    [Audit Log and Result]
```

The architecture must ensure that the action cannot bypass required approval or authorization checks.

Recommended controls include:

- Explicit allowlisting of permitted tools.
- Least-privilege tool permissions.
- Input and parameter validation.
- Independent authorization checks.
- Transaction limits and rate limits.
- Human approval for designated consequential actions.
- Logging of requests, approvals, executions, and results.
- Revocation mechanisms for compromised or unnecessary integrations.
- Testing against malicious instructions and unexpected tool selections.

A model should not be granted unrestricted access to enterprise systems simply because it is expected to follow instructions.

#### 10.6 Separation of Duties

Separation of duties reduces the possibility that one identity or process can perform an entire sensitive transaction without appropriate independent control.

For example, an AI service might prepare a financial adjustment, but a designated human approver should authorize the transaction before execution.

The appropriate control depends on the transaction's risk and the organization's policies.

| Action | Potential control |
|---|---|
| Drafting a customer email | User review before sending, depending on sensitivity. |
| Updating a low-impact internal classification | Automated execution within an approved scope, with monitoring. |
| Changing customer account details | Independent authorization and validation. |
| Issuing a financial refund | Transaction limits and appropriate approval. |
| Modifying access permissions | Privileged authorization and auditable approval. |
| Disabling security controls | Explicit authorization, strong restrictions, and enhanced monitoring. |

The objective is not to require human approval for every AI-generated action. It is to ensure that the level of oversight matches the potential consequences of failure.

### 11. Data Protection, Privacy Controls, and Secure Processing

Data protection controls must be implemented across the complete AI SaaS processing lifecycle, including information submitted by users, retrieved from enterprise sources, generated by the model, and retained by the provider.

The organization should validate the controls that apply to the specific service and data flows rather than relying on broad statements about encryption or enterprise security.

#### 11.1 Data Protection Control Layers

| Control layer | Objective | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Data classification | Identify information requiring special handling. | Approved classifications and restricted-data rules. |
| Data minimization | Avoid processing unnecessary information. | Limited prompts, restricted repositories, and selective field extraction. |
| Data-loss prevention | Detect or prevent prohibited data submission. | DLP rules, sensitive-information detection, and policy enforcement. |
| Encryption in transit | Protect data transmitted between components. | Approved transport encryption. |
| Encryption at rest | Protect stored information against unauthorized access. | Provider-managed or customer-managed encryption, where supported. |
| Access management | Limit who can access data and functions. | RBAC, least privilege, and access reviews. |
| Retention management | Limit unnecessary storage. | Configured retention and deletion schedules. |
| Privacy governance | Address lawful processing and individual rights. | Privacy assessment, processing records, and request-handling procedures. |
| Output governance | Control how generated information is used. | Validation, access restrictions, and approved-use policies. |
| Evidence preservation | Maintain appropriate records for assurance and investigations. | Access logs, configuration records, and incident evidence. |

The organization should evaluate the effectiveness of each applicable layer and identify gaps that require remediation or formal risk treatment.

#### 11.2 Data-Loss Prevention for AI Usage

Data-loss prevention can help identify and control attempts to submit sensitive information to AI services.

Depending on the available technology, controls may operate through endpoint protection, browser controls, network inspection, an AI gateway, SaaS security tools, or native provider features.

Potential policy rules include:

- Blocking the submission of passwords, private keys, and other authentication secrets.
- Restricting sensitive personal data to specifically approved services.
- Preventing uploads of restricted documents to unauthorized AI applications.
- Warning users when a prompt appears to contain confidential information.
- Restricting file uploads to approved repositories or business use cases.
- Logging policy violations and escalating repeated or high-risk events.

DLP should not be treated as a complete solution.

Natural-language prompts and complex documents may contain sensitive information that is difficult to detect reliably. Users may also disclose information through screenshots, copied text, or other channels outside the control's visibility.

DLP should therefore complement data classification, user training, access controls, approved-use policies, and supplier data-protection arrangements.

#### 11.3 Encryption and Key Management

The organization should verify encryption controls for relevant data flows and storage locations.

Questions include:

1. Is data encrypted during transmission between the user, enterprise environment, and provider?
2. Is data encrypted at rest in relevant storage systems?
3. Who manages the encryption keys?
4. Are customer-managed keys available where required?
5. What administrative roles can access or manage keys?
6. How are key rotation and revocation handled?
7. Do encryption arrangements cover relevant backups and derived artifacts?
8. Are there limitations or exceptions to the stated encryption protections?

Customer-managed keys may offer additional control in some architectures, but their availability and benefits depend on the service.

Encryption does not eliminate risks arising from authorized application access, compromised identities, insecure integrations, inappropriate data retention, or misuse of information after decryption.

#### 11.4 Privacy and Data-Processing Controls

Where personal data is involved, the organization should establish how the service supports applicable privacy obligations.

The assessment should cover:

- The purposes for which personal data is processed.
- The categories of data subjects and personal data.
- The relevant legal basis.
- The roles of the customer and provider.
- Applicable processing instructions and contractual requirements.
- Data retention and deletion.
- International transfers, where applicable.
- Data-subject rights.
- Security of processing.
- Incident management.
- Whether a DPIA is required.
- Whether the AI use creates risks involving profiling, significant decisions, or other impacts on individuals.

A privacy assessment should reflect the actual use case. For example, using an AI assistant to summarize a public policy is materially different from using AI to evaluate job applicants or determine eligibility for a service.

#### 11.5 Retention and Deletion Verification

The organization should document the lifecycle of relevant data artifacts.

| Artifact | Verification question |
|---|---|
| Prompt | Is the prompt stored, and for how long? |
| Uploaded file | Is the file retained separately from the conversation? |
| Retrieved content | Is the retrieved information cached or copied into another system? |
| Conversation history | Can administrators or other users access it? |
| Generated output | Where is it stored, and who can access it? |
| Embeddings and indexes | How are derived representations updated or deleted? |
| Security logs | What information do they contain, and how long are they retained? |
| Backups | What deletion limitations apply to backup copies? |
| Subprocessor records | How is deletion managed across relevant downstream providers? |

The organization should distinguish between deleting information from the user interface and deleting it from the provider's underlying processing and storage systems.

Where deletion cannot be immediate because of documented backup or legal-retention constraints, the applicable limitations and deletion timelines should be understood and contractually addressed where required.

#### 11.6 Privacy-Preserving Data Use

Depending on the use case, the organization may reduce exposure through:

- Data minimization.
- Redaction of unnecessary identifiers.
- Pseudonymization.
- Aggregation.
- Use of synthetic data for development and testing.
- Restriction of model context to relevant information.
- Segregation of sensitive workloads.
- Limitation of data access by role and purpose.

These measures should be selected according to the actual risk.

Synthetic data may be useful for testing, but its suitability depends on how it was generated and whether it adequately represents the behavior being evaluated.

Pseudonymization reduces direct identifiability but does not automatically make information anonymous or remove applicable privacy obligations.

### 12. AI Testing, Validation, and Security Assurance

AI SaaS security testing must evaluate both conventional technical controls and the behavior of the AI-enabled workflow.

Traditional security testing examines issues such as authentication, authorization, application vulnerabilities, and insecure integrations. AI-specific testing examines additional failure modes involving prompts, retrieved context, model outputs, and tool invocation.

Neither category replaces the other.

#### 12.1 Testing Objectives

The organization should define testing objectives based on the use case and identified risks.

The principal objectives are to verify that:

1. Only authorized users can access the service.
2. Data retrieval respects source permissions.
3. Sensitive information is not exposed through unauthorized outputs.
4. AI-generated instructions cannot bypass application authorization.
5. Tool invocation remains within approved permissions.
6. Outputs meet defined quality and safety criteria for the intended use.
7. Security events are captured and can support investigation.
8. Service changes do not introduce unacceptable regressions.
9. Recovery and containment procedures work as intended.
10. The deployment remains within its approved risk boundaries.

#### 12.2 AI Testing Categories

| Test category | Primary objective | Example test |
|---|---|---|
| Functional testing | Confirm that the AI service performs its intended function. | Verify that approved documents can be summarized. |
| Access-control testing | Verify that access is restricted appropriately. | Attempt to retrieve a document as a user without permission. |
| Prompt-injection testing | Evaluate whether malicious instructions influence the system improperly. | Place adversarial instructions in a test document and observe whether they trigger prohibited behavior. |
| Data-leakage testing | Evaluate unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. | Test whether restricted information can be obtained through prompts or generated responses. |
| Retrieval testing | Confirm that relevant content is retrieved without violating authorization boundaries. | Compare retrieval results for users with different permissions. |
| Tool-use testing | Verify that actions are authorized independently of model decisions. | Attempt to initiate a prohibited transaction through an AI-generated tool request. |
| Output validation | Assess whether generated content meets use-case-specific quality requirements. | Compare generated contract summaries against verified source clauses. |
| Resilience testing | Evaluate failure handling and continuity. | Simulate provider unavailability and verify the fallback procedure. |
| Logging validation | Confirm that required events are recorded. | Verify that administrative changes and prohibited actions generate appropriate records. |
| Regression testing | Check whether controls remain effective after changes. | Repeat selected security tests after a model or configuration update. |

#### 12.3 Prompt-Injection Testing

Prompt injection occurs when instructions supplied through user input or external content influence an AI system in ways that conflict with its intended behavior or security requirements.

Two common forms are:

**Direct prompt injection:** A user deliberately submits instructions intended to bypass restrictions.

**Indirect prompt injection:** Malicious instructions are embedded in content the AI later processes, such as documents, emails, web pages, or retrieved records.

For example, an enterprise assistant retrieves a document containing an instruction to reveal confidential information or invoke an external tool.

A suitable test should establish whether the system:

- Treats retrieved content as untrusted input.
- Maintains the requesting user's authorization boundaries.
- Prevents unapproved access to restricted information.
- Restricts tool invocation to authorized operations.
- Requires appropriate approval before consequential actions.
- Records relevant events for investigation.

A single successful test does not establish comprehensive protection against prompt injection.

Testing should cover relevant attack paths, and the architecture should use independent authorization and execution controls rather than relying solely on the model to reject malicious instructions.

#### 12.4 Testing Retrieval Authorization

Retrieval-augmented generation requires particular attention because documents may be indexed independently of the permissions that govern their original locations.

A practical test matrix might include:

| Test case | User permission | Expected result |
|---|---|---|
| TC-01 | User can read the document. | Relevant content may be retrieved according to approved policy. |
| TC-02 | User cannot read the document. | Restricted content must not enter the user's model context or response. |
| TC-03 | User loses document access after indexing. | Subsequent retrieval must respect the updated authorization state. |
| TC-04 | User changes department or role. | Retrieval permissions must reflect the approved role changes. |
| TC-05 | Document is deleted or reclassified. | Retrieval behavior must reflect the applicable deletion or classification process. |
| TC-06 | User requests another department's confidential records. | The service must deny unauthorized access. |
| TC-07 | A retrieved document contains malicious instructions. | The instructions must not grant new permissions or bypass required controls. |

The expected result should be defined before testing.

If the system returns a summary that reveals the contents of an unauthorized document, the control has failed even if the underlying file itself was never directly downloaded.

#### 12.5 Output Quality and Human Oversight

AI outputs should be evaluated against the risks of the intended use.

For low-impact drafting, a lightweight review may be sufficient. For contractual analysis, security recommendations, financial decisions, or customer-impacting actions, more rigorous validation may be necessary.

The organization should define:

- What constitutes an acceptable output.
- Which errors are tolerable and which are unacceptable.
- Whether source references or supporting evidence are required.
- When human review is mandatory.
- Who is authorized to approve the result.
- How disagreements or uncertainty are handled.
- How incorrect outputs are reported and corrected.
- How output quality is monitored over time.

A generated answer that sounds convincing is not necessarily accurate.

For high-impact uses, the validation process should examine substantive correctness, relevant omissions, consistency with authoritative sources, and compliance with the approved workflow.

Human oversight is only meaningful if the reviewer has sufficient competence, information, authority, and time to challenge the output.

#### 12.6 Test Results and Defect Management

Each test should produce a record containing:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Test ID | Unique identifier. |
| Requirement ID | Requirement being verified. |
| Risk ID | Related risk scenario. |
| Test objective | What the test is intended to establish. |
| Preconditions | Required configuration, identities, and data. |
| Test procedure | Steps performed. |
| Expected result | Required behavior. |
| Actual result | Observed behavior. |
| Evidence | Logs, screenshots, output samples, or test records. |
| Status | Pass, fail, blocked, or not applicable. |
| Severity | Impact of the observed defect. |
| Defect owner | Person responsible for remediation. |
| Retest result | Evidence that remediation is effective. |
| Risk decision | Treatment or escalation required if the defect remains unresolved. |

Failed tests should be linked to the risk register and tracked until they are remediated, formally accepted by an authorized risk owner, or addressed through another documented decision.

A failed security test must not be closed solely because the supplier has stated that the behavior is expected.

The organization must determine whether the observed behavior is compatible with its requirements and approved risk boundaries.

### 13. Operational Monitoring and Continuous Security Assurance

AI SaaS risk does not end when the service passes its initial assessment.

The provider may change models, features, retention settings, supporting infrastructure, or subprocessors. The customer may connect additional repositories, expand the user population, or grant the AI new capabilities.

Each of these changes may affect the deployment's risk profile.

Continuous assurance establishes whether the service remains within its approved security, privacy, operational, and business boundaries.

#### 13.2 Security Events to Monitor

| Event category | Examples | Potential significance |
|---|---|---|
| Authentication | Repeated failures, unusual login patterns, suspicious sessions. | Possible account compromise. |
| Privileged activity | Role changes, administrator actions, security-setting modifications. | Potential misuse or weakening of controls. |
| Data access | Unusual retrieval volume or access to sensitive repositories. | Potential unauthorized information access. |
| Data export | Large downloads or unusual information transfer. | Potential exfiltration. |
| Policy violations | Attempts to submit prohibited data or use unapproved features. | Misuse or control bypass. |
| Integration activity | Unexpected API calls or changes in integration permissions. | Potential credential compromise or excessive privilege. |
| AI tool invocation | Attempts to perform restricted actions or unusual action sequences. | Potential unsafe automation or exploitation. |
| Service configuration | New integrations, enabled capabilities, or changed data-sharing settings. | Expansion of the attack surface. |
| Provider notifications | Security incidents, model changes, or material service modifications. | Need for reassessment or response. |
| Service availability | Outages, degradation, or repeated failures. | Operational resilience concerns. |
| Output quality | Increased error rates or failed validation checks. | Potential business or safety impact. |
| Usage and cost | Unexpected request volume or consumption spikes. | Abuse, misconfiguration, or financial exposure. |

Monitoring should be risk-based. Not every event requires an alert, and not every unusual event represents a security incident.

#### 13.3 AI-Specific Monitoring Limitations

AI SaaS monitoring may be constrained by the provider's architecture and commercial service model.

The customer may not have direct access to:

- Internal model execution logs.
- Full provider-side infrastructure telemetry.
- All prompt-processing events.
- Detailed model-level security signals.
- Internal model evaluation results.
- Complete downstream processing records.
- Every subprocessor's security events.

The organization should identify these visibility limitations during supplier assessment.

Where direct telemetry is unavailable, alternative evidence may include provider security notifications, contractual reporting, available administrative audit logs, service health information, and independent assurance reports.

Compensating controls should not be described as equivalent to unavailable telemetry unless their effectiveness has been established.

#### 13.4 Key Risk Indicators and Key Performance Indicators

The organization should define measures that support risk-based governance.

**Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** provide signals about changes in risk exposure.

**Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)** measure operational or control performance.

Examples include:

| Metric | Type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|

| Percentage of deployed services with a completed risk assessment | KPI | Measures assessment coverage. |
| Number of unresolved high-risk security findings | KRI | Indicates outstanding material exposure. |
| Number of unapproved AI integrations detected | KRI | Indicates potential shadow-AI or integration risk. |
| Percentage of privileged accounts reviewed on schedule | KPI | Measures access-governance performance. |
| Number of unauthorized retrieval events | KRI | Indicates possible access-control failures. |
| Percentage of material model changes assessed before release | KPI | Measures change-governance coverage. |
| Number of incidents involving sensitive information disclosure | KRI | Indicates realized confidentiality exposure. |
| Percentage of required supplier evidence current | KPI | Measures assurance-maintenance performance. |
| Number of overdue remediation actions rated high risk | KRI | Indicates accumulated control exposure. |
| Percentage of critical workflows with tested fallback procedures | KPI | Measures resilience preparedness. |
| Rate of material AI-output validation failures | KRI | Indicates possible degradation in output reliability. |

Metrics require defined owners, calculation methods, reporting frequency, and escalation thresholds.

For example, a high number of detected unauthorized retrieval attempts may indicate a control weakness, but it may also reflect effective detection. The metric must be interpreted alongside context, severity, and control performance.

#### 13.5 Continuous Reassessment Triggers

The organization should reassess the deployment when material changes occur.

Common triggers include:

- Introduction of a new AI model or model provider.
- Changes to provider data-retention or model-training practices.
- Addition of a new enterprise data source.
- Expansion into a new business process.
- Introduction of automated tool execution.
- Significant changes to user permissions.
- Changes in the categories or sensitivity of processed data.
- Material supplier security incidents.
- Changes in applicable legal or contractual obligations.
- Repeated output-quality failures.
- Discovery of a significant vulnerability.
- Changes to the service's hosting or subprocessor arrangements.
- Changes to business criticality or operational dependencies.

Reassessment should be proportionate to the change. A minor user-interface update may not require a complete review, whereas granting the AI write access to a critical business system may require a substantial reassessment and renewed approval.

#### 13.6 Continuous Assurance Reporting

The AI service owner should maintain a concise operational assurance report containing:

1. Current service scope and approved use.
2. Current risk rating and material changes.
3. Open high-risk findings.
4. Security incidents and significant near misses.
5. Supplier assurance status.
6. Access-control review results.
7. Relevant monitoring indicators.
8. AI testing and output-quality results.
9. Changes to data processing or integrations.
10. Exceptions and compensating controls.
11. Remediation progress.
12. Required governance decisions.

This report supports decisions about continued operation, restriction, remediation, suspension, or termination.

### 14. Incident Response and Operational Containment

The organization must be prepared to respond when an AI SaaS service exposes sensitive information, produces harmful outputs, performs unauthorized actions, or becomes compromised or unavailable.

AI-related incidents may involve conventional cybersecurity events, privacy breaches, supplier incidents, AI-specific attacks, or failures in business-process controls.

The incident response process should integrate AI-related scenarios into the organization's existing incident-management framework.

#### 14.1 AI SaaS Incident Categories

| Incident category | Example | Initial concern |
|---|---|---|
| Data disclosure | The AI returns confidential information to an unauthorized user. | Containment, scope determination, and privacy or contractual assessment. |
| Account compromise | An attacker obtains access to an employee's AI account. | Account containment and investigation of activity. |
| Prompt-injection exploitation | Malicious content causes the system to attempt an unauthorized action. | Restrict affected workflows and investigate the attack path. |
| Unauthorized tool execution | The AI triggers a business action beyond the approved scope. | Stop further actions and assess downstream impact. |
| Provider security incident | The supplier reports unauthorized access affecting the service. | Establish the affected service scope, data, and required response actions. |
| Sensitive-data retention failure | Data remains accessible beyond an approved retention period. | Determine the scope, applicable obligations, and remediation options. |
| AI-output incident | Incorrect output contributes to a material business or customer-impacting decision. | Assess harm, correct the decision where possible, and prevent recurrence. |
| Service disruption | The provider becomes unavailable during a critical business process. | Activate continuity procedures and assess operational impact. |
| Integration compromise | A connector credential is compromised or abused. | Revoke or restrict credentials and investigate connected systems. |
| Configuration failure | A new configuration exposes previously restricted data. | Restore approved configuration and assess whether exposure occurred. |

Incident classification should consider both the technical event and its consequences.

For example, an incorrect AI response may be a quality issue in one context and a reportable or otherwise significant incident in another, depending on the information involved, the resulting harm, and applicable requirements.

Where applicable, notification deadlines must be assessed promptly. The organization should not wait for a complete technical investigation before escalating a potential incident to the functions responsible for determining legal and contractual notification obligations.

#### 14.3 Immediate Containment Actions

Depending on the incident, containment may involve:

- Disabling a compromised user account.
- Revoking API keys or integration credentials.
- Restricting access to an affected data source.
- Disabling a specific AI feature or tool.
- Suspending an affected workflow.
- Blocking further data submissions.
- Reverting an unsafe configuration.
- Disabling an integration.
- Restricting access to conversation histories or exposed outputs.
- Activating an approved fallback process.
- Requesting provider-side containment.
- Preserving relevant logs and evidence.

Containment should be targeted where possible.

For example, disabling a compromised connector may be preferable to disabling the entire AI service if the remaining service can operate safely. However, if the scope of exposure is unknown or the risk is severe, broader suspension may be justified.

#### 14.4 Evidence Preservation

The organization should preserve evidence needed to understand the incident and support investigation.

Potential evidence includes:

- User identity and authentication events.
- Relevant prompts and outputs, where available and lawful to retain.
- Retrieval requests and source-document references.
- Tool invocation records.
- API logs and integration events.
- Configuration changes.
- Administrative actions.
- Provider incident notifications.
- Relevant timestamps and affected system identifiers.
- Data-access and export records.
- Related business transactions.
- Communication and decision records.

Evidence collection must respect privacy, data minimization, legal restrictions, and the organization's evidence-handling procedures.

The organization should not assume that all provider-side records are available to customers. Logging limitations and evidence-access commitments should be established during supplier due diligence.

#### 14.5 Incident Notification and Escalation

The response team should determine which internal and external notifications may be required.

Potential stakeholders include:

| Stakeholder | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Security operations | Detection, triage, technical investigation, and containment coordination. |
| AI service owner | Service-level coordination and implementation of containment measures. |
| Business owner | Assessment of business impact and operational priorities. |
| Data owner | Assessment of affected information and appropriate data-handling decisions. |
| Privacy function or Data Protection Officer, where applicable | Assessment of privacy implications and relevant notification obligations. |
| Legal counsel | Contractual, legal, regulatory, and liability assessment. |
| Supplier manager | Coordination with the provider and enforcement of contractual commitments. |
| Provider security team | Investigation and containment within the provider's control. |
| Business continuity team | Activation of continuity and recovery arrangements. |
| Executive risk owner | Decisions concerning material exposure, suspension, and residual risk. |
| Communications team | Coordinated external or internal communications where required. |

The incident response plan should identify contact routes, escalation thresholds, decision authority, and relevant contractual time limits.

#### 14.6 Provider Incident Notification Requirements

Contracts should establish practical arrangements for supplier incident notification and cooperation.

The organization should evaluate whether the agreement addresses:

- Notification of relevant security incidents.
- The notification trigger and timing.
- Information supplied in the initial notification.
- Updates as the investigation progresses.
- Cooperation with the customer's investigation.
- Access to relevant evidence.
- Identification of affected data and service components.
- Subprocessor involvement.
- Remediation and recovery actions.
- Support for applicable legal or regulatory notifications.
- Post-incident reporting and corrective action.

A clause requiring notification only after the provider completes its investigation may be unsuitable if the customer needs earlier information to assess its own obligations.

The exact contractual requirements should be determined according to the service, risk, and applicable law.

#### 14.7 Recovery and Lessons Learned

Restoring the service should not automatically follow containment.

Before resuming normal operation, the organization should determine whether:

- The root cause has been identified sufficiently to support a safe decision.
- Compromised credentials have been revoked or replaced.
- Relevant permissions have been corrected.
- Affected configurations have been restored.
- Required security tests have passed.
- Data exposure and business impact have been assessed.
- Required approvals have been obtained.
- Monitoring has been strengthened where necessary.
- Remaining risks have been documented and addressed.

The post-incident review should identify both technical failures and governance weaknesses.

For example, an incident involving unauthorized document retrieval may reveal that the underlying problem was not simply a model failure. The organization may have failed to define retrieval authorization requirements, validate connector permissions, or assign ownership for access-control testing.

Corrective actions should therefore address the root cause rather than only the visible symptom.

#### 14.8 Incident Response Exercise

A practical tabletop exercise can evaluate the organization's readiness.

**Scenario:** An employee reports that an AI assistant generated a response containing confidential information from a restricted repository. The provider's logs are not immediately available, and the AI assistant is connected to several enterprise applications.

The exercise should assess:

1. Who owns the incident?
2. How is the affected service identified?
3. Can the organization disable the relevant connector quickly?
4. Can the organization determine which users and records may be affected?
5. What evidence is available without provider cooperation?
6. Which legal and privacy functions must be engaged?
7. What notification commitments apply?
8. How will the organization prevent further exposure?
9. What criteria must be satisfied before restoring the service?
10. Which control or governance gaps must be corrected?

The exercise should record decisions, response times, evidence gaps, unresolved dependencies, and corrective actions.

A tabletop exercise demonstrates procedural readiness, but it does not replace technical testing of actual containment mechanisms.

### Core Principle

**Technical implementation, testing, monitoring, and incident response must operate as one continuous control lifecycle.** Security requirements must be implemented, tested against realistic failure scenarios, monitored during operation, and reassessed when the service or its risk profile changes.

An approved AI SaaS service is not permanently secure simply because it passed an initial review. Its continued use depends on effective identity and access controls, protected data flows, constrained integrations, validated AI behavior, operational visibility, and the ability to contain incidents.

**Key conceptual distinctions:**

- **Configuration versus effectiveness:** A security feature being enabled does not prove that it works as intended.
- **Authentication versus authorization:** Establishing a user's identity does not establish permission to retrieve data or perform an action.
- **Model instruction versus security enforcement:** A prompt telling an AI not to perform an action is not equivalent to an independently enforced authorization control.
- **AI output versus trusted input:** Generated content must not automatically be treated as safe, accurate, or authorized for downstream execution.
- **Testing versus assurance:** A test provides evidence about specified scenarios; it does not prove the absence of every possible vulnerability.
- **Monitoring versus visibility:** Monitoring is only as effective as the events and evidence available to the organization.
- **Incident detection versus containment:** Identifying an incident does not stop it; the organization must be able to restrict access, revoke credentials, disable integrations, or suspend affected workflows.
- **Service restoration versus risk resolution:** Restoring availability does not establish that the original security or governance weakness has been corrected.
- **Provider security versus end-to-end security:** The provider may secure its platform while the customer remains exposed through its own permissions, data sources, integrations, or business processes.

## 15. Implementation Roadmap and Deployment Governance

### 15.1 Purpose of the Implementation Roadmap

A third-party AI SaaS deployment should follow a controlled implementation roadmap that integrates business objectives, cybersecurity requirements, AI governance, privacy obligations, supplier assurance, and operational readiness.

The roadmap must ensure that security and governance requirements are addressed before the organization commits to production use. Security should not be treated as a final validation activity performed immediately before launch.

The implementation roadmap translates the risk assessment and security requirements established during earlier activities into assigned actions, measurable deliverables, approval gates, and operational responsibilities.

The primary objectives are to:

- Establish clear accountability for implementation decisions.
- Translate identified risks into prioritized remediation activities.
- Validate supplier capabilities and contractual commitments.
- Implement technical and organizational security controls.
- Verify AI system behavior and security before production release.
- Establish monitoring, incident response, and ongoing assurance.
- Maintain traceable evidence for audits and management decisions.

### 15.2 Deployment Lifecycle and Security Gates

A practical deployment lifecycle consists of eight phases.

| Phase | Primary Activities | Required Deliverables | Approval Gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Business initiation | Define business purpose, use cases, users, expected benefits, and system boundaries | Business case, AI use-case description, initial system inventory entry | Business sponsor approval |
| 2. Initial classification | Classify data, determine AI risk category, identify affected individuals and business processes | Data classification, preliminary AI risk classification, initial risk screening | Business owner and governance review |
| 3. Supplier assessment | Evaluate supplier security, privacy, AI governance, subcontractors, and contractual protections | Supplier assessment, due diligence evidence, contractual gap analysis | Third-party risk approval |
| 4. Detailed risk assessment | Identify threats, vulnerabilities, regulatory requirements, and residual risks | Risk register, threat model, security requirements, risk treatment plan | Risk owner acceptance or remediation decision |
| 5. Secure implementation | Configure identity, access, integrations, data protection, logging, and AI-specific safeguards | Configuration evidence, integration diagrams, control implementation records | Security architecture and control validation |
| 6. Verification and testing | Conduct security testing, AI-specific testing, privacy validation, and remediation | Test reports, findings, remediation evidence, exception records | Security and business acceptance |
| 7. Production authorization | Confirm operational readiness, incident response, support, monitoring, and unresolved risk disposition | Go-live checklist, operational runbooks, approval record | Authorized production release |
| 8. Continuous assurance | Monitor changes, incidents, vulnerabilities, supplier performance, and AI behavior | KPI/KRI reports, reassessments, incident records, audit evidence | Periodic governance review |

The approval gates must be meaningful. A completed checklist does not automatically mean that a system is safe to deploy. Approval should depend on the evidence, the severity of unresolved risks, and the authority of the individuals making the decision.

### 15.4 Risk-Based Prioritization of Implementation Activities

Not all risks require the same treatment priority.

The organization should prioritize activities based on the potential impact of the risk, the likelihood of exploitation or failure, exposure, existing controls, and the business context.

A practical prioritization model is:

| Priority | Typical Conditions | Expected Response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Potential for severe data exposure, unauthorized high-impact actions, material legal violations, or compromise of a critical business process | Block deployment or suspend the affected capability until the issue is adequately addressed |
| High | Significant weakness in access control, sensitive-data handling, integration security, supplier assurance, or AI output security | Remediate before production unless a formally authorized decision establishes a safe and compliant alternative |
| Medium | Material but bounded weakness with compensating controls or limited exposure | Remediate according to an approved deadline and monitor the interim risk |
| Low | Limited impact, low exposure, or minor control deficiency | Address through routine improvement and documented tracking |

These categories are illustrative. The organization should use its approved risk methodology and consider whether any applicable legal, contractual, or internal requirement prohibits deployment regardless of the numerical risk score.

### 15.5 Change Management and Reassessment Triggers

Approval of an AI SaaS deployment applies to a defined configuration, use case, supplier arrangement, and operating environment. It should not be interpreted as permanent approval for every future change.

Reassessment should be triggered by changes such as:

- Introduction of a new AI use case or business process.
- Expansion to a new user group, business unit, or geographical region.
- Processing of a new category of personal, confidential, or regulated data.
- Changes to the supplier's AI model, model provider, or hosting arrangements.
- Introduction of new subprocessors or material supplier dependencies.
- Changes to model-training, data-retention, or data-use terms.
- New integrations with enterprise applications or external services.
- Enabling autonomous actions, tool calling, or write access.
- Material changes in model behavior, capabilities, or known limitations.
- Security incidents, significant vulnerabilities, or evidence of misuse.
- Changes in applicable law, regulatory interpretation, or contractual obligations.

The reassessment should be proportional to the change. A minor interface update may require limited verification, whereas a change that allows an AI agent to execute financial transactions requires a more substantial review.

**Required evidence:** Maintain a change record that identifies the change, its owner, the risk impact, required control updates, testing results, approvals, and any conditions imposed on continued operation.

## 16. AI SaaS Governance Operating Model and Accountability

### 16.1 Establishing Clear Governance Ownership

Third-party AI SaaS governance requires coordination across multiple functions. The business owner understands the intended use and business consequences, while security, privacy, legal, procurement, and technology teams assess different dimensions of the deployment.

The organization should avoid two opposite governance failures:

1. **Security-only ownership:** Security is expected to approve the deployment without adequate authority over business purpose, data use, legal obligations, or operational consequences.
2. **Business-only ownership:** The business sponsor authorizes the deployment while technical, privacy, regulatory, and supplier risks remain unresolved.

An effective operating model assigns responsibility according to decision rights, expertise, and risk ownership.

### 16.2 Key Stakeholders and Responsibilities

| Stakeholder | Primary Responsibilities | Decisions or Deliverables |
|---|---|---|
| Executive sponsor | Establish strategic objectives, resolve significant conflicts, provide resources | Strategic sponsorship and escalated business decisions |
| Business owner | Define the use case, business requirements, users, impact, and acceptable business outcomes | Use-case approval and business acceptance |
| AI system owner | Maintain system inventory, coordinate lifecycle governance, and ensure approved use | System accountability and lifecycle records |
| Cybersecurity / GRC | Coordinate risk assessments, control mapping, risk-register management, and governance reporting | Risk assessment, treatment tracking, governance recommendations |
| Security architecture | Review architecture, trust boundaries, integrations, identity, and security controls | Architecture assessment and security design decisions |
| AI security specialist | Assess model-specific threats, prompt injection, data leakage, unsafe outputs, and agent capabilities | AI threat model and AI-specific test requirements |
| Data owner | Determine permitted data use, classification, access, retention, and sharing | Data-use approval and classification decisions |
| Data protection officer / privacy team | Assess privacy impacts and applicable data protection requirements | Privacy advice, assessments, and escalation of privacy concerns |
| Legal | Review contractual terms, intellectual property, liability, regulatory exposure, and legal obligations | Legal assessment and contractual advice |
| Procurement / vendor management | Manage supplier onboarding, commercial terms, due diligence, and supplier performance | Supplier records and commercial governance |
| IT / platform engineering | Configure integrations, identity, endpoints, network controls, and system operations | Implementation records and technical configuration |
| SOC / security operations | Monitor security events, investigate suspicious activity, and coordinate detection and response | Security alerts, investigations, and incident records |
| Vulnerability management / penetration testing | Coordinate testing, validate findings, track remediation, and confirm closure | Test reports and remediation evidence |
| Internal audit | Independently assess control design and operating effectiveness | Audit findings and assurance reports |

| Supplier | Provide contracted services, security evidence, incident notifications, and agreed operational support | Supplier assurance artifacts and contractual deliverables |

One individual may perform several roles in a smaller organization. However, combining roles does not eliminate the need for independent review where the risk level, regulation, or internal policy requires it.

- Escalate risks that exceed operational authority.

**Risk and control level**

Typical participants:
- Cybersecurity / GRC.
- Privacy.
- Legal.
- Data governance.
- Third-party risk management.
- Relevant system and business owners.

Responsibilities:
- Review significant risk assessments.
- Validate treatment plans.
- Evaluate supplier assurance gaps.
- Review material changes.
- Challenge proposed risk acceptance.
- Determine whether additional controls or restrictions are required.

**Executive level**

Typical participants:
- Executive sponsor.
- Business risk owner.
- Relevant technology or security executive.
- Other authorized decision-makers.

Responsibilities:
- Decide on material business-risk trade-offs within their authority.
- Resolve significant resource conflicts.

- Decide whether to restrict, delay, suspend, or discontinue a high-risk use case.
- Review aggregate risk exposure and material incidents.

Escalation should be triggered by defined conditions, such as a critical vulnerability, an unacceptable residual risk, a supplier's failure to meet a contractual obligation, a material data incident, or a proposed use outside the approved purpose.

### 16.5 Separation of Duties and Independence

The organization should distinguish between:

- The person implementing a control.
- The person validating whether the control works.
- The person accepting the risk if the control is inadequate.
- The person independently auditing the control.

For example, the engineering team may implement access restrictions, cybersecurity may validate the configuration, the designated risk owner may accept a residual risk within their authority, and internal audit may later assess whether the governance process operated effectively.

This separation reduces conflicts of interest and prevents the project team from being the sole judge of its own security.

## 17. Audit Readiness, Evidence Management, and Control Effectiveness

### 17.1 Audit Readiness Is an Operational Capability

Audit readiness means the organization can demonstrate that relevant controls are appropriately designed, implemented, operated, and reviewed.

It does not mean that the organization has accumulated a large collection of policies and supplier certificates.

A policy may establish the required control, but it does not prove that the control is configured correctly or operated consistently.

For example:

- A policy requiring multifactor authentication does not prove that all privileged AI SaaS accounts enforce it.
- A supplier's ISO 27001 certificate does not prove that every relevant AI service, feature, subprocessor, or contractual obligation is within scope.
- A penetration-testing report does not prove that all identified findings were remediated.
- A privacy assessment does not prove that the deployed system follows the documented retention and deletion rules.
- An AI acceptable-use policy does not prove that users cannot access prohibited functionality.

Audit readiness therefore requires traceability between requirements, risks, controls, implementation evidence, testing results, and accountable owners.

### 17.2 The Control Traceability Chain

Each material risk should have a traceable relationship to its treatment and supporting evidence.

```text
Business Objective
       |
       v
AI Use Case and System Boundary
       |
       v
Applicable Requirement
       |
       v
Risk or Threat Scenario
       |
       v
Security / Governance Control
       |
       v
Control Owner and Implementation
       |
       v
Evidence of Operation
       |
       v
Control Testing and Validation
       |
       v
Finding or Effectiveness Conclusion
       |
       v
Residual Risk and Management Decision
```

This chain enables an auditor or risk reviewer to answer:

1. What business process or AI use case is being protected?
2. What requirement applies?
3. What could go wrong?
4. What control addresses the risk?
5. Who owns the control?
6. What evidence demonstrates implementation and operation?
7. Has the control been tested?
8. What deficiencies remain?
9. Who approved the resulting residual risk, if acceptance was permitted?

### 17.3 Core Evidence Repository

The organization should maintain an evidence repository with controlled access, version history, ownership, retention rules, and clear relationships between artifacts.

| Evidence Category | Examples | What It Demonstrates |
|---|---|---|
| Business and governance | Use-case approval, AI system inventory, business impact assessment | Defined purpose, ownership, and governance scope |
| Architecture | System context diagrams, trust-boundary diagrams, data-flow diagrams | Understanding of system boundaries, integrations, and data movement |
| Risk management | Risk assessment, threat model, risk register, treatment plan | Identification, prioritization, and treatment of risks |
| Supplier assurance | Security questionnaire, audit reports, certification scope, subprocessor list | Supplier control claims and supporting assurance |
| Contractual governance | Data-processing agreement, security schedule, incident clauses, deletion commitments | Documented obligations and remedies |
| Identity and access | Role matrix, access-review records, authentication settings, privileged-access evidence | Access-control design and operation |
| Data protection | Data classification, retention settings, deletion records, encryption configuration | Protection of information throughout its lifecycle |
| AI security | AI threat model, evaluation plan, adversarial test results, model-use restrictions | Evaluation of AI-specific threats and safeguards |
| Vulnerability management | Scan reports, penetration-test reports, findings, remediation tickets | Identification and treatment of technical weaknesses |
| Monitoring and response | Logging configuration, alert rules, incident runbooks, exercise reports | Detection and response capability |
| Change management | Change tickets, impact assessments, approval records, regression tests | Controlled changes and reassessment |
| Operational performance | Service-level reports, incident trends, supplier reviews, control metrics | Ongoing monitoring and service oversight |
| Risk acceptance | Exception records, compensating controls, expiry dates, approval evidence | Authorized and time-bounded risk decisions |

Evidence should be proportionate to the system's risk and complexity. Avoid collecting irrelevant artifacts merely to create the appearance of compliance.

### 17.4 Evidence Quality Requirements

Evidence should be evaluated for:

**Relevance:** Does it address the specific system, service, control, and requirement under review?

**Authenticity:** Can the source and integrity of the evidence be established?

**Currency:** Does it represent the current configuration, supplier arrangement, and operating conditions?

**Completeness:** Does it cover all relevant environments, accounts, integrations, and organizational units?

**Traceability:** Can the evidence be linked to a requirement, risk, control, owner, and test result?

**Reproducibility:** Can a reviewer understand how the conclusion was reached and, where appropriate, repeat the test?

**Independence:** Is the evidence sufficiently independent for the assurance purpose, especially when the control owner is also the person presenting the evidence?

**Scope accuracy:** Does the evidence cover the exact product, service, region, feature, and organizational scope relevant to the deployment?

A supplier report may be genuine and current but still be insufficient if its scope excludes the service the organization uses.

### 17.5 Control Design Versus Operating Effectiveness

These are distinct assessment dimensions.

**Design effectiveness** asks whether a control, if implemented and operated as intended, is capable of addressing the risk.

**Implementation** asks whether the control has actually been deployed in the relevant environment.

**Operating effectiveness** asks whether the control operates consistently and produces the intended result over the period being assessed.

Example: Privileged-access control for an AI SaaS platform.

| Assessment Dimension | Example Test | Possible Result |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Does the access policy define privileged roles, approval requirements, and review frequency? | The policy may be adequate or deficient |
| Implementation | Are the required roles and access restrictions configured in the platform? | Configuration may be present or absent |
| Operating effectiveness | Do sampled access records show that approvals, reviews, and removals occurred as required? | Evidence may demonstrate consistent operation or reveal failures |

A control can be well designed but poorly implemented. It can also be implemented correctly at a point in time but fail to operate consistently.

### 17.6 AI-Specific Audit Considerations

Auditors and assurance teams should consider whether the organization's evidence addresses risks that are particular to AI-enabled services.

Examples include:

- Whether prompts and outputs are retained, logged, or made available to supplier personnel.
- Whether customer inputs may be used to train or improve models.
- Whether the supplier can materially change the underlying model or its behavior.
- Whether the AI system can retrieve sensitive data through connected sources.
- Whether access restrictions are enforced across retrieval-augmented generation pipelines.
- Whether prompt injection and malicious content can influence model behavior.
- Whether tool-calling or agentic capabilities can perform unauthorized actions.
- Whether human oversight is appropriate for consequential decisions.
- Whether output evaluation considers accuracy, harmful behavior, leakage, and misuse.
- Whether AI-specific incidents can be detected and investigated.
- Whether the organization can suspend or disable the service or affected capability.
- Whether the supplier provides adequate notice of material changes.

These checks should be adapted to the actual architecture. A read-only writing assistant does not have the same risk profile as an AI agent with permission to modify customer records, initiate payments, or execute administrative actions.

### 17.7 Supplier Assurance and the Limits of Certifications

Independent certifications and audit reports are useful inputs to supplier risk assessment, but they are not substitutes for a deployment-specific assessment.

The organization should establish:

1. The exact legal entity and service covered by the assurance report.
2. The relevant service scope, locations, and organizational boundaries.
3. The reporting period and any exceptions or qualifications.
4. Whether the relevant AI features and subprocessors are covered.
5. Which controls remain the customer's responsibility.
6. Whether material complementary customer controls are required.
7. Whether identified deficiencies affect the organization's intended use.
8. Whether additional evidence or contractual commitments are necessary.

A supplier's general security certification should never be treated as automatic approval for every product feature or AI use case.

### 17.8 Audit Findings and Remediation Management

Every material finding should have a defined remediation process.

A finding record should include:

- Unique finding identifier.
- Related system, use case, and supplier.
- Description of the control deficiency.
- Evidence supporting the finding.
- Associated risk and potential business impact.
- Severity and remediation priority.
- Assigned owner.
- Corrective action and any compensating controls.
- Target completion date.
- Validation method.
- Closure evidence.
- Residual risk, if any.
- Escalation and risk-acceptance records where applicable.

Findings should not be closed solely because a project team states that remediation is complete. Closure should require appropriate evidence and validation.

Where immediate remediation is not possible, the organization should document the interim controls, exposure, decision authority, review frequency, and expiry conditions.

## 18. Executive Risk Decisions, Metrics, and Continuous Improvement

### 18.1 Translating Technical Findings into Business Decisions

Executives do not need every technical detail of an AI SaaS risk assessment. They do need a clear understanding of the business consequences, available choices, and accountability for the decision.

A useful executive risk summary answers five questions:

1. **What is the business objective?**
2. **What is the most significant risk?**
3. **What could happen if the risk materializes?**
4. **What options are available to reduce or manage the risk?**
5. **What decision is required, from whom, and by when?**

Technical findings should be translated into impacts such as:

- Exposure of confidential or personal information.
- Unauthorized changes to business records.
- Fraud or misuse of automated actions.
- Disruption of critical business operations.
- Regulatory or contractual non-compliance.
- Intellectual property leakage.
- Customer harm or loss of trust.
- Supplier dependency and service continuity risk.
- Increased cost or delayed deployment.
- Inability to investigate or demonstrate accountability after an incident.

The goal is not to simplify risk until it becomes vague. It is to preserve the causal connection between the technical weakness and the business consequence.

### 18.2 Example: Executive Risk Decision

**Scenario:** An AI SaaS assistant summarizes customer-service records and can retrieve information from a CRM platform. Testing identifies a weakness that could expose information from records the user should not be able to access.

**Technical finding:** Retrieval authorization is not consistently enforced across all data-access paths.

**Business consequence:** A user may receive confidential customer information outside their authorized scope. Depending on the information involved, this may result in a privacy incident, contractual breach, regulatory exposure, and loss of customer trust.

**Potential responses:**

| Option | Benefits | Limitations | Decision Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Option A: Delay production deployment | Prevents exposure while the underlying issue is investigated and corrected | Delays business benefits | Appropriate where the exposure is material and cannot be adequately contained |
| Option B: Disable the affected retrieval capability | May permit use of lower-risk functionality | Reduces functionality and may not eliminate every exposure path | Requires validation that the remaining system is safe for its intended use |
| Option C: Restrict the deployment to a controlled pilot | Limits scale and can support further testing | Does not automatically eliminate the underlying vulnerability | Appropriate only if the pilot's controls genuinely contain the risk and applicable requirements permit the arrangement |
| Option D: Proceed with the full deployment | Avoids immediate delivery delay | May expose the organization to an unacceptable risk | Should not proceed merely because the business deadline is important |

**Recommended governance position:** Do not authorize unrestricted production use while the authorization weakness creates an unacceptable exposure. Consider a restricted alternative only after technical validation demonstrates that the remaining permitted functionality is adequately protected and the relevant risk owner has made the required decision.

This is a risk-based recommendation, not a claim that every vulnerability must always result in complete system shutdown. The correct response depends on severity, exploitability, exposure, compensating controls, and applicable obligations.

### 18.3 Risk Acceptance and Exception Governance

Risk acceptance is a formal decision to retain a known risk. It is not the same as remediation, and it should not be used to bypass mandatory requirements.

A defensible risk-acceptance record should contain:

| Field | Required Information |
|---|---|
| Risk identifier | Link to the relevant risk-register entry |
| Risk statement | Cause, event, and potential consequence |
| Affected service | AI SaaS product, features, integrations, and business process |
| Current controls | Existing safeguards and their demonstrated effectiveness |
| Residual risk | Risk remaining after current controls |
| Alternatives considered | Remediation, restriction, transfer where meaningful, or avoidance |
| Justification | Why the risk is being retained rather than eliminated or reduced further |
| Compensating controls | Additional safeguards used to limit exposure |
| Risk owner | Individual authorized to accept the risk |
| Approval authority | Required approval under the organization's risk delegation |
| Expiry or review date | Date or condition that triggers reconsideration |
| Monitoring requirements | Metrics, alerts, or reviews required during the exception |
| Revocation triggers | Events that require the exception to be withdrawn |
| Evidence | Supporting analysis and approval records |

**Risk acceptance must not be used to legitimize prohibited processing, waive non-waivable legal obligations, or bypass mandatory organizational controls.** Where a requirement cannot lawfully or procedurally be waived, the organization must change the design or use case, implement the required control, or discontinue the affected activity.

### 18.4 Key Risk Indicators and Key Performance Indicators

Metrics help management understand whether the AI SaaS deployment remains within its approved risk boundaries.

A useful measurement framework combines:

- **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs):** Signals of increasing or unacceptable exposure.
- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):** Measures of how effectively the governance and control processes operate.
- **Control effectiveness measures:** Evidence that specific safeguards achieve their intended outcomes.

Illustrative metrics:

| Metric | Type | Example Measurement | Management Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical security findings overdue | KRI | Number of critical findings beyond approved remediation deadlines | Escalate unresolved exposure |
| High-risk findings without treatment plans | KRI | Number of high-risk findings lacking an approved treatment plan | Identify governance gaps |
| Unapproved AI SaaS services discovered | KRI | Number of identified services not covered by the approved governance process | Detect shadow AI and unmanaged supplier exposure |
| Sensitive-data exposure events | KRI | Confirmed events involving unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information | Trigger incident response and risk reassessment |
| AI use cases without completed risk assessments | KRI | Number and proportion of in-scope use cases lacking the required assessment | Identify incomplete governance coverage |
| Supplier assessments completed on time | KPI | Percentage of assessments completed by the required date | Evaluate supplier governance performance |
| Security requirements verified before go-live | KPI | Percentage of required controls validated before production release | Assess implementation discipline |
| Vulnerability remediation within SLA | KPI | Percentage of findings remediated within approved time limits | Evaluate remediation performance |
| Privileged access reviews completed | KPI | Percentage of required reviews completed and evidenced | Monitor identity governance |
| Incident response exercises completed | KPI | Number or percentage of planned exercises completed | Assess operational preparedness |
| AI security tests passed | Control measure | Results against defined acceptance criteria | Evaluate the tested behavior and safeguards |
| Evidence completeness | Control measure | Percentage of required evidence items current and traceable | Assess audit readiness |

Targets should be established using the organization's risk appetite, regulatory requirements, service criticality, historical performance, and operational capacity.

A metric is useful only when its definition, data source, owner, reporting frequency, threshold, and required response are clear.

### 18.5 Why Aggregate Scores Can Be Misleading

A dashboard can show an apparently healthy average while concealing a critical unresolved risk.

For example, a deployment might have 95% of its controls implemented but still lack an essential safeguard against unauthorized disclosure of sensitive customer data.

Therefore:

- Do not allow a high aggregate compliance percentage to override a critical failed control.
- Report critical and high-risk exceptions separately from routine findings.
- Distinguish verified controls from controls that are merely documented.
- Show trends, not just current snapshots.
- Identify the age of unresolved findings.
- Report risks by business process, data sensitivity, and AI capability.
- Explain the limitations of metrics that depend on incomplete supplier evidence.
- Track whether risk-treatment actions actually reduce exposure.

The executive dashboard should make significant exceptions visible rather than burying them in averages.

### 18.6 Continuous Monitoring and Reassessment

The organization should establish monitoring across four dimensions.

**Technical monitoring**
- Authentication and access events.
- Privileged activity.
- API and integration activity.
- Security alerts and anomalous data access.
- Vulnerability and configuration changes.
- Relevant AI service events and available model telemetry.

**AI behavior monitoring**
- Changes in output behavior or quality.
- Evidence of prompt injection or attempted instruction manipulation.
- Unauthorized disclosure through outputs.
- Unsafe or policy-violating responses.
- Unexpected tool calls or actions.
- Abuse patterns and suspicious usage.

**Supplier monitoring**
- Security incidents and service disruptions.
- Material changes to model or service capabilities.
- Changes in subprocessors or data locations.
- Contractual and service-level performance.
- New security advisories or material assurance findings.
- Changes in data retention, training, or processing terms.

**Governance monitoring**
- Risk-register updates.
- Outstanding control deficiencies.
- Risk-acceptance expiry dates.
- Completion of periodic assessments.
- Changes to approved use cases.
- Compliance obligations and evidence status.
- Ownership changes and overdue governance actions.

Monitoring must be realistic about the visibility the supplier provides. An organization cannot assume that it has direct access to model internals, infrastructure logs, or detailed telemetry from a third-party SaaS service. Where visibility is limited, contractual commitments, supplier assurance, application-level controls, usage restrictions, and alternative safeguards become more important.

### 18.7 Incident-Driven Reassessment

A security incident or near miss should trigger more than incident closure and restoration of service.

The organization should assess whether the incident reveals a broader weakness in:

- The original threat model.
- The risk assessment.
- Supplier due diligence.
- Contractual obligations.
- Access-control architecture.
- Data protection.
- AI evaluation and testing.
- Monitoring and detection.
- Incident response.
- Change management.
- Governance ownership.

For example, if an AI SaaS assistant exposes sensitive information because retrieval authorization was not correctly enforced, the response should consider whether the same weakness exists in other use cases, environments, integrations, or user groups.

Corrective actions may include additional testing, control redesign, restrictions on functionality, reassessment of other AI services, supplier escalation, or changes to governance standards.

### 18.9 Decommissioning and Exit Governance

The governance lifecycle must include a controlled exit from the AI SaaS service.

An organization may discontinue the service because of supplier failure, unacceptable risk, a business decision, regulatory change, replacement by another platform, or the end of a contract.

The exit plan should address:

- Revocation of user accounts, service accounts, API keys, tokens, and integration credentials.
- Termination of external connectivity and automated workflows.
- Export of required business records and audit evidence.
- Return or deletion of organizational data according to contractual and legal requirements.
- Verification of deletion where contractually available and appropriate.
- Treatment of backups, retained logs, and supplier-side copies.
- Preservation of records that must be retained for legal, regulatory, or operational reasons.
- Transfer of business processes and responsibilities to an alternative service.
- Review of dependencies on proprietary prompts, workflows, agents, and data formats.
- Closure of supplier access and third-party integrations.
- Completion of contractual and financial obligations.
- Final risk assessment and closure of the AI system inventory record.

Deletion commitments must be realistic about backup retention, legal holds, and other documented exceptions. The organization should obtain evidence of exit completion to the extent supported by the contract and service capabilities.

A service should not be considered fully decommissioned simply because users can no longer log in. Residual credentials, integrations, stored information, automated actions, and supplier access may remain.

## Core Principle

Third-party AI SaaS governance is a continuous lifecycle discipline, not a one-time security assessment or procurement approval.

A defensible deployment requires the organization to demonstrate that the AI use case is understood, the supplier has been assessed, the risks have been identified and treated, the required controls have been validated, and the remaining risks have been handled by authorized decision-makers. Once the service enters production, monitoring, supplier oversight, incident response, reassessment, and controlled decommissioning must sustain that assurance.

The governing objective is to ensure that **the organization can explain, evidence, and defend why the AI SaaS service is permitted to operate, what safeguards constrain it, who owns the remaining risk, and what conditions would require the organization to change or stop its use.**

### Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | What It Means | What It Does Not Mean |
|---|---|---|
| Deployment approval | Authorization to operate a defined service and use case under specified conditions | Permanent approval for every future feature or configuration |
| Risk acceptance | A formally authorized decision to retain a known residual risk where acceptance is permitted | Proof that the risk has been eliminated or that the control is effective |
| Supplier assurance | Evidence supporting an assessment of the supplier's relevant security and governance capabilities | Automatic assurance that the customer's deployment is secure |
| Audit readiness | Ability to demonstrate control design, implementation, operation, and governance decisions | Merely possessing policies, certificates, and completed questionnaires |
| Control effectiveness | Evidence that a safeguard addresses the risk it was designed to manage | Proof that the entire AI system is secure |
| Continuous monitoring | Ongoing observation of relevant technical, AI, supplier, and governance conditions | A guarantee that every incident or harmful output will be detected |
| AI governance | Accountability and oversight across the AI system lifecycle | A responsibility that belongs exclusively to the cybersecurity team |
| Continuous improvement | Updating controls and decisions based on evidence, changes, and experience | Repeating the same assessment without changing the underlying safeguards |
