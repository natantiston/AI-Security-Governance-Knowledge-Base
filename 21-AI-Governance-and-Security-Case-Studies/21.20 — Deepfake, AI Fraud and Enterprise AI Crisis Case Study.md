# 21.20 — Deepfake, AI Fraud and Enterprise AI Crisis Case Study
## Part 1: Business Context, Enterprise Architecture, Attack Scenarios, and Initial Risk Assessment

## 1. Business Context and Case Study Overview

### 1.1 Executive Summary

Deepfakes, AI-enabled fraud, and synthetic identity attacks represent a growing enterprise security challenge because artificial intelligence can be used to impersonate executives, manipulate business communications, fabricate evidence, and influence employees into authorizing fraudulent transactions.

Unlike conventional phishing, AI-enabled fraud can exploit trust in audio, video, written communication, and apparently legitimate business workflows. Attackers may imitate a chief executive's voice, generate a convincing video call, impersonate a supplier, or combine compromised email accounts with synthetic media to create a plausible but fraudulent business request.

The principal enterprise risk is not simply that an employee may believe a fake recording. It is that the organization may execute a consequential business action based on an unverified identity or manipulated instruction.

Examples include:

- A finance employee authorizing a fraudulent bank transfer after receiving a deepfake video call from an executive.
- A procurement team changing a supplier's bank details after receiving an AI-generated voice message.
- An attacker impersonating a customer to bypass identity verification.
- A fraudster creating synthetic identities to open accounts or obtain services.
- An employee disclosing confidential information to an impersonator posing as an authorized executive.
- A criminal using AI-generated documents, invoices, or identity materials to support a fraudulent transaction.
- An attacker combining business email compromise with synthetic audio to bypass established approval procedures.

From a Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) perspective, these scenarios involve several interconnected control domains:

1. Identity and access management.
2. Fraud prevention and transaction authorization.
3. Business process controls.
4. Cybersecurity and incident response.
5. Third-party risk management.
6. Privacy and personal-data protection.
7. Financial governance and segregation of duties.
8. AI governance and acceptable-use policies.
9. Business continuity and crisis management.
10. Legal, regulatory, and reputational risk management.

**Core risk principle:** A convincing voice, video, document, or message is not sufficient evidence of identity or authorization.

Organizations must verify the identity of the requester and independently validate the requested action through trusted channels and established business controls.

### 1.2 Enterprise Case Study: The Deepfake Executive Fraud Incident

Consider a multinational organization operating across Europe, the Middle East, and Asia.

The organization has:

- A Chief Executive Officer (CEO) and executive leadership team.
- A finance department responsible for payments and treasury operations.
- A procurement department managing supplier relationships.
- A customer relationship management (CRM) platform.
- An enterprise resource planning (ERP) platform.
- Corporate email and collaboration systems.
- Video conferencing and enterprise telephony.
- Identity verification services.
- A Security Operations Center (SOC).
- An established cybersecurity GRC function.

The company conducts high-value international payments and regularly coordinates with suppliers, subsidiaries, banks, and external service providers.

An attacker studies publicly available information about the CEO, finance leadership, and an upcoming acquisition. The attacker uses this information to construct a believable scenario involving an urgent confidential payment.

The attack unfolds as follows:

1. The attacker obtains information about the organization's leadership and payment processes.
2. The attacker generates synthetic audio resembling the CEO's voice.
3. The attacker compromises or impersonates a trusted business communication channel.
4. A finance employee receives an urgent request to process a confidential transfer.
5. The attacker uses the synthetic voice during a call to reinforce the request's legitimacy.
6. The employee believes the request is authentic and attempts to execute the payment.
7. The normal verification process is bypassed or inadequately enforced.
8. The payment is approved and transmitted to an attacker-controlled account.
9. The fraud is discovered when treasury staff or the legitimate executive questions the transaction.
10. The organization initiates incident response, attempts payment recovery, investigates the attack, and assesses legal and regulatory implications.

The incident demonstrates that the deepfake itself is only one component of the attack.

The fraud succeeds because the organization fails to maintain reliable identity verification and transaction authorization at the point where a business decision becomes a financial action.

### 1.3 Business Impact Assessment

The impact of an AI-enabled fraud incident extends beyond direct financial loss.

| Impact category | Potential consequence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Direct monetary loss | Fraudulent transfer to an attacker-controlled account |
| Operational | Business interruption | Payment operations suspended while transactions are investigated |
| Confidentiality | Unauthorized information disclosure | Executive shares confidential acquisition information with an impersonator |
| Integrity | Manipulation of business records | Fraudulent supplier bank details entered into the ERP system |
| Legal | Disputes and potential liability | Contractual or litigation consequences arising from an unauthorized transaction |
| Regulatory | Potential reporting or compliance obligations | Assessment of applicable financial, privacy, or sector-specific requirements |
| Reputational | Loss of stakeholder confidence | Customers or investors question the organization's fraud controls |
| Third-party | Supplier or banking relationship disruption | Legitimate payments are delayed while accounts are verified |
| Human | Employee stress and disciplinary consequences | Employee becomes the subject of an internal investigation after being deceived |
| Strategic | Reduced confidence in digital business processes | Management restricts legitimate remote approvals or digital services |

The organization must assess each impact according to the actual incident and applicable obligations.

For example, a fraudulent payment does not automatically constitute a personal-data breach. However, if the same attack exposes personal information, the organization must separately assess the relevant privacy implications.

### 1.4 Why Traditional Controls May Fail

Traditional cybersecurity and financial controls are still essential, but some organizations rely too heavily on signals that generative AI can reproduce.

Examples include:

- Recognizing a familiar executive's voice.
- Recognizing a person's face during a video call.
- Trusting the display name or profile picture associated with a message.
- Assuming an urgent request is legitimate because it references confidential business information.
- Accepting a document because it appears professionally formatted.
- Treating successful email authentication as proof that the sender is the person they claim to be.
- Assuming that a meeting participant is genuine because their video resembles the executive.

These signals may support an identity assessment, but they are not necessarily independent or authoritative proof.

An attacker can combine several manipulated signals into one convincing impersonation.

For example, a fraudulent payment request may include a spoofed email display name, an AI-generated voice recording, and a realistic-looking invoice. These artifacts may all derive from the same attacker-controlled process. Their apparent consistency does not establish independent authenticity.

**GRC implication:** Control design should focus on independent verification, authorization, and transaction integrity rather than on the apparent realism of the communication.

## 2. Understanding Deepfakes, AI Fraud, and Synthetic Identity Attacks

### 2.1 What Is a Deepfake?

A deepfake is synthetic or manipulated media generated or modified using AI techniques to imitate or alter a person's appearance, voice, or behavior.

Deepfakes can involve:

- Synthetic speech.
- Voice cloning.
- Face replacement.
- Facial reenactment.
- AI-generated video.
- Manipulated audiovisual recordings.
- Real-time synthetic representations of a person.

The technology can have legitimate applications, including accessibility, localization, entertainment, training, and authorized digital content production.

The security risk arises when synthetic media is used to deceive people, bypass controls, impersonate an identity, or manipulate a business decision.

A deepfake does not need to be visually or acoustically perfect to be effective. It only needs to be convincing enough within the context in which it is used.

For example, a short voice message played over a poor-quality telephone connection may be difficult for a recipient to authenticate reliably.

### 2.2 Major Categories of AI-Enabled Fraud

Organizations should distinguish between different attack types because their prevention and detection requirements differ.

| Attack category | Description | Enterprise example | Primary control domain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Voice cloning | Synthetic speech imitates a real person's voice | Fake executive requests an urgent transfer | Transaction authorization |
| Video deepfake | Synthetic or manipulated video imitates a person | Fraudulent video meeting with finance staff | Identity verification |
| Business email compromise | Attacker manipulates or compromises business email communications | Fraudulent supplier payment instructions | Email security and payment controls |
| Synthetic identity fraud | Real and fabricated identity attributes are combined to create an apparently plausible identity | Fraudulent customer account creation | Identity proofing and fraud detection |
| Document forgery | AI creates or modifies documents to support deception | Fake invoices, contracts, or identity documents | Document verification and business validation |
| Executive impersonation | Attacker pretends to be a senior leader | Request for confidential data or an exceptional payment | Identity and approval governance |
| Supplier impersonation | Attacker poses as a legitimate supplier | Bank-account change request | Supplier master-data controls |
| Customer impersonation | Attacker pretends to be a customer | Unauthorized account recovery or transaction | Customer authentication |
| AI-assisted phishing | AI creates targeted and convincing fraudulent communications | Personalized email referencing real projects | Email security and user verification |
| Automated social engineering | AI supports large-scale or adaptive manipulation | Automated impersonation attempts against employees | Fraud monitoring and incident response |

These categories can overlap.

A single incident may combine compromised credentials, synthetic voice, fraudulent documentation, and unauthorized changes to supplier records.

The organization should therefore assess the complete attack path rather than classify the incident according to the first suspicious artifact observed.

### 2.3 Deepfake Fraud Versus Conventional Phishing

Conventional phishing often relies on fraudulent messages, malicious links, credential theft, or deceptive attachments.

Deepfake-enabled fraud may use similar techniques but adds synthetic representations of trusted people.

| Dimension | Conventional phishing | Deepfake-enabled fraud |
|---|---|---|
| Primary deception | Fraudulent message or website | Synthetic or manipulated identity signals, potentially combined with fraudulent messages |
| Common trust mechanism | Brand familiarity, urgency, or apparent legitimacy | Voice, facial appearance, familiarity, urgency, and apparent authority |
| Potential target | Credentials, devices, or business processes | Credentials, financial transactions, sensitive information, or business decisions |
| Verification weakness | Users trust a link or message without checking its authenticity | Users treat voice or video resemblance as proof of identity |
| Typical controls | Email security, authentication, awareness, web filtering | Existing controls plus independent identity verification and stronger transaction authorization |
| Detection challenge | Malicious infrastructure, message patterns, links, and behavior | Synthetic media may be combined with otherwise legitimate communication channels |
| Business-process implication | A user may disclose credentials or execute a malicious action | A user may authorize a high-impact transaction based on impersonation |

Neither category is inherently more successful in every environment.

The effectiveness of an attack depends on the target, business process, available information, and effectiveness of existing controls.

### 2.4 Synthetic Identity Fraud

Synthetic identity fraud involves creating an identity using a combination of fabricated, manipulated, or legitimately sourced attributes.

Depending on the use case, an attacker may combine:

- A fabricated name.
- A real person's identifying information.
- A manipulated identity document.
- A synthetic face or voice.
- A newly created email address.
- A compromised phone number.
- Fabricated employment or business records.

Synthetic identity fraud differs from straightforward impersonation because the identity may not correspond to one specific victim.

For example, an attacker may create a fraudulent customer profile using a combination of real and fabricated attributes. The attacker may then attempt to pass onboarding checks, obtain credit, access services, or establish a seemingly legitimate transaction history.

**Security implications:**

- Identity attributes must be evaluated for consistency and provenance.
- Identity verification should not rely on a single document or biometric signal.
- High-risk onboarding should include appropriate verification and fraud controls.
- Account creation and subsequent account activity should be monitored for suspicious patterns.
- Identity verification controls must be tested against realistic attack scenarios.
- Exception processes must not allow verification requirements to be bypassed without authorization.

Organizations should balance fraud prevention with privacy, accessibility, and fair treatment. Biometric or identity-verification technologies should be selected and governed according to the actual risk and applicable legal requirements.

### 3.4 Trust Boundaries

A trust boundary represents a point where information, identity, or control passes between entities with different security assumptions.

For this case study, important boundaries include:

1. External communications entering the enterprise.
2. Employees receiving requests from purported executives.
3. Identity verification systems establishing confidence in a requester.
4. Business workflows converting requests into approvals.
5. Finance systems initiating transactions.
6. ERP integrations updating supplier or payment information.
7. Payment systems transmitting funds to external institutions.
8. Third-party providers supplying identity-verification or fraud-detection services.

Security controls must ensure that a request does not gain authority merely by crossing one of these boundaries.

For example, successful authentication to a corporate email account does not automatically authorize the account holder to approve a payment. Likewise, recognition of a familiar voice does not establish that the person has the authority to change supplier bank details.

**Architectural principle:** Authentication establishes identity confidence; authorization determines permitted actions; business validation confirms that the requested action is legitimate.

These are related but distinct control functions.

The most important control opportunities are steps 5–8.

If identity verification is independent, authorization is enforced, and financial approval is properly segregated, the attacker may be unable to convert the deception into a completed payment.

### 4.2 Attack Scenario A: Deepfake CEO Requests an Urgent Transfer

**Scenario**

An attacker creates synthetic audio resembling the CEO and contacts a finance employee. The request references a confidential acquisition and demands immediate payment to a new beneficiary.

**Attack path**

1. Public information reveals the CEO's identity, communication style, and business activities.
2. The attacker prepares a believable payment scenario.
3. Synthetic audio reinforces the apparent identity.
4. The employee is pressured to act quickly and maintain confidentiality.
5. The employee bypasses an independent callback or secondary approval.
6. The payment is initiated using an otherwise legitimate finance system.

**Potential control failures**

- No independent verification for exceptional payment requests.
- Excessive reliance on voice recognition.
- Weak separation between payment initiation and approval.
- Ability to create or modify beneficiaries without additional verification.
- Inadequate monitoring of unusual payment patterns.
- Pressure to bypass standard procedures when senior executives are involved.

**Potential impact**

- Direct financial loss.
- Delayed detection.
- Recovery and legal costs.
- Reputational damage.
- Disruption to treasury operations.

**Key preventive control:** Require independent verification and enforce the established payment-authorization workflow regardless of the apparent identity or seniority of the requester.

### 4.3 Attack Scenario B: Supplier Bank-Account Change

**Scenario**

An attacker impersonates a supplier and requests that future payments be redirected to a different bank account. The message includes realistic supplier details and supporting documents.

**Attack path**

1. The attacker gathers information about the supplier relationship.
2. A fraudulent bank-account change request is sent to procurement or accounts payable.
3. The request appears consistent with the supplier's normal business activities.
4. An employee updates the supplier master-data record.
5. A legitimate invoice is paid to the fraudulent beneficiary.
6. The legitimate supplier reports that payment has not been received.

**Potential control failures**

- Supplier changes accepted through email alone.
- No verification using a previously established trusted contact.
- One person can change beneficiary details and approve payments.
- Inadequate audit trails for supplier master-data modifications.
- No additional review for recently changed payment details.

**Key preventive control:** Independently validate changes to supplier payment details through a trusted, previously verified channel and require controlled approval of master-data changes.

### 4.4 Attack Scenario C: Deepfake Video Meeting

**Scenario**

Employees join a video conference in which a purported senior executive requests sensitive project information or directs staff to perform an exceptional action.

The apparent participant uses synthetic video, synthetic audio, or a compromised account.

**Potential control failures**

- Treating visual resemblance as conclusive proof of identity.
- Allowing confidential requests to bypass normal approval channels.
- Sharing sensitive information during unverified meetings.
- Accepting meeting invitations without validating the business context.
- Relying on one communication channel for both the request and its verification.

**Key preventive control:** Require appropriate identity verification and independent authorization before disclosing restricted information or executing sensitive actions.

The organization should not assume that video verification technology alone can eliminate this risk. Detection tools may produce false positives and false negatives, and attackers may adapt their methods.

### 4.5 Attack Scenario D: Synthetic Customer Identity

**Scenario**

A fraudster creates an apparently plausible customer identity using fabricated attributes and manipulated identity materials. The attacker attempts to open an account, obtain a service, or access a customer account.

**Potential control failures**

- Identity verification relies on one document or one biometric signal.
- Account creation is not monitored for suspicious patterns.
- Identity attributes are not validated against authoritative sources where appropriate.
- Manual exceptions bypass normal verification requirements.
- Account recovery processes rely on information available to an attacker.

**Key preventive control:** Apply risk-based identity proofing, independent validation of relevant attributes, secure account recovery, and monitoring of suspicious onboarding and account activity.

### 4.6 Attack Scenario E: Executive Impersonation and Confidential Information Disclosure

**Scenario**

An employee receives an urgent request from a purported executive seeking confidential merger information, employee records, customer data, or security architecture details.

The request may be supported by a convincing voice message or video call.

**Potential control failures**

- Confidentiality rules are overridden by apparent executive authority.
- Information access is based on organizational status rather than actual authorization.
- Sensitive information can be exported without additional controls.
- Employees are not provided with a clear escalation process for suspicious requests.

**Key preventive control:** Enforce data access based on approved permissions and business need, regardless of who appears to have requested the information.

### 4.7 Attack Scenario F: Combined Business Email Compromise and Deepfake

**Scenario**

An attacker compromises a real business email account and uses AI-generated voice or video to reinforce a fraudulent request.

The combination may be particularly convincing because part of the communication genuinely originates from an authorized account.

**Potential control failures**

- The organization assumes that a message from a valid account must be legitimate.
- Multifactor authentication protects account access but not every subsequent business decision.
- Unusual transactions are not checked against established business context.
- Payment controls rely on email approval rather than independent authorization.

**Key preventive control:** Combine account-security controls with transaction-specific verification, segregation of duties, and anomaly monitoring.

A valid account is not proof that every action performed through it is authorized. Accounts can be compromised, misused, or operated under coercion.

## 5. Initial Risk Assessment and Governance Decisions

### 5.1 Establishing the Risk Statement

AI-enabled fraud risks should be documented using a cause-event-consequence structure.

A practical risk statement identifies:

- **Cause:** A weakness, threat condition, or control gap.
- **Event:** The fraudulent activity that could occur.
- **Consequence:** The resulting business harm.

For example:

**Cause:** The organization relies on voice recognition and email correspondence to verify exceptional executive payment requests.

**Event:** An attacker uses synthetic audio and fraudulent communications to impersonate an executive and induce a finance employee to initiate an unauthorized payment.

**Consequence:** The organization suffers financial loss, operational disruption, investigation costs, and potential legal or reputational consequences.

This formulation is more useful than simply recording "deepfake risk" because it identifies the control weaknesses that need to be assessed.

### 5.3 Inherent Risk Versus Residual Risk

A deepfake-enabled fraud scenario may initially receive a high inherent risk rating because the potential financial and reputational impact is significant.

However, the residual risk depends on the effectiveness of the organization's controls.

For example:

| Control condition | Expected risk effect |
|---|---|
| Voice or video resemblance is the primary identity check | Limited risk reduction |
| Independent callback is required for exceptional requests | Reduces reliance on manipulated communication |
| Beneficiary changes require independent verification | Reduces supplier-payment fraud exposure |
| Payment initiation and approval are segregated | Limits the ability of one person or compromised account to execute a transaction |
| High-risk transactions trigger additional review | Reduces exposure to abnormal or exceptional requests |
| Logs support reconstruction of payment and identity events | Improves detection and investigation capability |
| Fraud and cybersecurity teams coordinate response | Improves containment and recovery |

These controls reduce risk only when properly implemented, tested, and consistently followed.

A documented procedure does not establish effectiveness if employees can bypass it without detection or approval.

### 5.4 Initial Governance Decisions

The organization should make several explicit decisions before considering the case study adequately governed.

**Decision 1: Which transactions require independent verification?**

Define thresholds and circumstances that require additional verification, including new beneficiaries, changed supplier bank details, unusual payment amounts, urgent requests, and exceptions to normal processes.

**Decision 2: Which identity signals are acceptable?**

Document that voice resemblance, video appearance, email display names, and synthetic-media detection tools are not independently sufficient to authorize high-impact transactions.

**Decision 3: Who can approve exceptions?**

Identify the authority permitted to approve exceptional transactions and ensure that approval requirements cannot be bypassed solely because a request appears to originate from a senior executive.

**Decision 4: How are supplier changes controlled?**

Define who may request, validate, enter, approve, and audit changes to supplier payment details.

**Decision 5: How are AI-enabled fraud incidents classified?**

Establish criteria for escalation to fraud operations, the SOC, finance leadership, legal, privacy, and executive management.

**Decision 6: What evidence must be retained?**

Define the records necessary to investigate suspicious requests, including relevant communication metadata, identity verification outcomes, payment approvals, supplier changes, and system audit logs.

**Decision 7: Who owns the residual risk?**

Assign an accountable business risk owner and define when the risk must be escalated because it exceeds the organization's approved risk appetite.

**Decision 8: How is effectiveness demonstrated?**

Require testing of identity verification, transaction approval, supplier-change controls, monitoring, and incident response rather than relying exclusively on policies and employee awareness.

### 5.5 Initial GRC Deliverables

The initial assessment should produce the following artifacts:

| Deliverable | Purpose |
|---|---|
| AI-enabled fraud risk assessment | Identifies threats, vulnerabilities, business impact, and initial risk ratings |
| Business process and data-flow diagrams | Shows how fraudulent requests could become transactions or disclosures |
| Asset and dependency inventory | Identifies systems, identities, and processes requiring protection |
| Control gap assessment | Compares current safeguards with required security outcomes |
| Risk register | Records risks, ratings, owners, treatment plans, and residual risk |
| Responsibility matrix | Establishes accountability across business, finance, security, and technology |

| Remediation roadmap | Assigns prioritized actions, owners, and target dates |

These deliverables establish the basis for a structured enterprise assessment.

They should be based on evidence gathered from the actual environment, including process walkthroughs, architecture reviews, configuration evidence, interviews, control testing, and relevant incident history.

## Core Principle

Deepfake and AI-enabled fraud exploit the gap between **believing that a person is genuine** and **proving that a request is authorized**.

The organization must not allow synthetic voice, video, documents, email correspondence, or apparent executive authority to bypass established business controls.

The strongest initial defense is to design business processes in which identity verification, transaction authorization, segregation of duties, and independent validation remain effective even when the communication itself is convincing but fraudulent.

**A trusted-looking request must never become an authorized transaction without passing the organization's required verification and approval controls.**

## Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning | Security implication |
|---|---|---|
| Deepfake vs. AI-enabled fraud | A deepfake is synthetic or manipulated media; AI-enabled fraud is the broader misuse of AI to facilitate deception or financial crime | Assess the entire fraud scenario, not just the media artifact |
| Identity recognition vs. identity verification | Recognition is a judgment based on familiar characteristics; verification uses an established process to establish identity confidence | Do not rely on voice or video resemblance alone |
| Authentication vs. transaction authorization | Authentication establishes identity confidence; authorization determines whether a specific action is permitted | A verified identity does not automatically authorize every transaction |
| Technical email validity vs. business legitimacy | A message may pass technical email checks while containing a fraudulent request | Validate the requested business action independently |
| Fraud prevention vs. fraud detection | Prevention blocks or constrains fraudulent actions; detection identifies suspicious activity | Use both preventive and detective controls |
| Synthetic identity vs. impersonation | Synthetic identity fraud constructs an apparently plausible identity; impersonation misrepresents an existing identity | Apply appropriate identity-proofing and account-security controls |
| Security awareness vs. enforced controls | Awareness influences employee behavior; enforced controls constrain what actions can be completed | Do not rely on employee judgment as the sole defense |
| Independent verification vs. repeated confirmation | Independent verification uses a separate trusted mechanism; repeated confirmation may rely on the same compromised channel | Avoid circular verification through attacker-controlled communications |
| Inherent risk vs. residual risk | Inherent risk is assessed before applicable controls; residual risk remains after effective controls | Base residual ratings on verified control effectiveness |
| Fraud incident vs. personal-data breach | A fraud incident concerns deceptive or unauthorized activity; a personal-data breach is a distinct classification under applicable data-protection rules | Assess financial, security, privacy, and regulatory consequences separately |

## 6. Threat Modeling for Deepfake and AI-Enabled Enterprise Fraud

### 6.1 Purpose of Threat Modeling

Threat modeling identifies how attackers can exploit AI-generated content, weaknesses in identity verification, gaps in business processes, and excessive trust in digital communications to cause enterprise harm.

For deepfake and AI-enabled fraud, the primary security question is not simply whether an organization can detect synthetic media. It is whether an attacker can persuade an authorized employee or automated system to perform an unauthorized action.

An attacker may not need to compromise an endpoint, bypass a firewall, or exploit a software vulnerability. Instead, the attacker may exploit trust in a senior executive, a supplier, a customer, or a trusted digital identity.

**Threat-modeling objective:** Identify the people, processes, technologies, identities, and business decisions that could be manipulated through AI-generated deception, then implement controls that prevent an unverified instruction from producing an unauthorized business outcome.

### 6.2 Threat Actors and Their Motivations

| Threat actor | Primary motivation | Typical target | Potential enterprise impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financially motivated cybercriminals | Direct financial gain | Finance teams, treasury, procurement | Fraudulent transfers and payment diversion |
| Organized fraud groups | Scalable financial exploitation | Customer onboarding, accounts payable, customer support | Synthetic identities, account takeover, and fraudulent transactions |
| Business email compromise operators | Payment manipulation and credential theft | Executives, finance, suppliers | Unauthorized payments and account compromise |
| Malicious insiders | Financial gain, retaliation, or sabotage | Sensitive workflows and privileged systems | Abuse of legitimate access and concealment of fraud |
| Competitors or commercial adversaries | Reputational or commercial advantage | Executives, public relations, strategic communications | Reputational damage and loss of business |
| State-linked or advanced threat actors | Espionage, disruption, or strategic influence | Senior leadership, critical operations | Intelligence compromise and operational disruption |
| Opportunistic impersonators | Fraud, harassment, or unauthorized influence | Employees, customers, public audiences | Social engineering, misinformation, and reputational harm |

The organization should avoid assuming that every deepfake incident is technically sophisticated. A low-cost synthetic voice combined with weak payment approval procedures can be more effective than a highly convincing video against a company with poor identity verification.

### 6.3 Threat Modeling Using STRIDE

The STRIDE model can help classify threats affecting the enterprise architecture. It was originally developed for software threat modeling, so its categories should be adapted carefully when analyzing human deception and business-process abuse.

| STRIDE category | Application to AI-enabled fraud | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spoofing | Impersonating a legitimate person, service, or identity | Synthetic executive voice used during a payment request |
| Tampering | Altering business information or transaction details | Supplier bank account details modified before payment |
| Repudiation | Disputing responsibility or creating uncertainty about an action | A fraudulent instruction is attributed to an executive who denies issuing it |
| Information disclosure | Obtaining confidential information through deception | An employee reveals customer records during a fabricated executive call |
| Denial of service | Disrupting legitimate operations or overwhelming response capacity | A deepfake incident triggers an unnecessary suspension of critical payments |
| Elevation of privilege | Using deception to obtain access or approvals beyond an attacker's authority | An impersonator persuades an administrator to reset an executive's credentials |

STRIDE is useful for categorizing threats, but it does not replace business impact analysis, fraud risk assessment, privacy assessment, or regulatory analysis.

### 6.4 Attack Trees for Enterprise Fraud

An attack tree starts with an attacker objective and decomposes it into possible routes to achieve that objective.

**Primary objective: Execute an unauthorized corporate payment.**

```text
ROOT: Unauthorized corporate payment
|
+-- A. Manipulate an authorized employee
|   |
|   +-- Impersonate the CEO using synthetic voice
|   +-- Send a fabricated urgent payment instruction
|   +-- Exploit a trusted supplier relationship
|   +-- Pressure the employee to bypass normal procedures
|
+-- B. Manipulate supplier payment information
|   |
|   +-- Compromise a supplier email account
|   +-- Submit fraudulent bank account change details
|   +-- Impersonate a supplier representative
|   +-- Exploit weak beneficiary verification
|
+-- C. Abuse an existing identity or workflow
|   |
|   +-- Compromise an authorized finance account
|   +-- Exploit excessive transaction privileges
|   +-- Abuse weak approval segregation
|   +-- Exploit inadequate transaction monitoring
|
+-- D. Exploit an automated process
    |
    +-- Submit a fraudulent instruction through an integrated channel
    +-- Exploit inadequate identity validation
    +-- Abuse an overprivileged AI agent or automation
    +-- Trigger a payment without independent authorization
```

The attack tree highlights an important governance issue: synthetic media is only one possible route to fraud. A comprehensive response must address the entire payment authorization process, including identity verification, beneficiary management, approval segregation, and transaction monitoring.

### 6.5 Trust Boundaries and Security Assumptions

Threat modeling should explicitly identify where trust changes between systems or participants.

| Trust boundary | Security assumption that may fail | Required governance question |
|---|---|---|
| External caller to employee | A familiar voice proves the caller's identity | What independent authentication is required? |
| Supplier to procurement | A message from a known email address is legitimate | How are supplier identity and payment changes verified? |
| Employee to finance platform | An authenticated employee request is authorized | Are transaction permissions and approval rules enforced? |
| AI assistant to business application | AI-generated output is trustworthy | What actions can the AI initiate, and what independent checks apply? |
| Customer to onboarding platform | Submitted identity documents are genuine | How is identity assurance established beyond document appearance? |
| Executive communication to public audience | Video and audio establish authenticity | What authoritative channel verifies sensitive announcements? |
| Enterprise to external AI provider | The provider's controls are sufficient for enterprise risk | What contractual, technical, privacy, and operational assurances are required? |

**Governance principle:** A trust boundary must be protected by an appropriate control, not by an assumption that the content crossing it is genuine.

## 7. Preventive Control Architecture

### 7.1 Control Design Philosophy

Preventive controls should be designed around the business action that could cause harm.

For example, an enterprise may deploy a deepfake detection system that identifies suspicious audio with reasonable accuracy. However, if the finance department can still release a high-value payment based on a single telephone instruction, the core business risk remains.

A stronger architecture combines:

1. Identity verification.
2. Independent authorization.
3. Segregation of duties.
4. Transaction limits and workflow enforcement.
5. Monitoring and evidence retention.
6. Incident response and recovery.

The objective is to ensure that **deceptive content alone cannot authorize a consequential business action**.

### 7.2 Identity Verification and Authentication Controls

Identity verification and authentication address different questions.

- **Identity verification:** Establishes who a person or organization is, using appropriate evidence and validation procedures.
- **Authentication:** Establishes whether a person or system presenting an identity can demonstrate the required authenticators.
- **Authorization:** Determines which actions the authenticated identity is permitted to perform.
- **Transaction verification:** Confirms that a specific high-impact action, such as a payment or beneficiary change, is legitimate and properly approved.

These controls should not be treated as interchangeable.

A successfully authenticated employee may still be manipulated into approving a fraudulent transaction. Likewise, a convincing synthetic voice does not establish that the caller has authority to issue a payment instruction.

#### Recommended control measures

| Control | Implementation approach | Risk addressed |
|---|---|---|
| Phishing-resistant MFA | Use suitable phishing-resistant authenticators for privileged and high-risk access | Credential theft and account takeover |
| Independent callback verification | Call a previously verified telephone number, not a number supplied in the suspicious message | Impersonation and fraudulent instructions |
| Approved communication channels | Require sensitive requests to be confirmed through designated corporate workflows | Channel manipulation |
| Strong account recovery | Apply robust identity verification before resetting credentials or changing authentication factors | Account recovery abuse |
| Privileged access management | Restrict administrative privileges and monitor privileged activity | Unauthorized access and privilege abuse |
| Transaction-specific authorization | Require approval of the actual transaction details, not merely confirmation of a caller's identity | Payment and business-process fraud |
| Identity lifecycle management | Promptly remove or modify access when employees, contractors, or suppliers change roles | Unauthorized access through stale identities |

**Important distinction:** A callback is effective only when the destination number comes from a trusted, independently maintained source. Calling a number supplied by the suspected impersonator does not provide meaningful independent verification.

### 7.3 Payment and Financial Transaction Controls

Payment controls are among the highest-priority measures because they directly interrupt the conversion of deception into financial loss.

#### A. Dual authorization

Require two authorized individuals to approve transactions that meet defined risk thresholds.

The approval workflow should prevent a single person from initiating, modifying, and releasing the same high-risk payment without appropriate independent review.

#### B. Segregation of duties

Separate responsibilities for:

- Creating a supplier record.
- Modifying beneficiary bank details.
- Approving supplier changes.
- Creating a payment batch.
- Approving payment execution.
- Reconciling completed transactions.

The exact separation should reflect the organization's size, transaction volumes, and risk appetite.

#### C. Independent beneficiary verification

Changes to supplier bank details should trigger a controlled verification process.

A robust process includes:

1. Recording the requested change.
2. Restricting the change from immediate payment use when risk warrants it.
3. Contacting the supplier through an established, independently verified channel.
4. Obtaining approval from an authorized person who did not solely rely on the original request.
5. Retaining evidence of the verification and approval.
6. Monitoring the first payment to the modified beneficiary where appropriate.

#### D. Transaction limits

Apply thresholds based on business risk, payment type, beneficiary status, geography, and other relevant indicators.

Potential controls include:

- Per-transaction limits.
- Daily aggregate limits.
- New-beneficiary restrictions.
- Additional approval for unusual transaction patterns.
- Temporary payment holds when credible fraud indicators are present.

Thresholds must be configured to match actual enterprise risk. A universal monetary limit is not appropriate for every organization.

#### E. Emergency-payment procedures

Urgency should trigger stronger verification, not weaker controls.

Emergency procedures should define:

- Who can request an exception.
- Which independent approvals are required.
- Which controls cannot be bypassed.
- How the exception is documented.
- Who reviews the transaction afterward.
- When the exception expires.

An executive's seniority should not automatically override payment controls.

### 7.4 Controls for AI Assistants and Agentic Systems

An enterprise AI assistant may summarize emails, draft responses, identify suppliers, extract payment details, or interact with financial applications.

If the assistant can initiate or modify business transactions, the threat model must include the possibility that manipulated content influences an automated action.

Consider the following example:

An AI assistant reads a fraudulent email claiming that a supplier has changed its bank account. It extracts the new account details, updates the supplier record, and prepares a payment request.

The AI system may be operating exactly as designed. The security failure is that untrusted content was allowed to influence a sensitive business action without sufficient independent validation.

#### Required controls

| Control domain | Recommended control | Governance objective |
|---|---|---|
| Identity and permissions | Give the AI agent a dedicated identity with narrowly scoped permissions | Prevent excessive access |
| Tool access | Allow only approved tools and explicitly permitted actions | Reduce attack surface |
| Human approval | Require authorized human approval for high-impact or irreversible actions | Prevent autonomous execution of sensitive transactions |
| Data provenance | Record the origin of information used to make consequential decisions | Support verification and investigation |
| Input handling | Treat emails, attachments, documents, and retrieved content as potentially untrusted | Reduce manipulation through external content |
| Workflow enforcement | Enforce authorization rules in the business application, independently of the model | Prevent model output from overriding business controls |
| Output validation | Validate critical fields and business rules before executing actions | Prevent malformed or manipulated output from causing harm |
| Audit logging | Record agent identity, tool calls, approvals, and transaction outcomes | Establish accountability and traceability |
| Fail-safe behavior | Stop or escalate when required verification is missing | Avoid unsafe execution under uncertainty |

#### Critical architectural rule

**Do not rely on the AI model itself as the final security boundary.**

A model instruction such as "verify this payment before proceeding" is not a substitute for application-enforced authorization. Sensitive actions should be constrained by deterministic controls outside the model, including permission checks, approval workflows, transaction limits, and independent verification.

### 7.5 Deepfake Detection and Content Authenticity

Detection technologies can contribute to defense, but their limitations must be reflected in the risk assessment.

Potential detection approaches include:

- Audio analysis for synthetic speech indicators.
- Video analysis for manipulation artifacts.
- Image and document forensic analysis.
- Metadata and provenance checks.
- Content credentials and cryptographic authenticity mechanisms where available.
- Anomaly detection across communication and transaction behavior.
- Human review for high-impact decisions.

These approaches have different capabilities and failure modes.

| Detection approach | Potential value | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic audio detection | Identifies suspicious voice characteristics | Performance varies with audio quality, language, recording conditions, and generation methods |
| Video deepfake detection | Flags potential visual manipulation | Compression, transformations, and new generation techniques can affect performance |
| Metadata analysis | Identifies inconsistencies in file history or properties | Metadata can be absent, altered, or stripped |
| Content provenance | Provides evidence about content origin or editing history when properly implemented | Does not establish that the speaker is authorized or that the content is truthful |
| Behavioral analytics | Detects unusual transaction or communication patterns | Legitimate activity can appear anomalous, while carefully designed fraud may look normal |
| Human verification | Adds contextual judgment and independent review | Humans can be deceived by persuasive content, authority pressure, or inadequate procedures |

Detection systems should be evaluated using realistic scenarios, representative data, documented performance metrics, and defined escalation procedures.

Where false positives could interrupt critical business operations, the organization should also establish a controlled review process.

### 7.6 Defense-in-Depth Control Model

The following model illustrates how controls should work together.

```text
LAYER 1 — TRUSTED IDENTITY
    |
    +-- Strong authentication
    +-- Identity lifecycle management
    +-- Independent contact verification
    |
    v
LAYER 2 — REQUEST VALIDATION
    |
    +-- Approved communication channels
    +-- Request authenticity checks
    +-- Supplier and beneficiary verification
    +-- Content provenance checks where available
    |
    v
LAYER 3 — BUSINESS AUTHORIZATION
    |
    +-- Role-based access control
    +-- Segregation of duties
    +-- Dual approval
    +-- Transaction thresholds
    |
    v
LAYER 4 — EXECUTION CONTROL
    |
    +-- Application-enforced authorization
    +-- Payment and workflow restrictions
    +-- Restricted AI agent permissions
    +-- Fail-safe handling of uncertainty
    |
    v
LAYER 5 — DETECTION AND RESPONSE
    |
    +-- Transaction monitoring
    +-- Security and fraud alerts
    +-- Audit logging
    +-- Incident containment and recovery
    |
    v
CONTROLLED BUSINESS OUTCOME
```

A single failed layer should not automatically result in a successful fraudulent transaction. The architecture should provide independent barriers that reduce the probability and impact of control failure.

## 8. Detective Controls, Monitoring, and Incident Response

### 8.1 Detection Strategy

Preventive controls reduce the likelihood of successful fraud. Detective controls identify suspicious activity, attempted fraud, and control failures that preventive measures did not stop.

A mature detection strategy combines technical telemetry with business-process indicators.

For example, a security monitoring platform may detect an unusual login, while the finance platform identifies a newly modified beneficiary followed by an unusually large payment. Correlating these signals can reveal a higher-risk event than either alert alone.

### 8.2 Detection Signals and Monitoring Rules

| Detection signal | Example indicator | Potential response |
|---|---|---|
| Unusual payment request | High-value transaction outside normal patterns | Hold or review the transaction according to policy |
| New beneficiary | First payment to a recently added or modified beneficiary | Require enhanced verification |
| Supplier bank change | Bank details changed shortly before payment | Independently verify the change |
| Executive impersonation | Unusual urgent request attributed to senior leadership | Verify through an independent approved channel |
| Authentication anomaly | Unusual access pattern affecting a finance account | Investigate and apply risk-based access controls |
| Approval anomaly | One individual initiates and approves an otherwise restricted transaction | Block execution and review the workflow |
| AI agent anomaly | An agent invokes a sensitive tool outside its normal scope | Suspend or restrict the action and investigate |
| Data exfiltration indicator | Unusual access to sensitive records following a suspicious interaction | Investigate potential compromise and contain exposure |
| Repeated failed verification | Multiple requests fail required identity or payment checks | Escalate for potential targeted fraud |
| Unusual account recovery | Authentication factors changed before a sensitive transaction | Apply enhanced review and investigate |

These are candidate detection rules, not universally valid thresholds. Organizations should tune them against normal business activity, fraud exposure, and operational requirements.

### 8.3 Security Information and Event Management Integration

A SIEM or equivalent monitoring platform can correlate relevant events from multiple systems.

Potential sources include:

- Identity and access management platforms.
- Endpoint detection and response systems.
- Email security gateways.
- Collaboration and communication platforms.
- ERP and financial applications.
- Supplier management systems.
- Payment processing platforms.
- AI application and agent audit logs.
- Data loss prevention systems.
- Fraud monitoring platforms.

The monitoring design should distinguish between systems that generate useful security evidence and systems that actually enforce business authorization.

A SIEM alert does not itself prevent a payment unless an explicitly designed and authorized response mechanism acts on it.

#### Example correlation rule

**Scenario:** A supplier bank account is modified and a high-value payment is initiated shortly afterward.

```text
EVENT A:
Supplier beneficiary bank details modified
        |
        v
EVENT B:
Payment initiated for the modified beneficiary
        |
        v
EVENT C:
Transaction exceeds configured risk threshold
OR
Independent verification is missing
        |
        v
CORRELATION:
Elevated supplier-payment fraud risk
        |
        v
RESPONSE:
Generate high-priority alert
Request verification or place a controlled hold
Preserve relevant evidence
Notify designated fraud and security personnel
```

A production implementation should specify event schemas, time windows, entity matching, deduplication, severity logic, exception handling, and response ownership.

### 8.4 Incident Classification and Severity

Deepfake-related incidents should be classified according to actual and potential business impact rather than the sophistication of the synthetic media.

| Severity | Example condition | Indicative response |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Confirmed major financial loss, significant privileged compromise, or material compromise of sensitive information | Activate major incident governance, executive escalation, and coordinated containment |
| High | Credible targeted fraud attempt involving senior leadership, sensitive transactions, or privileged access | Urgent investigation, transaction protection, and coordinated security and fraud response |
| Medium | Suspicious synthetic content or attempted impersonation with no confirmed consequential action | Investigate, preserve evidence, validate controls, and assess whether broader targeting exists |
| Low | Unsubstantiated report with limited evidence and no identified exposure | Record, triage, and determine whether further verification is necessary |

The organization should define its own severity criteria, including financial thresholds, privacy impact, operational criticality, and applicable regulatory obligations.

A fraudulent request that is blocked before execution may still warrant a high-priority investigation if it reveals a systemic weakness or an ongoing targeted campaign.

### 8.5 Incident Response Lifecycle

The response process should integrate cybersecurity, fraud management, finance, legal, privacy, communications, and business operations.

#### Phase 1 — Preparation

Establish:

- Incident response playbooks for executive impersonation and synthetic media fraud.
- Approved emergency contacts and escalation paths.
- Payment suspension and recall procedures.
- Evidence collection standards.
- Legal, privacy, and regulatory notification decision processes.
- Communication templates for employees, customers, and suppliers.
- Tabletop exercises involving finance, security, and executive leadership.

Preparation should include testing whether the organization can rapidly identify the appropriate payment processor, banking contact, application owner, and incident commander.

#### Phase 2 — Identification and triage

Determine:

- What communication or action triggered the incident.
- Whether the content was likely manipulated or impersonated.
- Which person or identity was targeted.
- Whether credentials, payment instructions, or sensitive information were exposed.
- Whether a payment was initiated, approved, executed, or settled.
- Whether the event is isolated or part of a broader campaign.

Avoid waiting for conclusive proof of a deepfake before protecting a potentially fraudulent transaction.

#### Phase 3 — Containment

Depending on the incident, actions may include:

- Requesting a temporary payment hold through authorized procedures.
- Contacting the relevant financial institution immediately when a fraudulent transfer is suspected.
- Disabling compromised accounts or sessions.
- Revoking exposed credentials or tokens.
- Suspending an affected AI agent or restricting its tool permissions.
- Preserving relevant emails, recordings, logs, and transaction records.
- Restricting access to compromised systems or sensitive data.

Containment actions should be proportionate to the risk and authorized under the incident response plan. Critical business services should not be broadly disabled without considering the operational consequences.

#### Phase 4 — Eradication

Identify and address the conditions that enabled the incident.

Potential activities include:

- Removing unauthorized accounts or access.
- Correcting fraudulent supplier or beneficiary records.
- Resetting compromised authentication factors.
- Removing malicious integrations or unauthorized automation.
- Correcting workflow and approval weaknesses.
- Updating detection logic and investigation procedures.
- Reviewing related accounts, transactions, and communications for additional compromise.

Eradication should address the underlying control failure, not merely the specific fraudulent artifact.

#### Phase 5 — Recovery

Before restoring normal operations:

- Verify affected identities and access permissions.
- Confirm that payment instructions and beneficiary details are legitimate.
- Validate that compromised systems have been remediated.
- Test affected controls.
- Reconcile financial transactions.
- Confirm that appropriate business owners authorize the resumption of affected processes.
- Increase monitoring where residual risk remains elevated.

#### Phase 6 — Lessons learned

Conduct a structured post-incident review to determine:

- Which control failed or was absent.
- Why existing procedures did not prevent the event.
- Whether employees were pressured to bypass controls.
- Whether monitoring identified the incident early enough.
- Whether the response team had sufficient authority and evidence.
- Whether similar weaknesses exist elsewhere.
- Which remediation actions require accountable owners and deadlines.

The post-incident review should produce measurable control improvements, not merely additional awareness training.

### 8.6 Evidence Preservation and Investigation

A deepfake-related investigation may involve both digital forensics and financial fraud analysis.

Potential evidence includes:

- Original emails and full message headers.
- Communication platform logs.
- Audio or video files in their original available form.
- File metadata and provenance information.
- Identity provider and authentication logs.
- AI agent execution traces and tool-call records.
- Payment requests, approvals, and transaction identifiers.
- Supplier master-data change history.
- Relevant endpoint and network telemetry.
- Interview records and employee reports.

Preserve the original available evidence and document how copies were collected and handled. Avoid modifying original media merely to perform an analysis.

Investigators should also record the limitations of any deepfake detection tool used. A detection result is an investigative indicator, not necessarily definitive proof of who created the content or whether the underlying communication was fraudulent.

Evidence handling must comply with applicable privacy, employment, data protection, and legal requirements.

### 8.7 Financial Fraud Response and Recovery

Cybersecurity containment and financial recovery are related but distinct workstreams.

The security team may contain an account compromise while the finance team works with a bank or payment processor to determine whether a transfer can be stopped, recalled, or recovered.

The incident plan should establish:

- Who has authority to contact the bank.
- Which transaction details must be provided.
- How rapidly the organization can initiate a recall request.
- Who approves emergency financial actions.
- How affected accounts and beneficiary records are secured.
- How financial losses are calculated and recorded.
- How recovery progress is reported to management.

**Critical operational rule:** If a fraudulent payment is suspected, escalate immediately through the organization's established financial fraud process. Do not delay the initial contact with the relevant financial institution while waiting for the forensic investigation to finish.

## Core Principle

**Deepfake defense must protect business decisions, not merely detect synthetic media.** Strong identity verification, independent transaction authorization, least-privilege access, application-enforced controls, monitoring, and coordinated incident response reduce the likelihood that deception can be converted into financial loss or unauthorized access.

## Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Threat modeling | Identifying attack paths, trust assumptions, and potential consequences | Focusing only on the deepfake-generation technology |
| Identity verification | Establishing the identity of a person or organization | Assuming a familiar voice proves identity |
| Authentication | Validating an identity claim through appropriate authenticators | Assuming authentication proves a transaction is legitimate |
| Authorization | Determining which actions an identity may perform | Giving an authenticated user unrestricted authority |
| Transaction verification | Validating the legitimacy and details of a specific business action | Treating a known caller as sufficient payment approval |
| Preventive control | Blocking or constraining an unauthorized action | Assuming detection alone prevents fraud |
| Detective control | Identifying suspicious activity or control failure | Assuming a SIEM alert automatically stops a transaction |
| Deepfake detection | Assessing whether media may be synthetic or manipulated | Treating detection output as definitive proof |
| Incident containment | Limiting immediate damage and further compromise | Waiting for attribution before taking protective action |
| Eradication | Removing the causes and mechanisms of compromise | Deleting a suspicious file without correcting the workflow weakness |
| Recovery | Restoring operations with validated controls | Resuming normal operations before verifying affected identities and transactions |
| AI agent governance | Controlling an AI system's permissions, actions, and accountability | Relying on model instructions instead of application-enforced security controls |

## 9. AI Governance and Enterprise Risk Management

### 9.1 Establishing Governance Accountability

Deepfake-enabled fraud is not exclusively a cybersecurity problem. It crosses organizational boundaries involving financial controls, identity management, fraud prevention, privacy, legal obligations, corporate communications, and business continuity.

An enterprise that assigns the entire problem to its Security Operations Center (SOC) without involving Finance, Legal, Human Resources, and business leadership creates a governance gap. The SOC may identify suspicious activity, but it may not have the authority to stop a payment, validate a supplier relationship, issue an external statement, or approve a regulatory notification.

The organization therefore needs a governance model that defines accountability for preventing, detecting, responding to, and recovering from AI-enabled fraud.

#### Governance objectives

1. Establish executive accountability for AI-related fraud and impersonation risks.
2. Define ownership of the business processes most exposed to deception.
3. Integrate AI security risks into enterprise risk management.
4. Establish clear escalation and decision-making authority.
5. Ensure that preventive and detective controls have accountable owners.
6. Define how risk acceptance decisions are made and documented.
7. Monitor the effectiveness of controls and report material weaknesses to management.

### 9.2 Three Lines Model Applied to AI-Enabled Fraud

The Three Lines Model provides a useful way to organize responsibilities across business management, risk oversight, and independent assurance.

| Organizational layer | Typical participants | Responsibilities | Example in the deepfake case |
|---|---|---|---|
| First line: Business and operations | Finance, Treasury, Procurement, Customer Operations, IT operations, AI product owners | Own business risks, operate controls, follow procedures, and remediate control failures | Finance enforces dual authorization and independently verifies changes to supplier bank details |
| Second line: Risk oversight and control functions | Enterprise Risk Management, Cybersecurity GRC, Compliance, Privacy, Financial Crime or Fraud Risk | Establish frameworks, advise the business, challenge risk assessments, monitor exposure, and oversee control effectiveness | Cybersecurity GRC establishes minimum controls for executive impersonation and AI-enabled fraud |
| Third line: Independent assurance | Internal Audit | Independently assess governance, risk management, and internal controls | Audit tests whether supplier changes and high-value payments comply with approved control requirements |

The precise organizational structure varies by enterprise. In some organizations, fraud risk, information security, and compliance responsibilities may be distributed differently.

The critical principle is that **control ownership, risk oversight, and independent assurance must not be confused**.

For example, Cybersecurity GRC may define the control requirements for high-risk payments, but Finance should own the payment execution process. Internal Audit should independently evaluate the design and operating effectiveness of the controls rather than operate them.

### 9.3 AI Security Governance Roles and Responsibilities

A practical governance model requires explicit ownership across the incident lifecycle.

| Role | Primary accountability | Key decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Board or relevant board committee | Oversight of material enterprise risks | Whether significant exposure and remediation plans are receiving appropriate attention |
| Executive sponsor | Executive accountability for the risk program | Resolution of cross-functional conflicts and allocation of resources |
| Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) | Cybersecurity risk management and security response | Security containment, technical investigation, and cybersecurity remediation |
| Chief Risk Officer (CRO) | Enterprise risk integration | Risk aggregation, risk appetite alignment, and escalation of material exposure |
| Chief Financial Officer (CFO) | Financial control environment | Financial control remediation and financial impact assessment |
| Treasury or Finance Director | Payment process ownership | Payment holds, transaction authorization, and financial recovery coordination |
| Procurement Director | Supplier lifecycle and procurement controls | Supplier validation and supplier master-data control remediation |
| Chief Information Officer (CIO) or IT leadership | Technology operations and application control implementation | Technical remediation and system availability |
| AI system owner | Accountability for the relevant AI system | AI system risk assessment, permissions, logging, testing, and remediation |
| Data Protection Officer (DPO), where applicable | Independent privacy advice and statutory responsibilities | Privacy impact assessment and advice on applicable data protection obligations |
| Legal Counsel | Legal interpretation and legal risk management | Legal exposure, preservation obligations, contractual issues, and notification advice |
| Compliance Officer | Compliance framework oversight | Applicable compliance obligations, reporting processes, and evidence requirements |
| SOC Manager | Security monitoring and operational detection | Alert triage, correlation, escalation, and security investigation |
| Fraud Risk Manager | Fraud prevention and fraud investigation | Fraud classification, transaction risk analysis, and coordination with financial institutions |
| Communications or Public Relations Director | External and internal communications | Approved statements and coordinated reputational response |
| Human Resources | Employee-related processes | Employee support, training, and handling of relevant workplace matters |
| Internal Audit | Independent assurance | Evaluation of governance and control effectiveness |

Not every organization needs a separate individual for each role. Smaller organizations may combine responsibilities, but they should preserve appropriate independence and avoid conflicting duties.

### 9.4 RACI Matrix for Deepfake and AI Fraud Risk Management

RACI defines who is **Responsible**, **Accountable**, **Consulted**, and **Informed**.

- **R — Responsible:** Performs the activity.
- **A — Accountable:** Ultimately owns the outcome.
- **C — Consulted:** Provides input or specialist advice.
- **I — Informed:** Receives relevant updates.

The following is an illustrative model. Actual assignments must be approved by the enterprise and aligned with its authority structure.

| Activity | Finance / Treasury | Cybersecurity | GRC / ERM | Procurement | Legal / Privacy | AI System Owner | Internal Audit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identify fraud scenarios | A/R | R | C | R | C | C | I |
| Conduct cybersecurity threat modeling | C | A/R | C | C | C | R | I |
| Maintain enterprise risk register | C | R | A/R | C | C | C | I |
| Define payment authorization controls | A/R | C | C | C | C | I | I |
| Verify supplier bank changes | C | C | I | A/R | I | I | I |
| Assess AI agent permissions | I | R | C | I | C | A/R | I |
| Monitor security telemetry | I | A/R | C | I | I | R | I |
| Investigate suspected impersonation | R | A/R | C | C | C | C | I |
| Coordinate financial recovery | A/R | C | I | C | C | I | I |
| Assess privacy and notification obligations | C | C | C | I | A/R | C | I |
| Approve material risk acceptance | C | C | R | C | C | C | I |
| Independently test controls | C | C | C | C | C | C | A/R |

**Governance note:** The matrix is a starting point, not a substitute for a formal responsibility model. For example, legal notification decisions may require executive approval, and the DPO's statutory role must be preserved where applicable. Material risk acceptance should be assigned to the appropriate authority under the organization's risk appetite and delegation-of-authority framework.

### 9.5 Integration with Enterprise Risk Management

Deepfake and AI-enabled fraud should be integrated into the enterprise risk register rather than tracked exclusively as an isolated cybersecurity issue.

This integration enables management to evaluate the combined effect of:

- Direct financial loss.
- Fraud-related operational disruption.
- Unauthorized disclosure of confidential information.
- Personal data breaches.
- Customer harm.
- Regulatory and contractual exposure.
- Reputational damage.
- Loss of trust in executive communications.
- Disruption to AI-enabled business processes.

#### Example enterprise risk statement

**Risk ID:** ER-AIF-001

**Risk title:** AI-enabled impersonation and synthetic-media fraud.

**Risk statement:** The organization may suffer financial loss, unauthorized disclosure, operational disruption, or reputational damage when threat actors use synthetic media, AI-generated communications, synthetic identities, or manipulated digital content to impersonate trusted individuals and exploit weaknesses in identity verification, transaction authorization, or business-process controls.

**Risk owner:** Designated executive accountable for the relevant enterprise risk, according to the organization's risk ownership model.

**Key control owners:** Finance, Procurement, Cybersecurity, IT, and relevant AI system owners.

**Risk drivers:**

- Increasing availability of synthetic-media generation.
- Public availability of voice, image, and video samples.
- Weak independent verification procedures.
- Excessive reliance on email, telephone, or video calls as proof of identity.
- Inadequate separation of payment duties.
- Excessive AI agent permissions.
- Insufficient monitoring and incident response readiness.

**Potential consequences:**

- Unauthorized financial transactions.
- Account compromise.
- Exposure of confidential or personal information.
- Disruption of critical business processes.
- Regulatory and contractual consequences.
- Reputational damage and loss of stakeholder confidence.

**Key risk indicators:**

- Number of reported impersonation attempts.
- Number of supplier bank changes requiring exception handling.
- Percentage of high-risk payments independently verified.
- Number of unauthorized AI agent actions.
- Number of fraud incidents involving control bypass.
- Time taken to detect and contain material incidents.

### 9.6 Risk Appetite and Risk Acceptance

Risk appetite defines the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

For deepfake and AI-enabled fraud, management should distinguish between residual risks that can be tolerated and control failures that violate mandatory requirements.

Examples of unacceptable control conditions may include:

- High-value payments executed without required authorization.
- AI agents granted unrestricted access to sensitive financial operations without adequate controls.
- Supplier bank changes accepted solely on the basis of an unverified email or telephone request.
- Material security incidents left without accountable ownership.
- Required legal or regulatory obligations deliberately ignored.

The exact thresholds and mandatory requirements must be established by the organization.

Risk acceptance should document:

1. The risk scenario.
2. The inherent and residual risk ratings.
3. Existing controls and their demonstrated effectiveness.
4. The business rationale for accepting the residual risk.
5. The applicable risk appetite and any policy exceptions.
6. The accountable risk owner.
7. The approval authority.
8. Any compensating controls.
9. The expiration or review date.
10. The conditions that trigger reassessment.

A risk owner should not be permitted to accept a risk on behalf of another function without appropriate authority. Nor should a business executive's approval override legal obligations or mandatory control requirements.

## 10. Regulatory, Privacy, and Compliance Considerations

### 10.1 Establishing the Applicable Regulatory Scope

Deepfake and AI fraud incidents can create several different types of legal and regulatory exposure. However, not every incident automatically triggers every notification obligation.

The organization must assess the actual facts, the affected jurisdictions, the type of information involved, the nature of the activity, and the applicable legal framework.

For an enterprise operating in Spain and the wider European Union, relevant areas may include:

- Data protection and personal data breach obligations.
- AI governance requirements.
- Cybersecurity and operational resilience obligations.
- Financial services or payment-related requirements, where applicable.
- Employment and workplace privacy rules.
- Consumer protection and unfair commercial practices.
- Criminal fraud and evidence preservation.
- Contractual and sector-specific obligations.

The applicability of each framework depends on the organization, its sector, the incident, and its role in the relevant processing or service.

### 10.2 GDPR and Personal Data Breaches

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a deepfake incident may involve personal data if identifiable individuals' voices, images, identity documents, account information, or other personal information are processed or exposed.

However, the presence of a deepfake does not automatically establish that a personal data breach has occurred. The organization must assess whether a security breach has led to accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorized disclosure of, or access to personal data.

Examples include:

- Unauthorized disclosure of employee or customer records following an impersonation attack.
- Exposure of identity documents used in synthetic identity fraud.
- Compromise of a customer account containing personal information.
- Unauthorized access to recordings or biometric-related information.
- Disclosure of confidential personal information to an impersonator.

#### Governance responsibilities

The organization should determine:

1. Whether personal data is involved.
2. Whether a personal data breach has occurred.
3. Which controller and processor responsibilities apply.
4. Whether the incident presents a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals.
5. Whether supervisory authority notification is required.
6. Whether affected individuals must be informed.
7. What evidence supports the decision.

Under GDPR Article 33, where the relevant conditions are met, a controller must notify the competent supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours after becoming aware of a personal data breach, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

Under Article 34, communication to affected individuals is required when the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms, subject to the applicable statutory conditions and exceptions.

These obligations should not be confused with a universal 72-hour deadline for every cybersecurity incident.

### 10.3 EU AI Act Considerations

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems and imposes different obligations depending on the system, provider, deployer, use case, and applicable implementation timetable.

Deepfake and synthetic-media scenarios may be relevant to the Act's transparency requirements.

Article 50 includes transparency obligations concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated content, including requirements relevant to synthetic audio, image, video, and text. The exact obligations depend on the relevant provision, the actor's role, the nature of the content, and applicable exceptions.

The Act also contains specific provisions concerning deepfakes and disclosure of artificially generated or manipulated content.

#### Practical governance implications

Organizations should determine:

- Whether they develop, provide, deploy, or merely consume the relevant AI system.
- Whether they generate or manipulate synthetic audio, image, or video content.
- Whether the content falls within a transparency obligation.
- Whether the content is clearly disclosed as AI-generated or manipulated where required.
- Whether applicable exceptions apply.
- Whether documentation, monitoring, and accountability requirements are relevant.
- Whether additional sector-specific rules apply.

**Important distinction:** The AI Act's transparency provisions do not mean that every organization using AI must label every internal AI-generated output in the same way. The applicable obligation must be assessed against the specific provision and use case.

The organization should also distinguish between using AI to create synthetic content and being deceived by synthetic content created by an external threat actor. The legal responsibilities can differ substantially.

### 10.4 NIS2 and Cybersecurity Governance

The EU Network and Information Security Directive 2 (NIS2) establishes cybersecurity risk-management and incident-reporting obligations for entities within its scope, subject to the Directive's requirements and national implementation.

A deepfake-related incident may become relevant where it causes or contributes to a significant cybersecurity incident affecting a covered entity's network and information systems or the services it provides.

The incident's classification depends on its impact, the entity's obligations, and the applicable legal requirements. A deepfake incident does not automatically constitute a reportable NIS2 incident merely because synthetic media was used.

Relevant governance considerations include:

- Whether the entity is within NIS2's scope.
- Whether the incident affects network and information systems or relevant services.
- Whether the incident meets applicable significance criteria.
- Whether the incident-reporting requirements apply.
- Who is responsible for regulatory assessment and notification.
- How management oversight and cybersecurity risk-management measures are evidenced.

Where applicable, the organization should coordinate NIS2-related analysis with other notification obligations to avoid inconsistent reporting and duplicated investigative work.

### 10.5 DORA and Financial-Sector Considerations

The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) applies to specified financial entities and certain ICT third-party service providers under its framework.

A deepfake-enabled fraud incident may have DORA relevance if it forms part of an ICT-related incident affecting a financial entity and the incident falls within the applicable classification and reporting requirements.

For an in-scope financial entity, governance should consider:

- ICT risk management.
- Incident detection and classification.
- Operational resilience.
- Third-party ICT risk.
- Business continuity and recovery.
- Incident reporting where required.
- Evidence and documentation supporting decisions.

DORA should not be assumed to apply to every company that uses financial technology or processes payments. Applicability depends on the organization's legal status and the relevant statutory provisions.

### 10.6 Compliance Decision Matrix

The following matrix is intended as a triage aid. It does not replace legal analysis.

| Incident condition | Regulatory or legal area to assess | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Customer personal data is exposed | GDPR and applicable national data protection law | Has a personal data breach occurred, and what notification duties apply? |
| AI-generated content is published or distributed | EU AI Act and applicable sectoral rules | Do transparency or disclosure obligations apply to the specific content and actor? |
| Critical services or relevant systems are materially disrupted | NIS2, national implementation, or other sector-specific requirements | Is the entity covered, and does the incident meet the applicable reporting criteria? |
| A financial entity experiences an ICT-related incident | DORA, where applicable | Does the incident fall within the relevant ICT incident classification and reporting requirements? |
| Customer payments or consumer transactions are manipulated | Applicable financial, consumer, payment, and contractual rules | Which customer-protection, payment-dispute, or notification requirements apply? |
| Employee voice or video is misused | Data protection, employment, privacy, and relevant national law | What lawful basis, investigation safeguards, and employee-related obligations apply? |
| A third-party provider is involved | Contractual, outsourcing, data protection, and sectoral requirements | Who owns investigation, evidence preservation, notification, and remediation? |
| Fraud or impersonation may constitute a criminal offense | Applicable criminal law and law-enforcement procedures | What evidence should be preserved, and when should authorities be contacted? |

### 10.7 Regulatory Notification Governance

Organizations should establish a documented decision process for evaluating notification obligations.

The process should include:

1. **Incident facts:** Establish what happened and which systems, people, and data were affected.
2. **Legal scope:** Identify the relevant jurisdictions, legal entities, and applicable regulations.
3. **Classification:** Determine whether the event meets the relevant legal definition and reporting criteria.
4. **Deadline assessment:** Establish the applicable deadline and the event that starts the reporting clock.
5. **Decision authority:** Identify who is authorized to approve the notification.
6. **Notification preparation:** Assemble accurate facts, known impacts, mitigation measures, and outstanding uncertainties.
7. **Submission and follow-up:** Submit required reports and maintain a record of communications.
8. **Evidence retention:** Preserve the rationale, supporting evidence, approvals, and subsequent updates.

Where several regulatory regimes may apply, the organization should maintain a single incident chronology and coordinate the different reporting workstreams.

A lack of complete forensic certainty should not automatically delay a legally required notification. The applicable law may permit an initial notification followed by additional information, depending on the regime.

## 11. Third-Party Risk Management and Supply Chain Exposure

### 11.1 Why Third Parties Increase Deepfake Risk

Enterprise fraud frequently exploits trust relationships between organizations.

A threat actor may impersonate a supplier, compromise a vendor's email account, manipulate a business partner's representative, or exploit weaknesses in a service provider's identity verification process.

The attack can cross organizational boundaries even when the victim enterprise's internal systems remain uncompromised.

For example:

A threat actor sends a convincing message claiming that a supplier has changed its banking arrangements. The message appears to come from the supplier's established email account. Procurement updates the supplier record, and Finance processes the next payment.

The enterprise may have strong endpoint security and no confirmed internal compromise. Nevertheless, the payment process has failed because the organization relied on the apparent authenticity of a third-party communication rather than independent verification.

### 11.2 Third-Party Threat Scenarios

| Third-party category | Threat scenario | Potential consequence | Priority control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suppliers | Synthetic voice or email used to request bank changes | Payment diversion | Independent beneficiary verification |
| Managed service providers | Impersonation of a support engineer | Unauthorized privileged access | Strong authentication and controlled support procedures |
| AI vendors | Inadequate security controls or unclear data-handling practices | Exposure of confidential information | Vendor due diligence and contractual controls |
| Identity verification providers | Synthetic identities bypass onboarding controls | Fraudulent accounts or transactions | Independent testing and layered identity assurance |
| Cloud service providers | Compromised vendor identity or exposed administrative access | Service or data compromise | Shared-responsibility validation and privileged access controls |
| Recruitment agencies | Synthetic candidate identities or manipulated credentials | Fraudulent hiring or insider access | Risk-based identity and qualification verification |
| External call centers | Impersonation or manipulation of customer support staff | Unauthorized account changes | Strong customer verification and controlled account recovery |
| Payment processors | Fraudulent instructions or compromised integration paths | Unauthorized payments or processing disruption | Transaction monitoring and secure integration design |
| Marketing agencies | Unauthorized publication of synthetic executive content | Reputational damage | Publication approval and authenticated content governance |
| AI integration partners | Overprivileged agents or insecure integrations | Unauthorized actions or data disclosure | Least privilege, logging, and independent authorization |

### 11.3 Third-Party Due Diligence

Due diligence should be proportionate to the risk of the service and the potential impact of compromise.

A vendor that provides a public website template does not necessarily require the same assurance as a provider with access to privileged infrastructure, customer identity data, or financial transaction workflows.

A structured assessment should cover the following areas.

#### A. Governance and accountability

- Does the provider maintain an information security governance program?
- Are responsibilities for AI security and fraud prevention defined?
- Does the provider have documented incident response procedures?
- Are material subcontractors identified?
- Is there a defined process for reporting security incidents?

#### B. Identity and access management

- Are privileged accounts protected by strong authentication?
- Is access restricted according to business need?
- Are access rights reviewed periodically?
- Are supplier support and account recovery procedures controlled?
- Can the provider demonstrate how authorized representatives are verified?

#### C. AI system governance

Where the vendor develops, provides, or operates AI systems, assess:

- Whether AI use cases and system capabilities are documented.
- Whether access to external tools and enterprise data is restricted.
- Whether sensitive actions require appropriate approval.
- Whether relevant AI-related risks are assessed and tested.
- Whether logs and evidence are available for investigations.
- Whether the provider has a defined process for addressing model or system changes that affect security.

#### D. Data protection

- What categories of enterprise and personal data are processed?
- Where is data stored and processed?
- Is data used to train or improve provider models?
- What retention and deletion arrangements apply?
- Are subprocessors involved?
- What contractual obligations govern security incidents and data breaches?

#### E. Operational resilience

- Are backup, recovery, and continuity procedures documented?
- Can the provider support incident investigation?
- Are recovery objectives appropriate for the service?
- Are service dependencies and single points of failure identified?
- Is the provider's continuity plan tested?

### 11.4 Contractual Security Requirements

Contracts should translate risk-management expectations into enforceable obligations where appropriate.

Potential contractual provisions include:

- Security and privacy requirements.
- Identity and access control requirements.
- Incident notification obligations.
- Cooperation with investigations.
- Evidence preservation and access.
- Subcontractor management.
- Data processing and retention requirements.
- Restrictions on using customer data for model training.
- Audit rights or alternative assurance mechanisms.
- Vulnerability remediation expectations.
- Business continuity requirements.
- Defined responsibilities for incident containment and recovery.
- Termination and data-return provisions.

Contractual language must be reviewed by Legal and adapted to the service, jurisdiction, and negotiating context.

A certification or assurance report can support due diligence, but it does not establish that every relevant risk is controlled. The organization should assess whether the available evidence covers the specific service, systems, data, and use cases involved.

### 11.6 Third-Party Risk Scoring

A practical scoring model can prioritize vendors according to the consequences of compromise.

Consider these dimensions:

| Dimension | Low exposure | Medium exposure | High exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data sensitivity | Public information | Internal business information | Sensitive personal, financial, or confidential data |
| System access | No enterprise access | Limited application access | Privileged or broad production access |
| Financial authority | No transaction involvement | Supports payment preparation | Can initiate, modify, or approve material transactions |
| AI capability | No relevant AI processing | AI supports limited workflows | AI can access sensitive systems or initiate consequential actions |

The organization can convert these qualitative dimensions into a documented numerical methodology, but it should avoid false precision.

A single high-impact attribute, such as access to privileged production systems or authority to change payment beneficiaries, may justify enhanced due diligence even if the aggregate score appears moderate.

### 11.7 Ongoing Monitoring and Reassessment

Third-party risk is not static. A vendor's risk profile may change because of new AI capabilities, changes in ownership, new subprocessors, altered data flows, or expanded access permissions.

Reassessment should be triggered by material changes, including:

- Introduction of a new AI-enabled service.
- Expanded access to sensitive enterprise data.
- New authority to execute or modify business transactions.
- A significant vendor security incident.
- Material changes in the vendor's control environment.
- Changes in relevant contractual or regulatory requirements.
- Repeated failure to meet agreed remediation commitments.

For high-risk providers, the enterprise should define assurance frequency, evidence requirements, remediation deadlines, and escalation paths.

## Core Principle

**AI-enabled fraud governance must connect business ownership, cybersecurity controls, enterprise risk management, legal obligations, and third-party assurance.** No single team or technology can independently manage the entire risk.

## Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Meaning | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| First line | Business and operational functions that own risks and operate controls | Assuming GRC owns every business risk |
| Second line | Risk and compliance functions that establish frameworks and oversee exposure | Treating oversight as a replacement for operational control ownership |
| Third line | Independent assurance, typically Internal Audit | Assigning auditors responsibility for operating the controls they assess |
| Enterprise risk | A risk evaluated in the context of overall business objectives and risk appetite | Keeping material fraud risks isolated within cybersecurity |
| Risk acceptance | A formally authorized decision to retain a residual risk | Treating executive approval as permission to ignore mandatory obligations |
| Regulatory applicability | Determining whether a legal framework applies to the entity and incident | Assuming every AI fraud incident triggers every notification regime |
| Personal data breach | A breach meeting the relevant legal definition under applicable data protection law | Assuming all deepfake incidents automatically constitute GDPR breaches |
| AI transparency | Applicable disclosure obligations concerning certain AI-generated or manipulated content | Assuming all AI outputs are subject to identical labeling requirements |
| Third-party due diligence | Risk-based assessment of a provider's controls and capabilities | Treating a certificate as proof that all relevant risks are controlled |
| Shared responsibility | Defined allocation of security and operational obligations between parties | Assuming outsourcing transfers all enterprise risk |
| Residual risk | Risk remaining after considering controls | Assuming documented controls are necessarily effective in operation |
| Independent assurance | Objective evaluation of governance and control effectiveness | Confusing a control owner's self-assessment with independent audit evidence |

## 12. Enterprise Implementation Roadmap

### 12.1 From Risk Assessment to Operational Capability

An enterprise can identify deepfake and AI-enabled fraud risks, establish governance responsibilities, and document controls without actually reducing its exposure. The gap between a documented control framework and an operationally effective security capability must be addressed through implementation, testing, and continuous improvement.

The implementation roadmap should prioritize controls according to business impact, exploitability, existing control weaknesses, regulatory obligations, and remediation effort.

The organization should not begin by purchasing a deepfake detection platform simply because synthetic media is the most visible aspect of the threat. It should first determine which business processes can be manipulated and which unauthorized actions an attacker could cause.

For the case study, the primary objective is to prevent an impersonator from causing an unauthorized financial transaction, obtaining sensitive information, or manipulating an AI-enabled business process.

### 12.2 Implementation Phases

| Phase | Timeframe | Primary objective | Key activities | Expected deliverables |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1: Immediate risk reduction | Days 0–15 | Address critical control weaknesses | Review payment approvals, verify supplier-change procedures, identify privileged AI agents, establish incident escalation contacts | Immediate action plan, critical control-gap register, executive risk briefing |
| Phase 2: Risk assessment and governance | Days 16–30 | Establish accountability and risk visibility | Complete threat modeling, identify business owners, assess existing controls, define risk acceptance authority | Approved risk assessment, RACI matrix, updated enterprise risk register |
| Phase 3: Preventive control implementation | Days 31–60 | Reduce the likelihood of successful fraud | Strengthen independent verification, enforce segregation of duties, restrict AI permissions, implement transaction controls | Updated procedures, implemented control changes, technical configuration evidence |
| Phase 4: Detection and response integration | Days 61–75 | Improve detection and incident handling | Integrate relevant logs, implement correlation rules, validate escalation procedures, prepare incident playbooks | Monitoring use cases, response playbooks, escalation matrix |
| Phase 5: Validation and assurance | Days 76–90 | Demonstrate that controls work | Conduct scenario-based tests, perform control sampling, run a tabletop exercise, document remediation | Test results, evidence repository, remediation plan, management report |
| Phase 6: Continuous improvement | Ongoing | Maintain control effectiveness as threats evolve | Monitor metrics, reassess suppliers, review incidents, update threat models, repeat exercises | Periodic risk reports, control effectiveness assessments, updated risk register |

The proposed timeframes are illustrative. A high-risk financial institution or enterprise with significant existing control deficiencies may require accelerated remediation, while complex technology changes may need additional implementation time.

### 12.3 Prioritization Using Risk-Based Decision Making

A useful prioritization model evaluates four dimensions:

1. **Business impact:** What would happen if the scenario succeeded?
2. **Exploitability:** How readily could an attacker exploit the weakness?
3. **Control deficiency:** Are existing controls absent, ineffective, or routinely bypassed?
4. **Implementation urgency:** Is immediate action required because of legal obligations, active exploitation, or a material exposure?

An illustrative prioritization matrix follows.

| Priority | Example condition | Required management response |
|---|---|---|
| P1 — Critical | High-value payments can be executed through a single unverified instruction | Immediate containment of the control gap and executive escalation |
| P2 — High | AI agents can modify sensitive records without independent authorization | Urgent permission review, restrictions, and remediation |
| P3 — Medium | Monitoring does not correlate supplier changes with subsequent payments | Planned implementation with accountable ownership and a defined deadline |
| P4 — Lower | Awareness materials do not explicitly cover synthetic-media impersonation | Include in the next appropriate training and awareness cycle |

Priority should reflect actual exposure, not merely the visibility of the technology involved.

For example, an unverified supplier bank-change process may deserve higher priority than an imperfect deepfake detection capability because the payment control can prevent the loss even if the synthetic media is never detected.

### 12.4 Control Implementation Plan

The organization should convert each identified weakness into a trackable remediation action.

| Action ID | Remediation action | Control owner | Target evidence | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IMP-01 | Enforce independent verification of supplier bank changes | Procurement | Approved procedure and sampled verification records | All in-scope changes follow the required verification workflow |
| IMP-02 | Enforce dual authorization for designated high-risk payments | Finance / Treasury | Workflow configuration and approval logs | Applicable payments cannot be released without required independent approval |
| IMP-03 | Review permissions assigned to AI agents interacting with financial systems | AI System Owner / IT Security | Permission inventory and access review evidence | No unjustified privileges remain within the defined scope |
| IMP-04 | Implement monitoring for beneficiary changes followed by unusual payments | SOC / Security Engineering | Detection rule, test results, and alert records | Defined test scenarios generate the expected alerts |
| IMP-05 | Establish a deepfake and executive-impersonation incident playbook | Incident Response Lead | Approved playbook and exercise records | Participants can identify escalation paths and execute required response actions |
| IMP-06 | Establish a regulatory assessment workflow | Legal / Compliance / Privacy | Decision tree and notification register | Relevant incidents receive documented applicability and deadline assessments |
| IMP-07 | Assess high-risk third parties supporting identity, payments, or AI workflows | Third-Party Risk Manager | Completed assessments and remediation records | Critical provider risks have documented treatment decisions |
| IMP-08 | Test employee reporting and independent verification procedures | Business Control Owners | Exercise records and test results | Employees use the approved verification and reporting channels |
| IMP-09 | Validate logging for sensitive AI-agent actions | AI System Owner / Security Engineering | Log samples and audit-trail test results | Relevant actions can be attributed to an identity and reconstructed |
| IMP-10 | Review residual risk after remediation | Enterprise Risk Owner | Updated risk register and approval records | Residual risk is reassessed and accepted or treated by the authorized owner |

A remediation item should not be marked complete solely because a policy has been published or a technical configuration has been changed. Completion requires evidence that the control operates as intended.

## 13. Control Testing and Assurance

### 13.1 Why Control Testing Matters

A control can exist in documentation and still fail under real operating conditions.

For example, a payment policy may require independent verification of supplier bank details. However, employees may still be able to modify a beneficiary and release a payment without the verification being recorded or enforced.

Control testing determines whether a control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

These are distinct assessments:

- **Design effectiveness:** Would the control, if performed as intended, adequately address the identified risk?
- **Implementation:** Has the control actually been deployed or established?
- **Operating effectiveness:** Does the control consistently operate as intended in practice?

A control may pass one assessment and fail another.

### 13.2 Control Testing Methodology

A structured control assessment should follow these steps.

**Step 1 — Define the risk.**

Identify the specific threat scenario and potential business consequence.

Example: An attacker impersonates a supplier and changes the supplier's bank details to divert payments.

**Step 2 — Identify the control objective.**

Define the outcome the organization needs to achieve.

Example: Unauthorized changes to supplier payment details must not result in payment to an unverified beneficiary.

**Step 3 — Identify the control activity.**

Determine which business or technical mechanism achieves the objective.

Example: Independent supplier verification, authorized master-data changes, and payment release restrictions.

**Step 4 — Define the control owner and frequency.**

Identify who performs the control and how often it is required.

Example: Procurement performs verification for each qualifying beneficiary change.

**Step 5 — Define the evidence.**

Identify the records needed to demonstrate operation.

Example: Change request, independently sourced contact details, verification record, approval evidence, and transaction logs.

**Step 6 — Design the test.**

Determine the population, sampling method, test procedure, and expected result.

**Step 7 — Execute the test.**

Inspect evidence, interview control operators, and where appropriate perform authorized technical tests.

**Step 8 — Evaluate the results.**

Record exceptions, identify root causes, and determine whether the control objective has been achieved.

**Step 9 — Remediate and retest.**

Assign remediation ownership, establish deadlines, and repeat testing to verify that the issue has been corrected.

### 13.3 Control Testing Matrix

| Control ID | Control objective | Test procedure | Expected result | Failure condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CT-01 | Prevent unauthorized supplier bank changes | Sample supplier changes and inspect independent verification evidence | Every sampled change meets the approved verification requirements | Change approved without required verification |
| CT-02 | Prevent unilateral release of high-risk payments | Inspect workflow configuration and test a controlled transaction scenario | Required independent authorization is enforced | Payment can be released without required approval |
| CT-03 | Restrict AI-agent privileges | Compare assigned permissions with approved business requirements | Only authorized permissions are present | Excessive or unjustified permissions remain |
| CT-04 | Detect suspicious beneficiary and payment sequences | Execute an authorized simulation using test data | Monitoring produces the expected alert | Relevant event sequence is not detected |
| CT-05 | Preserve accountability for AI-agent actions | Inspect logs for selected agent executions | Identity, action, relevant inputs or references, and outcome are traceable to the extent required | Sensitive action cannot be reconstructed |
| CT-06 | Ensure incident escalation is effective | Conduct a tabletop exercise | Required stakeholders are contacted and decisions are made within defined objectives | Escalation is delayed or authority is unclear |
| CT-07 | Ensure risk acceptance is controlled | Sample accepted risks and inspect approvals | Acceptance complies with delegated authority and review requirements | Unauthorized or undocumented acceptance |
| CT-08 | Validate third-party incident readiness | Review contractual provisions and conduct an agreed exercise where appropriate | Responsibilities and communication paths are understood and workable | Material ambiguity or inability to obtain required information |

### 13.4 Control Evidence Requirements

For each key control, maintain evidence that supports its existence and operation.

| Evidence category | Examples | Assurance value |
|---|---|---|
| Governance evidence | Approved policies, risk acceptance records, meeting decisions | Demonstrates authorization and accountability |
| Process evidence | Verification forms, workflow records, approval history | Demonstrates business-process execution |
| Technical evidence | Access configuration, authentication settings, agent permissions | Demonstrates technical control implementation |
| Monitoring evidence | Alerts, correlation results, incident tickets | Demonstrates detection activity |
| Testing evidence | Test scripts, expected results, observed results, defect records | Demonstrates validation |
| Remediation evidence | Change records, retest results, closure approvals | Demonstrates correction of identified deficiencies |
| Training evidence | Attendance, completion records, scenario-based assessments | Demonstrates participation, but not necessarily behavioral effectiveness |

Evidence should be relevant, authentic, sufficiently complete, and protected against unauthorized modification.

For sensitive incidents, the organization should preserve evidence in accordance with its retention requirements, privacy obligations, and investigation procedures.

### 13.5 Testing AI-Specific Controls

AI-enabled business workflows require additional testing because their behavior may depend on prompts, retrieved content, tool permissions, integrations, and model outputs.

A suitable testing program should include the following areas.

**A. Untrusted content handling**

Test whether emails, documents, and other external content can cause an AI system to bypass its intended role or initiate unauthorized actions.

**B. Tool permission enforcement**

Verify that the AI agent cannot invoke unauthorized functions, access restricted records, or perform actions outside its assigned scope.

**C. Human approval enforcement**

Test whether the application prevents consequential actions until the required independent approval is completed.

**D. Input and output validation**

Verify that critical transaction fields are validated against authoritative sources and business rules before execution.

**E. Logging and traceability**

Determine whether the organization can reconstruct which identity initiated an action, which tools were invoked, which approvals were obtained, and what outcome followed.

**F. Failure handling**

Test what happens when verification services are unavailable, required data is missing, or the system cannot establish sufficient confidence in a transaction.

For high-impact actions, the expected behavior should be safe escalation or refusal to proceed rather than silent execution.

Testing should be conducted within an authorized environment, using synthetic data and controlled scenarios wherever practical.

### 13.6 Control Effectiveness Scoring

Organizations may use a consistent scoring model to summarize control performance.

An illustrative model is:

| Score | Rating | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent | No relevant control exists |
| 1 | Ineffective | A control exists but does not adequately address the risk |
| 2 | Partially effective | The control addresses part of the risk, but material weaknesses remain |
| 3 | Mostly effective | The control generally works, with limited deficiencies requiring remediation |
| 4 | Effective | The control is appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively based on sufficient evidence |

This scale is illustrative, not a universal standard.

The organization should define its scoring criteria, evidence requirements, aggregation methodology, and treatment of critical exceptions before using the scores for management reporting.

A high average score should never conceal a critical control failure. For example, effective security awareness training cannot compensate for a payment system that permits unauthorized transactions without required approvals.

## 14. Crisis Simulation: Executive Deepfake and Fraudulent Payment

### 14.1 Scenario Overview

The enterprise conducts a crisis simulation to evaluate whether its preventive controls, detection capabilities, governance arrangements, and response procedures can withstand a coordinated AI-enabled fraud attempt.

The exercise involves the following conditions:

- A finance employee receives an urgent payment request apparently originating from the CEO.
- The request is reinforced by a synthetic voice call.
- The payment is described as a confidential acquisition-related transaction.
- The requested beneficiary is unfamiliar to the finance team.
- The attacker pressures the employee to bypass the normal approval process.
- A separate supplier communication contains a suspicious change to bank details.
- The enterprise's AI assistant has access to internal email and selected finance workflows.

The objective is not to test whether participants can identify a technically perfect deepfake. The objective is to determine whether the enterprise's control environment prevents deception from producing an unauthorized business outcome.

### 14.2 Exercise Objectives

The exercise should test whether the organization can:

1. Identify the suspicious request.
2. Verify the purported executive through an independent channel.
3. Prevent an unauthorized payment.
4. Escalate the incident to the correct functions.
5. Protect potentially compromised identities and systems.
6. Preserve relevant evidence.
7. Assess potential regulatory and contractual obligations.
8. Coordinate financial recovery if a transfer has already occurred.
9. Communicate consistently with affected stakeholders.
10. Identify and remediate control weaknesses.

### 14.3 Exercise Participants

| Participant | Role in the exercise |
|---|---|
| Exercise controller | Introduces scenario developments and records observations |
| Finance employee | Receives the suspicious payment request |
| Finance manager | Determines whether the payment workflow is being followed |
| Treasury representative | Evaluates transaction restrictions and financial recovery actions |
| Procurement representative | Investigates the suspicious supplier bank change |
| SOC analyst | Reviews security telemetry and related alerts |
| Incident response lead | Coordinates the cybersecurity investigation |
| Cybersecurity GRC representative | Evaluates control gaps and risk escalation |
| AI system owner | Investigates whether AI automation contributed to the incident |
| Legal representative | Advises on legal issues and evidence preservation |
| Privacy representative, where relevant | Assesses personal data implications |
| Communications representative | Coordinates internal and external communications |
| Executive sponsor | Resolves escalated business decisions |
| Observer or independent evaluator | Records control performance and identifies improvement opportunities |

Internal Audit may observe or independently assess the exercise according to its mandate, while preserving its independence from operational ownership.

### 14.4 Exercise Timeline and Injects

An exercise inject is a new piece of information introduced during a simulation to test how participants respond.

| Time | Inject | Expected decision | Evidence of effective response |
|---|---|---|---|
| T+00 | Finance receives an urgent payment request apparently from the CEO | Determine whether the request is authenticated and authorized | Employee follows approved verification procedures |
| T+05 | A convincing synthetic voice call reinforces the request | Decide whether the voice call is sufficient proof of identity | Employee uses an independent verification channel |
| T+10 | The request demands confidentiality and immediate execution | Determine whether urgency justifies an exception | Employee refuses to bypass mandatory controls |
| T+15 | The beneficiary is new and the payment exceeds the normal threshold | Determine whether the transaction can proceed | Required verification and approvals are enforced |
| T+20 | The AI assistant extracts payment details and offers to prepare the transaction | Determine whether the AI workflow may proceed | Agent permissions and application authorization prevent unapproved execution |
| T+25 | The SOC identifies an unusual authentication event affecting a finance account | Determine whether the event is related to the fraud attempt | Relevant alerts are correlated and escalated |
| T+30 | Procurement discovers a recent supplier bank-change request | Determine whether the supplier change is legitimate | Independent verification is initiated and the change is controlled |
| T+40 | The exercise controller reveals that a payment was released in a simulated environment | Activate the financial fraud response process | Treasury follows the defined escalation and recovery procedures |
| T+50 | The purported CEO denies issuing the request | Determine the scope of the investigation | The incident is treated as a confirmed impersonation attempt, subject to evidence assessment |
| T+60 | The controller introduces possible exposure of employee contact information | Assess privacy and legal implications | Relevant specialists evaluate the facts and applicable notification requirements |
| T+75 | A journalist requests comment on alleged executive impersonation | Determine who is authorized to communicate externally | Communications and Legal coordinate an approved response |
| T+90 | The exercise concludes | Identify control deficiencies and assign remediation | Findings are documented with owners and target dates |

The times are illustrative. The exercise controller should adapt them to the organization's operating environment.

### 14.5 Expected Decision Logic

The exercise should evaluate the reasoning behind each decision, not merely whether participants followed a script.

For example, when the finance employee receives the suspicious request, the correct response is not necessarily to declare that the voice is fake. The employee may have insufficient evidence to make that determination.

The required decision is to refuse to execute the transaction until the established identity verification and authorization requirements have been satisfied.

Similarly, the SOC should not need to prove that a recording is synthetic before escalating a credible attempt to manipulate a high-value transaction.

The exercise therefore evaluates the following control sequence:

```text id="crisis2120"
SUSPICIOUS REQUEST RECEIVED
          |
          v
IDENTITY AND REQUEST VERIFICATION
          |
          v
VERIFICATION INCOMPLETE OR SUSPICIOUS?
          |
          +------ YES ------+
          |                 |
          |                 v
          |          DO NOT EXECUTE
          |                 |
          |                 v
          |          ESCALATE AND PRESERVE EVIDENCE
          |                 |
          |                 v
          |          INVESTIGATE RELATED ACTIVITY
          |
          +------ NO -------+
                    |
                    v
         VALIDATE TRANSACTION AUTHORITY
                    |
                    v
         ENFORCE REQUIRED APPROVALS
                    |
                    v
         EXECUTE ONLY IF ALL CONDITIONS
         ARE SATISFIED
```

A successful identity check does not eliminate the need for transaction authorization. Both conditions must be satisfied.

### 14.6 Exercise Evaluation Scorecard

| Evaluation area | Measurement | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Verification discipline | Whether participants used independent verification | Exercise observation and procedure compliance |
| Payment control enforcement | Whether required approval gates prevented unauthorized execution | Workflow records and controller observations |
| Escalation effectiveness | Whether the correct stakeholders were notified | Incident timeline and escalation log |
| Detection capability | Whether relevant simulated events triggered expected alerts | SIEM or test-platform evidence |
| AI control effectiveness | Whether the AI assistant remained within authorized permissions | Agent logs and application authorization results |
| Evidence preservation | Whether relevant records were identified and protected | Evidence register |
| Regulatory assessment | Whether relevant notification obligations were evaluated | Documented decision record |
| Financial response | Whether the correct recovery process was initiated | Treasury and incident response records |
| Communications governance | Whether external statements were appropriately controlled | Approved communication record |
| Remediation quality | Whether findings were converted into actionable improvements | Corrective action register |

### 14.7 Post-Exercise Findings

The exercise should conclude with a structured lessons-learned review.

Potential findings include:

- Employees cannot easily identify the approved independent verification channel.
- The payment application permits an exception without adequate approval.
- Supplier bank changes are not linked to subsequent payment monitoring.
- The SOC cannot correlate AI-agent activity with financial transactions.
- The AI assistant has excessive privileges.
- Incident escalation depends on informal personal contacts.
- Legal and privacy teams are involved too late.
- The organization cannot establish whether a simulated payment was approved through the required workflow.
- External communications responsibilities are unclear.
- Remediation actions lack accountable owners or deadlines.

Each finding should include:

1. A description of the control deficiency.
2. The associated risk scenario.
3. The root cause.
4. The business impact.
5. The remediation action.
6. The accountable owner.
7. The target completion date.
8. The evidence required for closure.
9. The retesting approach.
10. The residual risk if remediation is delayed.

The organization should not close a finding merely because the exercise is complete. Closure requires evidence that the underlying weakness has been corrected or that an authorized, documented risk treatment decision has been made.

## 15. Key Risk Indicators, Key Performance Indicators, and Management Reporting

### 15.1 Why Metrics Matter

A mature AI security governance program must demonstrate whether the organization's exposure is decreasing and whether its controls are operating effectively.

However, metrics can create a false sense of assurance when they measure activity instead of outcomes.

For example, the number of employees who complete deepfake awareness training indicates training completion. It does not demonstrate that employees will independently verify a suspicious payment request under pressure.

The enterprise should combine:

- **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs):** Signals that exposure may be increasing or that risk thresholds may be approaching.
- **Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):** Measures of process performance and delivery.
- **Key Control Indicators (KCIs):** Measures of control operation and effectiveness, where the organization uses this category.

The definitions and calculations should be standardized so that different business units report comparable results.

### 15.2 Key Risk Indicators

| KRI | Measurement | Why it matters | Example escalation condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reported executive impersonation attempts | Number of substantiated or credible attempts per reporting period | Indicates targeting and potential exposure | Material increase or a coordinated campaign |
| Unverified supplier bank changes | Number or proportion of changes lacking required verification | Indicates potential payment-diversion exposure | Any prohibited unverified change |
| High-risk payment exceptions | Number of payments processed through exception procedures | Indicates possible control bypass | Exceeding an approved threshold or recurring unauthorized exceptions |
| Unauthorized AI-agent actions | Number of confirmed actions outside approved scope | Indicates AI governance or access-control failure | Any material unauthorized action |
| Fraud-related financial loss | Confirmed loss attributable to relevant incidents | Measures realized impact | Exceeding the approved loss tolerance |
| Material incident recurrence | Number of repeat incidents with the same underlying control weakness | Indicates ineffective remediation | Repeated occurrence after a control was declared effective |
| High-risk supplier deficiencies | Number of critical providers with unresolved material control gaps | Indicates supply-chain exposure | Critical deficiencies remain beyond the approved remediation deadline |
| Unresolved critical findings | Number of overdue critical remediation actions | Indicates accumulated control debt | Any finding exceeding its approved deadline without an authorized treatment decision |

Thresholds should be established using the organization's risk appetite, historical data, and operational context.

An increase in reported impersonation attempts does not necessarily mean controls are deteriorating. It may also reflect improved employee reporting or greater detection capability. KRIs must therefore be interpreted alongside incident outcomes, control performance, and changes in reporting behavior.

### 15.3 Key Performance Indicators

| KPI | Calculation or measurement | Management purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Time to acknowledge a high-priority alert | Time between alert generation and acknowledgment | Measures monitoring responsiveness |
| Time to contain a confirmed incident | Time between confirmation and effective containment | Measures incident response performance |
| Time to initiate financial recovery | Time between suspicion or confirmation and initiation of the applicable recovery process | Measures coordination with financial institutions |
| Remediation completion rate | Remediation actions completed by their approved deadlines divided by actions due | Measures delivery discipline |
| Exercise completion rate | Planned exercises completed divided by planned exercises | Measures execution of the assurance program |
| Supplier assessment coverage | In-scope high-risk suppliers assessed divided by total in-scope high-risk suppliers | Measures third-party assurance coverage |
| Control testing completion | Planned tests completed divided by tests scheduled | Measures assurance execution |
| Retest pass rate | Remediated findings that pass retesting divided by findings retested | Indicates whether remediation is effective |
| Employee verification exercise success | Participants correctly following required verification procedures divided by participants tested | Measures demonstrated procedural competence |

KPI definitions should specify the start and end points, exclusions, reporting frequency, and data source.

For example, incident containment time must distinguish between the time an incident is reported and the time the relevant threat is actually contained. Otherwise, different teams may report apparently comparable metrics that measure different things.

### 15.4 Key Control Indicators

The organization may also track direct evidence of control operation.

| KCI | Measurement | Intended assurance |
|---|---|---|
| Independent verification compliance | Percentage of sampled supplier changes with complete required verification evidence | Confirms operation of beneficiary-change controls |
| Dual authorization compliance | Percentage of applicable payments with valid independent approvals | Confirms payment authorization enforcement |
| AI-agent permission review coverage | Percentage of in-scope agents reviewed within the required period | Confirms access governance coverage |
| AI-agent auditability | Percentage of sampled consequential actions with sufficient traceability | Confirms investigation capability |
| Detection rule validation | Percentage of critical monitoring rules tested according to schedule | Confirms that detection logic is being validated |
| Incident playbook readiness | Percentage of required playbooks reviewed and exercised within the defined period | Confirms response preparation |
| Risk acceptance compliance | Percentage of sampled accepted risks with valid approval and review dates | Confirms risk governance discipline |

A high compliance percentage is not sufficient if the underlying control is poorly designed. Control testing must evaluate whether the control achieves the intended objective, not just whether a record exists.

### 15.5 Executive Dashboard Design

A management dashboard should communicate the organization's exposure, the effectiveness of its controls, and the decisions required from leadership.

A useful dashboard contains five sections.

**Section 1 — Enterprise risk posture**

- Overall AI-enabled fraud risk rating.
- Material changes since the previous reporting period.
- Highest-risk business processes.
- Significant risk acceptance decisions.
- Emerging threats requiring management attention.

**Section 2 — Control effectiveness**

- Payment authorization compliance.
- Supplier verification compliance.
- AI-agent access-control status.
- Critical control failures.
- Overdue remediation actions.

**Section 3 — Incident performance**

- Confirmed incidents and attempted fraud.
- Financial losses and recovery status.
- Incident containment times.
- Recurring root causes.
- Material regulatory or contractual implications.

**Section 4 — Third-party exposure**

- High-risk suppliers and AI providers.
- Critical unresolved vendor findings.
- Material incidents affecting third parties.
- Contractual remediation issues.

**Section 5 — Management decisions**

- Funding or resource requirements.
- Decisions needed to resolve control deficiencies.
- Risk acceptance requests.
- Overdue actions requiring escalation.
- Decisions that could affect business continuity or risk appetite.

The dashboard should distinguish verified facts, estimates, and unresolved uncertainties. For example, an amount suspected to have been diverted should not be reported as a confirmed financial loss until its status has been established.

### 15.6 Reporting Cadence

An illustrative reporting model is:

| Reporting level | Frequency | Primary audience | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operational monitoring | Continuous or near-real-time, where supported | SOC, Fraud Operations, Finance Operations | Alerts, transactions, suspicious activity |
| Control owner review | Monthly | Finance, Procurement, IT, AI system owners | Control performance and remediation |
| Enterprise risk review | Monthly or quarterly, according to risk | CRO, CISO, risk committee | Material risk exposure and risk treatment |
| Executive risk reporting | Quarterly or when significant events occur | Executive leadership and relevant committees | Enterprise exposure and decisions |
| Board-level oversight | According to governance requirements and materiality | Board or relevant committee | Significant risk, major incidents, resilience, and management accountability |
| Independent assurance | Risk-based audit schedule | Audit Committee and management | Control design and operating effectiveness |

The cadence should be increased when the organization experiences a material incident, a significant change in exposure, or repeated control failures.

## 16. Lessons Learned and Continuous Improvement

### 16.1 Lessons from the Enterprise Case Study

The central lesson is that synthetic media changes the economics and scale of impersonation, but many successful attacks still depend on familiar weaknesses in identity assurance, authorization, and human decision-making.

An attacker may use advanced generative AI to produce a convincing executive voice. Nevertheless, the attacker still needs the organization to trust the instruction, bypass a control, disclose information, or execute an action.

This creates several durable governance lessons.

#### Lesson 1 — Protect decisions, not appearances

An organization should not depend exclusively on determining whether a voice, image, or video is authentic.

Even authentic communication can contain an unauthorized request, and synthetic communication may be difficult to identify reliably.

The critical control is to verify the identity, authority, and legitimacy of the requested action through the appropriate independent process.

#### Lesson 2 — Business-process controls are cybersecurity controls

Payment approval, supplier verification, account recovery, and customer onboarding may be owned by business functions, but they directly affect cybersecurity and fraud exposure.

GRC should connect these processes to the threat model and ensure that control ownership is explicit.

#### Lesson 3 — AI agents can amplify existing weaknesses

An AI agent may accelerate the processing of emails, supplier information, and financial transactions. If the underlying workflow lacks independent verification, automation may increase the speed and scale at which an error or fraudulent instruction becomes an operational outcome.

Automation should therefore inherit appropriate authorization constraints, not bypass them.

#### Lesson 4 — Detection is not prevention

A deepfake detection system may identify suspicious content, but a payment can still be released before the alert is generated or reviewed.

Independent approval and transaction controls must remain effective even when detection technology fails.

#### Lesson 5 — Human awareness must be supported by enforceable controls

Employees should be trained to recognize urgency, secrecy, authority pressure, and requests to bypass normal procedures.

However, the enterprise should not rely on individual vigilance as its primary safeguard. The process should make unauthorized actions difficult or impossible, even when an employee is deceived.

#### Lesson 6 — Incident response must include Finance and Legal

Cybersecurity teams cannot independently manage financial recovery, regulatory interpretation, contractual issues, and public communications.

An effective incident response capability requires predefined responsibilities and tested coordination across the organization.

#### Lesson 7 — Third-party trust must be independently validated

An established supplier relationship does not make every communication from that supplier trustworthy.

High-impact changes must be validated through independent procedures, especially when they affect payment destinations, access privileges, or sensitive information.

#### Lesson 8 — Control effectiveness requires evidence

A policy, certification, risk assessment, or completed training course does not prove that a control works under pressure.

The organization should test critical controls using realistic scenarios and preserve evidence of the results.

#### Lesson 9 — Risk acceptance must remain accountable

Management may choose to accept certain residual risks, but acceptance must follow the approved authority structure and remain consistent with mandatory requirements.

A recurring control failure should not be normalized through repeated exceptions.

#### Lesson 10 — Governance must adapt as the threat changes

New AI capabilities, new business integrations, and changing attacker techniques may invalidate previous assumptions.

Threat models, risk assessments, vendor reviews, detection rules, and incident exercises should be updated when material changes occur.

The enterprise should establish a repeatable improvement cycle.

### 16.3 Maturity Model for AI-Enabled Fraud Governance

The following maturity model can help the organization determine its current capability and define realistic improvement targets.

| Level | Name | Characteristics | Typical weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1 | Ad hoc | Fraud response depends on individual judgment; procedures are incomplete | Inconsistent verification, unclear ownership, limited evidence |
| Level 2 | Defined | Policies, basic procedures, and named owners exist | Controls may not be technically enforced or consistently tested |
| Level 3 | Implemented | Standardized controls are deployed across relevant business processes | Limited cross-system correlation or inconsistent third-party assurance |
| Level 4 | Measured | Controls are regularly tested; metrics and risk reporting support management decisions | Some dependencies on manual processes or incomplete automation |
| Level 5 | Adaptive | Governance integrates lessons from incidents, threat changes, testing, and AI-system changes | Requires sustained investment, independent assurance, and continuous recalibration |

The organization should assess maturity by control domain rather than assigning one score to the entire enterprise.

For example, payment authorization may be at Level 4 while AI-agent governance remains at Level 2. A single enterprise-wide maturity score could conceal that difference.

### 16.4 Target-State Capabilities

A mature enterprise should be able to demonstrate that:

- Critical payment and identity workflows have documented threat models.
- High-impact actions require independently verified authorization.
- AI agents have restricted permissions and enforceable execution boundaries.
- Security monitoring can correlate relevant identity, application, and transaction events.
- Incident response procedures define cross-functional responsibilities.
- Third-party assurance addresses the risks relevant to the service.
- Legal and regulatory assessments are documented and time-sensitive.
- Critical controls are tested using representative scenarios.
- Risk acceptance is formally authorized and periodically reviewed.
- Management receives accurate metrics and actionable risk information.
- Remediation is verified before critical findings are closed.

These capabilities should be supported by evidence, not merely stated in policies.

- Have the important fraud scenarios been identified?

**Control design**
- Do critical business actions require independent verification and authorization?
- Are payment and identity controls enforced by the relevant systems?
- Are AI-agent permissions restricted according to business need?

**Control operation**
- Is there evidence that required controls operate consistently?
- Have critical controls been tested?
- Are exceptions investigated and resolved?

**Incident readiness**
- Can the enterprise contain a suspicious transaction quickly?
- Can the SOC, Finance, Procurement, Legal, and business leadership coordinate effectively?

- Have relevant legal frameworks been assessed for applicability?
- Are notification decisions documented?

- Does every material risk have an accountable owner?
- Are remediation actions assigned to people with the authority to complete them?
- Are residual risks accepted at the appropriate level?

**Management assurance**
- Are the metrics reliable and decision-oriented?
- Can management identify the most significant unresolved weaknesses?
- Is there evidence that corrective actions reduce risk?

If these questions cannot be answered with credible evidence, the enterprise should not claim that its AI-enabled fraud governance capability is fully effective.

## Core Principle

**The success of AI security governance is measured by the enterprise's ability to prevent unauthorized outcomes, detect control failures, respond effectively, and demonstrate sustained control effectiveness.** Detecting a deepfake is useful, but preventing an unverified instruction from becoming an unauthorized payment, disclosure, or system action is the more fundamental objective.

## Key Conceptual Distinctions

| Concept | Definition | Practical significance |
|---|---|---|
| Implementation roadmap | Sequenced plan for introducing or improving controls | Converts risk findings into accountable work |
| Control design effectiveness | Whether a control is capable of addressing the intended risk | Determines whether the chosen control is appropriate |
| Control operating effectiveness | Whether a control consistently operates as intended | Determines whether the control works in practice |
| Control testing | Structured evaluation of a control against defined criteria | Produces evidence for assurance and remediation |
| AI-specific testing | Evaluation of AI inputs, permissions, outputs, tools, and action boundaries | Identifies weaknesses that ordinary application testing may miss |
| Crisis simulation | Controlled exercise of incident decisions and response procedures | Tests coordination and readiness without requiring a real incident |
| KRI | Indicator of risk exposure or changing risk conditions | Supports early warning and risk escalation |
| KPI | Measure of process performance or delivery | Shows whether planned activities are being completed effectively |
| KCI | Measure of control operation or performance | Supports monitoring of control compliance and effectiveness |
