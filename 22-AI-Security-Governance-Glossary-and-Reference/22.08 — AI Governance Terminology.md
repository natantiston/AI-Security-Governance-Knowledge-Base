# 22.08 — AI Governance Terminology

## Part 1 — AI Governance Foundations, Principles, Organizational Structures, and Accountability

### 1. AI Governance Fundamentals

AI governance establishes the organizational structures, policies, processes, roles, and controls that direct and oversee the responsible development, procurement, deployment, operation, and retirement of AI systems.

It ensures that AI initiatives remain aligned with organizational objectives, applicable legal requirements, ethical principles, security requirements, and stakeholder expectations.

AI governance is not limited to technical teams. It involves executive management, business owners, cybersecurity, legal, compliance, privacy, risk management, procurement, internal audit, and other relevant functions.

#### 1.1 AI Governance

AI Governance refers to the system of leadership, organizational arrangements, policies, decision-making processes, accountability mechanisms, and oversight activities used to direct and control an organization's use of AI.

It establishes:

- Who can approve AI initiatives.
- Who owns AI systems and associated risks.
- Which AI use cases are permitted.
- What standards and controls must be followed.
- How AI systems are assessed and monitored.
- How incidents and governance violations are handled.
- How compliance and accountability are demonstrated.

Example:

A telecommunications operator wants to introduce an AI-powered customer-service platform using an external LLM.

AI governance determines:

1. Whether the business is authorized to use generative AI for customer interactions.
2. Whether customer information can be processed by the selected provider.
3. Which security and privacy assessments are required.
4. Who approves the AI architecture.
5. Whether the system can access Salesforce customer records.
6. Which activities require human approval.
7. Who accepts the remaining business and technology risks.
8. How the system will be monitored after deployment.

AI governance provides the organizational authority and control structure for these decisions.

#### 1.2 AI Governance Framework

An AI Governance Framework is a structured collection of principles, policies, standards, procedures, responsibilities, and oversight mechanisms used to manage AI activities.

A typical framework contains:

| Governance Component          | Purpose                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Governance Principles      | Establish the organization's expectations for AI use    |
| AI Policy                     | Define mandatory organizational requirements            |
| AI Standards                  | Establish minimum technical and governance requirements |
| AI Procedures                 | Explain how governance activities are performed         |
| AI Roles and Responsibilities | Assign ownership and accountability                     |
| AI Risk Management            | Identify, assess, treat, and monitor AI risks           |
| AI Lifecycle Governance       | Control AI from conception through retirement           |
| AI Compliance Management      | Identify and manage applicable obligations              |
| AI Assurance                  | Evaluate governance and control effectiveness           |
| AI Reporting                  | Provide management visibility into AI activities        |

The framework should be integrated with existing enterprise governance rather than creating unnecessary parallel structures.

#### 1.3 AI Governance Operating Model

An AI Governance Operating Model defines how AI governance is organized and executed across the enterprise.

It establishes the relationship between executive oversight, business operations, technical implementation, risk management, and independent assurance.

A typical operating model includes:

- Board and executive oversight.
- AI governance committee.
- AI governance office or center of excellence.
- Business and AI system ownership.
- AI engineering and technology operations.
- Cybersecurity and privacy oversight.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Internal audit and independent assurance.

The operating model should define decision rights, escalation paths, approval authorities, and reporting responsibilities.

#### 1.4 AI Governance Policy

An AI Governance Policy is a formally approved document that establishes mandatory organizational requirements for the responsible use and management of AI.

Typical policy requirements include:

- AI system registration.
- Acceptable and prohibited AI use.
- AI risk assessments.
- Data protection requirements.
- Security requirements.
- Human oversight.
- Third-party AI procurement.
- Model validation and monitoring.
- Transparency and documentation.
- Incident reporting.
- Risk acceptance.
- AI system retirement.

Example:

An organization may establish a policy requiring every AI system processing confidential business information to undergo security, privacy, and third-party risk assessments before production deployment.

The policy defines the requirement; supporting standards and procedures explain how to implement it.

#### 1.5 AI Governance Standard

An AI Governance Standard defines mandatory requirements and minimum control expectations that support the organization's AI governance policy.

Examples include:

- AI system inventory standard.
- AI risk classification standard.
- AI data governance standard.
- AI model validation standard.
- AI security standard.
- AI third-party assessment standard.
- AI logging and monitoring standard.
- AI change management standard.

Distinction: A policy establishes what the organization requires. A standard defines mandatory requirements for satisfying the policy. A procedure explains the steps for carrying out the requirements.

#### 1.6 AI Governance Procedure

An AI Governance Procedure describes the operational steps required to execute a governance activity.

Example: AI system approval procedure.

```
Business submits AI use case
          |
          v
AI system registration
          |
          v
Initial governance screening
          |
          v
Risk classification
          |
          v
Security / Privacy / Legal assessment
          |
          v
Architecture and technical review
          |
          v
Risk treatment and remediation
          |
          v
Approval by authorized stakeholders
          |
          v
Production deployment
          |
          v
Continuous monitoring
```

The procedure should identify required inputs, responsible functions, approval authorities, evidence, and expected outputs.

#### 1.7 AI Governance Charter

An AI Governance Charter defines the mandate, authority, responsibilities, membership, and operating rules of an AI governance committee or governance body.

It commonly establishes:

- Governance objectives.
- Scope of authority.
- Committee membership.
- Roles and responsibilities.
- Meeting frequency.
- Quorum and voting arrangements.
- Decision-making authority.
- Escalation requirements.
- Reporting obligations.
- Record-keeping requirements.

A charter prevents the governance committee from becoming an informal discussion group without enforceable decision rights.

#### 1.8 AI Governance Maturity

AI Governance Maturity describes the extent to which an organization has established, standardized, measured, and continuously improved its AI governance capabilities.

An illustrative maturity model:

| Level   | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                  |
| ------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Initial    | AI adoption is informal; ownership and controls are inconsistent                 |
| Level 2 | Developing | Basic policies, inventory, and assessments are emerging                          |
| Level 3 | Defined    | Governance processes and responsibilities are formally established               |
| Level 4 | Managed    | Governance performance and control effectiveness are measured                    |
| Level 5 | Optimized  | Governance is continuously improved using evidence, metrics, and lessons learned |

This is an internal maturity model, not an official maturity scale prescribed by ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF.

### 2. AI Governance Principles and Organizational Commitments

AI governance principles establish the values and expectations that guide organizational decisions about AI.

They provide a basis for policies, control requirements, system assessments, and management decisions.

#### 2.1 Responsible AI

Responsible AI refers to the development, deployment, and use of AI in ways that align with organizational responsibilities, applicable laws, ethical expectations, and stakeholder interests.

Responsible AI considers:

- Fairness.
- Accountability.
- Transparency.
- Privacy.
- Security.
- Safety.
- Reliability.
- Human oversight.
- Appropriate use.

Responsible AI is an organizational commitment, not a specific technology or certification.

#### 2.2 Trustworthy AI

Trustworthy AI refers to AI systems and their use that demonstrate relevant characteristics supporting trust, such as validity, reliability, safety, security, transparency, accountability, privacy, and fairness.

Trustworthiness must be evaluated in context.

For example, an AI system used to recommend telecommunications service plans may require different assurance measures from an AI system used to support medical diagnosis.

A system should not be described as trustworthy solely because it uses a recognized model or has passed a technical test.

#### 2.3 Human-Centric AI

Human-Centric AI is an approach that places human rights, human well-being, human agency, and human interests at the center of AI development and deployment.

It emphasizes:

- Human autonomy.
- Human rights.
- Meaningful human oversight.
- Accessibility.
- User understanding.
- Appropriate intervention.
- Protection against harmful outcomes.

Example:

An AI system recommends terminating a customer's telecommunications service because of suspected fraud. A human decision-maker reviews the evidence and has the authority to challenge the recommendation before the termination takes place.

#### 2.4 Fairness

Fairness refers to the consideration of whether AI systems produce unjustified or discriminatory outcomes for individuals or groups.

Fairness must be evaluated according to the system's context, intended purpose, affected population, applicable law, and relevant ethical considerations.

Governance measures may include:

- Representative data evaluation.
- Bias testing.
- Outcome analysis.
- Disparate impact assessment.
- Documented fairness criteria.
- Corrective actions.
- Ongoing monitoring.

Fairness is not a single universal mathematical metric. Different fairness measures may conflict, and the appropriate approach depends on context.

#### 2.5 Accountability

Accountability means that individuals and organizational functions can be held responsible for decisions, actions, and outcomes associated with AI systems.

It requires:

- Assigned ownership.
- Documented decision authority.
- Traceable approvals.
- Defined escalation paths.
- Evidence of control implementation.
- Management oversight.
- Consequences for governance violations.

An organization cannot establish accountability merely by stating that an AI model made a decision.

#### 2.6 Transparency

Transparency means providing appropriate information about an AI system, its purpose, operation, limitations, and use.

Transparency may involve:

- Disclosure that AI is being used.
- Identification of the AI provider.
- Explanation of system capabilities and limitations.
- Information about data usage.
- Disclosure of relevant automated processes.
- Documentation of system behavior and decisions.

Transparency requirements depend on the audience, system context, and applicable obligations.

#### 2.7 Explainability

Explainability refers to the ability to provide meaningful explanations about AI system behavior or outputs.

Examples include:

- Identifying factors influencing a model prediction.
- Explaining why a customer was assigned a particular risk score.
- Providing the principal factors behind a recommendation.
- Describing the limitations of a model-generated result.

Explainability does not necessarily mean that every internal model operation can be fully interpreted.

#### 2.8 Interpretability

Interpretability refers to the extent to which the internal mechanisms or behavior of a model can be understood by humans.

It is related to explainability but is not identical.

For example:

- A simple decision tree may be relatively interpretable because its decision logic can be inspected.
- A complex neural network may be less directly interpretable, requiring additional explanation techniques.

#### 2.9 Privacy by Design

Privacy by Design means incorporating privacy considerations into the architecture, development, and operation of systems from the beginning rather than treating privacy as a final compliance activity.

Examples:

- Data minimization.
- Purpose limitation.
- Privacy-aware architecture.
- Access restrictions.
- Retention controls.
- Pseudonymization where appropriate.
- Privacy risk assessment.
- Data subject rights processes.

#### 2.10 Security by Design

Security by Design means integrating security requirements into the design, development, procurement, deployment, and operation of AI systems.

Examples:

- Threat modeling.
- Secure architecture.
- Least-privilege access.
- Secure API integration.
- Model artifact integrity.
- Secrets management.
- Security testing.
- Vulnerability management.
- Security monitoring.

Security by Design requires enforceable controls, not merely security statements in project documentation.

#### 2.11 Safety by Design

Safety by Design means incorporating safeguards to prevent or reduce unacceptable harm arising from AI system behavior and use.

Examples:

- Restrictions on dangerous outputs.
- Safety testing.
- Fail-safe behavior.
- Human intervention.
- Operational constraints.
- Monitoring of harmful outcomes.
- Defined shutdown procedures.

Safety and cybersecurity are related but distinct. A system can be technically secure and still produce unsafe recommendations.

#### 2.12 Human Oversight

Human Oversight refers to the organizational and technical arrangements that allow appropriate human supervision, intervention, review, or control over AI systems.

Effective oversight requires more than assigning a person to observe system outputs.

It should establish:

- A competent human reviewer.
- Access to relevant information.
- Sufficient time to evaluate outputs.
- Authority to override or stop the system.
- Clear escalation procedures.
- Training and defined responsibilities.

#### 2.13 Proportionality

Proportionality means applying governance, risk management, and control measures appropriate to the nature, context, and level of risk associated with an AI system.

For example, an internal AI tool that summarizes publicly available information may require fewer controls than an AI system that influences credit eligibility or critical infrastructure operations.

Proportionality does not mean exempting low-risk systems from all governance requirements.

#### 2.14 Accountability and Human Responsibility

AI systems may generate recommendations, classifications, predictions, and automated actions, but organizations must establish responsibility for their development and use.

The existence of an AI system does not remove the need for accountable human and organizational decision-making.

A governance framework should prevent responsibility from becoming fragmented between the business owner, model provider, system integrator, and technology operator.

### 3. AI Governance Organizational Structures

An effective AI governance structure ensures that AI decisions are made by authorized stakeholders and that risk responsibilities are clearly assigned.

#### 3.1 Board-Level AI Oversight

Board-Level AI Oversight refers to the governing body's responsibility for overseeing the organization's strategic adoption and use of AI.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Reviewing the AI strategy.
- Understanding material AI-related risks.
- Overseeing AI governance arrangements.
- Reviewing significant incidents.
- Monitoring regulatory exposure.
- Challenging management decisions.
- Ensuring appropriate accountability and resources.

The board should receive information that supports meaningful oversight rather than excessive technical detail.

#### 3.2 Executive AI Steering Committee

An Executive AI Steering Committee is a senior management body that directs AI initiatives and ensures alignment with organizational strategy, business priorities, and risk appetite.

Potential members include:

- Chief Executive Officer.
- Chief Information Officer.
- Chief Technology Officer.
- Chief Information Security Officer.
- Chief Risk Officer.
- Chief Data Officer.
- Chief Legal Officer.
- Chief Financial Officer.
- Business unit executives.

Responsibilities may include:

- Approving strategic AI investments.
- Prioritizing AI initiatives.
- Resolving cross-functional conflicts.
- Reviewing material risks.
- Approving significant AI deployments.
- Allocating resources.
- Escalating matters to the board.

#### 3.3 AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee is a cross-functional governance body responsible for overseeing the organization's AI governance framework and associated operational activities.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Reviewing AI use cases.
- Reviewing risk classification.
- Monitoring governance compliance.
- Reviewing security and privacy assessments.
- Challenging risk treatment plans.
- Reviewing exceptions.
- Monitoring AI system inventories.
- Escalating material risks.

The committee's authority must be clearly defined. It should not assume responsibilities legally or operationally assigned to other functions.

#### 3.4 AI Governance Office

An AI Governance Office is a central organizational function that coordinates and supports AI governance activities.

It may be responsible for:

- Maintaining the AI governance framework.
- Managing AI policies and standards.
- Coordinating AI risk assessments.
- Maintaining the AI inventory.
- Tracking governance actions.
- Preparing management reports.
- Coordinating regulatory assessments.
- Supporting AI governance committees.
- Maintaining governance documentation.

The office can coordinate governance but should not automatically become the owner of every AI-related business risk.

#### 3.5 AI Center of Excellence (AI CoE)

An AI Center of Excellence (AI CoE) is a centralized or federated organizational capability that develops AI expertise, reusable practices, standards, and implementation guidance.

Typical activities include:

- AI architecture guidance.
- Reusable AI design patterns.
- Model development standards.
- Approved AI platforms.
- Engineering best practices.
- AI experimentation support.
- Technical training.
- Model lifecycle guidance.

An AI CoE may support governance, but technical enablement and independent risk oversight should remain appropriately separated.

#### 3.6 AI Governance vs. AI Center of Excellence

| AI Governance Office                             | AI Center of Excellence                            |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on governance and oversight coordination | Focuses on technical and organizational enablement |
| Maintains policies and governance processes      | Develops reusable AI practices and patterns        |
| Coordinates risk and compliance activities       | Supports AI engineering and implementation         |
| Tracks governance requirements and exceptions    | Promotes consistency and technical quality         |
| Supports management accountability               | Supports AI adoption and capability development    |

Some organizations combine these functions, but they should preserve clear accountability and independent challenge.

Illustrative enterprise structure:

Internal audit should retain its independent assurance role and should not be embedded as an operational owner of AI governance controls.

#### 3.8 Centralized AI Governance

Centralized AI Governance is an operating approach in which governance authority, policies, standards, and oversight are managed primarily through a central organizational function.

Advantages:

- Consistent policies.
- Centralized risk visibility.
- Standardized assessments.
- Easier enterprise reporting.
- Consistent regulatory interpretation.

Potential limitations:

- Slower approval processes.
- Limited business-specific flexibility.
- Governance bottlenecks.
- Reduced local ownership.

#### 3.9 Federated AI Governance

Federated AI Governance distributes certain governance responsibilities across business units while maintaining enterprise-wide policies, standards, and oversight.

Advantages:

- Business-specific expertise.
- Faster local decisions.
- Clearer alignment with operational needs.
- Greater business ownership.

Potential limitations:

- Inconsistent implementation.
- Fragmented reporting.
- Duplicated governance resources.
- Uneven control maturity.

#### 3.10 Hybrid AI Governance

Hybrid AI Governance combines centralized governance authority with distributed business and technical execution.

For many large enterprises, this model provides a practical balance.

Example:

- Central AI Governance Office defines policies and risk methodology.
- Business units register and own AI use cases.
- Cybersecurity performs security oversight.
- Privacy evaluates applicable privacy risks.
- AI engineering implements technical controls.
- Internal Audit independently assesses governance effectiveness.

### 4. AI Governance Lifecycle and Decision Gates

AI governance must operate throughout the AI lifecycle. It should not be limited to procurement approval or pre-production risk assessment.

#### 4.1 AI Lifecycle Governance

AI Lifecycle Governance refers to the application of governance requirements throughout the stages of an AI system's existence.

Typical stages include:

1. AI strategy and use-case identification.
2. Business justification.
3. AI system registration.
4. Initial risk screening.
5. Design and architecture.
6. Data acquisition and preparation.
7. Model development or procurement.
8. Testing and validation.
9. Approval and deployment.
10. Production operation.
11. Monitoring and reassessment.
12. Modification or retraining.
13. Decommissioning and retirement.

Governance requirements should be proportionate to the system's intended purpose, risk, and operating environment.

#### 4.2 AI Use-Case Identification

AI Use-Case Identification is the process of identifying a business activity or problem that an AI system may support or perform.

Examples:

- Customer-service automation.
- Fraud detection.
- Network anomaly detection.
- Predictive maintenance.
- Customer segmentation.
- Document processing.
- Cybersecurity threat analysis.
- Employee productivity assistance.

The organization should establish the intended business outcome before selecting a model or technology.

#### 4.3 AI Business Justification

AI Business Justification evaluates whether a proposed AI initiative provides sufficient business value to justify its costs, risks, and operational implications.

It may consider:

- Business objectives.
- Expected benefits.
- Cost and resource requirements.
- Technical feasibility.
- Data availability.
- Legal and regulatory constraints.
- Operational dependencies.
- Risk exposure.
- Alternative solutions.

An AI solution should not be adopted simply because the technology is available.

#### 4.4 AI System Registration

AI System Registration is the process of formally recording an AI system in the organization's AI inventory or governance repository.

Typical information includes:

| Information         | Description                                         |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| AI System ID        | Unique system identifier                            |
| System Name         | Official AI system name                             |
| Business Owner      | Accountable business function                       |
| Technical Owner     | Responsible technical function                      |
| Intended Purpose    | Approved business use                               |
| AI Type             | Predictive, generative, classification, etc.        |
| Model Information   | Model name, version, and provider                   |
| Data Classification | Sensitivity of processed information                |
| Deployment Model    | SaaS, cloud, on-premises, hybrid                    |
| External Providers  | Third-party dependencies                            |
| Risk Classification | Internal risk tier and applicable legal category    |
| Lifecycle Status    | Proposed, development, testing, production, retired |
| Approval Status     | Governance decision and date                        |

A complete AI system inventory is essential for enterprise visibility and risk oversight.

#### 4.5 AI Governance Screening

AI Governance Screening is an initial evaluation used to determine whether a proposed AI use case requires additional governance review.

It may examine:

- Business purpose.
- Data sensitivity.
- Personal data processing.
- External AI provider involvement.
- Customer-facing functionality.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Potential impact on individuals.
- Critical business dependencies.
- Regulatory considerations.

Screening helps determine the depth and type of subsequent assessments.

#### 4.6 AI Risk Classification Gate

An AI Risk Classification Gate is a formal decision point at which an AI use case is assigned an internal risk tier and, where applicable, assessed against legal classification requirements.

The outcome determines:

- Required risk assessments.
- Required technical testing.
- Approval authority.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Monitoring intensity.
- Documentation expectations.

Internal classification must remain distinct from legally defined classifications.

#### 4.7 AI Architecture Approval

AI Architecture Approval is the formal review of the proposed AI solution architecture to determine whether it meets organizational architecture, security, privacy, integration, and operational requirements.

Typical assessment areas:

- Model hosting and deployment.
- Data flows and trust boundaries.
- API integration.
- Identity and access management.
- Network security.
- Data retention.
- Encryption.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Third-party connectivity.
- Human approval mechanisms.
- Resilience and recovery.

#### 4.8 AI Production Approval

AI Production Approval is the formal authorization to deploy an AI system into a production environment.

Before approval, the organization should verify that:

- Governance assessments are completed.
- Critical risks are treated or formally escalated.
- Security testing is completed.
- Privacy requirements are addressed.
- Required documentation is available.
- Model and application validation are completed.
- Human oversight arrangements are established where required.
- Monitoring is operational.
- Support and incident response arrangements exist.
- Authorized stakeholders have approved deployment.

A completed assessment does not automatically mean a system is ready for production.

#### 4.9 AI Change Management

AI Change Management governs modifications that may affect an AI system's behavior, performance, security, compliance, or intended purpose.

Examples:

- Model version changes.
- Prompt modifications.
- Dataset changes.
- Fine-tuning.
- RAG knowledge-base updates.
- New API integrations.
- Changes to system permissions.
- Changes to business workflows.
- New deployment regions.
- Changes in intended use.

Material changes should trigger reassessment according to defined governance criteria.

#### 4.10 AI Decommissioning

AI Decommissioning is the controlled process of retiring an AI system from operational use.

It should address:

- Service termination.
- Access revocation.
- Credential removal.
- Data retention and deletion.
- Model artifact disposition.
- Contractual obligations.
- Dependency removal.
- Audit record retention.
- Business continuity.
- Knowledge transfer.

Retiring an AI system does not automatically remove all legal, contractual, or record-retention obligations associated with its previous operation.

### 5. AI Governance Documentation and Evidence

AI governance must be demonstrable. Organizations need documented evidence showing that governance requirements have been implemented and are operating effectively.

#### 5.1 AI Governance Documentation

AI Governance Documentation refers to the records, policies, standards, procedures, assessments, approvals, and reports that demonstrate how an organization governs its AI activities.

Typical documentation includes:

- AI strategy.
- AI governance framework.
- AI governance policy.
- AI acceptable-use policy.
- AI inventory.
- AI risk classification methodology.
- AI risk assessments.
- AI impact assessments.
- AI security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- Model documentation.
- Architecture diagrams.
- Supplier assessments.
- Production approval records.
- Monitoring reports.
- Incident records.
- Risk acceptance records.
- Internal audit reports.

#### 5.2 AI Governance Evidence

AI Governance Evidence is information demonstrating that a governance requirement, control, decision, or activity has been performed.

Examples:

| Governance Requirement                  | Example Evidence                              |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| AI systems must be registered           | Approved AI inventory                         |
| AI risks must be assessed               | Completed risk assessment                     |
| AI providers must be evaluated          | Third-party due diligence report              |
| Security controls must be implemented   | Access control configuration and test results |
| Production deployment requires approval | Signed production approval record             |
| AI performance must be monitored        | Monitoring dashboard and review records       |
| Risk acceptance requires authorization  | Approved risk acceptance record               |
| AI incidents must be managed            | Incident ticket and investigation report      |

A policy statement alone does not prove that a control has been implemented or operates effectively.

#### 5.3 Governance Traceability

Governance Traceability is the ability to connect governance requirements to implementation activities, control ownership, evidence, and management decisions.

A useful traceability chain is:

```
BUSINESS OBJECTIVE
       |
       v
AI GOVERNANCE REQUIREMENT
       |
       v
POLICY / STANDARD
       |
       v
RISK SCENARIO
       |
       v
CONTROL REQUIREMENT
       |
       v
CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
       |
       v
EVIDENCE
       |
       v
CONTROL TESTING
       |
       v
MANAGEMENT DECISION
```

This chain enables management and auditors to establish whether governance objectives are supported by operational controls and reliable evidence.

#### 5.4 AI Governance Exception

An AI Governance Exception is a formally approved deviation from an established AI governance policy, standard, or control requirement.

A governance exception should document:

- Requirement being waived.
- Business justification.
- Affected AI systems.
- Risk assessment.
- Compensating controls.
- Exception owner.
- Approval authority.
- Expiration date.
- Remediation plan.
- Review requirements.

An exception should not become a permanent alternative to implementing a required control without appropriate justification and authorization.

#### 5.5 AI Governance Audit Trail

An AI Governance Audit Trail is a record of relevant governance activities, approvals, changes, decisions, and actions associated with AI systems.

It may include:

- Use-case approvals.
- Risk classification decisions.
- Architecture review records.
- Model version changes.
- Access changes.
- Risk acceptance.
- Human intervention.
- Incident investigation.
- Governance committee decisions.
- Decommissioning approvals.

Audit trails support accountability, investigations, compliance verification, and independent assurance.

#### 5.6 AI Governance Assurance

AI Governance Assurance is the process of obtaining confidence that AI governance arrangements are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

Assurance activities may include:

- Policy compliance reviews.
- Risk assessment quality reviews.
- Control design assessments.
- Control operating-effectiveness testing.
- Technical security testing.
- Model validation.
- Supplier assurance reviews.
- Internal audits.
- External assessments.

Governance assurance should evaluate actual operating evidence, not merely the existence of documentation.

### 6. Telecom Operator AI Governance Case Study

Consider a telecommunications operator introducing an AI-powered customer-service platform.

The platform combines:

- Salesforce Service Cloud.
- Salesforce Data Cloud.
- External LLM services.
- Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).
- Enterprise knowledge repositories.
- Customer identity verification.
- E-commerce integration.
- Billing and provisioning systems.
- AI-assisted customer-service workflows.

The system can retrieve customer information, answer questions, recommend service plans, and initiate selected business workflows.

#### 6.1 Governance Challenges

The operator must address several governance questions:

| Governance Area         | Key Question                                             |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business accountability | Who owns the customer-service AI use case?               |
| AI inventory            | Is the system registered in the enterprise AI inventory? |
| Model governance        | Who approves the external LLM and model versions?        |
| Data governance         | Which customer information can be processed?             |
| Privacy                 | What personal data is transferred to external providers? |
| Cybersecurity           | How are APIs, identities, and integrations secured?      |
| Human oversight         | Which customer actions require human approval?           |
| Third-party governance  | Has the LLM provider been assessed?                      |
| Regulatory compliance   | Which legal obligations apply to the system and its use? |
| Operational governance  | Who monitors system performance and incidents?           |
| Risk ownership          | Who accepts the remaining business risks?                |
| Assurance               | How will governance effectiveness be demonstrated?       |

#### 6.2 Proposed Governance Structure

| Function                        | Responsibility                                          |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Executive AI Steering Committee | Strategic direction and major AI decisions              |
| AI Governance Committee         | Oversight of AI use cases and governance requirements   |
| Business Owner                  | Customer-service objectives and business accountability |
| AI System Owner                 | Overall system lifecycle management                     |
| AI Engineering                  | Model integration and application implementation        |
| Enterprise Architecture         | Architecture standards and integration review           |
| Cybersecurity GRC               | Security risk assessment and governance oversight       |
| Security Operations             | Security monitoring and incident response               |
| Data Governance                 | Data quality, classification, and access governance     |
| DPO / Privacy                   | Privacy assessment and data protection oversight        |
| Legal / Compliance              | Regulatory and contractual assessment                   |
| Procurement / TPRM              | Provider due diligence and supplier governance          |
| Internal Audit                  | Independent governance assurance                        |

#### 6.3 Governance Decision Gates

| Gate                         | Required Decision                                   | Primary Evidence                                 |

### 7. Summary

AI governance establishes how an organization directs, controls, and oversees its AI activities.

It connects executive accountability, business ownership, technical implementation, risk management, legal compliance, cybersecurity, privacy, and independent assurance.

The main governance building blocks include:

- Governance frameworks, policies, standards, and procedures.
- Responsible and trustworthy AI principles.
- Board and executive oversight.
- AI governance committees and governance offices.
- Centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models.
- Lifecycle decision gates.
- AI inventory and ownership.
- Governance documentation and evidence.
- Exception management and audit trails.
- Continuous governance assurance.

For GRC professionals, the central challenge is ensuring that governance requirements translate into real decision authority, operational responsibilities, enforceable controls, and demonstrable evidence.

### Core Principle

AI governance is the organizational system that establishes who has authority over AI, who is accountable for its use, which requirements must be followed, and how compliance and control effectiveness are demonstrated. A governance framework is only effective when its policies, decision rights, controls, and accountability mechanisms operate in practice.

### 8. AI Governance Policies and Policy Management

AI governance policies translate organizational principles and strategic commitments into formal requirements that govern the development, procurement, deployment, and use of AI.

A policy must establish clear expectations, ownership, and enforcement mechanisms. Without these elements, AI governance risks becoming a collection of voluntary guidelines with limited operational impact.

#### 8.1 AI Acceptable Use Policy (AI AUP)

An AI Acceptable Use Policy (AI AUP) defines how employees, contractors, and other authorized users may use AI systems within an organization.

It establishes permitted, restricted, and prohibited activities.

Typical requirements include:

- Use only approved AI applications for organizational activities.
- Do not enter confidential information into unauthorized public AI services.
- Do not upload personal or sensitive data without authorization.
- Validate AI-generated information before using it for business decisions.
- Do not use AI outputs as a substitute for required professional judgment.
- Disclose AI use where organizational or legal requirements apply.
- Report suspected AI security incidents.
- Follow intellectual property and licensing requirements.
- Do not bypass approved security controls.
- Obtain authorization before integrating AI with corporate systems.

Example:

A telecommunications operator permits employees to use an approved enterprise LLM for drafting internal reports but prohibits uploading customer records, network credentials, confidential architecture diagrams, or unredacted incident reports into unauthorized public AI services.

The AUP should distinguish between approved enterprise AI environments and external consumer AI services.

#### 8.2 AI Development Policy

An AI Development Policy defines mandatory governance, security, data, and engineering requirements for internally developed AI systems.

It may address:

- Secure AI development lifecycle.
- Approved development environments.
- Dataset acquisition and validation.
- Model selection.
- Training and fine-tuning.
- Model documentation.
- Security testing.
- Model validation.
- Deployment approval.
- Change management.
- Monitoring.
- Retirement.

The policy should cover AI applications and their supporting components, not only model development.

#### 8.3 AI Procurement Policy

An AI Procurement Policy establishes requirements for acquiring AI products, platforms, models, and services from external suppliers.

It should address:

- Business justification.
- Approved supplier selection.
- Third-party AI risk assessment.
- Data processing and privacy.
- Intellectual property.
- Model transparency and documentation.
- Security requirements.
- Service availability.
- Data residency.
- Contractual obligations.
- Subcontractors and fourth parties.
- Exit and transition arrangements.

GRC example:

Before purchasing an external LLM service, procurement must coordinate with cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and business stakeholders to evaluate whether the provider's security controls, data handling, contractual terms, and service commitments meet enterprise requirements.

#### 8.4 AI Data Governance Policy

An AI Data Governance Policy establishes requirements for managing data used by or generated through AI systems.

It addresses:

- Data ownership.
- Data classification.
- Data quality.
- Data provenance.
- Data lineage.
- Data access.
- Data minimization.
- Data retention.
- Data sharing.
- Data integrity.
- Data deletion.
- Training and inference data management.

The policy should cover training datasets, fine-tuning datasets, RAG knowledge repositories, prompts, user inputs, model outputs, and relevant telemetry.

#### 8.5 AI Model Governance Policy

An AI Model Governance Policy establishes requirements for the lifecycle management and oversight of AI models.

It may define:

- Model inventory.
- Model ownership.
- Model classification.
- Model development standards.
- Model validation.
- Model approval.
- Model versioning.
- Model performance monitoring.
- Model drift management.
- Model change management.
- Model retirement.

Model governance is especially important in organizations using multiple models from different providers and deployment environments.

#### 8.6 AI Third-Party Governance Policy

An AI Third-Party Governance Policy establishes requirements for managing risks associated with external AI providers, vendors, service providers, and technology dependencies.

It may require:

- Supplier due diligence.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- AI-specific risk assessments.
- Contractual security requirements.
- Provider documentation.
- Subprocessor disclosure.
- Incident notification.
- Performance monitoring.
- Periodic reassessment.
- Exit planning.

The organization remains responsible for managing risks arising from its use of third-party AI services, even when the provider operates the underlying model.

#### 8.7 AI Policy Exception

An AI Policy Exception is an authorized deviation from a formally established AI policy requirement.

Example:

A business unit needs to use an AI application that is not yet included in the organization's approved AI service catalogue.

The exception process should establish:

1. Business justification.
2. Affected systems and users.
3. Risk assessment.
4. Compensating controls.
5. Accountable owner.
6. Approval authority.
7. Expiration date.
8. Remediation plan.

An exception should be time-bound and reviewed periodically.

### 9. AI Governance Standards and Control Requirements

Policies establish organizational expectations. Standards translate those expectations into mandatory requirements that can be implemented and tested.

#### 9.1 AI Governance Control

An AI Governance Control is a measure designed to ensure that AI activities comply with organizational governance requirements, applicable obligations, and approved risk decisions.

Examples:

- AI inventory management.
- AI use-case approval.
- Risk classification.
- Model validation.
- Human oversight.
- Third-party assessment.
- Governance reporting.
- Exception management.

A governance control may be administrative, technical, operational, or managerial.

#### 9.2 Governance Control Objective

A Governance Control Objective describes the outcome a control is intended to achieve.

Example:

| Control Objective         | Expected Outcome                                     |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| AI inventory completeness | All relevant AI systems are identified and recorded  |
| AI risk accountability    | Every material AI risk has an accountable owner      |
| AI use authorization      | AI systems are approved before authorized use        |
| Model governance          | Models are validated and monitored according to risk |
| Third-party oversight     | External AI dependencies are appropriately assessed  |
| Regulatory compliance     | Applicable obligations are identified and addressed  |

A control objective defines the intended result; individual controls describe how the organization achieves it.

#### 9.3 Preventive Governance Control

A Preventive Governance Control aims to prevent unauthorized or non-compliant AI activities.

Examples:

- Mandatory AI registration before procurement.
- Approval before production deployment.
- Restrictions on unauthorized AI tools.
- Mandatory privacy assessments for qualifying processing.
- Defined approval authority for high-impact use cases.
- Mandatory security requirements for AI integrations.

#### 9.4 Detective Governance Control

A Detective Governance Control identifies governance deviations, control failures, or unauthorized AI activities.

Examples:

- Periodic AI inventory reconciliation.
- Monitoring of unauthorized AI applications.
- Review of AI system access logs.
- Compliance reviews.
- Periodic supplier reassessments.
- Review of overdue risk treatment actions.

#### 9.5 Corrective Governance Control

A Corrective Governance Control addresses identified governance failures and restores compliance.

Examples:

- Suspending an unauthorized AI application.
- Revoking inappropriate access.
- Correcting incomplete AI inventory records.
- Remediating missing documentation.
- Reassessing a system following an unapproved model change.
- Revising a deficient governance procedure.

#### 9.6 Administrative Control

An Administrative Control is a governance measure implemented through policies, procedures, standards, training, responsibilities, or management processes.

Examples:

- AI acceptable-use policy.
- AI risk assessment procedure.
- AI governance committee.
- Mandatory AI awareness training.
- Supplier assessment requirements.

#### 9.7 Technical Control

A Technical Control is implemented through technology or system configuration to enforce security or governance requirements.

Examples:

- Identity and access management.
- API authorization.
- DLP.
- Data encryption.
- Model endpoint restrictions.
- Prompt and output filtering.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Network segmentation.

#### 9.8 Operational Control

An Operational Control is performed through recurring business or technology activities.

Examples:

- Daily monitoring.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Model performance reviews.
- Supplier reassessments.
- Security incident handling.
- Dataset quality checks.
- AI change approvals.

#### 9.9 Management Control

A Management Control provides organizational direction, oversight, approval, and accountability.

Examples:

- Executive AI strategy approval.
- AI governance committee oversight.
- Risk acceptance authorization.
- Management review of AI risk reports.
- Resource allocation.
- Review of significant AI incidents.

### 10. AI Governance Compliance and Regulatory Management

AI governance compliance ensures that an organization identifies, understands, implements, and monitors obligations arising from applicable laws, regulations, contracts, and internal requirements.

It requires more than checking whether a policy exists.

#### 10.1 AI Regulatory Compliance

AI Regulatory Compliance refers to the organization's adherence to legal and regulatory requirements applicable to its AI systems, activities, and organizational role.

Potential regulatory considerations include:

- EU AI Act.
- General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
- Sector-specific regulations.
- Consumer protection requirements.
- Employment and labor requirements.
- Intellectual property legislation.
- Cybersecurity obligations.
- Contractual requirements.

Not every AI system is subject to every AI-specific obligation. Applicability must be assessed against the actual use case and legal context.

#### 10.2 Regulatory Applicability Assessment

A Regulatory Applicability Assessment determines which legal and regulatory requirements apply to an organization, AI system, provider, deployer, or particular processing activity.

It should consider:

- Organizational jurisdiction.
- System deployment location.
- Intended purpose.
- Affected individuals.
- Data categories.
- AI system classification.
- Organizational role.
- Sector.
- Provider and supplier relationships.

Example:

A Spanish telecommunications operator deploys an external LLM to support customer-service operations.

The applicability assessment should consider the EU AI Act, GDPR, relevant consumer and telecommunications requirements, contractual obligations, and other applicable legislation.

#### 10.3 Regulatory Obligations Register

A Regulatory Obligations Register is a controlled record of legal and regulatory requirements relevant to the organization.

A typical register includes:

| Field               | Purpose                                                        |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Obligation ID       | Unique identifier                                              |
| Regulation          | Applicable law or regulation                                   |
| Article / Provision | Specific legal requirement                                     |
| Applicability       | Explanation of why it applies                                  |
| Organizational Role | Relevant provider, deployer, or other role                     |
| Responsible Owner   | Accountable function                                           |
| Control Mapping     | Controls addressing the obligation                             |
| Evidence            | Required compliance evidence                                   |
| Review Frequency    | Reassessment schedule                                          |
| Compliance Status   | Compliant, partially compliant, non-compliant, or not assessed |
| Remediation         | Required corrective action                                     |

#### 10.4 Legal Applicability vs. Internal Governance Requirement

These concepts must not be confused.

| Legal Applicability                                | Internal Governance Requirement                                   |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Arises from applicable legislation or regulation   | Arises from organizational policy or risk management              |
| May create legally enforceable obligations         | Creates internal organizational requirements                      |
| Determined by legal scope and conditions           | Determined by organizational governance decisions                 |
| Example: Applicable EU AI Act provider obligations | Example: Internal approval required for all production AI systems |

An organization can impose stricter internal governance requirements than the minimum required by law.

#### 10.5 Regulatory Gap Assessment

A Regulatory Gap Assessment evaluates the difference between applicable legal requirements and the organization's current compliance arrangements.

Example:

| Requirement                     | Current State                  | Gap                               | Remediation                        |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Maintain required documentation | Documentation incomplete       | Missing evidence                  | Establish documentation repository |
| Establish required oversight    | Responsibilities unclear       | Inadequate oversight arrangements | Define oversight procedures        |
| Monitor applicable obligations  | No formal review               | Monitoring gap                    | Establish compliance monitoring    |
| Manage relevant incidents       | AI-specific escalation unclear | Incident governance gap           | Update incident procedures         |

The gap assessment should distinguish between missing documentation, missing control design, incomplete implementation, and ineffective operation.

#### 10.6 Regulatory Control Mapping

Regulatory Control Mapping connects legal requirements to internal policies, standards, controls, owners, and evidence.

Example:

```

       v

       |
       v

       |
       v
CONTROL REQUIREMENT
       |
       v

       |
       v

       |
       v

```

This structure supports regulatory traceability and audit readiness.

#### 10.7 Statement of Applicability (SoA)

A Statement of Applicability (SoA) is a formal document used within ISO/IEC 27001 to identify the information security controls determined to be necessary, their implementation status, and justifications for inclusion or exclusion.

For an organization operating AI systems within its ISMS, the SoA can document the applicability of relevant information security controls to AI-related assets and processes.

An SoA is not automatically a complete AI governance control register. AI-specific requirements may need additional mapping.

#### 10.8 AI Regulatory Compliance Monitoring

AI Regulatory Compliance Monitoring is the ongoing activity of evaluating whether applicable AI-related obligations continue to be met.

It may involve:

- Regulatory change monitoring.
- Periodic applicability reviews.
- Control testing.
- Documentation reviews.
- Supplier compliance monitoring.
- Incident reporting.
- Management reporting.
- Remediation tracking.

Changes in legal requirements, intended purpose, system capabilities, or organizational role may trigger reassessment.

### 11. AI Governance Frameworks, Standards, and Regulatory References

An AI governance program should use recognized frameworks and standards to establish a coherent governance structure.

However, the organization must understand what each reference provides and avoid treating every publication as a legal requirement.

#### 11.1 NIST AI RMF — Governance Function

The Govern function of the NIST AI RMF addresses organizational policies, processes, responsibilities, and oversight required to manage AI risks.

Its governance activities support:

- AI risk management policies.
- Accountability structures.
- Organizational culture.
- Workforce competency.
- Third-party risk management.
- Executive oversight.
- Risk management integration.

The Govern function is cross-cutting and supports the Map, Measure, and Manage functions.

#### 11.2 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 establishes requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System.

Its management-system approach covers areas such as:

- Organizational context.
- Leadership.
- Planning.
- Support.
- Operation.
- Performance evaluation.
- Improvement.

It enables organizations to establish formal AI governance arrangements and continually improve them.

#### 11.3 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Policy

ISO/IEC 42001 requires an AI policy within its AIMS requirements.

The policy provides top-management direction and commitments relevant to the organization's AI management system.

The organization must ensure that the policy is appropriate to its purpose and context and is communicated and maintained as required by the standard.

#### 11.4 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Risk and Impact Assessment

ISO/IEC 42001 includes requirements relating to AI risk assessment, AI risk treatment, and AI system impact assessment.

These processes help the organization understand and manage risks and impacts associated with AI systems within the AIMS scope.

The organization should establish criteria, responsibilities, and documented processes consistent with the standard.

#### 11.5 ISO/IEC 42001 — Internal Audit and Management Review

ISO/IEC 42001 includes management-system requirements for:

- Monitoring and measurement.
- Internal audit.
- Management review.
- Nonconformity and corrective action.
- Continual improvement.

These requirements help ensure that AI governance is evaluated and improved over time.

#### 11.6 ISO/IEC 38507 — Governance of AI Use

ISO/IEC 38507 provides guidance for governing bodies on the implications of AI use within organizations.

It is particularly relevant to:

- Strategic alignment.
- Governance responsibilities.
- Oversight.
- Accountability.
- Risk considerations.
- Organizational decision-making.

It supports governing-body understanding of AI but should not be treated as a substitute for operational AI risk controls.

#### 11.7 ISO/IEC 23894 — AI Risk Governance

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on integrating AI risk management into organizational risk management.

It supports the governance of risk identification, analysis, evaluation, treatment, and monitoring.

#### 11.8 ISO/IEC 27001 — AI Information Security Governance

ISO/IEC 27001 can support AI security governance through its ISMS requirements and applicable information security controls.

Organizations should ensure that AI-related systems, services, data, and suppliers are considered within the relevant ISMS scope and risk assessment.

#### 11.9 EU AI Act — Governance and Accountability

The EU AI Act establishes legally defined obligations for relevant AI system and GPAI model providers, deployers, and other actors.

Governance activities may include:

- Legal classification.
- Role identification.
- Risk management.
- Technical documentation.
- Record-keeping.
- Human oversight.
- Transparency.
- Monitoring.
- Incident handling.
- Provider and deployer obligations.

The exact obligations depend on the system, its intended purpose, classification, and the organization's legal role.

### 12. AI Governance Performance Measurement and Reporting

AI governance requires measurable indicators to determine whether governance arrangements are implemented and functioning effectively.

Management reporting should provide evidence of governance coverage, compliance, risk exposure, remediation progress, and control performance.

#### 12.1 AI Governance Key Performance Indicator (KPI)

An AI Governance KPI measures the performance of an AI governance activity against a defined objective.

Examples:

| KPI                             | Calculation                                               | Purpose                                  |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| AI Inventory Registration Rate  | Registered AI systems / Identified AI systems × 100       | Measures inventory coverage              |
| AI Assessment Completion Rate   | Completed assessments / Required assessments × 100        | Measures assessment execution            |
| AI Training Completion Rate     | Employees trained / Employees in scope × 100              | Measures workforce training              |
| AI Policy Acknowledgment Rate   | Employees acknowledging policy / Employees in scope × 100 | Measures policy communication            |
| AI Remediation Completion Rate  | Completed actions / Actions due × 100                     | Measures remediation progress            |
| AI Supplier Assessment Coverage | Assessed suppliers / AI suppliers in scope × 100          | Measures third-party assessment coverage |

These measures should have defined scope and reporting periods.

#### 12.2 AI Governance Key Risk Indicator (KRI)

An AI Governance KRI signals potential weaknesses or increasing exposure in the governance environment.

Examples:

| KRI                                                | Risk Signal                       |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Number of unregistered AI systems                  | Potential Shadow AI               |
| Number of AI systems without assigned owners       | Accountability weakness           |
| Number of overdue risk assessments                 | Governance coverage deterioration |
| Number of unauthorized AI deployments              | Approval process failure          |
| Number of expired policy exceptions                | Unmanaged governance deviations   |
| Number of AI suppliers without current assessments | Third-party exposure              |
| Number of production AI systems without monitoring | Oversight weakness                |
| Number of overdue critical remediation actions     | Accumulating risk exposure        |

#### 12.3 AI Governance Control Effectiveness

AI Governance Control Effectiveness evaluates whether a governance control is properly designed, implemented, and operating as intended.

Three distinct dimensions should be assessed:

1. Design effectiveness: Does the control address the relevant governance risk?
2. Implementation effectiveness: Has the control been implemented?
3. Operating effectiveness: Does the control operate consistently and produce the expected result?

Example:

An organization has a policy requiring every AI system to be registered before deployment.

- Design: The policy establishes a clear registration requirement.
- Implementation: A registration workflow and inventory repository exist.
- Operation: Testing confirms that production AI systems are registered and approved before deployment.

The existence of the policy and repository alone is insufficient evidence of operating effectiveness.

#### 12.4 AI Governance Dashboard

A governance dashboard provides management with a consolidated view of AI governance performance and exposure.

Recommended components:

| Dashboard Area         | Example Measures                                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Inventory           | Total systems, registered systems, unregistered systems |
| Risk Classification    | Systems by internal risk tier                           |
| Governance Compliance  | Policy compliance and assessment coverage               |
| Risk Management        | Open risks by severity                                  |
| Control Assurance      | Control testing results                                 |
| Third-Party Governance | Supplier assessment coverage                            |
| Regulatory Compliance  | Open regulatory gaps                                    |
| Exceptions             | Open, expired, and overdue exceptions                   |
| Remediation            | Open and overdue corrective actions                     |
| Incidents              | AI-related governance and security incidents            |

A dashboard should not present high completion rates as proof of effective governance if control failures or material residual risks remain unresolved.

#### 12.5 AI Governance Management Review

An AI Governance Management Review is a formal management activity for evaluating the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the AI governance arrangements.

Typical inputs include:

- Governance KPI and KRI results.
- AI inventory changes.
- Risk assessment outcomes.
- Control testing results.
- Audit findings.
- Regulatory developments.
- Supplier performance.
- AI incidents.
- Corrective action status.
- Resource requirements.
- Opportunities for improvement.

Management decisions may include:

- Changes to governance policies.
- Additional control requirements.
- Resource allocation.
- Risk treatment priorities.
- Governance operating model changes.
- Supplier restrictions.
- AI system suspension or retirement.

| 7. Implementation   | Establish inventory, workflows, and evidence repositories | Operational governance processes |
| 8. Assurance        | Test governance controls and evaluate effectiveness       | Assurance reports                |
| 9. Optimization     | Review metrics and improve governance                     | Improvement plan                 |

#### 13.2 Minimum Viable AI Governance

Organizations beginning their AI governance journey should prioritize a minimum set of foundational capabilities:

1. Approved AI governance policy.
2. Central AI system inventory.
3. Defined business and technical ownership.
4. AI acceptable-use policy.
5. AI risk classification methodology.
6. AI risk assessment process.
7. Security and privacy assessment requirements.
8. Third-party AI assessment process.
9. Production approval requirements.
10. Change management and incident escalation.
11. Residual risk acceptance process.
12. Governance reporting and periodic review.

This baseline should be expanded according to the organization's AI portfolio, risk exposure, legal obligations, and maturity.

### 14. Summary

AI governance policies, standards, controls, compliance processes, and performance measurements translate organizational governance commitments into operational requirements.

A mature AI governance program should establish:

- Formal AI policies and acceptable-use requirements.
- Mandatory governance standards and control objectives.
- Clear preventive, detective, and corrective controls.
- Regulatory applicability and obligations management.
- Traceability between legal requirements and internal controls.
- Management-system alignment with recognized standards.
- Governance KPIs, KRIs, and control effectiveness measurements.
- Structured implementation and continual improvement.

For GRC professionals, the critical responsibility is to ensure that governance commitments are translated into measurable requirements, assigned ownership, implemented controls, and verifiable evidence.

### Core Principle

AI governance must be enforceable, measurable, and auditable. Policies define organizational expectations, standards establish mandatory requirements, controls implement those requirements, compliance processes demonstrate adherence, and assurance determines whether the governance system actually works.

# 22.08 — AI Governance Terminology

## Part 3 — AI Governance Implementation, Lifecycle Oversight, Third-Party Governance, and Operational Assurance

### 15. AI Governance Implementation and Operational Management

AI governance becomes effective when organizational policies, standards, and control requirements are translated into operational processes.

An organization may have an AI governance framework, an approved policy, and a committee, but these alone do not demonstrate that AI activities are properly governed.

Implementation requires defined workflows, accountable owners, approval mechanisms, monitoring, and evidence.

#### 15.1 AI Governance Implementation

AI Governance Implementation is the process of putting an organization's AI governance framework into operation.

It involves translating governance requirements into activities performed by business, technical, risk, and compliance functions.

Typical implementation activities include:

- Establishing an AI governance operating model.
- Appointing governance stakeholders.
- Creating an AI system inventory.
- Defining AI risk classification.
- Implementing AI approval workflows.
- Establishing AI security and privacy assessments.
- Defining third-party AI assessment requirements.
- Implementing AI lifecycle controls.
- Establishing governance reporting.
- Performing periodic governance reviews.

Example:

A telecommunications operator approves an AI governance policy requiring all production AI systems to undergo risk assessment.

Implementation requires more than publishing the policy. The organization must create an assessment procedure, assign assessors, define approval authorities, establish an evidence repository, and verify that production systems follow the process.

#### 15.2 AI Governance Workflow

An AI Governance Workflow is a defined sequence of activities through which an AI system or use case passes to satisfy organizational governance requirements.

Example:

```
       BUSINESS AI USE CASE
                |
                v
       AI GOVERNANCE INTAKE
                |
                v
        SYSTEM REGISTRATION
                |
                v
        INITIAL SCREENING
                |
                v
        RISK CLASSIFICATION
                |
                v
       REQUIRED ASSESSMENTS
                |
       +--------+---------+
       |        |         |
       v        v         v
    SECURITY  PRIVACY   LEGAL /
    REVIEW    REVIEW   COMPLIANCE
       |        |         |
       +--------+---------+
                |
                v
       RISK TREATMENT REVIEW
                |
                v
       AUTHORIZED APPROVAL
                |
                v
       PRODUCTION DEPLOYMENT
                |
                v
       CONTINUOUS OVERSIGHT
```

A workflow should specify:

- Entry criteria.
- Required information.
- Responsible stakeholders.
- Decision authority.
- Approval conditions.
- Evidence requirements.
- Escalation procedures.
- Exit criteria.

#### 15.3 AI Governance Intake

AI Governance Intake is the process through which a business unit submits a proposed AI use case for initial governance evaluation.

The intake process establishes the minimum information required to understand the proposed AI activity.

Typical information includes:

| Information          | Purpose                                                   |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business Unit        | Identifies the requesting department                      |
| Business Sponsor     | Identifies the initiative sponsor                         |
| Use-Case Description | Explains the proposed AI application                      |
| Intended Purpose     | Defines the expected AI functionality                     |
| AI Technology        | Identifies the AI technology involved                     |
| Model Provider       | Identifies the model developer or supplier, where known   |
| Data Categories      | Identifies information processed                          |
| User Population      | Identifies employees, customers, or other affected groups |
| Business Impact      | Describes the expected operational impact                 |
| Integration Scope    | Identifies connected enterprise systems                   |
| Deployment Model     | Identifies SaaS, cloud, on-premises, or hybrid deployment |

The intake process should capture sufficient information to determine which governance assessments are necessary.

#### 15.4 AI Governance Approval

AI Governance Approval is the formal authorization granted by an appropriately delegated authority to proceed with an AI activity, system, or lifecycle stage.

Approval may be required for:

- AI use-case initiation.
- Procurement.
- Architecture.
- Development.
- Testing.
- Production deployment.
- Material system changes.
- Risk acceptance.
- Decommissioning.

Approval must be based on defined decision criteria and sufficient supporting evidence.

A committee recommendation is not necessarily an approval unless the committee has been formally delegated the relevant authority.

#### 15.5 AI Governance Decision Rights

AI Governance Decision Rights define which individuals or organizational bodies have authority to make particular AI-related decisions.

Examples:

| Decision                              | Potential Decision Authority                            |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Approve enterprise AI strategy        | Executive management                                    |
| Approve AI governance policy          | Authorized executive                                    |
| Approve business use case             | Business governance authority                           |
| Approve technical architecture        | Architecture authority                                  |
| Approve security control requirements | Authorized security authority                           |
| Determine privacy requirements        | Privacy function, with appropriate legal accountability |
| Accept business risk                  | Authorized business risk owner                          |
| Approve production deployment         | Delegated production authority                          |
| Approve policy exception              | Authorized policy owner or governance authority         |

Decision rights should be documented in governance charters, policies, procedures, and delegated authority matrices.

#### 15.6 AI Governance Escalation

AI Governance Escalation is the process of referring an AI-related issue, risk, control failure, or decision beyond the current authority level.

Typical escalation triggers include:

- Critical unresolved risks.
- Unauthorized AI deployment.
- Significant security incidents.
- Potential regulatory non-compliance.
- Unapproved processing of sensitive data.
- Expired risk acceptance.
- Material AI model behavior changes.
- Failure of mandatory controls.
- Significant customer or business impact.

Escalation procedures should define notification timelines, decision authorities, and required management actions.

#### 15.7 AI Governance Decision Log

An AI Governance Decision Log is a controlled record of significant decisions made by authorized governance stakeholders.

It may capture:

- Decision ID.
- AI system or use case.
- Decision description.
- Supporting assessment.
- Decision authority.
- Participants.
- Approval or rejection.
- Conditions.
- Assigned actions.
- Decision date.
- Review date.

The decision log provides traceability and supports management accountability and audit activities.

#### 15.8 AI Governance Action Register

An AI Governance Action Register tracks activities arising from governance reviews, risk assessments, audits, committees, and management decisions.

Typical fields include:

| Field              | Description                               |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Action ID          | Unique identifier                         |
| Source             | Committee, audit, assessment, or incident |
| Action Description | Required activity                         |
| Priority           | Critical, High, Medium, or Low            |
| Owner              | Responsible individual                    |
| Due Date           | Target completion date                    |
| Status             | Open, In Progress, Blocked, or Closed     |
| Evidence           | Proof of completion                       |
| Validation         | Confirmation that the action is effective |

An action should not be closed merely because the responsible person reports that work is complete. Appropriate evidence and validation should support closure.

### 16. AI System Lifecycle Governance and Oversight

AI governance must remain active throughout the system lifecycle because AI-related risks can change as systems are developed, integrated, updated, and used.

#### 16.1 AI Lifecycle Governance Gate

An AI Lifecycle Governance Gate is a formal checkpoint at which an AI system must satisfy defined requirements before progressing to the next lifecycle stage.

Examples:

| Gate             | Governance Objective                         | Required Decision                  |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Concept Gate     | Validate business justification              | Permit or reject proposed use case |
| Design Gate      | Evaluate architecture and risk               | Approve design progression         |
| Development Gate | Verify development governance                | Permit testing                     |
| Validation Gate  | Confirm suitability and control testing      | Approve readiness assessment       |
| Production Gate  | Confirm operational and governance readiness | Authorize deployment               |
| Change Gate      | Evaluate material modifications              | Approve or reject changes          |
| Retirement Gate  | Verify controlled decommissioning            | Authorize retirement               |

The depth of review should be proportionate to risk.

#### 16.2 AI Use-Case Governance

AI Use-Case Governance is the process of ensuring that proposed AI applications have an authorized purpose, appropriate ownership, acceptable risk, and defined operational boundaries.

It should establish:

- Business objectives.
- Intended users.
- Expected outputs.
- Decision-making authority.
- Permitted and prohibited uses.
- Affected stakeholders.
- Data requirements.
- Human involvement.
- Risk assessment.
- Approval requirements.

A model's technical capability does not establish authorization to use it for every possible business purpose.

#### 16.3 Intended Purpose Governance

Intended Purpose Governance ensures that an AI system is developed, deployed, and used within its documented and approved purpose.

It is particularly important where a general-purpose model can be integrated into multiple applications.

Example:

A telecom operator approves an LLM for answering customer questions using an authorized knowledge base.

The same model should not automatically be authorized to modify billing records, approve refunds, terminate subscriptions, or change customer identity information.

Those activities require separate business authorization and risk assessment.

#### 16.4 AI System Scope

AI System Scope defines the boundaries of an AI system and the organizational activities, assets, processes, interfaces, and dependencies included in governance and risk assessments.

It may include:

- AI models.
- Applications.
- Datasets.
- APIs.
- Infrastructure.
- User interfaces.
- External providers.
- Connected enterprise systems.
- Human operators.
- Operational processes.

A narrowly defined model scope can overlook important risks in the surrounding application and integration environment.

#### 16.5 AI System Owner

An AI System Owner is the individual or organizational function accountable for the overall governance and lifecycle management of an AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

- Maintaining system documentation.
- Coordinating risk assessments.
- Ensuring lifecycle approvals.
- Coordinating control implementation.
- Monitoring system performance.
- Managing material changes.
- Coordinating incident response.
- Initiating retirement activities.

The AI system owner may differ from the model owner, business owner, data owner, and control owner.

#### 16.6 AI Model Owner

An AI Model Owner is the individual or function accountable for governance of a particular AI model.

Responsibilities may include:

- Model documentation.
- Model inventory.
- Model validation.
- Model performance monitoring.
- Model version control.
- Model change management.
- Model limitations.
- Model retirement.

An organization using an external LLM may not control the underlying model lifecycle. It should nevertheless assign ownership for its internal evaluation, configuration, integration, and approved use.

#### 16.7 AI Business Owner

An AI Business Owner is accountable for the business purpose, operational use, and business outcomes of an AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

- Defining business objectives.
- Approving business requirements.
- Identifying affected processes.
- Establishing business rules.
- Defining human oversight.
- Evaluating business impacts.
- Coordinating risk acceptance.
- Ensuring business continuity.

The business owner should not delegate all AI accountability to engineering or cybersecurity.

#### 16.8 AI Governance Lifecycle Monitoring

AI Governance Lifecycle Monitoring is the ongoing evaluation of whether an AI system continues to operate within its approved purpose, governance conditions, and risk boundaries.

Monitoring may cover:

- System usage.
- Model performance.
- Data changes.
- Security events.
- Privacy incidents.
- Human intervention.
- Unauthorized access.
- Supplier changes.
- Regulatory developments.
- Governance exceptions.
- Risk treatment status.

Monitoring requirements should be established before production deployment.

#### 16.9 AI Governance Reassessment Trigger

An AI Governance Reassessment Trigger is an event or change that requires the organization to review an AI system's existing governance or risk assessment.

Examples:

| Trigger                          | Potential Reassessment                      |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| New intended purpose             | Business and regulatory assessment          |
| New data category                | Privacy and data governance assessment      |
| New model provider               | Third-party and security assessment         |
| Model version update             | Model validation and security review        |
| New API integration              | Architecture and cybersecurity review       |
| Increased autonomy               | Human oversight and operational risk review |
| Material performance degradation | Model risk reassessment                     |
| Security incident                | Threat and control reassessment             |
| Regulatory change                | Compliance applicability review             |
| New deployment geography         | Legal, privacy, and residency assessment    |

A reassessment should be proportional to the significance of the change.

#### 16.10 AI Governance Retirement Approval

AI Governance Retirement Approval is the authorization to discontinue an AI system after evaluating its business, technical, contractual, security, privacy, and compliance implications.

Before retirement, the organization should confirm:

- Business owner approval.
- Dependency identification.
- Service termination.
- Access revocation.
- Credential removal.
- Data handling.
- Model artifact disposition.
- Contractual closure.
- Evidence retention.
- Regulatory record retention.
- Business continuity arrangements.

### 17. AI Third-Party Governance and Supplier Accountability

External AI providers introduce risks that extend beyond traditional IT procurement.

Organizations may rely on third-party foundation models, cloud AI platforms, hosted LLMs, external datasets, AI agents, model APIs, and managed AI services.

Third-party governance must address both the supplier relationship and the AI-specific risks of the technology being acquired.

#### 17.1 AI Third-Party Governance

AI Third-Party Governance is the organizational framework used to manage external providers and suppliers involved in AI development, procurement, deployment, or operation.

It establishes requirements for:

- Supplier identification.
- Due diligence.
- Risk classification.
- Contractual controls.
- Data processing.
- Security assurance.
- Model documentation.
- Subcontractor oversight.
- Service monitoring.
- Incident notification.
- Exit management.

#### 17.2 AI Third-Party Risk Management (AI TPRM)

AI Third-Party Risk Management (AI TPRM) is the process of identifying, assessing, treating, monitoring, and managing risks arising from external AI providers and suppliers.

AI TPRM extends traditional vendor risk management by examining model-specific, data-related, and AI lifecycle dependencies.

#### 17.3 AI Provider Due Diligence

AI Provider Due Diligence is the structured evaluation of an external AI provider before entering into a business relationship or using its AI services.

Typical assessment areas:

| Assessment Domain    | Key Questions                                              |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Corporate Governance | Does the provider have defined AI governance arrangements? |
| Security             | Are security controls independently assessed?              |
| Privacy              | How is customer and personal data processed?               |
| Model Governance     | Is model documentation available?                          |
| Training Data        | Is information available about training data governance?   |
| Model Evaluation     | What testing and validation are performed?                 |
| Data Retention       | How long are prompts, inputs, and outputs retained?        |
| Data Usage           | Can customer data be used for provider training?           |
| Data Residency       | Where is customer information processed and stored?        |
| Subcontractors       | Which external providers support the service?              |
| Resilience           | What availability and recovery arrangements exist?         |
| Incident Management  | How are security incidents reported?                       |
| Change Management    | How are material model and service changes communicated?   |
| Exit Management      | Can data and services be migrated or terminated safely?    |

Provider responses should be supported by evidence where appropriate.

#### 17.4 AI Supplier Risk Classification

AI Supplier Risk Classification assigns risk levels to external AI providers based on their criticality, data access, operational dependency, and potential business impact.

Illustrative categories:

| Supplier Tier | Characteristics                                                                 | Oversight                                       |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Critical      | Supports critical business operations or processes highly sensitive information | Enhanced due diligence and continuous oversight |
| High          | Processes sensitive data or materially influences business outcomes             | Formal assessment and periodic reassessment     |
| Moderate      | Supports important but non-critical AI activities                               | Standard due diligence and periodic review      |
| Low           | Provides limited AI functionality with minimal business impact                  | Proportionate supplier checks                   |

Internal supplier tiers should be defined by organizational criteria and should not be confused with legal classifications under the EU AI Act.

#### 17.5 AI Supplier Security Assessment

An AI Supplier Security Assessment evaluates whether an external provider has adequate security measures to protect the AI services and associated data.

Relevant areas include:

- Identity and access management.
- Encryption.
- Network security.
- Vulnerability management.
- Secure development.
- Model artifact protection.
- Security logging.
- Incident response.
- Business continuity.
- Data segregation.
- API security.
- Supply chain security.

#### 17.6 AI Supplier Privacy Assessment

An AI Supplier Privacy Assessment evaluates how an external provider processes personal data and whether the processing arrangements meet applicable privacy requirements.

It should examine:

- Data processing roles.
- Purpose and legal basis.
- Data categories.
- Processing locations.
- Retention.
- Subprocessors.
- International transfers.
- Data subject rights.
- Security measures.
- Deletion and return arrangements.

The organization must assess the actual processing relationship rather than assuming the provider is always a processor or always a controller.

#### 17.7 AI Contractual Governance

AI Contractual Governance establishes how AI-related responsibilities and obligations are defined and enforced through supplier contracts.

Relevant contractual topics include:

- Permitted AI use.
- Data ownership.
- Data processing instructions.
- Data retention.
- Training-data restrictions.
- Confidentiality.
- Security requirements.
- Audit rights.
- Incident notification.
- Service levels.
- Model change notifications.
- Subprocessor controls.
- Intellectual property.
- Regulatory cooperation.
- Termination.
- Data return and deletion.

Contractual commitments should be consistent with the organization's actual technical and operational requirements.

#### 17.8 AI Service-Level Agreement (AI SLA)

An AI Service-Level Agreement (AI SLA) defines measurable service commitments for an AI service provider.

Potential measures include:

- Service availability.
- API response time.
- Incident notification.
- Support response time.
- Recovery objectives.
- Maintenance windows.
- Service restoration.
- Capacity commitments.

An SLA should also address service dependencies and exclusions that could materially affect availability.

#### 17.9 AI Provider Concentration Risk

AI Provider Concentration Risk arises when an organization becomes excessively dependent on one or a small number of AI providers.

Potential consequences:

- Provider outage.
- Commercial dependency.
- Pricing changes.
- Model discontinuation.
- Reduced bargaining power.
- Geopolitical or jurisdictional exposure.
- Limited alternatives.
- Business continuity disruption.

Mitigation may include:

- Alternative provider assessments.
- Model portability.
- Abstraction layers.
- Data portability.
- Exit strategies.
- Fallback services.
- Concentration monitoring.

Multi-provider architecture can reduce some dependencies but may also introduce integration complexity, additional security exposure, and governance overhead.

#### 17.10 AI Fourth-Party Risk

AI Fourth-Party Risk refers to risks arising from suppliers or service providers used by an organization's direct AI provider.

For example:

```
TELECOM OPERATOR
       |
       v
AI SERVICE PROVIDER
       |
       v
CLOUD INFRASTRUCTURE PROVIDER
       |
       v
DATA / MODEL / PLATFORM SUBSUPPLIERS
```

A failure at a downstream supplier may affect the AI service even when the direct provider's own controls remain operational.

Organizations should identify material dependencies and obtain appropriate supplier transparency and assurance.

#### 17.11 AI Provider Exit Strategy

An AI Provider Exit Strategy is a documented plan for transitioning away from an external AI provider or terminating the relationship without unacceptable disruption.

It may address:

- Data export.
- Data deletion.
- Model or application portability.
- Alternative providers.
- Integration replacement.
- Credential revocation.
- Service termination.
- Contractual obligations.
- Transition testing.
- Business continuity.

A provider exit strategy should be realistic and tested where the service is business-critical.

### 18. AI Governance Incident Management and Accountability

AI-related incidents can involve cybersecurity, privacy, safety, operational, legal, ethical, or governance failures.

An effective governance framework ensures that incidents are identified, reported, investigated, escalated, remediated, and used to improve organizational controls.

#### 18.1 AI Governance Incident

An AI Governance Incident is an event involving an AI system that violates or may violate established governance requirements, approved use conditions, or applicable obligations.

Examples:

- Unauthorized AI deployment.
- Unapproved use of customer information.
- Unapproved model change.
- Failure to perform mandatory risk assessment.
- Violation of AI acceptable-use requirements.
- Missing required human oversight.
- Failure to meet contractual AI obligations.
- Unapproved use of an AI system outside its intended purpose.

Not every AI governance incident is necessarily a cybersecurity incident.

#### 18.2 AI Security Incident

An AI Security Incident is an event involving an actual or suspected compromise of the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of an AI system or its supporting environment.

Examples:

- Prompt injection leading to unauthorized access.
- Model theft.
- Data exfiltration.
- Model poisoning.
- Unauthorized API access.
- Compromised model artifacts.
- Malicious modification of AI configurations.
- Denial-of-service attacks against AI infrastructure.

#### 18.3 AI Privacy Incident

An AI Privacy Incident is an event involving the unauthorized or otherwise inappropriate processing, disclosure, loss, alteration, or access to personal data in connection with AI.

Examples:

- Customer personal data exposed through an LLM response.
- Personal information entered into an unauthorized AI service.
- Excessive retention of prompts containing personal data.
- Unauthorized access to AI conversation histories.
- Inappropriate disclosure of personal data through RAG retrieval.

A personal data breach must be evaluated under applicable data protection requirements, including relevant GDPR notification obligations.

#### 18.4 AI Incident Reporting

AI Incident Reporting is the process of notifying designated organizational functions about suspected or confirmed AI-related incidents.

It should define:

- Reporting channels.
- Incident categories.
- Severity classification.
- Notification responsibilities.
- Escalation timelines.
- Evidence preservation.
- Regulatory assessment.
- Communication responsibilities.

Employees should have a clear process for reporting unexpected or harmful AI behavior.

#### 18.5 AI Incident Severity Classification

An organization may define internal AI incident severity levels.

| Severity | Description                                                 | Example                                                                       |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Major business, safety, security, or regulatory impact      | AI system causes a severe operational incident or large-scale data exposure   |
| High     | Significant impact requiring urgent management intervention | Unauthorized AI processing of sensitive customer information                  |
| Medium   | Limited but material governance or operational impact       | Unapproved model change affecting a business workflow                         |
| Low      | Minor deviation with limited impact                         | Incomplete governance documentation without immediate operational consequence |

Severity must be assessed against defined organizational criteria and actual impact. These labels are illustrative, not universal legal categories.

#### 18.6 AI Incident Response

AI Incident Response is the coordinated process of managing an AI-related incident from detection through containment, investigation, recovery, and lessons learned.

Typical activities:

1. Detection and reporting.
2. Initial assessment.
3. Severity classification.
4. Escalation.
5. Containment.
6. Investigation.
7. Impact assessment.
8. Recovery.
9. Regulatory and contractual notification assessment.
10. Corrective action.
11. Post-incident review.

#### 18.7 AI System Suspension

AI System Suspension is the temporary restriction or disabling of an AI system or capability when continued operation creates unacceptable risk or violates an approval condition.

Examples:

- Disabling an AI agent's access to billing transactions.
- Suspending an LLM integration after a suspected data exposure.
- Disabling an affected model endpoint during investigation.
- Restricting a system following unexpected unsafe behavior.

Suspension should be supported by defined decision authority and recovery criteria.

#### 18.8 AI Kill Switch

An AI Kill Switch is a technical or operational mechanism that enables authorized personnel to rapidly stop an AI system, model, agent, or selected capability.

Examples:

- Disable an AI endpoint.
- Revoke an agent's credentials.
- Block external tool execution.
- Disable automated transaction execution.
- Isolate an AI workload.

A kill switch should be tested and protected against unauthorized activation or bypass.

#### 18.9 AI Incident Lessons Learned

AI Incident Lessons Learned is the process of identifying governance, technical, operational, and organizational improvements following an AI-related incident.

It should consider:

- Root causes.
- Control weaknesses.
- Governance gaps.
- Ownership failures.
- Supplier issues.
- Monitoring limitations.
- Human oversight failures.
- Policy and procedure deficiencies.
- Required corrective actions.

The objective is to reduce recurrence and improve the governance framework.

### 19. AI Governance Assurance, Audit, and Independent Oversight

AI governance must be independently evaluated to establish whether governance arrangements are properly designed and operating effectively.

Internal audit and other assurance functions provide management with an independent perspective on governance maturity, risk management, and control performance.

#### 19.1 AI Governance Assurance

AI Governance Assurance is the process of obtaining evidence-based confidence that AI governance requirements are appropriately implemented and functioning as intended.

It may include:

- Governance framework reviews.
- Policy compliance testing.
- AI inventory validation.
- Risk assessment reviews.
- Control effectiveness testing.
- Third-party assurance.
- AI lifecycle reviews.
- Regulatory compliance reviews.

#### 19.2 AI Governance Audit

An AI Governance Audit is a systematic and independent examination of an organization's AI governance arrangements, processes, and controls.

Audit objectives may include evaluating:

- Governance accountability.
- AI inventory completeness.
- Risk classification.
- Policy implementation.
- AI approval processes.
- Model governance.
- Third-party oversight.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Incident management.
- Management reporting.

An audit should be based on defined criteria, sufficient evidence, and appropriate independence.

#### 19.3 AI Governance Audit Scope

An AI governance audit may cover:

| Audit Domain          | Example Audit Procedures                                                   |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Governance Structure  | Review charter, roles, and decision authority                              |
| AI Inventory          | Reconcile registered AI systems against procurement and technology records |
| AI Policy             | Test policy communication and compliance                                   |
| Risk Management       | Review risk assessment quality and ownership                               |
| Model Governance      | Review validation and monitoring records                                   |
| Data Governance       | Examine data classification and lineage evidence                           |
| Third-Party Risk      | Review supplier due diligence and contracts                                |
| Regulatory Compliance | Test applicable obligation mappings                                        |
| Lifecycle Management  | Sample AI approvals and changes                                            |
| Incident Management   | Review incident records and escalation                                     |
| Reporting             | Validate management KPIs and KRIs                                          |
| Risk Acceptance       | Verify authorized approvals and review dates                               |

#### 19.4 AI Governance Audit Evidence

AI Governance Audit Evidence is information used to support audit conclusions about governance and control effectiveness.

Examples include:

- Approved policies.
- Committee minutes.
- AI inventories.
- Risk registers.
- Assessment reports.
- Architecture approvals.
- Access records.
- Model validation reports.
- Supplier assessments.
- Contracts.
- Incident tickets.
- Change records.
- Monitoring reports.
- Risk acceptance records.

Evidence should be relevant, reliable, sufficient, and traceable to the audit objective.

#### 19.5 AI Governance Audit Finding

An AI Governance Audit Finding is a documented condition identified during an audit that demonstrates a weakness, nonconformity, control failure, or improvement opportunity.

A finding should typically describe:

- Criteria: What should exist or happen?
- Condition: What was observed?
- Cause: Why did the issue occur?
- Effect or risk: Why does it matter?
- Recommendation: What should be improved?
- Management action: What will the organization do?
- Owner and target date.

#### 19.6 AI Governance Nonconformity

An AI Governance Nonconformity is a failure to meet an applicable governance requirement.

Examples:

- A mandatory AI assessment was not completed.
- A required approval was missing.
- A governance control was not implemented.
- An established procedure was not followed.

A nonconformity should be evaluated and addressed through corrective action.

Not every audit observation is automatically a formal nonconformity. The applicable criteria and audit methodology determine its classification.

#### 19.7 AI Corrective Action

An AI Corrective Action is an action taken to eliminate the cause of an identified nonconformity or undesirable condition and prevent recurrence.

Example:

An audit discovers that multiple AI applications were deployed without registration.

A corrective action should address more than updating the inventory. It may require implementing a mandatory procurement registration gate, reconciling existing systems, assigning ownership, and testing the new process.

#### 19.8 AI Governance Continuous Improvement

AI Governance Continuous Improvement is the ongoing process of evaluating and improving AI governance policies, processes, controls, and operating arrangements.

Improvement inputs include:

- Audit findings.
- AI incidents.
- Risk assessments.
- Regulatory changes.
- New technology.
- Supplier changes.
- Control testing.
- Management reviews.
- Stakeholder feedback.
- Lessons learned.

This creates a governance feedback loop rather than a static compliance program.

#### 19.9 AI Governance Maturity Assessment

An AI Governance Maturity Assessment evaluates the development and effectiveness of an organization's AI governance capabilities.

Potential assessment dimensions:

### 21. Summary

AI governance implementation requires a coordinated operating model that translates policies and standards into business processes, technical controls, lifecycle decisions, supplier oversight, and independent assurance.

The major implementation areas include:

- AI governance intake and approval workflows.
- Decision rights and escalation mechanisms.
- Lifecycle governance gates.
- Intended-purpose and system-scope management.
- Business, system, model, and control ownership.
- Third-party AI due diligence and contractual governance.
- Supplier concentration and fourth-party risk management.
- AI incident reporting, response, and suspension.
- Governance audit, corrective action, and continuous improvement.
- Enterprise governance operating models and RACI.

For GRC professionals, the central challenge is to make AI governance operational across organizational boundaries. This requires connecting the business, engineering, cybersecurity, privacy, legal, procurement, risk, and audit functions through clear responsibilities and enforceable processes.

### Core Principle

AI governance is not effective because an organization has a committee, policy, or framework. It is effective when decision rights are enforced, lifecycle gates operate, suppliers are controlled, incidents are managed, and independent assurance demonstrates that governance requirements work in practice.

## Part 4 — AI Governance Framework Integration, Regulatory Accountability, Enterprise Assurance, and Strategic Implementation

### 22. AI Governance Framework Integration and Enterprise Alignment

AI governance should not operate as an isolated management discipline. It must be integrated into existing enterprise governance structures, including corporate governance, enterprise risk management, information security, privacy, data governance, procurement, architecture, legal, compliance, and internal audit.

The objective is to establish a unified governance environment in which AI-related decisions are consistent with organizational strategy, risk appetite, legal obligations, and operational requirements.

#### 22.1 Enterprise AI Governance Integration

Enterprise AI Governance Integration is the process of incorporating AI governance requirements into existing enterprise management systems, governance committees, risk processes, policies, and assurance activities.

An organization should avoid creating a completely separate governance structure for every AI initiative. Instead, AI governance should extend existing control environments while addressing risks specific to AI systems.

For example:

- Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) should incorporate AI-related strategic, operational, regulatory, and third-party risks.
- Information Security Management Systems (ISMS) should address AI application security, model security, data protection, infrastructure security, and AI supply chain risks.
- Privacy Information Management Systems (PIMS) should address personal data processing, profiling, automated decision-making, retention, and privacy impact assessments.
- Data Governance should address dataset ownership, data quality, provenance, lineage, classification, and permitted data usage.
- Procurement and Third-Party Risk Management should evaluate AI providers, model suppliers, cloud platforms, and external AI services.
- Enterprise Architecture should incorporate AI security architecture, integration requirements, data flows, identity controls, and technology standards.
- Internal Audit should independently assess whether AI governance controls are appropriately designed and operating effectively.

Enterprise integration model:

```
                  BOARD / EXECUTIVE MANAGEMENT
                             |
                 Enterprise Governance
                             |
          +------------------+------------------+
          |                  |                  |
         ERM                ISMS               PIMS
          |                  |                  |
    AI Risk Profile    AI Security Controls   AI Privacy Controls
          |                  |                  |
          +------------------+------------------+
                             |
                     AI GOVERNANCE
                             |
          +------------------+------------------+
          |                  |                  |
     Data Governance    Enterprise Architecture  Procurement
          |                  |                  |
          +------------------+------------------+
                             |
                    AI SYSTEM LIFECYCLE
                             |
        Design -> Development -> Deployment -> Monitoring
                             |
                      Internal Audit
```

The integration model ensures that AI governance is connected to the organization's established management and assurance processes.

#### 22.2 Enterprise Risk Management Integration

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) is the organization-wide process of identifying, assessing, treating, monitoring, and reporting risks that could affect strategic objectives.

AI risk should be incorporated into the enterprise risk register rather than managed exclusively within technical AI teams.

Typical enterprise AI risk categories include:

| Risk category      | Example                                                                   |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strategic risk     | Dependence on AI capabilities that do not deliver expected business value |
| Operational risk   | AI system failure disrupts customer-service operations                    |
| Cybersecurity risk | Prompt injection exposes confidential corporate information               |
| Privacy risk       | Personal data is disclosed through an AI application                      |
| Regulatory risk    | An AI system is deployed without satisfying applicable legal obligations  |
| Third-party risk   | External AI provider changes its service or data-retention practices      |
| Model risk         | Model performance deteriorates after deployment                           |
| Reputational risk  | AI-generated decisions produce discriminatory or misleading outcomes      |
| Financial risk     | Uncontrolled model usage generates excessive inference costs              |

AI risk aggregation is important because a single AI system may create several interconnected enterprise exposures.

For example, an AI-powered customer-service assistant could create cybersecurity, privacy, operational, regulatory, and reputational risks simultaneously. These should be represented as distinct risk consequences where appropriate, while avoiding unnecessary duplication of the same underlying risk event.

#### 22.3 Integration with ISMS and Information Security Governance

An Information Security Management System (ISMS) provides a systematic approach to managing information security risks through policies, responsibilities, risk assessments, controls, monitoring, and continual improvement.

AI governance should extend the ISMS to address AI-specific assets and threats.

Examples include:

- AI model and model artifact protection.
- Training and inference data protection.
- Prompt and response security.
- AI application and API security.
- RAG pipeline security.
- Agent and tool authorization.
- Model supply chain security.
- AI infrastructure and workload isolation.
- AI security monitoring and incident response.

ISO/IEC 27001 can provide the broader information security management structure, while AI-specific risks require additional assessment and control design.

An organization should not assume that implementing ISO/IEC 27001 automatically provides complete AI security governance.

#### 22.4 Integration with Privacy Governance

AI systems may process personal data during training, fine-tuning, inference, evaluation, monitoring, and analytics.

Privacy governance should therefore be incorporated throughout the AI lifecycle.

Important privacy governance activities include:

1. Identify whether personal data is processed.
2. Determine the applicable legal basis and purpose of processing.
3. Identify data subjects and categories of personal data.
4. Assess data minimization and retention requirements.
5. Evaluate data transfers and international processing.
6. Determine whether a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is required.
7. Evaluate automated decision-making and profiling implications.
8. Establish appropriate technical and organizational safeguards.
9. Define data subject rights handling procedures.
10. Monitor compliance throughout the processing lifecycle.

Important distinction: A DPIA is not automatically mandatory for every AI system. Under GDPR, it is required where the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

#### 22.5 Integration with Data Governance

AI governance depends heavily on the quality, integrity, provenance, and permitted use of data.

Data governance should establish:

- Data ownership and stewardship.
- Data classification and handling requirements.
- Data quality standards.
- Data lineage and provenance.
- Data access authorization.
- Dataset approval and usage restrictions.
- Data retention and deletion.
- Data sharing and transfer requirements.
- Training-data and inference-data governance.
- Dataset change management.

A dataset being technically accessible does not mean it is legally, contractually, or operationally authorized for AI training or inference.

#### 22.6 Integration with Procurement and Third-Party Risk Management

AI capabilities are frequently acquired through external providers, including cloud platforms, foundation model providers, SaaS vendors, and specialist AI suppliers.

Procurement must therefore incorporate AI-specific due diligence before contractual commitment.

Typical assessment areas include:

| Assessment domain      | Governance question                                                                       |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provider transparency  | Does the provider disclose relevant information about the AI service and its limitations? |
| Data usage             | Can customer data be used for model training or service improvement?                      |
| Data retention         | How long are prompts, responses, and associated logs retained?                            |
| Data residency         | Where are customer data and model-processing activities located?                          |
| Security               | What security controls protect the service and its infrastructure?                        |
| Subprocessors          | Which fourth parties participate in service delivery?                                     |
| Regulatory obligations | Which legal obligations apply to the provider and the organization?                       |
| Incident management    | How are security and privacy incidents communicated?                                      |
| Service continuity     | What happens if the provider discontinues the service?                                    |
| Exit strategy          | Can data, configurations, and business processes be migrated?                             |

Contractual assurances should be validated against actual service architecture, technical capabilities, and independent assurance evidence.

Control Mapping establishes relationships between organizational controls and external requirements.

             |

             |

#### 23.5 Statement of Applicability and AI Governance

A Statement of Applicability (SoA) is a documented record of selected and excluded controls, including justification and implementation status, where required by the applicable management-system standard.

For an ISO/IEC 27001 ISMS, the SoA identifies the applicable Annex A controls and justifies inclusion or exclusion.

An organization may maintain an additional AI governance control applicability register to document which AI controls apply to its AI systems.

Do not assume that an AI governance control register automatically replaces the ISO/IEC 27001 SoA.

### 24. Regulatory Accountability and AI Legal Obligations

AI governance must translate legal requirements into operational responsibilities, technical controls, contractual commitments, and auditable evidence.

Regulatory Accountability means that the organization can identify which legal obligations apply, determine who is responsible for fulfilling them, implement appropriate controls, and demonstrate compliance.

#### 24.1 AI Regulatory Obligations Register

An AI Regulatory Obligations Register is a structured record of applicable legal and regulatory requirements affecting AI systems and their lifecycle.

Typical fields include:

| Field               | Description                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Obligation ID       | Unique identifier                                                  |
| Regulation          | Applicable law or regulation                                       |
| Article / provision | Specific legal reference                                           |
| Requirement         | Description of the obligation                                      |
| Applicability       | Whether and why the requirement applies                            |
| Responsible entity  | Provider, deployer, importer, distributor, or other relevant actor |
| Accountable owner   | Internal role responsible for compliance                           |
| Control reference   | Internal control implementing the obligation                       |
| Evidence            | Records demonstrating compliance                                   |
| Review date         | Date of last applicability or compliance review                    |
| Status              | Compliant, partially compliant, non-compliant, or not applicable   |

The register should be reviewed whenever there is a material change in system purpose, functionality, deployment context, legal status, or regulatory requirements.

#### 24.2 Provider and Deployer Accountability

Under the EU AI Act, obligations depend on the relevant economic operator's role and the AI system's classification and circumstances.

Provider: An entity that develops an AI system or general-purpose AI model, or has it developed, and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, subject to the Act's definitions.

Deployer: An entity using an AI system under its authority, except where the system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

Other relevant economic operators include importers, distributors, authorised representatives, and manufacturers in specified circumstances.

A company may perform different roles for different AI systems. Its role should be determined for each relevant deployment and contractual arrangement.

#### 24.3 General-Purpose AI Accountability

General-Purpose AI (GPAI) refers to AI models with significant generality that are capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks and can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications.

The EU AI Act establishes obligations for providers of GPAI models, with additional requirements for models presenting systemic risk.

Important governance considerations include:

- Technical documentation.
- Information for downstream AI system providers.
- Copyright-related policy obligations.
- Training-content summaries, where applicable.
- Evaluation and risk management for systemic-risk models.
- Serious incident reporting and cybersecurity measures where applicable.

A GPAI model is not automatically an EU AI Act high-risk AI system. Model-level obligations and AI-system-level classification must be assessed separately.

#### 24.4 High-Risk AI System Accountability

For AI systems classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act, applicable requirements may include:

- Risk management system.
- Data and data governance requirements.
- Technical documentation.
- Record-keeping and logging.
- Transparency and information to deployers.
- Human oversight.
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.
- Quality management system.
- Conformity assessment.
- Post-market monitoring.
- Serious incident reporting and corrective action, where applicable.

The precise obligations depend on the system's classification, the operator's role, and the applicable provisions and implementation dates.

Organizations must not classify an AI system as high-risk merely because it is important to the business. Legal classification requires assessment against the Act's actual criteria.

#### 24.5 Transparency and Explainability Obligations

Transparency concerns providing appropriate information about an AI system, its use, limitations, and relevant characteristics.

Explainability concerns the ability to provide meaningful explanations of an AI system's outputs or decisions.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable.

For example, a customer interacting with an AI-powered service assistant may need to know that they are interacting with an AI system. A regulator or internal reviewer may require additional documentation about the system's purpose, data processing, decision logic, limitations, and oversight.

Transparency does not automatically require disclosure of proprietary model weights, source code, or confidential trade secrets. Applicable legal requirements and context determine the information that must be provided.

#### 24.6 Human Oversight and Accountability

Human oversight should provide meaningful authority to understand, monitor, intervene in, or override AI-supported processes where required.

A human reviewer who merely approves every AI recommendation without sufficient information, competence, time, or authority does not constitute effective oversight.

An organization should define:

- Which decisions require human review.
- Which outputs can be used automatically.
- Which decisions require approval before execution.
- What information reviewers must receive.
- How reviewers can challenge or override AI outputs.
- How decisions and overrides are recorded.
- How human oversight effectiveness is tested.

### 25. AI Governance Assurance, Audit, and Control Effectiveness

AI Governance Assurance is the process of evaluating whether AI governance policies, processes, and controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

The existence of an AI governance policy does not demonstrate that governance is effective.

Assurance must verify actual operational behavior and supporting evidence.

#### 25.1 AI Governance Control Lifecycle

A governance control should be assessed across three distinct dimensions:

| Dimension                    | Assessment question                                                   |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Design effectiveness         | Is the control appropriately designed to address the identified risk? |
| Implementation effectiveness | Has the control been implemented in the relevant environment?         |
| Operating effectiveness      | Does the control consistently operate as intended over time?          |

Example:

Control: All new AI systems must undergo a documented risk assessment before production approval.

- Design: The policy clearly defines assessment requirements, risk criteria, ownership, and approval authority.
- Implementation: The organization has configured an AI intake and assessment workflow.
- Operating effectiveness: Sampled AI deployments demonstrate completed assessments and approvals before production release.

A control may be well designed but not implemented. It may also be implemented but fail to operate consistently.

#### 25.2 AI Governance Audit

An AI Governance Audit is an independent or appropriately objective examination of AI governance arrangements, processes, controls, and evidence.

Typical audit domains include:

1. AI governance structure and accountability.
2. AI system inventory completeness.
3. AI risk classification and assessments.
4. AI policies and standards.
5. Model and dataset governance.
6. AI security and privacy controls.
7. Third-party AI governance.
8. Regulatory obligations and compliance.
9. AI lifecycle approvals and change management.
10. Monitoring, incident response, and retirement.
11. Governance metrics and management reporting.
12. Evidence retention and auditability.

An audit should be risk-based. It should prioritize systems with greater potential impact, material business dependency, sensitive data processing, significant autonomy, or relevant regulatory exposure.

#### 25.3 Audit Evidence

AI governance evidence should demonstrate that controls operate in practice.

Examples include:

| Control area      | Evidence                                                         |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Governance        | Approved AI governance charter and committee minutes             |
| Inventory         | AI system inventory and reconciliation records                   |
| Risk management   | Completed risk assessments and treatment plans                   |
| Data governance   | Dataset lineage and data approval records                        |
| Model governance  | Model cards, validation reports, and version history             |
| Security          | Threat models, penetration-test reports, and remediation records |
| Privacy           | DPIAs, data-flow diagrams, and privacy reviews                   |
| Third-party       | Supplier assessments and contractual clauses                     |
| Regulatory        | Obligations register and compliance assessments                  |
| Monitoring        | Performance dashboards and security monitoring reports           |
| Incidents         | Incident records, root-cause analysis, and corrective actions    |
| Change management | Approved change requests and reassessment records                |

Evidence should be attributable, timestamped, retained according to organizational requirements, and linked to the relevant control and AI system.

#### 25.4 AI Governance Findings and Corrective Actions

An AI Governance Finding is a documented deficiency identified through monitoring, assessment, audit, or compliance review.

Typical finding classifications include:

- Critical deficiency.
- High-severity deficiency.
- Medium-severity deficiency.
- Low-severity deficiency.
- Observation or improvement opportunity.

Organizations should define their own classification criteria and escalation thresholds.

A corrective action process should include:

1. Finding identification.
2. Root-cause analysis.
3. Risk and impact assessment.
4. Corrective action plan.
5. Action owner assignment.
6. Target completion date.
7. Implementation evidence.
8. Validation of corrective action.
9. Formal closure.

A finding should not be closed solely because an action owner reports that an action has been completed. Closure should require appropriate validation.

Examples:

| Overdue risk assessments                 | AI deployment without current risk evaluation |
| High-risk findings overdue               | Unresolved material control weaknesses        |
| Unapproved AI provider usage             | Third-party and data exposure                 |
| Repeated AI incidents                    | Ineffective controls or recurring weaknesses  |
| Excessive privileged AI access           | Unauthorized action or data exposure          |
| Unresolved model performance degradation | Reliability and business-impact exposure      |
| Overdue supplier reassessments           | Deteriorating third-party assurance           |

A KRI threshold should trigger defined investigation, escalation, or treatment actions. A metric without a response mechanism is merely reporting, not effective risk management.

#### 25.7 AI Governance Maturity Assessment

An AI Governance Maturity Assessment evaluates how consistently and effectively an organization has established its AI governance capabilities.

An illustrative five-level maturity model:

| Level | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                              |
| ----- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Initial    | Ad hoc AI usage; limited visibility and informal decisions                   |
| 2     | Developing | Basic policies, inventory, and risk assessments introduced                   |
| 3     | Defined    | Approved governance framework, roles, controls, and lifecycle gates          |
| 4     | Managed    | Consistent execution, metrics, monitoring, and assurance                     |
| 5     | Optimized  | Continuous improvement, integrated governance, and proactive risk management |

This is an illustrative internal maturity model, not an official maturity scale prescribed by NIST or ISO/IEC 42001.

Maturity should be assessed using evidence rather than management self-declarations alone.

### 26. AI Governance Strategic Roadmap and Enterprise Implementation

AI Governance Strategic Roadmap is a structured implementation plan that establishes the capabilities, controls, responsibilities, and assurance mechanisms required to govern AI across the organization.

The roadmap should be risk-based and proportionate to the organization's AI adoption, regulatory exposure, operational dependency, and available resources.

#### 26.1 Governance Implementation Phases

A practical enterprise implementation can be divided into six phases.

| Phase        | Focus                   | Key deliverables                                                       |
| ------------ | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Discover  | Establish AI visibility | AI inventory, stakeholder map, initial use-case register               |
| 2. Assess    | Understand exposure     | AI risk assessments, regulatory applicability assessment, gap analysis |
| 3. Design    | Establish governance    | AI governance framework, policies, RACI, control framework             |
| 4. Implement | Operationalize controls | Intake workflow, approval gates, risk treatment, supplier controls     |
| 5. Assure    | Validate effectiveness  | KPIs, KRIs, control testing, audit evidence, management reporting      |
| 6. Improve   | Mature governance       | Corrective actions, maturity reassessment, roadmap updates             |

The sequence is logical, but implementation can be iterative. For example, basic inventory and risk controls should not be delayed until every policy has been finalized.

#### 26.2 Minimum Viable AI Governance

Minimum Viable AI Governance (MVAG) is the smallest practical set of governance capabilities needed to establish meaningful oversight of an organization's AI usage.

A minimum viable framework should include:

1. Approved AI governance policy.
2. AI system and use-case inventory.
3. Defined governance ownership and decision rights.
4. AI risk classification methodology.
5. Documented risk assessment.
6. Acceptable-use requirements.
7. Security and privacy assessment.
8. Third-party AI assessment.
9. Production approval process.
10. Monitoring and incident reporting.
11. Change management and reassessment.
12. Evidence retention and management reporting.

A minimum viable framework is not a substitute for applicable legal obligations or risk-specific controls. It is a starting governance capability that must scale with actual exposure.

#### 26.3 AI Governance Target Operating Model

An AI Governance Target Operating Model (TOM) defines how the organization will execute AI governance across its structure, processes, technology, responsibilities, and assurance functions.

The target operating model should define:

- Governance committees and delegated authorities.
- Central AI governance function.
- Business and technical ownership.
- AI risk management processes.
- AI security and privacy responsibilities.
- Architecture and procurement approval.
- Regulatory compliance responsibilities.
- Independent assurance.
- Management reporting and escalation.
- Technology platforms supporting governance workflows.

The target model should be integrated into existing enterprise processes rather than relying entirely on manual governance activities.

#### 26.4 Centralized, Federated, and Hybrid Governance Models

| Model       | Characteristics                                                        | Advantages                          | Limitations                                        |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Centralized | A central function controls most AI governance decisions               | Consistency and strong oversight    | Potential bottlenecks and limited business context |
| Federated   | Business units manage governance within common enterprise requirements | Local ownership and scalability     | Inconsistent implementation risk                   |
| Hybrid      | Central policy and oversight with delegated business execution         | Balance between control and agility | Requires clear decision rights and escalation      |

A hybrid model is often practical for large organizations with multiple business units, technology platforms, and AI use cases. However, it is not automatically the best model; organizational size, risk exposure, and regulatory obligations should determine the design.

#### 26.5 AI Governance Implementation Success Factors

Common implementation success factors include:

- Executive sponsorship and clear accountability.
- A reliable AI inventory.
- Risk-based prioritization.
- Integration with ERM, ISMS, PIMS, and procurement.
- Clear ownership of systems, models, data, and controls.
- Enforceable approval gates.
- Adequate technical and business expertise.
- Effective third-party oversight.
- Meaningful monitoring and reporting.
- Independent assurance and continuous improvement.

Common failure patterns include:

- Treating AI governance as a documentation exercise.
- Establishing committees without decision-making authority.
- Relying on voluntary declarations of AI usage.
- Confusing framework adoption with regulatory compliance.
- Approving systems without understanding their data flows and integrations.
- Treating vendor certifications as proof of complete service security.
- Allowing production deployment before risk treatment and approval.
- Measuring policy publication rather than control effectiveness.

#### 27.6 Governance Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. What is AI governance?

AI governance is the organizational framework of policies, responsibilities, decision rights, processes, and controls used to direct and oversee AI systems throughout their lifecycle. It ensures that AI is developed, acquired, deployed, and monitored in alignment with business objectives, risk appetite, and applicable legal obligations.

Q2. How would you implement AI governance in a large enterprise?

I would begin by establishing an AI inventory and understanding the organization's existing AI use cases, providers, and deployment environments. I would then assess regulatory applicability and risk exposure, define governance roles and decision rights, establish policies and a risk-based control framework, and integrate the process into existing ERM, ISMS, privacy, architecture, and procurement workflows. Finally, I would implement monitoring, KPIs, KRIs, control testing, and independent assurance.

Q3. What is the difference between AI governance and AI risk management?

AI governance establishes the overall direction, accountability, authority, and oversight of AI activities. AI risk management is one of the key processes operating within that governance structure. Governance defines who makes decisions and under which principles; risk management identifies, assesses, treats, and monitors AI-related risks.

Q4. What is the difference between NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001?

NIST AI RMF is a voluntary risk management framework organized around GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE. ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI Management System. They are complementary but have different structures and purposes.

Q5. How do you integrate AI governance with ISO/IEC 27001?

I would identify the AI systems and associated information security risks, integrate AI-specific risks into the ISMS risk assessment, and determine which existing controls are relevant. I would then identify additional controls required for model security, prompt injection, AI data protection, RAG, agentic execution, and AI supply chain security. The resulting control framework should be linked to risk treatment, evidence, monitoring, and assurance.

Q6. What is an AI regulatory obligations register?

It is a structured record of applicable AI-related legal requirements, their applicability, responsible roles, internal controls, evidence, and compliance status. It enables the organization to translate legal obligations into operational responsibilities and auditable controls.

Q7. How do you determine whether an AI system is high-risk under the EU AI Act?

I would examine the system's intended purpose, functionality, deployment context, and the Act's applicable classification provisions, including relevant Annex III use cases and Article 6 criteria. I would also evaluate applicable exceptions and the system's role in a regulated product where relevant. I would not rely solely on an internal risk score or the perceived business criticality of the system.

Q8. What is the role of the AI Governance Committee?

The committee provides oversight and decision-making for AI governance matters within its delegated authority. It reviews material AI risks, approves or escalates decisions, monitors governance performance, resolves cross-functional issues, and ensures that significant AI deployments receive appropriate oversight.

Q9. How do you measure AI governance effectiveness?

I would use a combination of governance KPIs, KRIs, control testing, audit findings, incident trends, risk treatment progress, inventory completeness, and maturity assessments. Metrics should be linked to clear thresholds and escalation actions. A high policy completion rate alone does not prove governance effectiveness.

Q10. What is the difference between AI governance assurance and AI compliance?

Compliance evaluates whether applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, or internal requirements are being met. Governance assurance evaluates whether the broader governance framework and its controls are suitably designed, implemented, and operating effectively. Compliance can be one part of governance assurance, but the concepts are not identical.

Q11. How would you govern an AI system provided by a third party?

I would establish the organization's role, intended use, data flows, and dependencies, then perform supplier due diligence covering security, privacy, transparency, data usage, retention, subprocessors, resilience, regulatory responsibilities, and contractual protections. I would also establish ongoing monitoring, change notification, incident escalation, and an exit strategy.

Q12. What is the most common mistake organizations make when implementing AI governance?

Treating AI governance as a policy-writing exercise rather than an operational management system. Governance requires actual decision authority, system visibility, risk-based approvals, implemented controls, evidence, monitoring, and accountability. Without those capabilities, the organization may have documentation but no effective control over its AI environment.

### 30. Summary

AI governance is an enterprise management discipline that establishes how AI systems are directed, approved, monitored, and controlled throughout their lifecycle.

Effective governance requires integration with existing enterprise capabilities, including ERM, ISMS, privacy management, data governance, procurement, enterprise architecture, regulatory compliance, and internal audit.

The key concepts covered in this lesson are:

- AI governance must operate through defined accountability, decision rights, policies, processes, and controls.
- Enterprise integration prevents AI governance from becoming an isolated compliance function.
- Framework harmonization aligns standards and regulatory requirements without assuming that they are equivalent.
- Regulatory accountability requires identifying applicable obligations and translating them into operational controls and evidence.
- Provider and deployer responsibilities must be assessed according to actual roles and applicable legal definitions.
- AI governance assurance evaluates control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness.
- KPIs measure governance performance, while KRIs signal potential risk exposure.
- Governance maturity measures organizational capability, not automatic regulatory compliance.
- A strategic roadmap should establish governance incrementally, prioritizing visibility, accountability, risk assessment, and enforceable lifecycle controls.
- Enterprise governance must produce measurable outcomes, not merely policies, committees, and documentation.

### Core Principle

AI governance is effective only when organizational authority, accountability, risk management, regulatory obligations, and operational controls are integrated into enforceable processes supported by reliable evidence and independent assurance.
