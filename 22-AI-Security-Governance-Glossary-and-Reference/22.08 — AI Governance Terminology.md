# 22.08 — AI Governance Terminology

## Part 1 — AI Governance Foundations, Principles, Organizational Structures, and Accountability

### 1. AI Governance Fundamentals

AI governance establishes the organizational structures, policies, processes, roles, and controls that direct and oversee the responsible development, procurement, deployment, operation, and retirement of AI systems.

It ensures that AI initiatives remain aligned with organizational objectives, applicable legal requirements, ethical principles, security requirements, and stakeholder expectations.

AI governance is not limited to technical teams. It involves executive management, business owners, cybersecurity, legal, compliance, privacy, risk management, procurement, internal audit, and other relevant functions.

#### 1.1 AI Governance

AI Governance refers to the system of leadership, organizational arrangements, policies, decision-making processes, accountability mechanisms, and oversight activities used to direct and control an organization's use of AI.

It establishes:

- Who can approve AI initiatives.
- Who owns AI systems and associated risks.
- Which AI use cases are permitted.
- What standards and controls must be followed.
- How AI systems are assessed and monitored.
- How incidents and governance violations are handled.
- How compliance and accountability are demonstrated.

Example:

A telecommunications operator wants to introduce an AI-powered customer-service platform using an external LLM.

AI governance determines:

1. Whether the business is authorized to use generative AI for customer interactions.
2. Whether customer information can be processed by the selected provider.
3. Which security and privacy assessments are required.
4. Who approves the AI architecture.
5. Whether the system can access Salesforce customer records.
6. Which activities require human approval.
7. Who accepts the remaining business and technology risks.
8. How the system will be monitored after deployment.

AI governance provides the organizational authority and control structure for these decisions.

#### 1.2 AI Governance Framework

An AI Governance Framework is a structured collection of principles, policies, standards, procedures, responsibilities, and oversight mechanisms used to manage AI activities.

A typical framework contains:

| Governance Component          | Purpose                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Governance Principles      | Establish the organization's expectations for AI use    |
| AI Policy                     | Define mandatory organizational requirements            |
| AI Standards                  | Establish minimum technical and governance requirements |
| AI Procedures                 | Explain how governance activities are performed         |
| AI Roles and Responsibilities | Assign ownership and accountability                     |
| AI Risk Management            | Identify, assess, treat, and monitor AI risks           |
| AI Lifecycle Governance       | Control AI from conception through retirement           |
| AI Compliance Management      | Identify and manage applicable obligations              |
| AI Assurance                  | Evaluate governance and control effectiveness           |
| AI Reporting                  | Provide management visibility into AI activities        |

The framework should be integrated with existing enterprise governance rather than creating unnecessary parallel structures.

#### 1.3 AI Governance Operating Model

An AI Governance Operating Model defines how AI governance is organized and executed across the enterprise.

It establishes the relationship between executive oversight, business operations, technical implementation, risk management, and independent assurance.

A typical operating model includes:

- Board and executive oversight.
- AI governance committee.
- AI governance office or center of excellence.
- Business and AI system ownership.
- AI engineering and technology operations.
- Cybersecurity and privacy oversight.
- Legal and regulatory compliance.
- Internal audit and independent assurance.

The operating model should define decision rights, escalation paths, approval authorities, and reporting responsibilities.

#### 1.4 AI Governance Policy

An AI Governance Policy is a formally approved document that establishes mandatory organizational requirements for the responsible use and management of AI.

Typical policy requirements include:

- AI system registration.
- Acceptable and prohibited AI use.
- AI risk assessments.
- Data protection requirements.
- Security requirements.
- Human oversight.
- Third-party AI procurement.
- Model validation and monitoring.
- Transparency and documentation.
- Incident reporting.
- Risk acceptance.
- AI system retirement.

Example:

An organization may establish a policy requiring every AI system processing confidential business information to undergo security, privacy, and third-party risk assessments before production deployment.

The policy defines the requirement; supporting standards and procedures explain how to implement it.

#### 1.5 AI Governance Standard

An AI Governance Standard defines mandatory requirements and minimum control expectations that support the organization's AI governance policy.

Examples include:

- AI system inventory standard.
- AI risk classification standard.
- AI data governance standard.
- AI model validation standard.
- AI security standard.
- AI third-party assessment standard.
- AI logging and monitoring standard.
- AI change management standard.

Distinction: A policy establishes what the organization requires. A standard defines mandatory requirements for satisfying the policy. A procedure explains the steps for carrying out the requirements.

#### 1.6 AI Governance Procedure

An AI Governance Procedure describes the operational steps required to execute a governance activity.

Example: AI system approval procedure.

```
Business submits AI use case
          |
          v
AI system registration
          |
          v
Initial governance screening
          |
          v
Risk classification
          |
          v
Security / Privacy / Legal assessment
          |
          v
Architecture and technical review
          |
          v
Risk treatment and remediation
          |
          v
Approval by authorized stakeholders
          |
          v
Production deployment
          |
          v
Continuous monitoring
```

The procedure should identify required inputs, responsible functions, approval authorities, evidence, and expected outputs.

#### 1.7 AI Governance Charter

An AI Governance Charter defines the mandate, authority, responsibilities, membership, and operating rules of an AI governance committee or governance body.

It commonly establishes:

- Governance objectives.
- Scope of authority.
- Committee membership.
- Roles and responsibilities.
- Meeting frequency.
- Quorum and voting arrangements.
- Decision-making authority.
- Escalation requirements.
- Reporting obligations.
- Record-keeping requirements.

A charter prevents the governance committee from becoming an informal discussion group without enforceable decision rights.

#### 1.8 AI Governance Maturity

AI Governance Maturity describes the extent to which an organization has established, standardized, measured, and continuously improved its AI governance capabilities.

An illustrative maturity model:

| Level   | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                  |
| ------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Initial    | AI adoption is informal; ownership and controls are inconsistent                 |
| Level 2 | Developing | Basic policies, inventory, and assessments are emerging                          |
| Level 3 | Defined    | Governance processes and responsibilities are formally established               |
| Level 4 | Managed    | Governance performance and control effectiveness are measured                    |
| Level 5 | Optimized  | Governance is continuously improved using evidence, metrics, and lessons learned |

This is an internal maturity model, not an official maturity scale prescribed by ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF.

### 2. AI Governance Principles and Organizational Commitments

AI governance principles establish the values and expectations that guide organizational decisions about AI.

They provide a basis for policies, control requirements, system assessments, and management decisions.

#### 2.1 Responsible AI

Responsible AI refers to the development, deployment, and use of AI in ways that align with organizational responsibilities, applicable laws, ethical expectations, and stakeholder interests.

Responsible AI considers:

- Fairness.
- Accountability.
- Transparency.
- Privacy.
- Security.
- Safety.
- Reliability.
- Human oversight.
- Appropriate use.

Responsible AI is an organizational commitment, not a specific technology or certification.

#### 2.2 Trustworthy AI

Trustworthy AI refers to AI systems and their use that demonstrate relevant characteristics supporting trust, such as validity, reliability, safety, security, transparency, accountability, privacy, and fairness.

Trustworthiness must be evaluated in context.

For example, an AI system used to recommend telecommunications service plans may require different assurance measures from an AI system used to support medical diagnosis.

A system should not be described as trustworthy solely because it uses a recognized model or has passed a technical test.

#### 2.3 Human-Centric AI

Human-Centric AI is an approach that places human rights, human well-being, human agency, and human interests at the center of AI development and deployment.

It emphasizes:

- Human autonomy.
- Human rights.
- Meaningful human oversight.
- Accessibility.
- User understanding.
- Appropriate intervention.
- Protection against harmful outcomes.

Example:

An AI system recommends terminating a customer's telecommunications service because of suspected fraud. A human decision-maker reviews the evidence and has the authority to challenge the recommendation before the termination takes place.

#### 2.4 Fairness

Fairness refers to the consideration of whether AI systems produce unjustified or discriminatory outcomes for individuals or groups.

Fairness must be evaluated according to the system's context, intended purpose, affected population, applicable law, and relevant ethical considerations.

Governance measures may include:

- Representative data evaluation.
- Bias testing.
- Outcome analysis.
- Disparate impact assessment.
- Documented fairness criteria.
- Corrective actions.
- Ongoing monitoring.

Fairness is not a single universal mathematical metric. Different fairness measures may conflict, and the appropriate approach depends on context.

#### 2.5 Accountability

Accountability means that individuals and organizational functions can be held responsible for decisions, actions, and outcomes associated with AI systems.

It requires:

- Assigned ownership.
- Documented decision authority.
- Traceable approvals.
- Defined escalation paths.
- Evidence of control implementation.
- Management oversight.
- Consequences for governance violations.

An organization cannot establish accountability merely by stating that an AI model made a decision.

#### 2.6 Transparency

Transparency means providing appropriate information about an AI system, its purpose, operation, limitations, and use.

Transparency may involve:

- Disclosure that AI is being used.
- Identification of the AI provider.
- Explanation of system capabilities and limitations.
- Information about data usage.
- Disclosure of relevant automated processes.
- Documentation of system behavior and decisions.

Transparency requirements depend on the audience, system context, and applicable obligations.

#### 2.7 Explainability

Explainability refers to the ability to provide meaningful explanations about AI system behavior or outputs.

Examples include:

- Identifying factors influencing a model prediction.
- Explaining why a customer was assigned a particular risk score.
- Providing the principal factors behind a recommendation.
- Describing the limitations of a model-generated result.

Explainability does not necessarily mean that every internal model operation can be fully interpreted.

#### 2.8 Interpretability

Interpretability refers to the extent to which the internal mechanisms or behavior of a model can be understood by humans.

It is related to explainability but is not identical.

For example:

- A simple decision tree may be relatively interpretable because its decision logic can be inspected.
- A complex neural network may be less directly interpretable, requiring additional explanation techniques.

#### 2.9 Privacy by Design

Privacy by Design means incorporating privacy considerations into the architecture, development, and operation of systems from the beginning rather than treating privacy as a final compliance activity.

Examples:

- Data minimization.
- Purpose limitation.
- Privacy-aware architecture.
- Access restrictions.
- Retention controls.
- Pseudonymization where appropriate.
- Privacy risk assessment.
- Data subject rights processes.

#### 2.10 Security by Design

Security by Design means integrating security requirements into the design, development, procurement, deployment, and operation of AI systems.

Examples:

- Threat modeling.
- Secure architecture.
- Least-privilege access.
- Secure API integration.
- Model artifact integrity.
- Secrets management.
- Security testing.
- Vulnerability management.
- Security monitoring.

Security by Design requires enforceable controls, not merely security statements in project documentation.

#### 2.11 Safety by Design

Safety by Design means incorporating safeguards to prevent or reduce unacceptable harm arising from AI system behavior and use.

Examples:

- Restrictions on dangerous outputs.
- Safety testing.
- Fail-safe behavior.
- Human intervention.
- Operational constraints.
- Monitoring of harmful outcomes.
- Defined shutdown procedures.

Safety and cybersecurity are related but distinct. A system can be technically secure and still produce unsafe recommendations.

#### 2.12 Human Oversight

Human Oversight refers to the organizational and technical arrangements that allow appropriate human supervision, intervention, review, or control over AI systems.

Effective oversight requires more than assigning a person to observe system outputs.

It should establish:

- A competent human reviewer.
- Access to relevant information.
- Sufficient time to evaluate outputs.
- Authority to override or stop the system.
- Clear escalation procedures.
- Training and defined responsibilities.

#### 2.13 Proportionality

Proportionality means applying governance, risk management, and control measures appropriate to the nature, context, and level of risk associated with an AI system.

For example, an internal AI tool that summarizes publicly available information may require fewer controls than an AI system that influences credit eligibility or critical infrastructure operations.

Proportionality does not mean exempting low-risk systems from all governance requirements.

#### 2.14 Accountability and Human Responsibility

AI systems may generate recommendations, classifications, predictions, and automated actions, but organizations must establish responsibility for their development and use.

The existence of an AI system does not remove the need for accountable human and organizational decision-making.

A governance framework should prevent responsibility from becoming fragmented between the business owner, model provider, system integrator, and technology operator.

### 3. AI Governance Organizational Structures

An effective AI governance structure ensures that AI decisions are made by authorized stakeholders and that risk responsibilities are clearly assigned.

#### 3.1 Board-Level AI Oversight

Board-Level AI Oversight refers to the governing body's responsibility for overseeing the organization's strategic adoption and use of AI.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Reviewing the AI strategy.
- Understanding material AI-related risks.
- Overseeing AI governance arrangements.
- Reviewing significant incidents.
- Monitoring regulatory exposure.
- Challenging management decisions.
- Ensuring appropriate accountability and resources.

The board should receive information that supports meaningful oversight rather than excessive technical detail.

#### 3.2 Executive AI Steering Committee

An Executive AI Steering Committee is a senior management body that directs AI initiatives and ensures alignment with organizational strategy, business priorities, and risk appetite.

Potential members include:

- Chief Executive Officer.
- Chief Information Officer.
- Chief Technology Officer.
- Chief Information Security Officer.
- Chief Risk Officer.
- Chief Data Officer.
- Chief Legal Officer.
- Chief Financial Officer.
- Business unit executives.

Responsibilities may include:

- Approving strategic AI investments.
- Prioritizing AI initiatives.
- Resolving cross-functional conflicts.
- Reviewing material risks.
- Approving significant AI deployments.
- Allocating resources.
- Escalating matters to the board.

#### 3.3 AI Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee is a cross-functional governance body responsible for overseeing the organization's AI governance framework and associated operational activities.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Reviewing AI use cases.
- Reviewing risk classification.
- Monitoring governance compliance.
- Reviewing security and privacy assessments.
- Challenging risk treatment plans.
- Reviewing exceptions.
- Monitoring AI system inventories.
- Escalating material risks.

The committee's authority must be clearly defined. It should not assume responsibilities legally or operationally assigned to other functions.

#### 3.4 AI Governance Office

An AI Governance Office is a central organizational function that coordinates and supports AI governance activities.

It may be responsible for:

- Maintaining the AI governance framework.
- Managing AI policies and standards.
- Coordinating AI risk assessments.
- Maintaining the AI inventory.
- Tracking governance actions.
- Preparing management reports.
- Coordinating regulatory assessments.
- Supporting AI governance committees.
- Maintaining governance documentation.

The office can coordinate governance but should not automatically become the owner of every AI-related business risk.

#### 3.5 AI Center of Excellence (AI CoE)

An AI Center of Excellence (AI CoE) is a centralized or federated organizational capability that develops AI expertise, reusable practices, standards, and implementation guidance.

Typical activities include:

- AI architecture guidance.
- Reusable AI design patterns.
- Model development standards.
- Approved AI platforms.
- Engineering best practices.
- AI experimentation support.
- Technical training.
- Model lifecycle guidance.

An AI CoE may support governance, but technical enablement and independent risk oversight should remain appropriately separated.

#### 3.6 AI Governance vs. AI Center of Excellence

| AI Governance Office                             | AI Center of Excellence                            |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on governance and oversight coordination | Focuses on technical and organizational enablement |
| Maintains policies and governance processes      | Develops reusable AI practices and patterns        |
| Coordinates risk and compliance activities       | Supports AI engineering and implementation         |
| Tracks governance requirements and exceptions    | Promotes consistency and technical quality         |
| Supports management accountability               | Supports AI adoption and capability development    |

Some organizations combine these functions, but they should preserve clear accountability and independent challenge.

Illustrative enterprise structure:

Internal audit should retain its independent assurance role and should not be embedded as an operational owner of AI governance controls.

#### 3.8 Centralized AI Governance

Centralized AI Governance is an operating approach in which governance authority, policies, standards, and oversight are managed primarily through a central organizational function.

Advantages:

- Consistent policies.
- Centralized risk visibility.
- Standardized assessments.
- Easier enterprise reporting.
- Consistent regulatory interpretation.

Potential limitations:

- Slower approval processes.
- Limited business-specific flexibility.
- Governance bottlenecks.
- Reduced local ownership.

#### 3.9 Federated AI Governance

Federated AI Governance distributes certain governance responsibilities across business units while maintaining enterprise-wide policies, standards, and oversight.

Advantages:

- Business-specific expertise.
- Faster local decisions.
- Clearer alignment with operational needs.
- Greater business ownership.

Potential limitations:

- Inconsistent implementation.
- Fragmented reporting.
- Duplicated governance resources.
- Uneven control maturity.

#### 3.10 Hybrid AI Governance

Hybrid AI Governance combines centralized governance authority with distributed business and technical execution.

For many large enterprises, this model provides a practical balance.

Example:

- Central AI Governance Office defines policies and risk methodology.
- Business units register and own AI use cases.
- Cybersecurity performs security oversight.
- Privacy evaluates applicable privacy risks.
- AI engineering implements technical controls.
- Internal Audit independently assesses governance effectiveness.

### 4. AI Governance Lifecycle and Decision Gates

AI governance must operate throughout the AI lifecycle. It should not be limited to procurement approval or pre-production risk assessment.

#### 4.1 AI Lifecycle Governance

AI Lifecycle Governance refers to the application of governance requirements throughout the stages of an AI system's existence.

Typical stages include:

1. AI strategy and use-case identification.
2. Business justification.
3. AI system registration.
4. Initial risk screening.
5. Design and architecture.
6. Data acquisition and preparation.
7. Model development or procurement.
8. Testing and validation.
9. Approval and deployment.
10. Production operation.
11. Monitoring and reassessment.
12. Modification or retraining.
13. Decommissioning and retirement.

Governance requirements should be proportionate to the system's intended purpose, risk, and operating environment.

#### 4.2 AI Use-Case Identification

AI Use-Case Identification is the process of identifying a business activity or problem that an AI system may support or perform.

Examples:

- Customer-service automation.
- Fraud detection.
- Network anomaly detection.
- Predictive maintenance.
- Customer segmentation.
- Document processing.
- Cybersecurity threat analysis.
- Employee productivity assistance.

The organization should establish the intended business outcome before selecting a model or technology.

#### 4.3 AI Business Justification

AI Business Justification evaluates whether a proposed AI initiative provides sufficient business value to justify its costs, risks, and operational implications.

It may consider:

- Business objectives.
- Expected benefits.
- Cost and resource requirements.
- Technical feasibility.
- Data availability.
- Legal and regulatory constraints.
- Operational dependencies.
- Risk exposure.
- Alternative solutions.

An AI solution should not be adopted simply because the technology is available.

#### 4.4 AI System Registration

AI System Registration is the process of formally recording an AI system in the organization's AI inventory or governance repository.

Typical information includes:

| Information         | Description                                         |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| AI System ID        | Unique system identifier                            |
| System Name         | Official AI system name                             |
| Business Owner      | Accountable business function                       |
| Technical Owner     | Responsible technical function                      |
| Intended Purpose    | Approved business use                               |
| AI Type             | Predictive, generative, classification, etc.        |
| Model Information   | Model name, version, and provider                   |
| Data Classification | Sensitivity of processed information                |
| Deployment Model    | SaaS, cloud, on-premises, hybrid                    |
| External Providers  | Third-party dependencies                            |
| Risk Classification | Internal risk tier and applicable legal category    |
| Lifecycle Status    | Proposed, development, testing, production, retired |
| Approval Status     | Governance decision and date                        |

A complete AI system inventory is essential for enterprise visibility and risk oversight.

#### 4.5 AI Governance Screening

AI Governance Screening is an initial evaluation used to determine whether a proposed AI use case requires additional governance review.

It may examine:

- Business purpose.
- Data sensitivity.
- Personal data processing.
- External AI provider involvement.
- Customer-facing functionality.
- Degree of autonomy.
- Potential impact on individuals.
- Critical business dependencies.
- Regulatory considerations.

Screening helps determine the depth and type of subsequent assessments.

#### 4.6 AI Risk Classification Gate

An AI Risk Classification Gate is a formal decision point at which an AI use case is assigned an internal risk tier and, where applicable, assessed against legal classification requirements.

The outcome determines:

- Required risk assessments.
- Required technical testing.
- Approval authority.
- Human oversight requirements.
- Monitoring intensity.
- Documentation expectations.

Internal classification must remain distinct from legally defined classifications.

#### 4.7 AI Architecture Approval

AI Architecture Approval is the formal review of the proposed AI solution architecture to determine whether it meets organizational architecture, security, privacy, integration, and operational requirements.

Typical assessment areas:

- Model hosting and deployment.
- Data flows and trust boundaries.
- API integration.
- Identity and access management.
- Network security.
- Data retention.
- Encryption.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Third-party connectivity.
- Human approval mechanisms.
- Resilience and recovery.

#### 4.8 AI Production Approval

AI Production Approval is the formal authorization to deploy an AI system into a production environment.

Before approval, the organization should verify that:

- Governance assessments are completed.
- Critical risks are treated or formally escalated.
- Security testing is completed.
- Privacy requirements are addressed.
- Required documentation is available.
- Model and application validation are completed.
- Human oversight arrangements are established where required.
- Monitoring is operational.
- Support and incident response arrangements exist.
- Authorized stakeholders have approved deployment.

A completed assessment does not automatically mean a system is ready for production.

#### 4.9 AI Change Management

AI Change Management governs modifications that may affect an AI system's behavior, performance, security, compliance, or intended purpose.

Examples:

- Model version changes.
- Prompt modifications.
- Dataset changes.
- Fine-tuning.
- RAG knowledge-base updates.
- New API integrations.
- Changes to system permissions.
- Changes to business workflows.
- New deployment regions.
- Changes in intended use.

Material changes should trigger reassessment according to defined governance criteria.

#### 4.10 AI Decommissioning

AI Decommissioning is the controlled process of retiring an AI system from operational use.

It should address:

- Service termination.
- Access revocation.
- Credential removal.
- Data retention and deletion.
- Model artifact disposition.
- Contractual obligations.
- Dependency removal.
- Audit record retention.
- Business continuity.
- Knowledge transfer.

Retiring an AI system does not automatically remove all legal, contractual, or record-retention obligations associated with its previous operation.

### 5. AI Governance Documentation and Evidence

AI governance must be demonstrable. Organizations need documented evidence showing that governance requirements have been implemented and are operating effectively.

#### 5.1 AI Governance Documentation

AI Governance Documentation refers to the records, policies, standards, procedures, assessments, approvals, and reports that demonstrate how an organization governs its AI activities.

Typical documentation includes:

- AI strategy.
- AI governance framework.
- AI governance policy.
- AI acceptable-use policy.
- AI inventory.
- AI risk classification methodology.
- AI risk assessments.
- AI impact assessments.
- AI security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- Model documentation.
- Architecture diagrams.
- Supplier assessments.
- Production approval records.
- Monitoring reports.
- Incident records.
- Risk acceptance records.
- Internal audit reports.

#### 5.2 AI Governance Evidence

AI Governance Evidence is information demonstrating that a governance requirement, control, decision, or activity has been performed.

Examples:

| Governance Requirement                  | Example Evidence                              |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| AI systems must be registered           | Approved AI inventory                         |
| AI risks must be assessed               | Completed risk assessment                     |
| AI providers must be evaluated          | Third-party due diligence report              |
| Security controls must be implemented   | Access control configuration and test results |
| Production deployment requires approval | Signed production approval record             |
| AI performance must be monitored        | Monitoring dashboard and review records       |
| Risk acceptance requires authorization  | Approved risk acceptance record               |
| AI incidents must be managed            | Incident ticket and investigation report      |

A policy statement alone does not prove that a control has been implemented or operates effectively.

#### 5.3 Governance Traceability

Governance Traceability is the ability to connect governance requirements to implementation activities, control ownership, evidence, and management decisions.

A useful traceability chain is:

```
BUSINESS OBJECTIVE
       |
       v
AI GOVERNANCE REQUIREMENT
       |
       v
POLICY / STANDARD
       |
       v
RISK SCENARIO
       |
       v
CONTROL REQUIREMENT
       |
       v
CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
       |
       v
EVIDENCE
       |
       v
CONTROL TESTING
       |
       v
MANAGEMENT DECISION
```

This chain enables management and auditors to establish whether governance objectives are supported by operational controls and reliable evidence.

#### 5.4 AI Governance Exception

An AI Governance Exception is a formally approved deviation from an established AI governance policy, standard, or control requirement.

A governance exception should document:

- Requirement being waived.
- Business justification.
- Affected AI systems.
- Risk assessment.
- Compensating controls.
- Exception owner.
- Approval authority.
- Expiration date.
- Remediation plan.
- Review requirements.

An exception should not become a permanent alternative to implementing a required control without appropriate justification and authorization.

#### 5.5 AI Governance Audit Trail

An AI Governance Audit Trail is a record of relevant governance activities, approvals, changes, decisions, and actions associated with AI systems.

It may include:

- Use-case approvals.
- Risk classification decisions.
- Architecture review records.
- Model version changes.
- Access changes.
- Risk acceptance.
- Human intervention.
- Incident investigation.
- Governance committee decisions.
- Decommissioning approvals.

Audit trails support accountability, investigations, compliance verification, and independent assurance.

#### 5.6 AI Governance Assurance

AI Governance Assurance is the process of obtaining confidence that AI governance arrangements are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

Assurance activities may include:

- Policy compliance reviews.
- Risk assessment quality reviews.
- Control design assessments.
- Control operating-effectiveness testing.
- Technical security testing.
- Model validation.
- Supplier assurance reviews.
- Internal audits.
- External assessments.

Governance assurance should evaluate actual operating evidence, not merely the existence of documentation.

### 6. Telecom Operator AI Governance Case Study

Consider a telecommunications operator introducing an AI-powered customer-service platform.

The platform combines:

- Salesforce Service Cloud.
- Salesforce Data Cloud.
- External LLM services.
- Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG).
- Enterprise knowledge repositories.
- Customer identity verification.
- E-commerce integration.
- Billing and provisioning systems.
- AI-assisted customer-service workflows.

The system can retrieve customer information, answer questions, recommend service plans, and initiate selected business workflows.

#### 6.1 Governance Challenges

The operator must address several governance questions:

| Governance Area         | Key Question                                             |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business accountability | Who owns the customer-service AI use case?               |
| AI inventory            | Is the system registered in the enterprise AI inventory? |
| Model governance        | Who approves the external LLM and model versions?        |
| Data governance         | Which customer information can be processed?             |
| Privacy                 | What personal data is transferred to external providers? |
| Cybersecurity           | How are APIs, identities, and integrations secured?      |
| Human oversight         | Which customer actions require human approval?           |
| Third-party governance  | Has the LLM provider been assessed?                      |
| Regulatory compliance   | Which legal obligations apply to the system and its use? |
| Operational governance  | Who monitors system performance and incidents?           |
| Risk ownership          | Who accepts the remaining business risks?                |
| Assurance               | How will governance effectiveness be demonstrated?       |

#### 6.2 Proposed Governance Structure

| Function                        | Responsibility                                          |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Executive AI Steering Committee | Strategic direction and major AI decisions              |
| AI Governance Committee         | Oversight of AI use cases and governance requirements   |
| Business Owner                  | Customer-service objectives and business accountability |
| AI System Owner                 | Overall system lifecycle management                     |
| AI Engineering                  | Model integration and application implementation        |
| Enterprise Architecture         | Architecture standards and integration review           |
| Cybersecurity GRC               | Security risk assessment and governance oversight       |
| Security Operations             | Security monitoring and incident response               |
| Data Governance                 | Data quality, classification, and access governance     |
| DPO / Privacy                   | Privacy assessment and data protection oversight        |
| Legal / Compliance              | Regulatory and contractual assessment                   |
| Procurement / TPRM              | Provider due diligence and supplier governance          |
| Internal Audit                  | Independent governance assurance                        |

#### 6.3 Governance Decision Gates

| Gate                         | Required Decision                                   | Primary Evidence                                 |

### 7. Summary

AI governance establishes how an organization directs, controls, and oversees its AI activities.

It connects executive accountability, business ownership, technical implementation, risk management, legal compliance, cybersecurity, privacy, and independent assurance.

The main governance building blocks include:

- Governance frameworks, policies, standards, and procedures.
- Responsible and trustworthy AI principles.
- Board and executive oversight.
- AI governance committees and governance offices.
- Centralized, federated, and hybrid operating models.
- Lifecycle decision gates.
- AI inventory and ownership.
- Governance documentation and evidence.
- Exception management and audit trails.
- Continuous governance assurance.

For GRC professionals, the central challenge is ensuring that governance requirements translate into real decision authority, operational responsibilities, enforceable controls, and demonstrable evidence.

### Core Principle

AI governance is the organizational system that establishes who has authority over AI, who is accountable for its use, which requirements must be followed, and how compliance and control effectiveness are demonstrated. A governance framework is only effective when its policies, decision rights, controls, and accountability mechanisms operate in practice.

## Part 2 — AI Governance Policies, Standards, Controls, Compliance, and Regulatory Accountability

### 8. AI Governance Policies and Policy Management

AI governance policies translate organizational principles and strategic commitments into formal requirements that govern the development, procurement, deployment, and use of AI.

A policy must establish clear expectations, ownership, and enforcement mechanisms. Without these elements, AI governance risks becoming a collection of voluntary guidelines with limited operational impact.

#### 8.1 AI Acceptable Use Policy (AI AUP)

An AI Acceptable Use Policy (AI AUP) defines how employees, contractors, and other authorized users may use AI systems within an organization.

It establishes permitted, restricted, and prohibited activities.

Typical requirements include:

- Use only approved AI applications for organizational activities.
- Do not enter confidential information into unauthorized public AI services.
- Do not upload personal or sensitive data without authorization.
- Validate AI-generated information before using it for business decisions.
- Do not use AI outputs as a substitute for required professional judgment.
- Disclose AI use where organizational or legal requirements apply.
- Report suspected AI security incidents.
- Follow intellectual property and licensing requirements.
- Do not bypass approved security controls.
- Obtain authorization before integrating AI with corporate systems.

Example:

A telecommunications operator permits employees to use an approved enterprise LLM for drafting internal reports but prohibits uploading customer records, network credentials, confidential architecture diagrams, or unredacted incident reports into unauthorized public AI services.

The AUP should distinguish between approved enterprise AI environments and external consumer AI services.

#### 8.2 AI Development Policy

An AI Development Policy defines mandatory governance, security, data, and engineering requirements for internally developed AI systems.

It may address:

- Secure AI development lifecycle.
- Approved development environments.
- Dataset acquisition and validation.
- Model selection.
- Training and fine-tuning.
- Model documentation.
- Security testing.
- Model validation.
- Deployment approval.
- Change management.
- Monitoring.
- Retirement.

The policy should cover AI applications and their supporting components, not only model development.

#### 8.3 AI Procurement Policy

An AI Procurement Policy establishes requirements for acquiring AI products, platforms, models, and services from external suppliers.

It should address:

- Business justification.
- Approved supplier selection.
- Third-party AI risk assessment.
- Data processing and privacy.
- Intellectual property.
- Model transparency and documentation.
- Security requirements.
- Service availability.
- Data residency.
- Contractual obligations.
- Subcontractors and fourth parties.
- Exit and transition arrangements.

GRC example:

Before purchasing an external LLM service, procurement must coordinate with cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and business stakeholders to evaluate whether the provider's security controls, data handling, contractual terms, and service commitments meet enterprise requirements.

#### 8.4 AI Data Governance Policy

An AI Data Governance Policy establishes requirements for managing data used by or generated through AI systems.

It addresses:

- Data ownership.
- Data classification.
- Data quality.
- Data provenance.
- Data lineage.
- Data access.
- Data minimization.
- Data retention.
- Data sharing.
- Data integrity.
- Data deletion.
- Training and inference data management.

The policy should cover training datasets, fine-tuning datasets, RAG knowledge repositories, prompts, user inputs, model outputs, and relevant telemetry.

#### 8.5 AI Model Governance Policy

An AI Model Governance Policy establishes requirements for the lifecycle management and oversight of AI models.

It may define:

- Model inventory.
- Model ownership.
- Model classification.
- Model development standards.
- Model validation.
- Model approval.
- Model versioning.
- Model performance monitoring.
- Model drift management.
- Model change management.
- Model retirement.

Model governance is especially important in organizations using multiple models from different providers and deployment environments.

#### 8.6 AI Third-Party Governance Policy

An AI Third-Party Governance Policy establishes requirements for managing risks associated with external AI providers, vendors, service providers, and technology dependencies.

It may require:

- Supplier due diligence.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy assessments.
- AI-specific risk assessments.
- Contractual security requirements.
- Provider documentation.
- Subprocessor disclosure.
- Incident notification.
- Performance monitoring.
- Periodic reassessment.
- Exit planning.

The organization remains responsible for managing risks arising from its use of third-party AI services, even when the provider operates the underlying model.

#### 8.7 AI Policy Exception

An AI Policy Exception is an authorized deviation from a formally established AI policy requirement.

Example:

A business unit needs to use an AI application that is not yet included in the organization's approved AI service catalogue.

The exception process should establish:

1. Business justification.
2. Affected systems and users.
3. Risk assessment.
4. Compensating controls.
5. Accountable owner.
6. Approval authority.
7. Expiration date.
8. Remediation plan.

An exception should be time-bound and reviewed periodically.

### 9. AI Governance Standards and Control Requirements

Policies establish organizational expectations. Standards translate those expectations into mandatory requirements that can be implemented and tested.

#### 9.1 AI Governance Control

An AI Governance Control is a measure designed to ensure that AI activities comply with organizational governance requirements, applicable obligations, and approved risk decisions.

Examples:

- AI inventory management.
- AI use-case approval.
- Risk classification.
- Model validation.
- Human oversight.
- Third-party assessment.
- Governance reporting.
- Exception management.

A governance control may be administrative, technical, operational, or managerial.

#### 9.2 Governance Control Objective

A Governance Control Objective describes the outcome a control is intended to achieve.

Example:

| Control Objective         | Expected Outcome                                     |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| AI inventory completeness | All relevant AI systems are identified and recorded  |
| AI risk accountability    | Every material AI risk has an accountable owner      |
| AI use authorization      | AI systems are approved before authorized use        |
| Model governance          | Models are validated and monitored according to risk |
| Third-party oversight     | External AI dependencies are appropriately assessed  |
| Regulatory compliance     | Applicable obligations are identified and addressed  |

A control objective defines the intended result; individual controls describe how the organization achieves it.

#### 9.3 Preventive Governance Control

A Preventive Governance Control aims to prevent unauthorized or non-compliant AI activities.

Examples:

- Mandatory AI registration before procurement.
- Approval before production deployment.
- Restrictions on unauthorized AI tools.
- Mandatory privacy assessments for qualifying processing.
- Defined approval authority for high-impact use cases.
- Mandatory security requirements for AI integrations.

#### 9.4 Detective Governance Control

A Detective Governance Control identifies governance deviations, control failures, or unauthorized AI activities.

Examples:

- Periodic AI inventory reconciliation.
- Monitoring of unauthorized AI applications.
- Review of AI system access logs.
- Compliance reviews.
- Periodic supplier reassessments.
- Review of overdue risk treatment actions.

#### 9.5 Corrective Governance Control

A Corrective Governance Control addresses identified governance failures and restores compliance.

Examples:

- Suspending an unauthorized AI application.
- Revoking inappropriate access.
- Correcting incomplete AI inventory records.
- Remediating missing documentation.
- Reassessing a system following an unapproved model change.
- Revising a deficient governance procedure.

#### 9.6 Administrative Control

An Administrative Control is a governance measure implemented through policies, procedures, standards, training, responsibilities, or management processes.

Examples:

- AI acceptable-use policy.
- AI risk assessment procedure.
- AI governance committee.
- Mandatory AI awareness training.
- Supplier assessment requirements.

#### 9.7 Technical Control

A Technical Control is implemented through technology or system configuration to enforce security or governance requirements.

Examples:

- Identity and access management.
- API authorization.
- DLP.
- Data encryption.
- Model endpoint restrictions.
- Prompt and output filtering.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Network segmentation.

#### 9.8 Operational Control

An Operational Control is performed through recurring business or technology activities.

Examples:

- Daily monitoring.
- Periodic access reviews.
- Model performance reviews.
- Supplier reassessments.
- Security incident handling.
- Dataset quality checks.
- AI change approvals.

#### 9.9 Management Control

A Management Control provides organizational direction, oversight, approval, and accountability.

Examples:

- Executive AI strategy approval.
- AI governance committee oversight.
- Risk acceptance authorization.
- Management review of AI risk reports.
- Resource allocation.
- Review of significant AI incidents.

### 10. AI Governance Compliance and Regulatory Management

AI governance compliance ensures that an organization identifies, understands, implements, and monitors obligations arising from applicable laws, regulations, contracts, and internal requirements.

It requires more than checking whether a policy exists.

#### 10.1 AI Regulatory Compliance

AI Regulatory Compliance refers to the organization's adherence to legal and regulatory requirements applicable to its AI systems, activities, and organizational role.

Potential regulatory considerations include:

- EU AI Act.
- General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
- Sector-specific regulations.
- Consumer protection requirements.
- Employment and labor requirements.
- Intellectual property legislation.
- Cybersecurity obligations.
- Contractual requirements.

Not every AI system is subject to every AI-specific obligation. Applicability must be assessed against the actual use case and legal context.

#### 10.2 Regulatory Applicability Assessment

A Regulatory Applicability Assessment determines which legal and regulatory requirements apply to an organization, AI system, provider, deployer, or particular processing activity.

It should consider:

- Organizational jurisdiction.
- System deployment location.
- Intended purpose.
- Affected individuals.
- Data categories.
- AI system classification.
- Organizational role.
- Sector.
- Provider and supplier relationships.

Example:

A Spanish telecommunications operator deploys an external LLM to support customer-service operations.

The applicability assessment should consider the EU AI Act, GDPR, relevant consumer and telecommunications requirements, contractual obligations, and other applicable legislation.

#### 10.3 Regulatory Obligations Register

A Regulatory Obligations Register is a controlled record of legal and regulatory requirements relevant to the organization.

A typical register includes:

| Field               | Purpose                                                        |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Obligation ID       | Unique identifier                                              |
| Regulation          | Applicable law or regulation                                   |
| Article / Provision | Specific legal requirement                                     |
| Applicability       | Explanation of why it applies                                  |
| Organizational Role | Relevant provider, deployer, or other role                     |
| Responsible Owner   | Accountable function                                           |
| Control Mapping     | Controls addressing the obligation                             |
| Evidence            | Required compliance evidence                                   |
| Review Frequency    | Reassessment schedule                                          |
| Compliance Status   | Compliant, partially compliant, non-compliant, or not assessed |
| Remediation         | Required corrective action                                     |

#### 10.4 Legal Applicability vs. Internal Governance Requirement

These concepts must not be confused.

| Legal Applicability                                | Internal Governance Requirement                                   |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Arises from applicable legislation or regulation   | Arises from organizational policy or risk management              |
| May create legally enforceable obligations         | Creates internal organizational requirements                      |
| Determined by legal scope and conditions           | Determined by organizational governance decisions                 |
| Example: Applicable EU AI Act provider obligations | Example: Internal approval required for all production AI systems |

An organization can impose stricter internal governance requirements than the minimum required by law.

#### 10.5 Regulatory Gap Assessment

A Regulatory Gap Assessment evaluates the difference between applicable legal requirements and the organization's current compliance arrangements.

Example:

| Requirement                     | Current State                  | Gap                               | Remediation                        |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Maintain required documentation | Documentation incomplete       | Missing evidence                  | Establish documentation repository |
| Establish required oversight    | Responsibilities unclear       | Inadequate oversight arrangements | Define oversight procedures        |
| Monitor applicable obligations  | No formal review               | Monitoring gap                    | Establish compliance monitoring    |
| Manage relevant incidents       | AI-specific escalation unclear | Incident governance gap           | Update incident procedures         |

The gap assessment should distinguish between missing documentation, missing control design, incomplete implementation, and ineffective operation.

#### 10.6 Regulatory Control Mapping

Regulatory Control Mapping connects legal requirements to internal policies, standards, controls, owners, and evidence.

Example:

```

       |
       v

       |
       v
INTERNAL POLICY
       |
       v
CONTROL REQUIREMENT
       |
       v
CONTROL OWNER
       |
       v

       |
       v

```

This structure supports regulatory traceability and audit readiness.

#### 10.7 Statement of Applicability (SoA)

A Statement of Applicability (SoA) is a formal document used within ISO/IEC 27001 to identify the information security controls determined to be necessary, their implementation status, and justifications for inclusion or exclusion.

For an organization operating AI systems within its ISMS, the SoA can document the applicability of relevant information security controls to AI-related assets and processes.

An SoA is not automatically a complete AI governance control register. AI-specific requirements may need additional mapping.

#### 10.8 AI Regulatory Compliance Monitoring

AI Regulatory Compliance Monitoring is the ongoing activity of evaluating whether applicable AI-related obligations continue to be met.

It may involve:

- Regulatory change monitoring.
- Periodic applicability reviews.
- Control testing.
- Documentation reviews.
- Supplier compliance monitoring.
- Incident reporting.
- Management reporting.
- Remediation tracking.

Changes in legal requirements, intended purpose, system capabilities, or organizational role may trigger reassessment.

### 11. AI Governance Frameworks, Standards, and Regulatory References

An AI governance program should use recognized frameworks and standards to establish a coherent governance structure.

However, the organization must understand what each reference provides and avoid treating every publication as a legal requirement.

#### 11.1 NIST AI RMF — Governance Function

The Govern function of the NIST AI RMF addresses organizational policies, processes, responsibilities, and oversight required to manage AI risks.

Its governance activities support:

- AI risk management policies.
- Accountability structures.
- Organizational culture.
- Workforce competency.
- Third-party risk management.
- Executive oversight.
- Risk management integration.

The Govern function is cross-cutting and supports the Map, Measure, and Manage functions.

#### 11.2 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 establishes requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System.

Its management-system approach covers areas such as:

- Organizational context.
- Leadership.
- Planning.
- Support.
- Operation.
- Performance evaluation.
- Improvement.

It enables organizations to establish formal AI governance arrangements and continually improve them.

#### 11.3 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Policy

ISO/IEC 42001 requires an AI policy within its AIMS requirements.

The policy provides top-management direction and commitments relevant to the organization's AI management system.

The organization must ensure that the policy is appropriate to its purpose and context and is communicated and maintained as required by the standard.

#### 11.4 ISO/IEC 42001 — AI Risk and Impact Assessment

ISO/IEC 42001 includes requirements relating to AI risk assessment, AI risk treatment, and AI system impact assessment.

These processes help the organization understand and manage risks and impacts associated with AI systems within the AIMS scope.

The organization should establish criteria, responsibilities, and documented processes consistent with the standard.

#### 11.5 ISO/IEC 42001 — Internal Audit and Management Review

ISO/IEC 42001 includes management-system requirements for:

- Monitoring and measurement.
- Internal audit.
- Management review.
- Nonconformity and corrective action.
- Continual improvement.

These requirements help ensure that AI governance is evaluated and improved over time.

#### 11.6 ISO/IEC 38507 — Governance of AI Use

ISO/IEC 38507 provides guidance for governing bodies on the implications of AI use within organizations.

It is particularly relevant to:

- Strategic alignment.
- Governance responsibilities.
- Oversight.
- Accountability.
- Risk considerations.
- Organizational decision-making.

It supports governing-body understanding of AI but should not be treated as a substitute for operational AI risk controls.

#### 11.7 ISO/IEC 23894 — AI Risk Governance

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on integrating AI risk management into organizational risk management.

It supports the governance of risk identification, analysis, evaluation, treatment, and monitoring.

#### 11.8 ISO/IEC 27001 — AI Information Security Governance

ISO/IEC 27001 can support AI security governance through its ISMS requirements and applicable information security controls.

Organizations should ensure that AI-related systems, services, data, and suppliers are considered within the relevant ISMS scope and risk assessment.

#### 11.9 EU AI Act — Governance and Accountability

The EU AI Act establishes legally defined obligations for relevant AI system and GPAI model providers, deployers, and other actors.

Governance activities may include:

- Legal classification.
- Role identification.
- Risk management.
- Technical documentation.
- Record-keeping.
- Human oversight.
- Transparency.
- Monitoring.
- Incident handling.
- Provider and deployer obligations.

The exact obligations depend on the system, its intended purpose, classification, and the organization's legal role.

### 12. AI Governance Performance Measurement and Reporting

AI governance requires measurable indicators to determine whether governance arrangements are implemented and functioning effectively.

Management reporting should provide evidence of governance coverage, compliance, risk exposure, remediation progress, and control performance.

#### 12.1 AI Governance Key Performance Indicator (KPI)

An AI Governance KPI measures the performance of an AI governance activity against a defined objective.

Examples:

| KPI                             | Calculation                                               | Purpose                                  |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| AI Inventory Registration Rate  | Registered AI systems / Identified AI systems × 100       | Measures inventory coverage              |
| AI Assessment Completion Rate   | Completed assessments / Required assessments × 100        | Measures assessment execution            |
| AI Training Completion Rate     | Employees trained / Employees in scope × 100              | Measures workforce training              |
| AI Policy Acknowledgment Rate   | Employees acknowledging policy / Employees in scope × 100 | Measures policy communication            |
| AI Remediation Completion Rate  | Completed actions / Actions due × 100                     | Measures remediation progress            |
| AI Supplier Assessment Coverage | Assessed suppliers / AI suppliers in scope × 100          | Measures third-party assessment coverage |

These measures should have defined scope and reporting periods.

#### 12.2 AI Governance Key Risk Indicator (KRI)

An AI Governance KRI signals potential weaknesses or increasing exposure in the governance environment.

Examples:

| KRI                                                | Risk Signal                       |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Number of unregistered AI systems                  | Potential Shadow AI               |
| Number of AI systems without assigned owners       | Accountability weakness           |
| Number of overdue risk assessments                 | Governance coverage deterioration |
| Number of unauthorized AI deployments              | Approval process failure          |
| Number of expired policy exceptions                | Unmanaged governance deviations   |
| Number of AI suppliers without current assessments | Third-party exposure              |
| Number of production AI systems without monitoring | Oversight weakness                |
| Number of overdue critical remediation actions     | Accumulating risk exposure        |

#### 12.3 AI Governance Control Effectiveness

AI Governance Control Effectiveness evaluates whether a governance control is properly designed, implemented, and operating as intended.

Three distinct dimensions should be assessed:

1. Design effectiveness: Does the control address the relevant governance risk?
2. Implementation effectiveness: Has the control been implemented?
3. Operating effectiveness: Does the control operate consistently and produce the expected result?

Example:

An organization has a policy requiring every AI system to be registered before deployment.

- Design: The policy establishes a clear registration requirement.
- Implementation: A registration workflow and inventory repository exist.
- Operation: Testing confirms that production AI systems are registered and approved before deployment.

The existence of the policy and repository alone is insufficient evidence of operating effectiveness.

#### 12.4 AI Governance Dashboard

A governance dashboard provides management with a consolidated view of AI governance performance and exposure.

Recommended components:

| Dashboard Area         | Example Measures                                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI Inventory           | Total systems, registered systems, unregistered systems |
| Risk Classification    | Systems by internal risk tier                           |
| Governance Compliance  | Policy compliance and assessment coverage               |
| Risk Management        | Open risks by severity                                  |
| Control Assurance      | Control testing results                                 |
| Third-Party Governance | Supplier assessment coverage                            |
| Regulatory Compliance  | Open regulatory gaps                                    |
| Exceptions             | Open, expired, and overdue exceptions                   |
| Remediation            | Open and overdue corrective actions                     |
| Incidents              | AI-related governance and security incidents            |

A dashboard should not present high completion rates as proof of effective governance if control failures or material residual risks remain unresolved.

#### 12.5 AI Governance Management Review

An AI Governance Management Review is a formal management activity for evaluating the suitability, adequacy, and effectiveness of the AI governance arrangements.

Typical inputs include:

- Governance KPI and KRI results.
- AI inventory changes.
- Risk assessment outcomes.
- Control testing results.
- Audit findings.
- Regulatory developments.
- Supplier performance.
- AI incidents.
- Corrective action status.
- Resource requirements.
- Opportunities for improvement.

Management decisions may include:

- Changes to governance policies.
- Additional control requirements.
- Resource allocation.
- Risk treatment priorities.
- Governance operating model changes.
- Supplier restrictions.
- AI system suspension or retirement.

| 7. Implementation   | Establish inventory, workflows, and evidence repositories | Operational governance processes |
| 8. Assurance        | Test governance controls and evaluate effectiveness       | Assurance reports                |

#### 13.2 Minimum Viable AI Governance

Organizations beginning their AI governance journey should prioritize a minimum set of foundational capabilities:

1. Approved AI governance policy.
2. Central AI system inventory.
3. Defined business and technical ownership.
4. AI acceptable-use policy.
5. AI risk classification methodology.
6. AI risk assessment process.
7. Security and privacy assessment requirements.
8. Third-party AI assessment process.
9. Production approval requirements.
10. Change management and incident escalation.
11. Residual risk acceptance process.
12. Governance reporting and periodic review.

This baseline should be expanded according to the organization's AI portfolio, risk exposure, legal obligations, and maturity.

### 14. Summary

AI governance policies, standards, controls, compliance processes, and performance measurements translate organizational governance commitments into operational requirements.

A mature AI governance program should establish:

- Formal AI policies and acceptable-use requirements.
- Mandatory governance standards and control objectives.
- Clear preventive, detective, and corrective controls.
- Regulatory applicability and obligations management.
- Traceability between legal requirements and internal controls.
- Management-system alignment with recognized standards.
- Governance KPIs, KRIs, and control effectiveness measurements.
- Structured implementation and continual improvement.

For GRC professionals, the critical responsibility is to ensure that governance commitments are translated into measurable requirements, assigned ownership, implemented controls, and verifiable evidence.

### Core Principle

AI governance must be enforceable, measurable, and auditable. Policies define organizational expectations, standards establish mandatory requirements, controls implement those requirements, compliance processes demonstrate adherence, and assurance determines whether the governance system actually works.
