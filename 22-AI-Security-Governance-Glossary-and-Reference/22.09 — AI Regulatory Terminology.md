# 22.09 — AI Regulatory Terminology

## Part 1 — AI Regulatory Foundations, Legal Concepts, Regulatory Classification, and Accountability

### 1. Introduction to AI Regulatory Terminology

Artificial Intelligence regulation establishes legal requirements governing how AI systems are developed, placed on the market, put into service, deployed, operated, monitored, and, where applicable, withdrawn or discontinued.

For organizations implementing AI governance, understanding regulatory terminology is essential because technical risk, organizational risk, and legal obligations are not interchangeable concepts.

An AI system may present significant cybersecurity or operational risk without necessarily falling into a legally defined high-risk category. Conversely, an AI system may trigger specific legal obligations even when its internal organizational risk score is relatively low.

AI regulatory governance must therefore distinguish among:

1. Legal applicability — Whether a law applies to an organization, activity, system, or model.
2. Regulatory classification — How a system, model, or activity is categorized under an applicable law.
3. Regulated entity or operator — Which organization has specific legal responsibilities.
4. Regulatory obligation — What the applicable law requires.
5. Compliance implementation — How the organization satisfies the requirement.
6. Regulatory evidence — How compliance can be demonstrated.
7. Regulatory oversight — Which authority supervises or enforces the requirements.
8. Regulatory enforcement — What actions may follow non-compliance.

For an AI Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) professional, the objective is not simply to memorize regulatory definitions. It is to translate legal requirements into accountable, testable, and auditable enterprise controls.

#### 1.1 AI Regulatory Governance

AI Regulatory Governance is the organizational process of identifying, interpreting, assigning, implementing, monitoring, and demonstrating compliance with legal requirements applicable to AI systems, models, and related activities.

It connects legal interpretation with enterprise governance and operational execution.

```
                 AI REGULATORY GOVERNANCE
                           |
             +-------------+-------------+
             |                           |
      Legal Applicability          Regulatory Scope
             |                           |

```

The regulatory governance process must remain current as legislation, regulatory guidance, technical standards, organizational activities, and AI systems change.

#### 1.2 AI Regulation versus AI Governance

| Dimension           | AI regulation                                                         | AI governance                                                                |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary purpose     | Establish legally enforceable requirements                            | Direct and control organizational AI activities                              |
| Source of authority | Applicable legislation and legal instruments                          | Board, executive management, internal policies, and adopted frameworks       |
| Scope               | Defined by legal applicability                                        | Defined by organizational governance requirements                            |
| Accountability      | Legal responsibilities of regulated entities                          | Internal decision rights and control ownership                               |
| Enforcement         | Competent authorities, courts, or other legally authorized mechanisms | Management oversight, internal controls, audit, and disciplinary processes   |
| Evidence            | Records demonstrating fulfillment of legal requirements               | Governance records, risk assessments, approvals, and control testing         |
| Consequences        | Potential regulatory sanctions, liability, or legal remedies          | Operational, financial, strategic, compliance, and reputational consequences |

Critical distinction: An organization may have a mature AI governance framework while remaining non-compliant with a specific law. Governance maturity does not establish legal compliance.

### 2. Fundamental AI Regulatory Concepts

#### 2.1 Regulation

A Regulation is a legal instrument that establishes rules, rights, obligations, or restrictions within a defined jurisdiction.

The legal meaning and effect of a regulation depend on the jurisdiction and legal system.

Within the European Union, an EU regulation is generally directly applicable in Member States under Article 288 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, although implementation and enforcement arrangements may require national measures.

Example: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, commonly known as the EU Artificial Intelligence Act.

#### 2.2 Directive

A Directive, within EU law, is a legal instrument binding Member States as to the result to be achieved while generally leaving national authorities the choice of form and methods of implementation.

Directives normally require transposition into national law.

Example: Directive (EU) 2022/2555, commonly known as NIS2.

#### 2.3 Legislation

Legislation refers to laws enacted or adopted through an authorized legislative process.

AI-related legislation may address:

- AI-specific obligations.
- Data protection.
- Cybersecurity.
- Consumer protection.
- Product safety.
- Fundamental rights.
- Intellectual property.
- Employment and discrimination.
- Financial services.
- Digital services.

An AI system can be subject to several legal regimes simultaneously.

#### 2.4 Regulatory Requirement

A Regulatory Requirement is a legally applicable obligation, restriction, condition, or duty established by a regulatory instrument.

Examples include:

- Maintaining required technical documentation.
- Providing specified information to users or deployers.
- Implementing required risk management measures.
- Maintaining logs where legally required.
- Reporting specified incidents.
- Establishing appropriate human oversight.
- Meeting applicable cybersecurity requirements.

A requirement must be linked to a specific legal provision and applicability determination.

#### 2.5 Regulatory Obligation

A Regulatory Obligation is a duty imposed on an organization or person by applicable law.

A regulatory obligation should be documented with:

| Attribute                  | Description                                                 |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Legal source               | Regulation, directive, statute, or other binding instrument |
| Legal provision            | Article, section, or other authoritative reference          |
| Obligation description     | What must be done or avoided                                |
| Applicability              | Conditions under which the obligation applies               |
| Responsible legal entity   | Organization or actor subject to the obligation             |
| Internal accountable owner | Person or function accountable for implementation           |
| Implementation control     | Control used to satisfy the obligation                      |
| Compliance evidence        | Records supporting fulfillment                              |
| Effective date             | Date from which the requirement applies                     |
| Compliance status          | Current assessment against the requirement                  |

#### 2.6 Regulatory Guidance

Regulatory Guidance consists of interpretations, recommendations, explanations, or implementation guidance issued by regulators or other authoritative bodies.

Guidance can help organizations understand regulatory expectations, but its legal status depends on the issuing authority, instrument, and jurisdiction.

Guidance should not automatically be treated as equivalent to binding legislation.

#### 2.7 Regulatory Standard

A Regulatory Standard is a technical, procedural, or management requirement that has a defined relationship to a regulatory framework.

Standards may be:

- Voluntary.
- Contractually mandatory.
- Required through legislation.
- Referenced by regulation.
- Used to support conformity assessment.
- Harmonized under an applicable EU legal framework.

Important: ISO certification does not automatically establish compliance with every applicable AI regulation.

#### 2.8 Harmonized Standard

Within the EU, a Harmonised Standard is a European standard adopted on the basis of a request made by the European Commission for the application of Union harmonisation legislation.

Where the relevant legislation provides for it, conformity with harmonised standards or applicable parts of them, whose references have been published in the Official Journal of the European Union, may confer a presumption of conformity with corresponding legal requirements.

This presumption applies only within the relevant legal conditions and scope.

It does not mean that any standard labeled ISO, IEC, or EN automatically provides a presumption of conformity with the EU AI Act.

#### 2.9 Code of Practice

A Code of Practice is a structured set of practices or commitments intended to support consistent implementation of requirements or responsible conduct.

Its legal effect depends on the relevant regulatory framework.

Under the EU AI Act, codes of practice have a specific role in supporting the application of certain general-purpose AI model obligations.

A code of practice should not be confused with an internal corporate policy or a harmonized technical standard.

### 3. AI Regulatory Scope and Legal Applicability

Before assessing regulatory compliance, an organization must establish whether the relevant law applies.

This process is called Regulatory Applicability Assessment.

#### 3.1 Regulatory Scope

Regulatory Scope defines the persons, organizations, activities, systems, products, locations, or circumstances covered by a legal instrument.

Regulatory scope can depend on:

- Geographic location.
- Market placement.
- Intended use.
- Type of AI system.
- Type of AI model.
- Role of the organization.
- Sector or industry.
- Categories of affected persons.
- Nature of the data processed.
- Legal exclusions or exemptions.

#### 3.2 Territorial Scope

Territorial Scope defines the geographic connection required for a law to apply.

A regulation may apply because an organization:

- Is established in the jurisdiction.
- Offers products or services within the jurisdiction.
- Places a regulated product on the jurisdiction's market.
- Processes data connected to persons in the jurisdiction.
- Uses AI outputs within the jurisdiction.

The precise territorial rules must be determined from the applicable legislation.

#### 3.3 Extraterritorial Application

Extraterritorial Application occurs when a law applies to certain activities or entities outside the territory of the jurisdiction adopting the law.

For example, the EU AI Act can apply to certain providers and deployers established outside the EU where its specified territorial conditions are met.

Extraterritorial application should never be assumed merely because an AI service is accessible over the internet.

#### 3.4 Material Scope

Material Scope identifies the subject matter, activities, technologies, or types of processing governed by a law.

Examples include:

- AI systems.
- General-purpose AI models.
- Personal data processing.
- Cybersecurity risk management.
- Product safety.
- Automated decision-making.
- Digital services.

A technology may fall within the scope of one regulatory regime while remaining outside another.

#### 3.5 Personal Scope

Personal Scope identifies the persons or entities subject to the law.

Examples include:

- AI providers.
- AI deployers.
- Importers.
- Distributors.
- Authorized representatives.
- Data controllers.
- Data processors.
- Product manufacturers.
- Employers.
- Public authorities.

The same organization can hold different legal roles under different regulatory frameworks.

#### 3.6 Regulatory Exemption

A Regulatory Exemption is a legally defined circumstance in which specified requirements do not apply or apply differently.

Exemptions must be established from the actual legal text.

Examples of potentially relevant distinctions under particular legal regimes include:

- Certain research activities.
- Certain personal non-professional activities.
- Specific national security or defense contexts.
- Certain systems or activities outside the defined scope.

The availability and conditions of exemptions differ between laws.

An organization should document the legal basis for claiming an exemption rather than treating it as an informal risk acceptance.

#### 3.7 Regulatory Applicability Assessment Matrix

| Assessment question                               | Example response                            | Required evidence                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Which legal instrument is being assessed?         | EU AI Act                                   | Legal applicability record                                |
| Is the technology within the relevant definition? | AI system potentially within scope          | Technical architecture and intended-purpose documentation |
| Is there a territorial connection?                | AI system used or marketed in the EU        | Deployment and commercial records                         |
| What is the organization's legal role?            | Deployer                                    | Contractual and operational analysis                      |
| Does an exclusion apply?                          | No identified exclusion                     | Legal assessment                                          |
| Does a specific classification apply?             | Requires further analysis                   | Classification record                                     |
| Which obligations apply?                          | Role- and classification-dependent          | Obligations register                                      |
| Who is accountable?                               | Designated legal entity and internal owner  | RACI and ownership register                               |
| What evidence is required?                        | Documentation, logs, assessments, approvals | Compliance evidence repository                            |

Core assessment rule: Applicability must be established before assigning legal obligations.

### 4. AI Regulatory Classification Terminology

Regulatory Classification is the process of assigning a system, model, activity, or organization to a category defined by an applicable legal framework.

Classification determines which legal requirements may apply.

Regulatory classification is not the same as internal enterprise risk scoring.

#### 4.1 Risk-Based Regulation

Risk-Based Regulation is a regulatory approach that differentiates requirements according to defined risk characteristics, potential harms, or regulated activities.

The EU AI Act incorporates a risk-based structure, but its legal categories must be interpreted according to the Act's provisions rather than a generic likelihood-and-impact matrix.

#### 4.2 Prohibited AI Practices

Prohibited AI Practices are AI-related uses or activities that a law expressly prohibits under defined conditions.

Under the EU AI Act, Article 5 establishes specified prohibited AI practices.

A governance assessment should determine:

1. Whether the relevant activity falls within a defined prohibition.
2. Whether all elements of the legal provision are satisfied.
3. Whether a legally specified exception or qualification applies.
4. Whether the proposed activity must be stopped, redesigned, or prohibited internally.

A prohibited practice should not be treated as an ordinary risk that management can simply accept.

#### 4.3 High-Risk AI System

A High-Risk AI System, under the EU AI Act, is an AI system meeting the applicable criteria in Article 6 and the relevant provisions and annexes.

Classification may arise through specified regulated product contexts or designated use cases, subject to the Act's conditions and exceptions.

Potentially relevant areas include certain uses in:

- Employment and worker management.
- Education and vocational training.
- Essential private and public services.
- Critical infrastructure.
- Law enforcement.
- Migration, asylum, and border control.
- Administration of justice and democratic processes.
- Certain biometric applications.

The category depends on the precise use case and legal criteria, not merely the broad sector.

#### 4.4 Transparency-Related AI Obligations

Certain AI systems are subject to specific transparency requirements under the EU AI Act.

These requirements may concern:

- Informing natural persons that they are interacting with AI.
- Identifying certain AI-generated or manipulated content.
- Disclosure requirements in specified emotion recognition or biometric categorization contexts.
- Disclosure concerning certain deepfakes or AI-generated public-interest text, subject to the legal conditions and exceptions.

These requirements should not be described as a universal legal category called "limited-risk AI" without qualification.

"Limited risk" is commonly used in explanatory descriptions of the Act, but the binding obligations must be traced to the relevant provisions.

#### 4.5 Minimal or No-Risk AI

Minimal or No-Risk AI is a commonly used descriptive category for AI applications not subject to the Act's prohibited-practice, high-risk, or specified transparency requirements.

It is not a blanket exemption from all law.

Other legislation may still apply, including:

- GDPR.
- Consumer protection.
- Employment law.
- Intellectual property law.
- Cybersecurity legislation.
- Product safety rules.

#### 4.6 General-Purpose AI Model

A General-Purpose AI Model (GPAI Model) is defined under the EU AI Act by reference to significant generality, the ability to competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks, and the capacity to be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications, subject to the statutory definition and exclusions.

A GPAI model is not automatically a high-risk AI system.

Model-level requirements and downstream AI system requirements must be evaluated separately.

#### 4.7 GPAI Model with Systemic Risk

A General-Purpose AI Model with Systemic Risk is a GPAI model meeting the EU AI Act's applicable criteria for systemic-risk classification.

Relevant considerations include the model's capabilities and the statutory designation mechanisms.

The classification may trigger additional provider obligations.

It should not be confused with an organization's internal designation of a business-critical AI system.

#### 4.8 Regulatory Classification versus Enterprise Risk Classification

| Dimension    | Regulatory classification                   | Enterprise risk classification                                              |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source       | Applicable legal framework                  | Organizational risk methodology                                             |
| Purpose      | Determine legal obligations                 | Prioritize organizational risk treatment                                    |
| Criteria     | Statutory definitions and conditions        | Likelihood, impact, criticality, risk appetite, and other internal criteria |
| Authority    | Applicable law and competent interpretation | Organizational governance                                                   |
| Outcome      | Legal category and obligations              | Internal risk tier and treatment priority                                   |
| Modification | Requires applicable legal basis             | Can be revised under approved organizational methodology                    |
| Example      | EU AI Act high-risk classification          | Enterprise Tier 1 critical AI system                                        |

Example:

A telecommunications operator uses an AI assistant to summarize internal network incident tickets.

The operator may classify the assistant as internally high-risk because it processes sensitive infrastructure information.

That internal classification does not automatically make the assistant a high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act.

A separate legal classification assessment is required.

### 5. AI Regulatory Actors and Legal Responsibilities

Regulatory obligations must be assigned to the correct legal entity and role.

A company may act as a provider for one AI system, a deployer for another, and a data controller for personal data processing associated with both.

#### 5.1 AI Provider

Under the EU AI Act, a Provider is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that develops an AI system or GPAI model, or has it developed, and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, subject to the Act's definitions.

Provider obligations depend on the relevant type of system or model and applicable provisions.

#### 5.2 AI Deployer

A Deployer is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body using an AI system under its authority, except where the system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

Deployers may have obligations concerning use, oversight, monitoring, record-keeping, information, or other matters depending on the AI system and applicable law.

#### 5.3 Importer

An Importer, in the relevant EU AI Act context, is an entity established or located in the EU that places on the market an AI system bearing the name or trademark of a person established outside the EU.

The legal definition and obligations should be assessed for the specific supply chain.

#### 5.4 Distributor

A Distributor is an entity in the AI supply chain, other than the provider or importer, that makes an AI system available on the EU market.

Distributors have specific obligations where the Act applies.

#### 5.5 Authorized Representative

An Authorized Representative is an entity established or located in the EU that has received and accepted a written mandate from a provider to perform specified obligations on the provider's behalf.

The scope of responsibility depends on the mandate and applicable legal provisions.

#### 5.6 Product Manufacturer

A Product Manufacturer may have responsibilities under the EU AI Act where an AI system is associated with a product governed by relevant Union harmonisation legislation.

The applicable rules depend on the product, system integration, and statutory conditions.

#### 5.7 Data Controller

Under GDPR, a Data Controller is the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of processing personal data.

#### 5.8 Data Processor

Under GDPR, a Data Processor processes personal data on behalf of the controller.

The controller–processor relationship is distinct from the provider–deployer relationship under the EU AI Act.

An AI service provider may be a processor for certain personal data processing activities while holding a different role for other activities.

#### 5.9 AI Regulatory Role Comparison

| Role                      | Primary legal context           | Core distinction                                                                              |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provider                  | EU AI Act                       | Develops or has developed and places on market or puts into service under relevant conditions |
| Deployer                  | EU AI Act                       | Uses AI system under its authority                                                            |
| Importer                  | EU AI Act                       | Places qualifying non-EU provider AI system on EU market                                      |
| Distributor               | EU AI Act                       | Makes AI system available in supply chain                                                     |
| Authorized Representative | EU AI Act                       | Performs specified provider obligations under written mandate                                 |
| Product Manufacturer      | EU AI Act / product legislation | Relevant manufacturer responsibilities in regulated product context                           |
| Data Controller           | GDPR                            | Determines purposes and means of personal data processing                                     |
| Data Processor            | GDPR                            | Processes personal data on behalf of controller                                               |

#### 5.10 Legal Accountability versus Internal Control Ownership

Legal Accountability concerns obligations imposed on an entity or person under applicable law.

Internal Control Ownership concerns responsibility assigned within an organization for implementing or maintaining a control.

For example:

- Legal entity: Telecommunications operator.
- Regulatory role: Deployer.
- Business owner: Customer Experience Director.
- AI system owner: CRM Platform Manager.
- Security control owner: Cybersecurity Engineering Manager.
- Privacy oversight: Data Protection Officer.
- Compliance coordination: AI Governance and Compliance Manager.

Internal delegation does not, by itself, eliminate the legal entity's obligations.

### 6. Regulatory Conformity, Compliance, and Assurance Terminology

#### 6.1 Regulatory Compliance

Regulatory Compliance is the state of meeting applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

Compliance must be evaluated against actual obligations rather than a generic AI governance checklist.

#### 6.2 Conformity Assessment

Conformity Assessment is the process of demonstrating whether specified requirements relating to a product, system, service, process, or other subject have been fulfilled.

Under the EU AI Act, applicable conformity assessment procedures depend on the relevant AI system and legal provisions.

#### 6.3 Presumption of Conformity

Presumption of Conformity is a legally recognized presumption that specified requirements are satisfied when applicable conditions are met.

In relevant EU regulatory contexts, compliance with referenced harmonized standards may support such a presumption for the requirements covered.

It is not equivalent to unrestricted regulatory immunity.

#### 6.4 Technical Documentation

Technical Documentation consists of records describing relevant aspects of an AI system or model, as required by applicable law or governance arrangements.

Depending on the context, documentation may cover:

- Intended purpose.
- System architecture.
- Model characteristics.
- Data requirements.
- Performance and limitations.
- Risk management.
- Security measures.
- Testing and validation.
- Human oversight.
- Monitoring arrangements.

#### 6.5 Declaration of Conformity

A Declaration of Conformity is a formal declaration by the responsible economic operator that a product or system satisfies applicable requirements under the relevant legislation.

Under the EU AI Act, an EU declaration of conformity is required for relevant high-risk AI systems according to the Act's provisions.

#### 6.6 CE Marking

CE Marking is a marking used under relevant EU product legislation to indicate conformity with applicable requirements.

Where the EU AI Act requires CE marking for an AI system, it must be applied in accordance with the applicable provisions.

CE marking should not be interpreted as a universal quality certification or a guarantee that an AI system is free from cybersecurity vulnerabilities.

#### 6.7 Compliance Evidence

Compliance Evidence consists of records, technical artifacts, approvals, logs, assessments, reports, or other information demonstrating the fulfillment of an applicable requirement.

Examples include:

| Regulatory objective    | Example evidence                               |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Risk management         | Risk management records and treatment evidence |
| Technical documentation | Approved system documentation                  |
| Data governance         | Dataset governance records                     |
| Human oversight         | Oversight procedures and testing records       |
| Cybersecurity           | Security assessment and remediation evidence   |
| Logging                 | Relevant system logs and retention records     |
| Monitoring              | Monitoring reports and corrective actions      |
| Supplier accountability | Contracts and due diligence records            |
| Regulatory reporting    | Submission records and correspondence          |

The precise evidence requirements must be derived from applicable obligations.

#### 6.8 Compliance Gap

A Compliance Gap is a documented difference between an applicable requirement and the organization's current implementation or evidence.

Example:

Requirement: Maintain required technical documentation.

Current state: The AI system has architecture diagrams but lacks required risk management and validation records.

Gap: Technical documentation is incomplete.

Remediation: Assign an owner, prepare missing documentation, validate completeness, and establish maintenance requirements.

#### 6.9 Regulatory Non-Compliance

Regulatory Non-Compliance occurs when an applicable legal obligation has not been satisfied.

Non-compliance may lead to corrective measures, restrictions, administrative sanctions, civil liability, or other consequences depending on the relevant legal framework.

#### 6.10 Regulatory Assurance

Regulatory Assurance is the process of evaluating whether an organization has appropriately identified applicable obligations and implemented effective measures to satisfy them.

It may include:

- Legal applicability reviews.
- Compliance assessments.
- Control testing.
- Documentation reviews.
- Internal audits.
- Independent assessments.
- Management reporting.
- Regulatory inspections.

An assurance review should distinguish verified compliance from management assertions and untested control descriptions.

### 7. AI Regulatory Governance Workflow

An enterprise should establish a repeatable workflow connecting AI use-case intake to regulatory obligations, implementation, and ongoing monitoring.

#### 7.1 Regulatory Governance Lifecycle

```
               AI USE-CASE IDENTIFICATION
                           |
                           v
                 AI SYSTEM REGISTRATION
                           |
                           v
                 LEGAL SCOPE ASSESSMENT
                           |
                           v
                REGULATORY CLASSIFICATION
                           |
                           v
                  LEGAL ROLE ANALYSIS
                           |
                           v
               APPLICABLE OBLIGATIONS
                           |
                           v
                COMPLIANCE GAP ASSESSMENT
                           |
                           v
                   CONTROL MAPPING
                           |
                           v
                CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION
                           |
                           v
                 COMPLIANCE VALIDATION
                           |
                           v
                DEPLOYMENT AUTHORIZATION
                           |
                           v
             MONITORING AND REASSESSMENT
```

This is an illustrative governance workflow. The order and specific mandatory procedures may vary by regulatory regime.

#### 7.2 Regulatory Governance Decision Gates

| Gate   | Decision                                               | Accountable function          |
| ------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| Gate 1 | Is the activity within the scope of the relevant law?  | Legal / Compliance            |
| Gate 2 | What legal classification applies?                     | Legal / AI Governance         |
| Gate 3 | What is the organization's legal role?                 | Legal / Compliance            |
| Gate 4 | Which obligations apply?                               | Legal / Regulatory Compliance |
| Gate 5 | Have compliance gaps been identified and addressed?    | Control Owners / Compliance   |
| Gate 6 | Is required evidence available and validated?          | Compliance / Assurance        |
| Gate 7 | Is deployment legally and organizationally authorized? | Authorized Decision Maker     |
| Gate 8 | Are ongoing obligations monitored?                     | AI Governance / System Owner  |

Legal interpretation and control implementation are separate responsibilities. Technical teams should not independently declare legal compliance without appropriate legal and compliance review.

### 8. Enterprise Case Study — Regulatory Assessment of an AI-Powered CRM Platform

#### 8.1 Business Scenario

A telecommunications operator deploys an AI-powered customer-service platform integrated with:

- Salesforce Service Cloud.
- Salesforce Data Cloud.
- An external LLM provider.
- An enterprise RAG knowledge base.
- Customer identity verification.
- Billing Support Systems (BSS).
- E-commerce order management.
- Service provisioning systems.

The assistant answers customer questions, summarizes account records, recommends services, and may initiate selected customer-service transactions.

#### 8.2 Initial Regulatory Assessment

| Regulatory question                                | Preliminary assessment                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Is AI technology involved?                         | Yes, an LLM-based application                                                       |
| Is the system deployed in the EU?                  | Assumed yes for this scenario                                                       |
| Does the EU AI Act potentially apply?              | Yes, subject to scope and exclusions                                                |
| Is the operator a deployer?                        | Likely for the externally supplied AI system, subject to actual arrangements        |
| Could the operator also become a provider?         | Potentially, depending on development, branding, modification, and legal conditions |
| Is personal data processed?                        | Yes, customer account information                                                   |
| Does GDPR apply?                                   | Likely, subject to processing context and territorial scope                         |
| Is the AI system automatically high-risk?          | No                                                                                  |
| Are transparency obligations potentially relevant? | Yes, depending on the interaction and applicable provisions                         |
| Is a DPIA automatically required?                  | No; assess whether processing is likely to result in high risk                      |
| Are cybersecurity obligations relevant?            | Yes, subject to applicable laws and organizational obligations                      |

#### 8.3 Regulatory Analysis by System Function

| AI function                                | Regulatory consideration                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Answering general service questions        | AI transparency and consumer protection requirements                                                       |
| Retrieving customer account information    | GDPR, confidentiality, and access authorization                                                            |
| Summarizing billing records                | GDPR and data accuracy considerations                                                                      |
| Recommending mobile plans                  | Consumer protection, profiling, and data protection                                                        |
| Initiating customer orders                 | Contractual, consumer, security, and transaction integrity requirements                                    |
| Supporting identity verification           | Data protection and potentially additional biometric or identity-related rules depending on implementation |
| Automatically deciding service eligibility | Requires analysis of the actual decision, its effects, and applicable legal regimes                        |
| Generating customer communications         | Accuracy, transparency, consumer protection, and relevant AI requirements                                  |

The operator must evaluate each function according to its intended purpose and actual implementation.

The presence of Salesforce, an LLM, or customer data does not by itself determine EU AI Act high-risk classification.

#### 9.1 Regulatory Assessment Deliverables

The minimum practical deliverables for an enterprise AI regulatory assessment include:

1. AI Regulatory Applicability Assessment.
2. AI System Regulatory Classification Record.
3. AI Regulatory Role and Responsibility Matrix.
4. AI Regulatory Obligations Register.
5. Regulatory Compliance Gap Assessment.
6. Regulatory Control Mapping Matrix.
7. AI Compliance Evidence Register.
8. Regulatory Change Monitoring Procedure.
9. Regulatory Compliance Assurance Report.

These deliverables should be proportionate to the system's regulatory exposure and the organization's existing compliance management processes.

### 10. Summary

AI regulatory terminology establishes the legal vocabulary needed to determine whether an AI system or model is subject to specific laws, which organizations are responsible, and what obligations must be fulfilled.

The foundational concepts include:

- Regulation, directive, legislation, guidance, and standards have different legal functions and authority.
- Regulatory scope determines whether a law applies before obligations are assigned.
- Regulatory classification is a legal determination, not an internal risk-scoring exercise.
- Prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, transparency-related obligations, and GPAI model requirements must be distinguished.
- Provider, deployer, importer, distributor, authorized representative, controller, and processor are different legal roles.
- Conformity assessment, presumption of conformity, technical documentation, and compliance evidence serve different purposes.
- Regulatory compliance requires demonstrable fulfillment of applicable obligations.
- Enterprise regulatory governance connects legal interpretation with operational controls, evidence, monitoring, and assurance.

### Core Principle

AI regulatory compliance begins with determining the applicable law, the legal classification of the AI system or model, and the organization's regulatory role. Only then can legal obligations be translated into enforceable controls, accountable responsibilities, and auditable compliance evidence.

## Part 2 — AI Regulatory Obligations, EU AI Act Requirements, GDPR, and Regulatory Compliance Implementation

### 11. AI Regulatory Obligations and Compliance Management

Understanding regulatory terminology is only the first step. An enterprise must translate legal requirements into operational processes, technical controls, assigned responsibilities, and verifiable evidence.

AI Regulatory Compliance Management is the structured process of identifying applicable legal obligations, evaluating compliance gaps, implementing controls, monitoring compliance, and maintaining evidence demonstrating that the organization meets its legal responsibilities.

A regulatory obligation should not remain an isolated legal statement. It must be translated into an operational requirement that can be implemented and tested.

#### 11.1 Regulatory Obligations Mapping

Regulatory Obligations Mapping is the process of connecting legal requirements to internal policies, controls, responsible owners, and compliance evidence.

The mapping process establishes traceability between legal provisions and operational implementation.

Example:

| Mapping element        | Example                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Regulation             | EU AI Act                                                   |
| Legal provision        | Applicable high-risk AI system requirement                  |
| Legal obligation       | Establish and maintain a risk management system             |
| Internal policy        | AI Risk Management Policy                                   |
| Control objective      | Ensure AI risks are systematically identified and managed   |
| Control implementation | AI risk assessment, treatment, monitoring, and reassessment |
| Control owner          | AI Risk Manager                                             |
| Evidence               | Risk assessments, treatment plans, review records           |
| Assurance              | Periodic control testing and audit                          |

A single legal obligation may require several controls. Similarly, one enterprise control may support multiple legal obligations.

#### 11.2 Regulatory Control Mapping

Regulatory Control Mapping is the process of linking legal requirements to organizational controls designed to satisfy those requirements.

The mapping should include:

- Legal source and provision.
- Obligation description.
- Applicability conditions.
- Internal control reference.
- Control objective.
- Control owner.
- Implementation status.
- Evidence requirements.
- Testing methodology.
- Compliance status.

An organization should avoid claiming that a control satisfies an entire legal article when it only addresses one part of the article.

#### 11.3 Regulatory Gap Assessment

A Regulatory Gap Assessment evaluates differences between applicable legal requirements and the organization's current governance arrangements.

Typical assessment results include:

| Status              | Meaning                                                             |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Compliant           | Requirement is satisfied and supported by sufficient evidence       |
| Partially compliant | Some requirements are implemented, but gaps remain                  |
| Non-compliant       | An applicable requirement is not satisfied                          |
| Not applicable      | Documented analysis establishes that the requirement does not apply |
| Not assessed        | No sufficient assessment has been completed                         |
| Pending validation  | Implementation is reported but has not yet been adequately verified |

A gap assessment should distinguish between:

1. A control that does not exist.
2. A control that exists but is inadequately designed.
3. A control that is designed but not implemented.
4. A control that is implemented but does not operate effectively.
5. A control that operates but lacks sufficient evidence.

These conditions require different remediation strategies.

#### 11.4 Regulatory Remediation Plan

A Regulatory Remediation Plan defines actions necessary to close identified compliance gaps.

A remediation record should contain:

| Field             | Description                                         |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Finding ID        | Unique identifier                                   |
| Legal reference   | Relevant legal requirement                          |
| Gap description   | Identified deficiency                               |
| Root cause        | Reason the deficiency exists                        |
| Risk rating       | Organizational assessment of potential consequences |
| Corrective action | Required remediation                                |
| Action owner      | Responsible person or function                      |
| Target date       | Planned completion                                  |
| Evidence          | Proof of implementation                             |
| Validation        | Confirmation that remediation is effective          |
| Status            | Open, in progress, pending validation, or closed    |

A remediation action should not be closed merely because a document has been created. The organization must verify that the required control has been implemented and, where appropriate, is operating effectively.

#### 11.5 Regulatory Change Management

Regulatory Change Management is the process of identifying, assessing, communicating, and implementing changes in laws, regulations, regulatory interpretations, and relevant supervisory expectations.

Regulatory changes may affect:

- AI system classification.
- Legal roles and responsibilities.
- Documentation requirements.
- Technical controls.
- Contractual arrangements.
- Data processing activities.
- Monitoring and reporting.
- Internal policies and procedures.

An effective regulatory change process should include:

1. Regulatory intelligence gathering.
2. Change identification.
3. Legal impact assessment.
4. Applicability analysis.
5. Gap assessment.
6. Control and policy updates.
7. Implementation planning.
8. Stakeholder communication.
9. Compliance validation.
10. Evidence retention.

A regulatory change that does not affect the organization's current AI systems may still affect future procurement, development, or deployment decisions.

### 12. EU AI Act Regulatory Terminology and Classification

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act is a major legal framework establishing harmonized rules for AI within the European Union.

It adopts a risk-based regulatory approach and establishes obligations for different AI system and model categories, as well as different economic operators.

#### 12.1 Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 is the formal legal reference for the EU AI Act.

It establishes rules concerning:

- Prohibited AI practices.
- High-risk AI systems.
- Transparency requirements for certain AI systems.
- General-purpose AI models.
- General-purpose AI models presenting systemic risk.
- Responsibilities of providers and deployers.
- Market surveillance and enforcement.
- Governance and conformity assessment.

The Act must be interpreted through its actual articles, annexes, definitions, and applicable implementation dates.

#### 12.2 AI System

AI System, under the EU AI Act, refers to a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that infers from received inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

The statutory definition is important because not every conventional software application is necessarily an AI system under the Act.

#### 12.3 Intended Purpose

Intended Purpose is the use for which an AI system is intended by the provider, including the specific context and conditions of use as described in relevant documentation, instructions, promotional or sales materials, and technical documentation.

Intended purpose is a critical input into regulatory classification.

For example:

An AI assistant designed to answer general telecom service questions may have a different regulatory classification from an AI system designed to make decisions about employee recruitment or access to essential services.

The underlying model may be similar, but the system's intended purpose and deployment context differ.

#### 12.4 Prohibited AI Practices — Article 5

Article 5 of the EU AI Act identifies specific AI practices that are prohibited, subject to the detailed conditions and exceptions in the legislation.

Examples include certain practices involving:

- Harmful manipulation or deception.
- Exploitation of vulnerabilities of particular persons or groups.
- Social scoring in specified circumstances.
- Individual criminal risk assessments based solely on profiling or personality traits, subject to the statutory wording.
- Certain forms of untargeted scraping of facial images to create or expand facial recognition databases.
- Certain emotion recognition practices in workplaces and educational institutions.
- Certain forms of biometric categorization involving sensitive characteristics.
- Certain forms of real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, subject to narrowly defined exceptions and safeguards.

The precise scope of each prohibition must be assessed against the full legal provision.

GRC implication: Where a proposed AI use falls within a prohibited practice, the organization cannot treat ordinary risk mitigation or management risk acceptance as a substitute for compliance with the prohibition.

#### 12.5 High-Risk AI Systems — Article 6

Article 6 establishes criteria for determining whether an AI system is high-risk.

Broadly, the classification covers:

1. Certain AI systems that are safety components of products, or are themselves products, covered by specified Union harmonisation legislation, where the relevant third-party conformity assessment requirements apply.
2. AI systems falling within the use cases listed in Annex III, subject to the Act's applicable conditions and exceptions.

Annex III identifies use cases in areas including:

- Biometrics.
- Critical infrastructure.
- Education and vocational training.
- Employment and worker management.
- Access to essential private and public services.
- Law enforcement.
- Migration, asylum, and border control.
- Administration of justice and democratic processes.

Not every system in these areas automatically qualifies as high-risk. The applicable legal criteria and exceptions must be examined.

#### 12.6 High-Risk AI System Risk Management

Risk Management System, under the high-risk AI system requirements, is a continuous and iterative process operating throughout the AI system lifecycle.

It includes activities such as:

- Risk identification and analysis.
- Estimation and evaluation of relevant risks.
- Evaluation of risks arising from intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse.
- Identification of appropriate risk management measures.
- Testing and verification of risk controls.
- Reassessment when relevant changes occur.

The risk management system should be proportionate to the nature, intended purpose, and risks of the AI system.

It should not be confused with a one-time enterprise risk assessment conducted only before deployment.

#### 12.7 High-Risk AI Data and Data Governance Requirements

The EU AI Act establishes specific data and data governance requirements for applicable high-risk AI systems.

Relevant considerations include:

- Training datasets.
- Validation datasets.
- Testing datasets.
- Data collection processes.
- Data preparation.
- Data annotation.
- Data cleaning.
- Data representativeness.
- Data quality.
- Data bias examination.
- Data gaps and limitations.

The precise requirements depend on the applicable provision and system context.

An enterprise should maintain evidence of dataset provenance, quality assessment, and governance decisions.

#### 12.8 Technical Documentation

Technical Documentation is the documented information required to demonstrate and support compliance with applicable AI system requirements.

For relevant high-risk AI systems, documentation may address:

- General system description.
- Intended purpose.
- System architecture.
- Design specifications.
- Development processes.
- Data requirements.
- Risk management.
- Testing and validation.
- Performance characteristics.
- Human oversight.
- Cybersecurity measures.
- Post-market monitoring.

Documentation must be sufficiently detailed to support regulatory assessment and the relevant operator's obligations.

#### 12.9 Record-Keeping and Logging

Record-Keeping concerns the preservation of required records relating to an AI system's development, operation, and compliance.

Logging concerns the automatic recording of events and information generated or collected during system operation.

For applicable high-risk AI systems, logging requirements support:

- Traceability.
- Monitoring.
- Incident investigation.
- Compliance assessment.
- Detection of abnormal behavior.
- Accountability.

Logging obligations should be implemented with appropriate data protection, access control, and retention safeguards.

#### 12.10 Human Oversight — Article 14

Human Oversight refers to measures designed to enable natural persons to oversee the operation of high-risk AI systems in accordance with the applicable requirements.

The objective is to reduce risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights.

Human oversight should enable appropriately assigned persons to:

- Understand the relevant capabilities and limitations.
- Monitor system operation.
- Detect anomalies or unexpected behavior.
- Interpret outputs appropriately.
- Decide not to use or disregard an output.
- Intervene in operation.
- Interrupt the system where appropriate.

A human reviewer who lacks competence, time, authority, or meaningful access to relevant information does not provide effective oversight simply by clicking an approval button.

#### 12.11 Accuracy, Robustness, and Cybersecurity — Article 15

Applicable high-risk AI systems must meet relevant requirements concerning accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.

Accuracy concerns the ability of an AI system to perform its intended task correctly according to appropriate metrics and context.

Robustness concerns the system's ability to maintain appropriate performance under relevant conditions, including errors, faults, and inconsistencies.

Cybersecurity concerns protecting the AI system against vulnerabilities and attacks, including relevant attempts to manipulate system behavior, compromise assets, or disrupt operation.

These properties require technical evaluation and supporting evidence.

#### 12.12 Post-Market Monitoring

Post-Market Monitoring is the process through which providers systematically collect, document, and analyze relevant information about AI systems placed on the market or put into service, where required.

It supports the identification of:

- Performance deterioration.
- Emerging risks.
- Unexpected behavior.
- Serious incidents.
- Non-compliance.
- Required corrective measures.

Post-market monitoring should not be confused with ordinary IT infrastructure monitoring. It must address the applicable AI-specific requirements.

#### 12.13 Serious Incident

A Serious Incident, under the EU AI Act, is an incident or malfunction meeting the relevant statutory definition, which includes specified consequences such as death or serious harm to health, serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure, infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights, or serious damage to property or the environment.

Organizations must assess applicable reporting obligations and timelines rather than treating every AI operational incident as a legally reportable serious incident.

### 13. General-Purpose AI Model Regulatory Terminology

The EU AI Act establishes specific requirements for providers of general-purpose AI models.

These requirements are distinct from obligations applicable to providers and deployers of downstream AI systems.

#### 13.1 General-Purpose AI Model Provider

A GPAI Model Provider is an entity that develops or has developed a general-purpose AI model and places it on the market under its own name or trademark, subject to the Act's definitions.

The provider has model-level responsibilities under the applicable provisions.

#### 13.2 Downstream Provider

A Downstream Provider is an organization that integrates a GPAI model into another AI system or application.

For example, a telecommunications operator or software vendor may integrate an externally provided LLM into a customer-service platform.

The organization's legal obligations depend on its actual role and activities under the Act.

It should not assume that the GPAI provider is responsible for every obligation relating to the downstream AI system.

#### 13.3 Technical Documentation for GPAI Models

Providers of GPAI models have applicable documentation obligations under the EU AI Act.

Documentation requirements support understanding of:

- Model capabilities.
- Limitations.
- Intended tasks.
- Training and testing processes.
- Evaluation results.
- Relevant technical characteristics.

Specific obligations differ according to the model provider's legal circumstances and applicable provisions.

#### 13.4 Information for Downstream Providers

GPAI providers must provide relevant information and documentation to downstream providers to enable them to understand the model's capabilities and limitations and comply with applicable obligations.

This information is important for downstream AI system risk assessment and integration.

#### 13.5 Copyright Policy

The EU AI Act establishes copyright-related obligations for providers of GPAI models, including requirements concerning a policy to comply with Union copyright law and to identify and comply with applicable rights reservations under the relevant legal framework.

This does not mean that all copyright issues associated with AI training are automatically resolved by publishing a policy.

#### 13.6 Training Content Summary

Providers of GPAI models are subject to an obligation to make publicly available a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training, according to the applicable requirements.

The purpose is to improve transparency concerning training content.

The summary is not equivalent to disclosure of the complete training dataset.

#### 13.7 GPAI Models with Systemic Risk

A GPAI model may be classified as presenting systemic risk under the applicable EU AI Act provisions.

The Act provides criteria and mechanisms for identifying systemic-risk models, including relevant technical capability considerations and designation procedures.

Additional obligations may include:

- Model evaluation.
- Adversarial testing.
- Assessment and mitigation of systemic risks.
- Tracking and reporting relevant information.
- Serious incident reporting.
- Appropriate cybersecurity protection.

Important distinction: Systemic-risk classification at the GPAI model level is not the same as classifying every downstream application using that model as a high-risk AI system.

#### 13.8 Open-Source GPAI Models

The EU AI Act provides specific treatment for certain GPAI models released under free and open-source licences, subject to statutory conditions and exceptions.

Some obligations may not apply where the relevant conditions are met. However, important exceptions exist, including provisions concerning GPAI models with systemic risk.

An open-source or open-weight label does not automatically remove all regulatory obligations.

### 14. GDPR and AI Regulatory Terminology

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is a central legal framework for AI systems processing personal data.

The GDPR applies independently of whether an AI system is classified as high-risk under the EU AI Act.

An AI system may fall outside the EU AI Act's high-risk classification while still being subject to significant GDPR obligations.

#### 14.1 Personal Data

Personal Data, under GDPR Article 4(1), means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.

Examples include:

- Name.
- Email address.
- Telephone number.
- Customer account identifier.
- Location data.
- Online identifiers.
- Identification numbers.
- Certain inferred information relating to an individual.

AI systems may process personal data through prompts, training datasets, embeddings, retrieved documents, model outputs, and operational logs.

#### 14.2 Special Categories of Personal Data

Special Categories of Personal Data are categories defined under GDPR Article 9, including:

- Racial or ethnic origin.
- Political opinions.
- Religious or philosophical beliefs.
- Trade union membership.
- Genetic data.
- Biometric data used for uniquely identifying a natural person.
- Health data.
- Data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation.

Processing is generally prohibited unless an applicable Article 9 condition is satisfied.

The mere fact that data is processed by an AI model does not create a lawful basis for processing special category data.

#### 14.3 Data Controller

A Data Controller determines the purposes and means of processing personal data, alone or jointly with others.

In an enterprise AI deployment, the organization deciding why and how customer information is processed will commonly be a controller for that processing activity.

#### 14.4 Data Processor

A Data Processor processes personal data on behalf of a controller.

An external AI provider may act as a processor where it processes personal data on the organization's documented instructions and within the applicable legal arrangement.

The provider may have a different role for separate processing activities where it determines its own purposes and means.

#### 14.5 Joint Controller

Joint Controllers are two or more controllers that jointly determine the purposes and means of processing.

Organizations using AI services must assess whether they are independent controllers, joint controllers, or controller–processor parties.

Contractual labels alone do not determine the correct legal role. The actual allocation of decision-making authority matters.

#### 14.6 Lawfulness of Processing

Lawfulness of Processing means that personal data processing must have a valid legal basis under GDPR Article 6.

Potential legal bases include:

- Consent.
- Performance of a contract.
- Compliance with a legal obligation.
- Protection of vital interests.
- Performance of a task carried out in the public interest or exercise of official authority.
- Legitimate interests, subject to applicable conditions and balancing requirements.

An organization must assess the legal basis for each relevant processing purpose rather than assuming that one legal basis covers every AI activity.

#### 14.7 Purpose Limitation

Purpose Limitation requires personal data to be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes, subject to GDPR provisions.

For example, customer information collected to provide telecommunications services cannot automatically be reused to train an external AI model without assessing the applicable legal requirements.

#### 14.8 Data Minimization

Data Minimization requires personal data to be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed.

AI systems should not receive unrestricted customer datasets when a limited subset is sufficient to perform the intended task.

#### 14.9 Storage Limitation

Storage Limitation requires personal data to be kept in identifiable form for no longer than necessary for the purposes for which it is processed, subject to applicable legal exceptions.

AI governance should address retention across:

- Training datasets.
- Prompt and response logs.
- RAG indexes.
- Vector databases.
- Conversation histories.
- Model evaluation datasets.
- Backups and archives.

Deleting information from a primary database does not necessarily remove it from every AI-related storage location.

#### 14.10 Data Protection by Design and by Default

Data Protection by Design and by Default, under GDPR Article 25, requires controllers to implement appropriate technical and organizational measures designed to integrate data protection principles into processing and ensure that, by default, only personal data necessary for each specific purpose is processed.

Examples in AI systems include:

- Restricting data access.
- Minimizing prompt content.
- Applying retention controls.
- Separating customer environments.
- Limiting training-data reuse.
- Using appropriate pseudonymization.
- Enforcing privacy-aware retrieval.
- Restricting personal data in generated outputs.

#### 14.11 Data Protection Impact Assessment

A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is an assessment required under GDPR Article 35 where a type of processing, particularly using new technologies, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

A DPIA may be relevant to AI systems involving:

- Systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing, including profiling, where it produces significant effects.
- Large-scale processing of special category data.
- Large-scale systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas.
- Other processing activities likely to result in high risk.

A DPIA should examine:

1. Description of processing operations and purposes.
2. Necessity and proportionality.
3. Risks to individuals' rights and freedoms.
4. Measures addressing identified risks.
5. Safeguards and security measures.
6. Residual risk and relevant consultation requirements.

A DPIA is not simply a cybersecurity risk assessment. It focuses on risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

#### 14.12 Automated Decision-Making — Article 22

Automated Individual Decision-Making, under GDPR Article 22, concerns decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produce legal effects concerning an individual or similarly significantly affect them.

The provision is subject to defined conditions and exceptions.

Examples requiring careful analysis may include:

- Automated credit decisions.
- Automated eligibility decisions for significant services.
- Automated decisions affecting employment.
- Certain automated insurance decisions.

Not every AI-assisted recommendation or decision falls under Article 22.

The assessment must examine whether the decision is solely automated and whether the relevant effects and legal conditions are met.

#### 14.13 Profiling

Profiling, under GDPR Article 4(4), means automated processing of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, particularly to analyze or predict aspects concerning that person's performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements.

AI-based customer segmentation and personalized recommendations may involve profiling depending on how personal data is processed.

#### 14.14 International Data Transfers

International Data Transfer refers to the transfer of personal data to a third country or international organization under the GDPR framework.

AI services hosted or supported outside the European Economic Area may create international transfer considerations.

Relevant mechanisms may include:

- Adequacy decisions.
- Standard Contractual Clauses.
- Binding Corporate Rules.
- Applicable derogations under GDPR Article 49.

Organizations must also assess supplementary safeguards where required by the applicable transfer framework.

### 15. Regulatory Interactions Between the EU AI Act and GDPR

The EU AI Act and GDPR are complementary legal frameworks, but they regulate different subject matter and establish different responsibilities.

The EU AI Act focuses on AI systems and models, including their risks, market placement, use, and specified operator obligations.

The GDPR governs the processing of personal data and protects the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

An AI system may be subject to both frameworks simultaneously.

#### 15.1 Dual Regulatory Applicability

Example:

A telecom operator deploys an AI-powered customer-service assistant that retrieves customer account information and generates responses through an external LLM.

Potential regulatory considerations include:

| Area                 | EU AI Act                                                                   | GDPR                                                               |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| AI system definition | Determines whether the application qualifies as an AI system                | Not the primary classification test                                |
| Intended purpose     | Relevant to system classification and obligations                           | Relevant to processing purpose                                     |
| Transparency         | Certain AI interaction and content disclosure obligations                   | Information duties concerning personal data processing             |
| Data governance      | Specific requirements for applicable high-risk systems and GPAI obligations | Principles and requirements for personal data processing           |
| Risk management      | AI system and model-related risk requirements                               | Risks to rights and freedoms                                       |
| Human oversight      | Specific requirements for applicable high-risk systems                      | Relevant safeguards for automated decision-making where applicable |
| Logging              | Applicable AI system record-keeping requirements                            | Data protection, minimization, and retention requirements          |
| Cybersecurity        | Applicable AI system cybersecurity requirements                             | Security of processing under Article 32                            |
| Accountability       | Operator-specific AI Act obligations                                        | Controller and processor responsibilities                          |

#### 15.2 Avoiding Duplicate Assessments

Organizations should integrate AI Act and GDPR assessments where practical while maintaining their distinct legal objectives.

For example:

- A consolidated system inventory can support both frameworks.
- A common architecture review can identify AI system boundaries and personal data flows.
- A shared risk register can reference separate AI Act and GDPR risk scenarios.
- A coordinated evidence repository can support both compliance functions.
- A single governance committee can oversee both domains if responsibilities and legal decision-making remain clear.

However, an AI Act risk assessment cannot automatically replace a GDPR DPIA.

Likewise, a GDPR DPIA does not automatically satisfy AI Act risk management requirements.

#### 15.3 Example: Customer Data Used for LLM Fine-Tuning

A telecom operator proposes using historical customer-service conversations to fine-tune an LLM.

The assessment should examine:

1. Whether the conversations contain personal data.
2. Whether the proposed purpose is compatible with the original collection purpose.
3. Which GDPR legal basis applies.
4. Whether special category data or confidential information is present.
5. Whether data minimization and retention controls are sufficient.
6. Whether an external provider receives the data.
7. Whether international data transfers occur.
8. Whether a DPIA is required.
9. Which AI Act obligations apply to the model and resulting system.
10. Whether the operator's provider/deployer role changes under the actual deployment arrangement.

Critical distinction: Technical anonymization, removal of names, or replacing customer identifiers with random values does not automatically establish that personal data has been irreversibly anonymized.

### 17. Regulatory Compliance Monitoring and Reporting

Regulatory compliance must be monitored continuously or at defined intervals appropriate to the relevant obligation.

A one-time assessment does not guarantee continued compliance.

#### 17.1 Regulatory Compliance Monitoring

Regulatory Compliance Monitoring is the ongoing process of checking whether the organization continues to satisfy applicable legal obligations.

### 19. GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. What is the difference between AI regulation and AI governance?

AI regulation establishes legally enforceable requirements for defined entities, systems, and activities. AI governance is the organization's internal framework for directing and controlling AI activities. Governance provides the operating structure for implementing regulatory requirements, but having a governance framework does not automatically demonstrate legal compliance.

Q2. How do you determine whether the EU AI Act applies to an AI system?

I would first assess the system against the Act's definition of an AI system, then examine territorial and material scope, relevant exclusions, intended purpose, and the organization's role. I would then determine whether prohibited-practice, high-risk, transparency, or GPAI-related provisions apply. The assessment should be documented and reviewed by the appropriate legal and compliance stakeholders.

Q3. What is the difference between a high-risk AI system and an internally high-risk AI system?

A high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act is a legal classification determined by the Act's criteria. An internally high-risk AI system is classified by the organization under its own risk methodology. Internal classification may reflect business criticality, sensitive information, or operational impact, but it does not establish the system's legal classification.

Q4. What is the difference between an AI provider and an AI deployer?

A provider develops or has developed an AI system or GPAI model and places it on the market or puts the system into service under its name or trademark, subject to the Act's definitions. A deployer uses an AI system under its authority. An organization can have different roles for different systems, and its actual activities determine the applicable legal responsibilities.

Q5. Does every AI system require a DPIA?

No. Under GDPR Article 35, a DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. AI use involving extensive profiling, sensitive data, or other high-risk processing may trigger the requirement. The organization should perform documented DPIA screening rather than automatically treating every AI deployment as requiring a full DPIA.

Q6. Does GDPR apply only to high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act?

No. GDPR applies independently whenever its territorial and material conditions are met for personal data processing. An AI system can be outside the EU AI Act's high-risk classification and still be subject to substantial GDPR obligations.

Q7. How do you map EU AI Act requirements to internal controls?

I would identify the applicable legal provisions, extract the relevant obligations, assess existing policies and controls, identify gaps, and map each obligation to one or more internal controls. Each control should have a defined owner, implementation method, evidence requirement, testing frequency, and effectiveness assessment.

Q8. What is the difference between conformity assessment and compliance assessment?

A conformity assessment evaluates whether specified requirements for a system, product, or process have been fulfilled under an applicable conformity framework. A compliance assessment evaluates whether the organization satisfies applicable legal or regulatory obligations. A conformity assessment may be one component of demonstrating compliance where the legislation requires it.

Q9. How would you manage regulatory changes affecting AI systems?

I would establish regulatory intelligence monitoring, assess each identified change for applicability and impact, identify affected AI systems and legal roles, update the obligations register, perform a gap assessment, assign remediation actions, and validate implementation. Material changes should also trigger reassessment of the relevant AI system and its governance approvals.

Q10. What evidence would you request during an AI regulatory compliance audit?

I would request the AI inventory, intended-purpose documentation, legal applicability assessment, classification records, provider/deployer analysis, obligations register, risk assessments, technical documentation, privacy assessments, contracts, control implementation evidence, monitoring reports, incident records, and corrective action tracking. I would test samples to confirm that the controls actually operate.

Q11. Can an ISO/IEC 42001 certification demonstrate EU AI Act compliance?

No. ISO/IEC 42001 provides requirements for an AI Management System and can support organizational AI governance. However, certification does not automatically establish compliance with every EU AI Act obligation. The organization must still assess the applicable legal requirements and demonstrate that they have been fulfilled.

Q12. What is the difference between AI Act risk management and GDPR DPIA?

AI Act risk management addresses risks associated with the AI system under the relevant Act requirements. A GDPR DPIA evaluates risks to the rights and freedoms of individuals arising from personal data processing. Both may be necessary for one system, and they can share evidence, but they have different legal purposes and assessment criteria.

### 20. Summary

AI regulatory compliance requires an organization to translate legal obligations into practical governance and control activities.

The key concepts covered in this lesson are:

- Regulatory obligations must be mapped to internal policies, controls, owners, and evidence.
- Regulatory gap assessments distinguish missing controls, inadequate design, implementation failures, and operating-effectiveness weaknesses.
- EU AI Act requirements depend on the system's intended purpose, legal classification, and the organization's role.
- High-risk AI system requirements include risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, and applicable monitoring obligations.
- GPAI model obligations are distinct from downstream AI system obligations.
- GDPR applies independently of EU AI Act high-risk classification where its own legal conditions are met.
- DPIA requirements depend on the likelihood of high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms.
- Regulatory control frameworks require traceability from legal provisions to operational controls and evidence.
- Regulatory compliance monitoring must account for changes in systems, providers, business processes, and legislation.
- Compliance reporting and incident notification must follow the specific legal definitions and deadlines.

### Core Principle

Regulatory compliance is not achieved by identifying a law or publishing a policy. It is achieved by establishing legal applicability, determining the correct regulatory roles, mapping obligations to effective controls, and maintaining verifiable evidence that those obligations are continuously fulfilled.

## Part 3 — International AI Regulatory Frameworks, Global Legislation, Sectoral Requirements, and Cross-Border Compliance

### 21. International AI Regulatory Landscape

AI regulation is not governed by a single global legal framework. Different jurisdictions have adopted, or are developing, different approaches to AI regulation, ranging from comprehensive AI-specific legislation to sectoral laws, executive policies, regulatory guidance, and voluntary standards.

For multinational organizations, this creates a complex regulatory environment.

An AI system developed in one country, hosted in another, and deployed in several markets may be subject to multiple legal frameworks simultaneously.

International AI Regulatory Compliance is the process of identifying and managing legal requirements applicable to AI systems across the jurisdictions in which an organization operates, provides services, processes data, or places AI products on the market.

#### 21.1 Global AI Regulatory Approaches

International AI regulation generally follows several approaches:

| Regulatory approach                 | Description                                                                                       | Example                                      |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Comprehensive AI legislation        | Establishes broad legal obligations specifically for AI                                           | EU AI Act                                    |
| Sectoral regulation                 | Applies existing laws to AI use in regulated industries                                           | Financial services and healthcare regulation |
| Data protection regulation          | Regulates personal data processing by AI systems                                                  | GDPR                                         |
| Consumer protection regulation      | Addresses deceptive, unfair, or harmful AI-related practices                                      | Consumer protection laws                     |
| Executive and administrative policy | Establishes government direction and requirements                                                 | US executive and agency policies             |
| Risk management guidance            | Provides frameworks for managing AI risks                                                         | NIST AI RMF                                  |
| Voluntary standards                 | Establishes technical or management requirements without automatically creating legal obligations | ISO/IEC 42001                                |
| Regulatory sandbox                  | Allows controlled testing of innovative systems under defined regulatory arrangements             | EU AI regulatory sandboxes                   |

These approaches may overlap. An organization should not assume that a jurisdiction without comprehensive AI legislation has no AI-related regulatory obligations.

#### 21.2 Global Regulatory Jurisdiction

Regulatory Jurisdiction refers to the legal authority of a government or regulatory body to regulate specified persons, entities, activities, or systems.

For AI systems, jurisdictional analysis may involve:

- Where the AI provider is established.
- Where the AI system is developed.
- Where the AI system is placed on the market.
- Where the AI system is deployed.
- Where individuals affected by the system are located.
- Where personal data is processed.
- Where relevant business activities take place.

A cloud-hosted AI system may involve several jurisdictions even when the organization interacts with a single commercial service provider.

#### 21.3 Regulatory Extraterritoriality

Regulatory Extraterritoriality refers to the application of a jurisdiction's laws to activities, entities, or conduct outside its territorial boundaries under specified legal conditions.

Examples include:

- The EU AI Act's specified application to certain providers and deployers outside the EU.
- GDPR's application to certain organizations outside the EU under Article 3.
- US laws that apply to certain foreign entities or transactions under their specific statutory provisions.

Extraterritorial applicability must be assessed separately for each legal instrument.

#### 21.4 Regulatory Conflict

Regulatory Conflict occurs when applicable legal requirements create competing obligations or practical implementation challenges.

For example:

- One jurisdiction may impose data localization requirements.
- Another jurisdiction may require access to specified records.
- An AI provider may be subject to different transparency or disclosure requirements.
- Cross-border investigations may create competing confidentiality obligations.

Organizations should use legal analysis to identify the actual conflict and determine whether mechanisms such as data segregation, local processing, contractual arrangements, or regulatory consultation are appropriate.

#### 21.5 Regulatory Fragmentation

Regulatory Fragmentation occurs when different jurisdictions adopt inconsistent definitions, classifications, obligations, and enforcement approaches.

For a multinational AI provider, fragmentation may increase:

- Legal assessment costs.
- Product development complexity.
- Documentation requirements.
- Contractual complexity.
- Compliance monitoring costs.
- Market-entry barriers.

A global AI governance framework can provide a common control foundation, but local legal applicability must still be assessed.

### 22. European Union AI Regulatory Framework

The EU has developed a broader digital regulatory environment in which AI governance interacts with data protection, cybersecurity, digital services, product safety, and platform regulation.

The EU AI Act is central to AI-specific regulation, but it does not operate in isolation.

#### 22.1 EU AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes harmonized rules for AI systems and general-purpose AI models.

Its regulatory framework includes:

- Prohibited AI practices.
- Requirements for high-risk AI systems.
- Transparency obligations for specified AI systems.
- Obligations for GPAI model providers.
- Additional obligations for GPAI models presenting systemic risk.
- Responsibilities of economic operators.
- Conformity assessment and market surveillance.
- Governance and enforcement mechanisms.

The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with its provisions applying progressively according to the statutory timetable and subsequent applicable legal developments.

For compliance planning, organizations must verify the actual application dates of the specific provisions relevant to their systems rather than assuming that all requirements started simultaneously.

#### 22.2 GDPR — Regulation (EU) 2016/679

The General Data Protection Regulation regulates the processing of personal data.

Its relevance to AI includes:

- Lawfulness of processing.
- Purpose limitation.
- Data minimization.
- Accuracy.
- Storage limitation.
- Integrity and confidentiality.
- Accountability.
- Data protection by design and by default.
- Data subject rights.
- DPIAs.
- Automated individual decision-making.

GDPR applies independently of the EU AI Act's risk classification.

#### 22.3 NIS2 Directive — Directive (EU) 2022/2555

The NIS2 Directive establishes cybersecurity risk-management and incident-reporting requirements for entities within its scope.

AI-related implications may include:

- Cybersecurity risk management for AI-supported services.
- Security of network and information systems.
- Incident handling.
- Business continuity.
- Supply chain security.
- Access control.
- Vulnerability handling.
- Management accountability.

NIS2 does not establish a universal AI-specific compliance framework. Its relevance depends on whether the entity and its activities fall within its scope.

#### 22.4 Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)

The Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) establishes digital operational resilience requirements for financial entities within its scope.

Relevant AI governance considerations include:

- ICT risk management.
- Third-party ICT risk.
- ICT incident management.
- Digital operational resilience testing.
- Business continuity.
- ICT service-provider oversight.
- Contractual requirements.
- Exit strategies.

DORA is particularly relevant when AI systems are used by regulated financial entities or form part of their ICT services.

It should not be applied automatically to every organization that provides AI services to financial institutions.

#### 22.5 Digital Services Act (DSA)

The Digital Services Act establishes obligations for intermediary services and online platforms within its scope.

AI-related considerations may include:

- Transparency of recommender systems.
- Platform risk assessments.
- Content moderation.
- Advertising transparency.
- Systemic risk mitigation for very large online platforms and search engines.
- Accountability and audit requirements.

The DSA does not regulate all AI applications. Its applicability depends on the organization's service and legal classification.

#### 22.6 Digital Markets Act (DMA)

The Digital Markets Act establishes obligations for designated gatekeepers operating core platform services.

AI-related considerations may involve:

- Data access and combination.
- Interoperability.
- Self-preferencing.
- Platform competition.
- Restrictions concerning certain data-use practices.

The DMA is not a general AI regulation. It is relevant where the organization and services fall within its scope.

#### 22.7 Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)

The Cyber Resilience Act establishes cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements.

Relevant concepts include:

- Security by design.
- Secure-by-default configurations.
- Vulnerability handling.
- Security updates.
- Technical documentation.
- Conformity assessment.
- Manufacturer responsibilities.
- Reporting obligations.

The CRA may be relevant to AI-enabled products that fall within its scope. It should not be assumed to cover every AI SaaS application or purely cloud-hosted service.

#### 22.8 Product Liability Directive — Directive (EU) 2024/2853

The revised Product Liability Directive modernizes EU product liability rules and addresses software and other digital elements within its legal framework.

AI-related considerations include:

- Defective software.
- Product safety expectations.
- Damage caused by defective products.
- Evidence and disclosure.
- Causation.
- Liability allocation across supply chains.

The directive must be considered alongside national transposition and applicable implementation provisions.

#### 22.9 EU Data Act — Regulation (EU) 2023/2854

The EU Data Act establishes rules concerning access to and use of data, including connected product and related service data.

AI-related implications may include:

- Data access rights.
- Data sharing arrangements.
- Cloud switching.
- Interoperability.
- Contractual data-sharing requirements.
- Protection of trade secrets.
- Restrictions and safeguards for certain data transfers.

It can affect organizations using connected products and industrial or IoT data as inputs to AI systems.

### 23. United States AI Regulatory Framework

The United States has historically used a combination of federal executive policy, sectoral regulation, existing laws, agency enforcement, and state-level legislation to govern AI.

Unlike the EU AI Act, the US does not have one comprehensive federal AI statute covering all AI systems.

The legal and policy landscape can change through legislation, executive action, agency rules, and judicial decisions. Organizations should verify the current status of specific requirements before relying on them for compliance decisions.

#### 23.1 NIST AI Risk Management Framework

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides voluntary guidance for managing AI risks.

Its four functions are:

- GOVERN.
- MAP.
- MEASURE.
- MANAGE.

The framework supports AI risk governance, but it is not itself a federal AI regulation.

Organizations may use it as an internal control framework or as a reference when designing AI risk management processes.

#### 23.2 NIST AI 600-1 — Generative AI Profile

The NIST AI 600-1 is a companion resource addressing risks associated with generative AI.

It provides guidance for identifying and managing GenAI-related risks, including:

- Confabulation.
- Data privacy.
- Information security.
- Harmful bias.
- Intellectual property.
- Human-AI interaction.
- Information integrity.
- Value-chain and component integration.

It is guidance rather than a binding regulation.

#### 23.3 Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

AI-related concerns may include:

- Deceptive AI claims.
- Misleading product performance representations.
- Unfair business practices.
- Misuse of consumer data.
- Misleading AI-generated content.
- Unsubstantiated claims concerning accuracy or safety.

Organizations should not assume that an AI-related activity is exempt from existing consumer protection laws simply because AI is involved.

#### 23.4 Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)

- Automated candidate screening.
- Resume ranking.
- Employee performance evaluation.
- Promotion recommendations.
- Scheduling and workforce allocation.
- Automated assessment tools.

Organizations using AI in employment decisions should assess potential discriminatory outcomes and applicable legal requirements.

### 30. Summary

International AI regulatory compliance requires organizations to understand the legal environment across all relevant jurisdictions and integrate those requirements into a consistent enterprise governance structure.

The principal concepts covered are:

- AI regulatory approaches differ across jurisdictions and include comprehensive legislation, sectoral regulation, data protection laws, executive policies, and voluntary standards.
- The EU AI Act operates alongside GDPR, NIS2, DORA, DSA, DMA, CRA, and other legal instruments where applicable.
- The United States combines federal policy, sectoral regulation, existing legislation, agency enforcement, and state-level requirements.
- The United Kingdom follows a largely sector-led and principles-based approach.
- China has specific regulatory measures concerning algorithmic recommendations, deep synthesis, generative AI, data security, and personal information protection.
- Other jurisdictions, including Singapore, Japan, South Korea, Canada, Brazil, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia, have different AI regulatory and governance approaches.
- OECD, UNESCO, the Council of Europe, ISO, IEC, and NIST influence international AI governance through instruments with different legal authority.
- Multinational organizations need a global regulatory inventory, local applicability assessments, and a common control baseline.
- Cross-border AI compliance must account for territorial scope, data transfers, contractual responsibilities, and regulatory differences.
- Proposed legislation must be distinguished from enacted and effective law.

### Core Principle

Global AI compliance requires a common enterprise governance baseline combined with jurisdiction-specific legal assessments. A global policy can establish consistent organizational controls, but only a verified local applicability assessment can determine which legal obligations actually apply in each market.

## Part 4 — Regulatory Enforcement, Legal Liability, Regulatory Audits, Emerging Requirements, and Enterprise Compliance Implementation

### 31. AI Regulatory Enforcement and Supervisory Authorities

AI regulatory compliance does not end when an organization implements policies, completes assessments, or documents its controls. Regulatory authorities may investigate, inspect, request information, impose corrective measures, and apply sanctions where legal requirements are not satisfied.

AI Regulatory Enforcement refers to the actions taken by legally authorized authorities to supervise and enforce compliance with applicable AI-related laws and regulations.

For GRC professionals, understanding enforcement is important because it establishes the potential consequences of non-compliance and helps determine which compliance controls require stronger oversight.

#### 31.1 Regulatory Authority

A Regulatory Authority is a public body legally empowered to supervise, administer, interpret, or enforce specific legal requirements within its jurisdiction.

Depending on the legal framework, an authority may have powers to:

- Request information and documentation.
- Conduct investigations and inspections.
- Examine technical records.
- Request access to relevant systems or facilities.
- Require corrective actions.
- Order restrictions or suspension of activities.
- Impose administrative penalties where legally authorized.
- Refer matters to other competent authorities.

An organization should identify the authorities relevant to its legal entities, business activities, AI systems, and jurisdictions.

#### 31.2 Competent Authority

A Competent Authority is an authority formally designated or legally empowered to perform specific functions under a regulatory framework.

Under the EU AI Act, competent authorities have responsibilities established by the Act and relevant national arrangements.

Depending on the activity, different authorities may be involved in:

- Market surveillance.
- Fundamental rights protection.
- Data protection.
- Product safety.
- Cybersecurity.
- Sector-specific supervision.

The organization should not assume that one regulator is responsible for every AI-related obligation.

#### 31.3 Market Surveillance Authority

A Market Surveillance Authority is an authority responsible for monitoring whether products or systems made available on the market comply with applicable legislation.

Under the EU AI Act, market surveillance authorities perform functions relating to AI systems within the scope of their responsibilities.

Activities may include:

- Reviewing technical documentation.
- Investigating suspected non-compliance.
- Requesting information from economic operators.
- Examining complaints.
- Requiring corrective actions.
- Taking appropriate enforcement measures.

#### 31.4 Regulatory Investigation

A Regulatory Investigation is a formal or otherwise authorized examination of suspected non-compliance, incidents, conduct, or activities within a regulator's jurisdiction.

An investigation may be initiated because of:

- A serious incident.
- A complaint.
- A reported violation.
- A market surveillance activity.
- A suspected prohibited practice.
- A failure to meet applicable obligations.
- Information received from another authority.

Organizations should establish procedures for managing regulatory inquiries, preserving evidence, coordinating legal responses, and ensuring that communications are accurate and authorized.

#### 31.5 Regulatory Inspection

A Regulatory Inspection is an examination conducted by an authorized authority to assess compliance with applicable legal requirements.

An inspection may examine:

- AI governance documentation.
- Risk assessments.
- Technical documentation.
- Training and testing records.
- Data governance arrangements.
- Human oversight.
- Security controls.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Supplier agreements.
- Incident records.
- Corrective actions.

An inspection should not be confused with an internal audit. A regulator exercises legal authority, whereas an internal audit operates under the organization's assurance mandate.

#### 31.6 Regulatory Information Request

A Regulatory Information Request is a formal or authorized request for information from an organization by a competent authority.

Requested information may include:

- AI system descriptions.
- Intended purpose.
- Model documentation.
- Data processing information.
- Risk assessments.
- Security testing reports.
- Logs.
- Contracts.
- Governance approvals.
- Incident investigation records.

An organization should have a controlled process to receive, validate, approve, and respond to such requests within applicable deadlines.

#### 31.7 Corrective Measure

A Corrective Measure is an action required or taken to address identified non-compliance, reduce associated risks, or restore conformity with applicable legal requirements.

Examples include:

- Updating technical documentation.
- Correcting inaccurate information.
- Modifying system functionality.
- Restricting certain uses.
- Implementing additional safeguards.
- Suspending deployment.
- Withdrawing an affected system from the market where legally required.

Corrective measures must address the underlying issue, not merely the audit finding's wording.

#### 31.8 Regulatory Restriction

A Regulatory Restriction is a legally authorized limitation imposed on the use, supply, distribution, or operation of a system or service.

Depending on the legal framework, restrictions may include:

- Prohibition of a specific use.
- Suspension of deployment.
- Restriction of access.
- Withdrawal from the market.
- Recall or other corrective action.

A regulatory restriction is materially different from an internal risk treatment decision because it may be legally binding.

#### 31.9 Regulatory Administrative Fine

An Administrative Fine is a financial penalty imposed by an authorized administrative authority under applicable legislation.

The EU AI Act establishes different maximum fine levels depending on the type and severity of infringement and the applicable provisions.

For example, Article 99 establishes administrative fine provisions, including different thresholds for prohibited AI practices and other specified infringements. The applicable amount must be determined using the statutory conditions, including the relevant turnover-based provisions and rules for particular categories of organizations.

An organization should not calculate its potential exposure using a single universal AI Act penalty percentage.

### 32. AI Legal Liability and Accountability

Regulatory compliance and legal liability are related but distinct concepts.

An organization may violate a regulatory requirement without every affected person automatically having a private claim for compensation. Conversely, an organization may face civil liability even where a specific regulatory infringement has not been established.

AI Legal Liability concerns the legal responsibility of an individual or organization for harm, loss, damage, or unlawful conduct associated with the development, supply, deployment, or operation of an AI system.

#### 32.1 Legal Liability

Legal Liability is the obligation to answer legally for conduct, omissions, or consequences under applicable law.

AI-related liability may arise through:

- Regulatory violations.
- Contractual breaches.
- Product defects.
- Negligence.
- Privacy violations.
- Intellectual property infringement.
- Employment discrimination.
- Consumer protection violations.
- Other civil or criminal legal provisions.

The applicable legal basis determines the liability conditions.

#### 32.2 Civil Liability

Civil Liability concerns legal responsibility for harm or loss that may give rise to remedies such as compensation, damages, or other judicial relief.

AI-related civil liability may involve:

- Incorrect automated decisions.
- Defective AI-enabled products.
- Unauthorized disclosure of personal information.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Negligent system design or deployment.
- Breach of contractual obligations.

Civil liability must be assessed under the applicable national and EU legal frameworks.

#### 32.3 Contractual Liability

Contractual Liability arises when a party fails to fulfill its contractual obligations.

In AI service arrangements, contractual liability may involve:

- Failure to meet agreed security requirements.
- Unauthorized use of customer data.
- Failure to maintain service availability.
- Failure to meet agreed performance commitments.
- Breach of confidentiality.
- Failure to provide required documentation.
- Failure to notify incidents.
- Failure to support regulatory obligations.

A contract should clearly allocate responsibilities between AI providers, cloud providers, integrators, and enterprise customers.

However, contractual allocation cannot automatically remove statutory responsibilities imposed directly by law.

#### 32.4 Product Liability

Product Liability concerns legal responsibility for damage caused by defective products under applicable product liability legislation.

AI-enabled products may create product liability considerations where software, AI functionality, or digital components fall within the relevant legal framework.

Under the revised EU Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853, software is expressly addressed within the updated product liability framework.

Potential issues include:

- Defective software.
- Inadequate safety measures.
- Security vulnerabilities affecting product safety.
- Insufficient warnings.
- Failure to provide necessary updates where relevant.
- Damage caused by defective product functionality.

The directive's application depends on its legal provisions, national transposition, and applicable temporal rules.

#### 32.5 Negligence

Negligence generally refers to a failure to exercise the level of care required under applicable law, resulting in legally recognized harm.

In AI contexts, negligence allegations may involve:

- Inadequate risk assessment.
- Failure to test a system appropriately.
- Failure to address known vulnerabilities.
- Insufficient human oversight.
- Inadequate monitoring.
- Failure to implement reasonable safeguards.

The legal elements and standards differ across jurisdictions.

#### 32.6 Vicarious Liability

Vicarious Liability refers to circumstances in which one party may be legally responsible for the conduct of another because of a legally recognized relationship.

AI-related situations may raise questions about the responsibilities of:

- Employers for employees' conduct.
- Organizations for actions performed by personnel using AI tools.
- Principals for agents acting within relevant authority.
- Other parties under applicable statutory or common-law principles.

The use of AI does not automatically transfer responsibility from the organization to the model provider.

#### 32.7 Intellectual Property Liability

AI systems may create intellectual property issues involving:

- Copyright-protected training material.
- Unauthorized use of protected content.
- Generated outputs resembling protected works.
- Trademark misuse.
- Trade secret disclosure.
- Ownership and licensing of generated content.

Organizations should assess intellectual property rights during procurement, development, deployment, and content publication.

#### 32.8 Accountability

Accountability is the obligation of an entity or person to take responsibility for decisions, actions, and outcomes and to demonstrate how relevant responsibilities have been fulfilled.

In AI governance, accountability requires:

- Clearly assigned responsibilities.
- Defined decision authority.
- Documented risk acceptance.
- Appropriate oversight.
- Evidence of control operation.
- Escalation procedures.
- Remediation of deficiencies.

#### 32.9 Responsibility versus Accountability versus Liability

| Concept         | Meaning                                                | Example                                                          |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Responsibility  | Assigned duty to perform an activity                   | Security team implements AI access controls                      |
| Accountability  | Obligation to answer for a decision or outcome         | System owner remains accountable for system governance           |
| Legal liability | Legal responsibility under applicable law              | A legal entity may face penalties or damages                     |
| Authority       | Power to make or approve a decision                    | AI governance committee approves a deployment within its mandate |
| Ownership       | Assigned stewardship or control of an asset or process | Model owner maintains model documentation                        |

An employee may be responsible for operating a control while the legal entity retains statutory responsibility.

### 33. AI Regulatory Sandboxes and Innovation Governance

Regulatory frameworks must balance innovation with the protection of individuals, organizations, and society.

Regulatory Sandbox refers to a controlled environment in which eligible organizations can test innovative products, services, or technologies under defined supervisory arrangements.

A sandbox may provide structured engagement with regulators and help clarify regulatory expectations.

It does not automatically exempt participants from applicable laws.

#### 33.1 AI Regulatory Sandbox

Under the EU AI Act, Article 57 establishes requirements for AI regulatory sandboxes at Member State level.

These sandboxes are intended to support the development, training, testing, and validation of innovative AI systems under regulatory supervision before placement on the market or putting into service, within the relevant legal conditions.

#### 33.2 Sandbox Objectives

Typical objectives include:

- Supporting responsible AI innovation.
- Clarifying regulatory requirements.
- Identifying legal and technical compliance gaps.
- Testing safeguards in controlled conditions.
- Encouraging cooperation between regulators and innovators.
- Improving understanding of emerging AI risks.
- Supporting the development of compliant AI products.

#### 33.3 Sandbox Participant

A Sandbox Participant is an organization or other eligible actor admitted to a regulatory sandbox under the applicable admission criteria.

Participants may include:

- AI startups.
- Technology providers.
- Research organizations.
- Enterprises developing AI systems.
- Public-sector innovators.
- Other eligible entities.

Participation conditions depend on the relevant sandbox framework.

#### 33.4 Controlled Testing Environment

A Controlled Testing Environment is a setting in which system functionality, data processing, security, and operational behavior can be evaluated under predefined limitations and safeguards.

Controls may include:

- Restricted datasets.
- Limited user groups.
- Isolated infrastructure.
- Monitoring.
- Defined testing objectives.
- Incident procedures.
- Human supervision.
- Controlled access.
- Documented exit criteria.

#### 33.5 Sandbox Exit Criteria

Sandbox Exit Criteria define the conditions that must be met before an AI system exits the testing environment.

Examples include:

- Completion of testing objectives.
- Closure of critical security findings.
- Completion of required legal assessments.
- Approval of risk treatment.
- Sufficient documentation.
- Defined production monitoring.
- Formal deployment authorization.

Sandbox completion should not be treated as automatic regulatory approval or a guarantee of future compliance.

#### 33.6 Regulatory Sandbox versus Internal AI Testing

| Dimension    | Regulatory sandbox                                     | Internal AI testing                 |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Authority    | Established or supervised under a regulatory framework | Established by the organization     |
| Objective    | Controlled innovation and regulatory learning          | Technical and business validation   |
| Oversight    | Regulatory authority involvement                       | Internal governance                 |
| Admission    | Subject to applicable eligibility criteria             | Internal approval                   |
| Legal effect | Depends on the specific sandbox framework              | Does not create regulatory approval |
| Evidence     | May support regulatory engagement                      | Supports internal assurance         |

Regulatory Audit refers to an examination conducted by or on behalf of an authorized regulatory body, or under an applicable regulatory assurance mechanism, to assess compliance with specified requirements.

It is important to distinguish regulatory inspections from internal audits and independent conformity assessments.

#### 34.1 Regulatory Audit Scope

3. Approved communication procedures.
4. Evidence preservation processes.
5. Legal review and privilege management where applicable.
6. Traceable control ownership.
7. Accurate AI system inventories.
8. Current regulatory obligations registers.
9. Documented incident records.
10. Tested escalation procedures.

The organization should avoid creating evidence retrospectively after an authority requests it. Compliance evidence should be generated and maintained as part of normal operations.

### 35. Emerging AI Regulatory Requirements and Governance Trends

AI regulation continues to evolve as AI capabilities, deployment models, and associated risks develop.

Organizations should monitor emerging regulatory developments without treating proposals, consultation papers, policy announcements, and enacted laws as equivalent.

#### 35.1 Regulatory Foresight

Regulatory Foresight is the process of identifying emerging legal, policy, technological, and supervisory developments that may affect future organizational obligations.

It enables organizations to anticipate potential changes and prepare governance capabilities.

#### 35.2 Regulatory Horizon Scanning

Regulatory Horizon Scanning is the systematic monitoring of developments that may create future regulatory or compliance requirements.

Relevant sources include:

- Legislative proposals.
- Official journals.
- Regulatory consultations.
- Supervisory guidance.
- Enforcement decisions.
- Court judgments.
- International policy developments.
- Technical standards.
- Industry codes of practice.

A regulatory horizon-scanning process should distinguish the legal status of each development.

#### 35.3 Emerging Regulatory Themes

| Regulatory theme                | Governance implication                                                        |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Foundation model accountability | Stronger model documentation and provider oversight                           |
| Agentic AI                      | Greater focus on autonomy, tool access, authorization, and human intervention |
| AI-generated content            | Transparency, provenance, labeling, and content integrity                     |
| Deepfakes                       | Identity, consent, disclosure, and misuse prevention                          |
| AI cybersecurity                | Secure AI lifecycle and adversarial resilience                                |
| AI in employment                | Fairness, discrimination, and human oversight                                 |
| AI in financial services        | Model governance, consumer protection, and operational resilience             |
| AI in healthcare                | Safety, clinical validation, privacy, and product regulation                  |
| AI and children                 | Age-appropriate safeguards and protection of vulnerable users                 |
| AI intellectual property        | Training data rights, licensing, and generated-content ownership              |
| Cross-border AI services        | Territorial scope, data transfers, and supplier responsibilities              |
| AI environmental impact         | Resource efficiency and sustainability reporting where applicable             |

These are areas of regulatory attention, not a declaration that every listed theme has a universal standalone AI law.

#### 35.4 Regulatory Technology (RegTech)

Regulatory Technology (RegTech) refers to the use of technology to improve regulatory compliance, monitoring, reporting, and risk management.

AI-enabled RegTech may support:

- Regulatory change monitoring.
- Legal document classification.
- Obligations extraction.
- Regulatory mapping.
- Compliance gap analysis.
- Evidence collection.
- Control testing.
- Regulatory reporting support.

However, automated legal interpretation must be subject to appropriate human review and validation.

An AI-generated compliance conclusion is not itself legal evidence that an organization meets an obligation.

#### 35.5 Supervisory Technology (SupTech)

Supervisory Technology (SupTech) refers to technology used by regulatory authorities to improve supervision, monitoring, and enforcement.

Examples include:

- Automated regulatory reporting analysis.
- Anomaly detection.
- Data analytics.
- Supervisory dashboards.
- Risk-based inspection selection.
- AI-supported market surveillance.

SupTech is primarily associated with regulatory authorities, whereas RegTech is primarily associated with regulated organizations and compliance functions.

#### 35.6 Regulatory Technology Risk

Organizations using AI for compliance activities must also govern the AI tools supporting their regulatory processes.

Potential risks include:

- Incorrect legal interpretation.
- Missing obligations.
- Outdated legal references.
- Fabricated regulatory citations.
- Incorrect applicability determinations.
- Inaccurate control mapping.
- Unauthorized access to legal records.
- Leakage of confidential regulatory correspondence.
- Overreliance on automated compliance conclusions.

A human accountable for compliance should validate material legal determinations.

### 39. AI Regulatory GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. What is regulatory enforcement in the context of AI?

Regulatory enforcement refers to actions taken by authorized authorities to supervise and enforce applicable legal requirements. This may include investigations, information requests, inspections, corrective measures, restrictions, and administrative penalties. An organization should maintain regulatory response procedures and evidence demonstrating compliance.

Q2. What is the difference between regulatory enforcement and regulatory compliance?

Regulatory compliance is the organization's state of fulfilling applicable legal requirements. Regulatory enforcement is the action taken by an authorized authority to ensure those requirements are followed. Compliance is an organizational obligation; enforcement is an external supervisory and legal mechanism.

Q3. What is the difference between civil liability and regulatory liability?

Civil liability concerns legal responsibility for harm or loss and may result in compensation or other judicial remedies. Regulatory liability concerns consequences imposed under applicable regulatory legislation, potentially including administrative sanctions. Both can arise from the same AI-related event, depending on the legal circumstances.

Q4. Can an organization transfer its AI Act obligations to an external provider?

No, not simply through outsourcing. An organization may contractually allocate operational responsibilities and obtain supplier commitments, but contractual arrangements do not automatically eliminate statutory obligations imposed on the organization in its legal role. The actual provider, deployer, and other operator roles must be assessed.

Q5. What is an AI regulatory sandbox?

An AI regulatory sandbox is a controlled environment established under a relevant regulatory framework for testing eligible AI innovations under defined supervision and safeguards. It supports innovation and regulatory learning but does not automatically exempt the participant from applicable legal requirements or constitute general regulatory approval.

Q6. What is the difference between RegTech and SupTech?

RegTech refers to technology used by organizations to improve regulatory compliance, reporting, and risk management. SupTech refers to technology used by regulatory authorities to improve supervision and enforcement.

Q7. How would you prepare an organization for an AI regulatory inspection?

I would ensure the AI inventory is complete, regulatory classifications are documented, operator roles are clear, and the obligations register is current. I would verify technical documentation, risk assessments, security and privacy controls, contracts, logs, and remediation records. I would also establish an authorized response team and evidence-preservation process.

Q8. What is the difference between regulatory guidance and binding legislation?

Binding legislation creates enforceable legal obligations within its scope. Regulatory guidance explains expectations, interpretation, or recommended practices, although its legal significance depends on the issuing authority and legal framework. Organizations must verify the legal status of each instrument.

Q9. How do you manage AI regulatory compliance across multiple countries?

I would establish a global regulatory inventory, assess applicability by legal entity and AI use case, identify local operator roles, and map obligations into a common global control baseline. Local legal and compliance teams would validate jurisdiction-specific requirements. The global function would monitor performance, remediation, and assurance.

Q10. What is regulatory horizon scanning?

Regulatory horizon scanning is the systematic monitoring of proposed and emerging legislation, regulatory guidance, supervisory expectations, enforcement decisions, and standards. It helps organizations anticipate changes and assess potential impacts before requirements become operationally urgent.

Q11. What is the difference between a regulatory audit and an internal audit?

A regulatory audit or inspection is conducted under the authority of a regulator or applicable regulatory mechanism to examine compliance with legal requirements. Internal Audit is an independent organizational assurance function that evaluates governance, risk management, and controls. The authority, mandate, and legal consequences differ.

Q12. What are the biggest regulatory risks associated with agentic AI?

Important regulatory risks include unclear provider/deployer responsibilities, excessive autonomy, unauthorized processing, insufficient human oversight, data protection violations, inaccurate or misleading outputs, unapproved consequential actions, inadequate logging, and failure to reassess changes. The legal assessment must consider the actual purpose and function of the agent rather than assuming that agentic AI has a separate universal legal classification.

Q13. Does the EU AI Act apply to a US-based AI provider?

It can, where the Act's territorial applicability conditions are met. For example, certain providers established outside the EU may fall within scope where they place AI systems or GPAI models on the EU market or where the output of an AI system is used in the EU, subject to the Act's provisions. The assessment must consider the actual facts and applicable exceptions.

Q14. Can an AI system be compliant with GDPR but non-compliant with the EU AI Act?

Yes. The two legal frameworks have different purposes and obligations. A system may satisfy GDPR requirements while failing an applicable AI Act requirement, or the reverse. Both frameworks must be assessed independently.

Q15. How do you demonstrate regulatory accountability to senior management?

I would present the organization's regulatory applicability, system classifications, assigned legal and internal responsibilities, open compliance gaps, risk exposure, control effectiveness, remediation status, and upcoming regulatory changes. The reporting should clearly distinguish verified compliance from untested management assertions.

### 42. Summary

AI regulatory governance extends beyond understanding legislation. It requires the organization to maintain the capabilities needed to respond to regulatory supervision, legal liability, emerging requirements, and cross-border compliance obligations.

The main concepts covered in this lesson are:

- Regulatory enforcement involves legally authorized supervisory and corrective actions.
- Regulatory authorities, competent authorities, and market surveillance authorities have different functions.
- Regulatory investigations and inspections require controlled organizational response procedures and reliable evidence.
- Regulatory corrective measures, restrictions, and administrative fines have different legal purposes.
- AI-related legal liability may arise through regulatory, civil, contractual, product, privacy, or other applicable legal frameworks.
- Contractual allocation of responsibilities does not automatically eliminate statutory obligations.
- Regulatory sandboxes support controlled innovation but do not provide automatic legal exemption or general regulatory approval.
- Regulatory inspections require accurate system inventories, documentation, risk assessments, contracts, and evidence.
- Regulatory foresight and horizon scanning help organizations anticipate legal developments.
- RegTech supports organizational compliance, while SupTech supports regulatory supervision.
- A global regulatory compliance operating model requires central oversight combined with jurisdiction-specific legal assessment.
- Effective regulatory compliance requires clear ownership, control implementation, monitoring, remediation, and independent assurance.

### Core Principle

AI regulatory compliance is an ongoing legal and operational responsibility, not a one-time assessment. An organization must understand its obligations, establish accountable ownership, implement effective controls, maintain defensible evidence, respond appropriately to regulatory authorities, and continuously reassess its compliance as AI systems and legal requirements evolve.
