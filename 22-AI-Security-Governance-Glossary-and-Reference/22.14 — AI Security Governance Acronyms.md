# 22.14 — AI Security Governance Acronyms

## Part 1 — Core AI, Machine Learning, Generative AI, and AI Security Acronyms

### 1. Introduction to AI Security Governance Acronyms

AI security governance involves multiple disciplines, including artificial intelligence, machine learning, cybersecurity, risk management, privacy, regulatory compliance, and technology governance.

Each discipline introduces its own terminology and abbreviations. Understanding these acronyms is essential for professionals working in AI governance, cybersecurity GRC, AI risk management, and regulatory compliance.

This lesson establishes a structured reference of commonly used acronyms across the AI security governance landscape.

The objective is not simply to memorize abbreviations. A governance professional must understand:

- What each acronym represents.
- Which technical or governance domain it belongs to.
- How it is used in enterprise environments.
- Why it matters from a cybersecurity, risk, or compliance perspective.
- How it relates to other standards, technologies, and governance processes.

The acronyms are organized by subject area to make this reference useful for professional study, consulting engagements, risk assessments, and technical discussions.

### 2. Artificial Intelligence (AI) Acronyms

| Acronym | Full term                                 | Explanation                                                                                                                                               |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| AGI     | Artificial General Intelligence           | A hypothetical or conceptual form of AI with broad capabilities across different intellectual tasks, rather than being limited to specific applications.  |
| ANI     | Artificial Narrow Intelligence            | AI designed to perform specific tasks within defined operational boundaries.                                                                              |
| ASI     | Artificial Superintelligence              | A hypothetical form of AI exceeding human capabilities across a broad range of intellectual activities.                                                   |

| HAI     | Human-AI Interaction                      | The interaction between humans and AI systems, including how users provide inputs, interpret outputs, and supervise decisions.                            |
| HCAI    | Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence    | An approach to AI design and governance that emphasizes human needs, oversight, accountability, and well-being.                                           |
| XAI     | Explainable Artificial Intelligence       | Methods and techniques designed to make AI system outputs and behavior more understandable to humans.                                                     |

| FAI     | Fairness in Artificial Intelligence       | Methods and governance practices addressing unfair discrimination and inequitable outcomes in AI systems.                                                 |

Governance distinction:

AI governance, responsible AI, trustworthy AI, and AI risk management are related but not interchangeable.

- AI governance establishes organizational accountability and decision-making structures.
- Responsible AI describes the principles and practices guiding responsible development and use.
- Trustworthy AI describes the characteristics and requirements expected of reliable and acceptable AI.
- AI risk management identifies and manages potential adverse outcomes.

GRC relevance:

These terms frequently appear in AI system architecture documentation, vendor assessments, model inventories, technical risk assessments, and AI security reviews.

A GRC professional does not necessarily need to implement these architectures but should understand their security and governance implications.

For example:

- Federated learning introduces distributed training and participant trust considerations.
- Reinforcement learning introduces risks associated with reward design and unintended agent behavior.
- Computer vision introduces image-data privacy, biometric, and adversarial-input considerations.
- Automated machine learning introduces governance concerns around model selection, reproducibility, and validation.

### 4. Generative AI (GenAI) Acronyms

| Acronym  | Full term                                  | Explanation                                                                                                                                                   |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| GenAI    | Generative Artificial Intelligence         | AI systems capable of generating new content, such as text, images, audio, video, or code.                                                                    |
| LLM      | Large Language Model                       | A large-scale language model trained to process and generate language.                                                                                        |
| SLM      | Small Language Model                       | A language model with a comparatively smaller parameter count and resource footprint.                                                                         |
| VLM      | Vision-Language Model                      | A model that processes and relates visual and textual information.                                                                                            |
| MLLM     | Multimodal Large Language Model            | A large language model capable of processing multiple modalities, such as text, images, and audio.                                                            |
| FMs      | Foundation Models                          | Broadly capable models that can be adapted or used across multiple downstream tasks.                                                                          |
| GPT      | Generative Pre-trained Transformer         | A model family based on generative pretraining and Transformer architectures.                                                                                 |
| MoE      | Mixture of Experts                         | A model architecture that routes inputs through selected specialized subnetworks or experts.                                                                  |
| RAG      | Retrieval-Augmented Generation             | An approach that retrieves relevant external information and supplies it as context to a generative model.                                                    |
| PE       | Prompt Engineering                         | Designing and refining prompts to guide AI system behavior.                                                                                                   |
| CoT      | Chain of Thought                           | An approach involving intermediate reasoning steps in model problem-solving.                                                                                  |
| ToT      | Tree of Thoughts                           | A reasoning framework that explores multiple candidate reasoning paths.                                                                                       |
| ICL      | In-Context Learning                        | A model's ability to adapt its responses based on examples or instructions supplied within its context.                                                       |
| FIM      | Fill-In-the-Middle                         | A training or inference format in which a model completes missing portions of content between supplied prefixes and suffixes.                                 |
| SFT      | Supervised Fine-Tuning                     | Further training a pretrained model using labelled examples of desired behavior.                                                                              |
| RLHF     | Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback | A training approach that uses human preferences or feedback to guide model behavior.                                                                          |
| DPO      | Direct Preference Optimization             | A preference-optimization method that directly trains a model using preference data without a separate reinforcement-learning reward-model optimization loop. |
| ORPO     | Odds Ratio Preference Optimization         | A preference-alignment method that combines supervised fine-tuning and preference optimization in a single objective.                                         |
| PPO      | Proximal Policy Optimization               | A reinforcement-learning algorithm used in various applications, including some RLHF implementations.                                                         |
| LoRA     | Low-Rank Adaptation                        | A parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that introduces trainable low-rank matrices into selected model layers.                                              |
| QLoRA    | Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation              | A method combining quantized base models with LoRA adapters to reduce fine-tuning memory requirements.                                                        |
| PEFT     | Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning            | Fine-tuning techniques that update a limited number of model parameters rather than the entire model.                                                         |
| KV Cache | Key-Value Cache                            | A mechanism used by Transformer models to retain attention key and value representations during autoregressive generation.                                    |
| RoPE     | Rotary Position Embedding                  | A positional encoding method used in some Transformer architectures.                                                                                          |
| BPE      | Byte Pair Encoding                         | A subword tokenization method used to represent text as tokens.                                                                                               |
| VQ       | Vector Quantization                        | A technique that maps continuous representations to discrete codebook entries, used in certain generative architectures.                                      |
| CFG      | Classifier-Free Guidance                   | A technique used in some generative models, particularly diffusion models, to guide generation toward conditioning information.                               |
| T2I      | Text-to-Image                              | A generative AI task that produces images from text prompts.                                                                                                  |
| T2V      | Text-to-Video                              | A generative AI task that produces video from text prompts.                                                                                                   |
| TTS      | Text-to-Speech                             | A generative AI application that converts text into synthesized speech.                                                                                       |

Important distinctions:

- Foundation models are not synonymous with LLMs. Foundation models may be designed for language, vision, audio, or multimodal tasks.
- RAG retrieves external information but does not guarantee that generated answers are accurate.
- Fine-tuning changes model parameters, whereas prompt engineering changes the instructions or context supplied at inference time.
- Open-weight does not automatically mean open-source.
- RLHF and DPO are different approaches to preference alignment.
- LoRA and QLoRA are parameter-efficient adaptation techniques, not independent model architectures.

Important distinctions for AI security:

| Threat               | Primary target                                    | Main objective                                    |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Data poisoning       | Training or learning data                         | Influence model behavior through manipulated data |
| Model poisoning      | Model artifacts, parameters, or training pipeline | Compromise model integrity                        |
| Adversarial examples | Model inputs                                      | Cause incorrect or unintended predictions         |
| Model extraction     | Model behavior through queries                    | Reproduce or approximate a model                  |
| Model inversion      | Model outputs or behavior                         | Infer sensitive information                       |
| Membership inference | Model outputs or behavior                         | Determine whether a record was used in training   |

These attacks are not interchangeable. A proper AI threat model must identify the target, attack stage, adversary capability, potential impact, and applicable mitigations.

### 8. Key Acronyms Commonly Confused in AI Security

Some acronyms are similar or are used inconsistently across technical and governance communities.

| Acronym pair                            | Correct distinction                                                                                                     |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI vs. ML                               | AI is the broader discipline; ML is one approach within AI.                                                             |
| ML vs. DL                               | Deep learning is a subset of machine learning based on multilayer neural networks.                                      |
| GenAI vs. LLM                           | Generative AI is a broader category; LLMs are models focused on language.                                               |
| LLM vs. Foundation Model                | LLMs focus on language; foundation models may cover different modalities and tasks.                                     |
| RAG vs. Fine-Tuning                     | RAG retrieves external information; fine-tuning updates model parameters.                                               |
| SFT vs. RLHF                            | SFT learns from labelled demonstrations; RLHF uses human preference feedback in an optimization process.                |
| RLHF vs. DPO                            | RLHF is a broader training approach; DPO is a specific preference-optimization method.                                  |
| LoRA vs. QLoRA                          | QLoRA combines quantization with LoRA-based adaptation.                                                                 |
| MLOps vs. LLMOps                        | MLOps covers machine learning operations broadly; LLMOps focuses on LLM application and operational requirements.       |
| AI Security vs. AI Safety               | AI security protects systems against threats; AI safety focuses on preventing unintended or harmful behavior.           |
| SIEM vs. SOAR                           | SIEM focuses on collecting and analyzing security events; SOAR coordinates and automates response workflows.            |
| IAM vs. PAM                             | IAM manages identities and access; PAM focuses on privileged accounts and permissions.                                  |
| RBAC vs. ABAC                           | RBAC uses roles; ABAC uses attributes to make access decisions.                                                         |
| Open-weight vs. Open-source             | Publicly available model weights do not necessarily mean that the model satisfies open-source criteria.                 |
| Model Extraction vs. Model Inversion    | Extraction attempts to reproduce model behavior; inversion attempts to infer information about inputs or training data. |
| Data Poisoning vs. Adversarial Examples | Poisoning targets learning data; adversarial examples target model inputs.                                              |

### 9. Applying Acronyms in Enterprise GRC Assessments

Acronyms should not be treated as isolated technical vocabulary. They should help a GRC professional identify the relevant risks, controls, owners, and evidence.

Consider an enterprise organization deploying a third-party LLM through an API.

A practical GRC assessment may involve the following:

| Domain                | Acronyms                  | Example GRC assessment                                                               |
| --------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| AI governance         | AI, AIG, AIMS, RAI        | Is the AI use case approved under the organization's governance framework?           |
| Model architecture    | LLM, FM, VLM, MoE         | Does the organization understand the model capabilities and limitations?             |
| Integration           | API, SDK, MCP, OAuth      | Are model and tool integrations authenticated and authorized?                        |
| Data protection       | PII, PHI, DLP, DSPM       | Can sensitive information be exposed through prompts, outputs, or retrieval sources? |
| Identity security     | IAM, PAM, MFA, RBAC, ABAC | Are users and AI agents assigned appropriate permissions?                            |
| AI security           | AML, AE, DP, MP, MIA      | Have relevant AI-specific threats been assessed?                                     |
| Infrastructure        | GPU, TPU, VPC, KMS, HSM   | Is the supporting infrastructure appropriately secured?                              |
| Operations            | MLOps, LLMOps, SIEM, SOAR | Are model changes, security events, and operational risks monitored?                 |
| Software supply chain | SBOM, DevSecOps, CI/CD    | Are software dependencies and deployment pipelines controlled?                       |

Example GRC questions:

1. Has the organization identified all LLMs and foundation models used within the application?
2. Is the model provider subject to appropriate third-party risk assessment?
3. Are APIs secured through strong authentication and authorization?
4. Are MCP-connected tools restricted to approved operations?
5. Are prompts and retrieved documents protected against sensitive-data exposure?
6. Are model outputs monitored for unsafe or unexpected behavior?
7. Are model changes subject to formal change management?
8. Are relevant AI security threats incorporated into the risk register?
9. Are security events integrated into the enterprise SIEM?
10. Is there an established process for investigating AI-related incidents?

### 10. Summary and Core Principle

This part establishes the foundational vocabulary used across artificial intelligence, machine learning, generative AI, LLM architecture, AI security, and enterprise security architecture.

The most important distinctions are:

- AI is the broader discipline; machine learning and deep learning are subsets.
- Generative AI includes multiple model families and is not limited to LLMs.
- Foundation models can support multiple downstream tasks and modalities.
- AI security addresses threats to models, data, applications, integrations, and infrastructure.
- AI security architecture relies on established cybersecurity capabilities such as IAM, PAM, API security, cryptography, DLP, SIEM, and secure development.
- AI operations require lifecycle governance, monitoring, change management, and evidence.

Core Principle: Knowing AI security acronyms is not about memorizing terminology. It is about understanding the technology and control domains behind each term so that technical discussions can be translated into meaningful enterprise risks, governance requirements, and verifiable security controls.

### 18. Summary and Core Principle

This part covers the acronyms most relevant to AI governance, enterprise risk management, international standards, regulatory compliance, privacy, audit assurance, and third-party risk management.

The main conceptual distinctions are:

- AIMS and ISMS are management systems, not individual controls.
- AI risk management is part of broader enterprise risk governance.
- Risk owners and control owners have different accountability.
- ISO standards, NIST frameworks, and legal regulations have different purposes and authority.
- FRIA and DPIA address different legal requirements.
- SOC reports provide assurance within defined scopes and criteria; they do not establish universal AI Act compliance.
- Vendor and supply-chain governance must include external models, datasets, infrastructure, and subprocessors.

Core Principle: AI governance professionals must understand both technical and regulatory terminology. The ability to translate these acronyms into organizational responsibilities, risk assessments, control objectives, and audit evidence is what makes the terminology operationally valuable.
