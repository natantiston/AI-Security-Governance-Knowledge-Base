# 22.03 — Generative AI Terminology

## Part 1 — Foundations of Generative AI, Core Concepts, and Learning Architectures

## 1. Introduction

Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI or GenAI) represents a major evolution in artificial intelligence because it enables systems to generate new content rather than simply classify, predict, or analyze existing information.

Traditional machine learning systems are commonly designed to identify patterns, classify observations, detect anomalies, or predict outcomes. Generative AI systems use learned representations and generative models to produce new outputs, including:

- Text and documents.
- Images and graphics.
- Audio and speech.
- Video.
- Software code.
- Synthetic datasets.
- Three-dimensional content.
- Structured business information.

Generative AI is increasingly being integrated into enterprise environments, including cybersecurity operations, customer service, software development, financial services, telecommunications, healthcare, legal services, and business process automation.

However, the introduction of generative capabilities creates additional security and governance challenges.

Unlike a conventional predictive model that produces a predefined classification or numerical score, a generative model can produce open-ended content that may be inaccurate, misleading, inappropriate, confidential, or potentially harmful.

For example, an enterprise employee may use a generative AI assistant to:

- Summarize confidential business documents.
- Draft customer communications.
- Analyze security incidents.
- Generate software code.
- Produce management reports.
- Retrieve internal corporate information.
- Generate recommendations for business decisions.

Each activity introduces different risks involving data protection, intellectual property, accuracy, accountability, access control, and human oversight.

From an AI Security Governance perspective, understanding Generative AI requires understanding its underlying technologies, training approaches, generation mechanisms, limitations, and operational boundaries.

## 2. Generative Artificial Intelligence Fundamentals

### 2.1 Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI)

Definition:

Generative Artificial Intelligence is a category of AI that uses learned patterns and representations to generate new content, data, or outputs based on information provided to the system.

Generative AI models learn characteristics of their training data and use those learned representations to produce new outputs that resemble or are consistent with aspects of the learned data distribution.

Examples:

| Generative capability | Example output                             |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Text generation       | Business reports, emails, summaries        |
| Image generation      | Product images, illustrations, diagrams    |
| Audio generation      | Speech, music, sound effects               |
| Video generation      | Short videos and animations                |
| Code generation       | Software functions and scripts             |
| Data generation       | Synthetic datasets                         |
| 3D generation         | Three-dimensional objects and environments |

Enterprise example:

A telecommunications operator deploys a Generative AI assistant for its customer service department.

The assistant can:

- Draft responses to customer questions.
- Summarize customer interactions.
- Explain service policies.
- Generate troubleshooting instructions.
- Produce internal knowledge articles.

The system receives a prompt, processes the available context, and generates a response.

GRC implications:

Generative AI introduces risks that must be considered throughout its lifecycle:

- Inaccurate or fabricated information.
- Confidential data disclosure.
- Copyright and intellectual property infringement.
- Prompt injection.
- Harmful or inappropriate content generation.
- Bias and discriminatory outputs.
- Unauthorized use of generated content.
- Inadequate human review.
- Unclear accountability.

Important distinction:

Generative AI does not necessarily retrieve or reproduce an existing document. It can generate new content by applying patterns learned during training and information available during inference.

However, some systems combine generation with retrieval mechanisms, allowing them to use external documents or enterprise information when producing responses.

### 2.2 Generative Model

Definition:

A Generative Model is a machine learning model designed to learn the underlying distribution or structure of data and generate new samples that follow or approximate that learned distribution.

The generated output may be text, images, audio, video, or other types of data.

Example:

An image generation model is trained using a large collection of images.

During training, it learns visual characteristics such as:

- Shapes.
- Textures.
- Colors.
- Objects.
- Spatial relationships.
- Visual patterns.

When given a text prompt, the model can generate a new image that reflects the requested description.

GRC implications:

The organization must understand:

- Which data was used to train the model.
- Whether the training data was appropriately sourced.
- What types of content the model can generate.
- Whether the model may reproduce protected or confidential material.
- Whether generated content requires human validation.
- Whether model outputs are appropriately monitored.

### 2.3 Generative vs. Discriminative Models

This is one of the most important distinctions in machine learning and Generative AI.

Discriminative Model — Definition:

A Discriminative Model learns relationships between input data and target labels or outcomes, with the objective of distinguishing between categories or predicting specific outcomes.

Generative Model — Definition:

A Generative Model learns characteristics of the underlying data distribution and uses that learned information to generate new samples or content.

Example: Email security

A discriminative model:

- Receives an email.
- Analyzes its characteristics.
- Classifies it as legitimate or phishing.

A generative model:

- Receives a prompt describing an email.
- Generates a new email message.
- Produces text based on its learned language patterns.

Comparison:

| Dimension               | Discriminative model                                     | Generative model                                           |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary purpose         | Classification or prediction                             | Content or sample generation                               |
| Learning objective      | Distinguish outcomes                                     | Learn data distribution or representation                  |
| Typical output          | Class, probability, score                                | Text, image, audio, video, or data                         |
| Example                 | Phishing classifier                                      | AI email generator                                         |
| Common techniques       | Logistic Regression, SVM, classification neural networks | GANs, VAEs, autoregressive models, diffusion models        |
| Main governance concern | Prediction reliability and decision errors               | Generated content accuracy, safety, provenance, and misuse |

Important distinction:

Not every neural network is generative, and not every generative model is a Large Language Model.

A neural network may be used for classification, prediction, representation learning, or content generation.

### 2.4 Content Generation

Definition:

Content Generation is the process through which an AI system produces new information or media based on learned patterns, input instructions, contextual information, or other conditioning signals.

Examples:

- Generating a cybersecurity incident report.
- Creating an image from a text description.
- Producing a voice response.
- Generating a video from a written prompt.
- Drafting software code.
- Producing synthetic customer records.

GRC implications:

Organizations should establish rules governing:

- Permitted content generation.
- Restricted content categories.
- Confidential information handling.
- Human review requirements.
- Copyright and licensing.
- Content attribution.
- Record retention.
- Accountability for generated outputs.

### 2.5 Prompt

Definition:

A Prompt is an input provided to a generative AI model to instruct, guide, or condition the generation of an output.

A prompt may contain:

- Instructions.
- Questions.
- Context.
- Reference information.
- Examples.
- Constraints.
- Desired output formats.

Example:

A SOC analyst enters:

> Analyze the following firewall logs, identify suspicious activity, summarize potential security incidents, and provide recommendations for further investigation.

The model processes the prompt and generates a response based on its learned capabilities and the information available to it.

GRC implications:

Prompts can contain confidential information, personal data, credentials, proprietary business information, or sensitive security details.

Organizations should establish:

- Prompt handling policies.
- Data classification requirements.
- Approved AI usage rules.
- Logging and monitoring requirements.
- Restrictions on sensitive information.
- Secure integration with enterprise systems.

### 2.6 Prompt Engineering

Definition:

Prompt Engineering is the practice of designing and structuring prompts to guide a generative AI model toward more useful, relevant, and controlled outputs.

It involves selecting appropriate instructions, context, examples, constraints, and output formats.

Example:

A general prompt:

> Explain cybersecurity risk.

A more structured prompt:

> Act as an enterprise cybersecurity GRC analyst. Explain cybersecurity risk in the context of a telecommunications operator. Include the risk scenario, business impact, likelihood, inherent risk, control requirements, residual risk, and recommended remediation.

The second prompt provides a more specific task and expected structure.

Common prompt engineering techniques:

- Zero-shot prompting.
- Few-shot prompting.
- Role-based prompting.
- Instruction-based prompting.
- Structured output prompting.
- Chain-of-thought prompting.
- Contextual prompting.
- Constraint-based prompting.

GRC implications:

Prompt engineering can improve output quality but does not guarantee accuracy, security, or compliance.

Prompts should not be treated as security boundaries. Sensitive information, access permissions, and prohibited actions must be enforced through technical controls rather than relying solely on instructions given to the model.

## 3. Major Generative AI Model Categories

Generative AI includes several different model architectures and learning approaches.

Understanding these categories is important because each has different capabilities, training requirements, security characteristics, and operational limitations.

### 3.1 Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)

Definition:

A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) is a generative model architecture consisting of two neural networks that compete during training:

1. Generator.
2. Discriminator.

The generator attempts to produce synthetic samples that resemble real data.

The discriminator attempts to distinguish generated samples from real samples.

Through this adversarial training process, the generator learns to produce increasingly realistic outputs.

GAN architecture:

Random Noise / Latent Vector

Input to the generator

Generator

Creates synthetic data

Generated Sample

Discriminator

Evaluates real vs. generated samples

Real Data

Reference samples

Generated Data

Synthetic samples

The generator and discriminator are trained adversarially to improve the quality of generated samples and the discriminator's ability to distinguish them.

Example:

A company uses a GAN to generate synthetic images of industrial equipment for training an image recognition system.

The generated images supplement the available training dataset.

Common applications:

- Synthetic image generation.
- Image enhancement.
- Image-to-image translation.
- Data augmentation.
- Deepfake generation.
- Synthetic data generation.

GRC and security implications:

- GANs can be used to generate realistic fake images or identities.
- Synthetic data may reproduce sensitive characteristics of training data.
- Generated media can be used in impersonation and fraud.
- Model training data and artifacts require protection.
- Organizations should assess misuse scenarios involving synthetic media.

### 3.2 Variational Autoencoder (VAE)

Definition:

A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is a generative neural network architecture that learns a probabilistic representation of input data in a latent space and uses that representation to generate new samples.

A VAE consists primarily of:

1. Encoder.
2. Latent representation.
3. Decoder.

The encoder transforms input data into a probability distribution over latent variables. The decoder uses sampled latent representations to reconstruct or generate data.

VAE architecture:

Input Data

Encoder

Learns latent distribution parameters

Latent Space

Probabilistic representation

Decoder

Reconstructs or generates data

Generated Output

Example:

A healthcare research organization uses a VAE to generate synthetic medical images for research and model development.

The model learns latent representations of the original images and generates new samples from the learned latent space.

Common applications:

- Synthetic data generation.
- Image reconstruction.
- Anomaly detection.
- Representation learning.
- Generative modeling.

GRC implications:

- Evaluate privacy leakage from generated samples.
- Assess whether synthetic data reproduces identifiable characteristics.
- Validate the quality of generated information.
- Protect model artifacts and training data.
- Document the limitations of generated samples.

### 3.3 Diffusion Model

Definition:

A Diffusion Model is a generative model that learns to generate data by reversing a process that progressively adds noise to training examples.

During training, the model learns how to estimate or remove noise at different stages.

During generation, it starts with noise and progressively transforms it into a structured output.

Diffusion models are widely used in image generation and are also applied to audio, video, and other modalities.

Conceptual process:

Original Training Image

Noise Added

The image becomes progressively less recognizable

Highly Noisy Representation

Learned Denoising Process

The model learns to reverse the noise process

Generated Image

Example:

A user enters:

> Generate a realistic image of a modern telecommunications data center.

A diffusion model starts from a noisy representation and progressively generates an image matching the requested description.

Common applications:

- Text-to-image generation.
- Image editing.
- Image restoration.
- Video generation.
- Audio generation.
- Synthetic data creation.

GRC implications:

- Generated images may resemble copyrighted or protected material.
- Models may generate misleading or deceptive content.
- Training data provenance must be assessed.
- Content safety requirements should be established.
- Generated media may require provenance and labeling controls.

### 3.4 Autoregressive Generative Model

Definition:

An Autoregressive Generative Model generates a sequence by predicting the next element based on previously generated or available elements.

For text generation, the model predicts the next token based on the preceding tokens and context.

Example:

A language model receives:

> The primary objective of cybersecurity governance is to...

It predicts a likely next token, then continues generating subsequent tokens based on the updated context.

Simplified generation process:

```
Input:
"The primary objective of cybersecurity governance is to"

Step 1:
Predict next token → "establish"

Step 2:
Context becomes:
"The primary objective of cybersecurity governance is to establish"

Predict next token → "appropriate"

Step 3:
Context becomes:
"The primary objective of cybersecurity governance is to establish appropriate"

Predict next token → "controls"

Continue until a stopping condition is reached.
```

Common applications:

- Large Language Models.
- Text generation.
- Code generation.
- Speech generation.
- Sequential data generation.

GRC implications:

- Generated outputs can be inaccurate.
- Output quality depends on context and training.
- Sensitive prompts may expose confidential information.
- Generated code may contain security vulnerabilities.
- The model's output should not automatically be treated as verified information.

### 3.5 Transformer-Based Generative Model

Definition:

A Transformer-Based Generative Model is a generative architecture that uses Transformer components, particularly attention mechanisms, to process input sequences and generate outputs.

Many modern LLMs use Transformer-based architectures.

Depending on their design, they may use:

- Encoder-only architecture.
- Encoder-decoder architecture.
- Decoder-only architecture.

Examples:

| Architecture    | Typical purpose                            |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Encoder-only    | Representation learning and classification |
| Encoder-decoder | Sequence-to-sequence tasks                 |
| Decoder-only    | Autoregressive text generation             |

GRC implications:

Transformer-based generative models can process large amounts of contextual information, but their capabilities introduce risks involving hallucination, prompt injection, sensitive data handling, and output reliability.

The architecture itself does not establish whether a model is safe, accurate, or compliant.

## 4. Generative AI Learning and Generation Mechanisms

### 4.1 Generative Modeling

Definition:

Generative Modeling is the process of developing a model that learns the statistical structure or distribution of training data and uses that knowledge to generate new samples.

The model attempts to capture relevant characteristics of the data rather than simply memorizing a list of predefined outputs.

Example:

A model trained on thousands of business reports learns patterns involving:

- Document structure.
- Sentence construction.
- Terminology.
- Relationships between concepts.
- Formatting conventions.

It can then generate a new report based on an instruction and supplied context.

GRC implications:

Organizations should assess the model's training data, intended use, generation limitations, and potential reproduction of sensitive or protected material.

### 4.2 Probability Distribution

Definition:

A Probability Distribution describes the likelihood of possible values or outcomes of a random variable.

Generative models use learned probability distributions or related probabilistic representations to generate new samples.

Example:

A text generation model assigns probabilities to possible next tokens.

Given the context:

> The capital of France is...

The model may assign a high probability to "Paris" and lower probabilities to other possible tokens.

GRC implications:

A model selecting a highly probable token does not guarantee that the resulting statement is factually correct.

Probability is not equivalent to truth, evidence, or verified knowledge.

### 4.3 Conditional Generation

Definition:

Conditional Generation is the process of generating output based on additional information or constraints supplied to a generative model.

The model's generation is conditioned on one or more inputs.

Examples:

- Generating an image from a text prompt.
- Generating a report using supplied source documents.
- Producing speech in a specified language.
- Generating a response based on a user instruction.
- Creating an image with a specified visual style.

Example:

A cybersecurity manager requests:

> Generate a risk assessment summary using the following vulnerability findings. Organize the results by severity, business impact, and remediation priority.

The model generates a structured response based on the supplied information.

GRC implications:

The organization should assess whether the conditioning information is trustworthy, authorized, complete, and appropriate for the intended output.

### 4.4 Unconditional Generation

Definition:

Unconditional Generation is the process of generating samples without requiring a specific external condition or detailed user-provided instruction.

The model generates outputs based on its learned data distribution and generation mechanism.

Example:

A generative image model produces a random image sample without receiving a detailed text description.

Important distinction:

Conditional generation uses supplied conditions to guide output. Unconditional generation does not depend on a specific external condition in the same way.

### 4.5 Sampling

Definition:

Sampling is the process of selecting values, tokens, or other elements from a probability distribution during generation.

In text generation, sampling determines which token is selected from the model's predicted token distribution.

Example:

A model generates the sentence:

> The organization should implement appropriate security controls.

At each generation step, the model evaluates possible next tokens and selects one according to its sampling strategy.

GRC implications:

Sampling configuration can affect output diversity, predictability, and consistency.

Organizations should understand whether generation settings are controlled, particularly when outputs are used in regulated or high-impact business processes.

### 4.6 Temperature

Definition:

Temperature is a generation parameter that influences the distribution of probabilities used when selecting the next token.

Lower temperature generally makes generation more deterministic by concentrating probability on higher-probability tokens.

Higher temperature generally increases output variability by allowing lower-probability alternatives to be selected more frequently.

Illustrative behavior:

| Temperature setting | General behavior                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0 or near 0         | More deterministic selection, subject to implementation |
| Low                 | More predictable outputs                                |
| Moderate            | Balanced variability                                    |
| High                | More diverse and less predictable outputs               |

Example:

A company generates standard compliance reports.

It may use a low temperature to encourage consistent language and structure.

For creative brainstorming, it may use a higher temperature to encourage more varied responses.

GRC implications:

Temperature is not a security control and does not guarantee factual accuracy.

Generation settings should be documented where output consistency is important.

### 4.7 Top-k Sampling

Definition:

Top-k Sampling is a token selection technique that restricts the candidate next-token choices to the K highest-probability tokens before sampling.

Example:

If K equals 5, the generation process considers the five highest-probability candidate tokens and selects among them according to their relative probabilities.

GRC implications:

Top-k sampling affects output variability and should be treated as a model configuration rather than a mechanism for ensuring correctness.

### 4.8 Top-p Sampling

Definition:

Top-p Sampling, also known as nucleus sampling, selects from the smallest set of candidate tokens whose cumulative probability reaches or exceeds a specified threshold p.

Unlike top-k sampling, the number of candidate tokens can vary.

Example:

If the configured threshold is 0.90, the model selects from a set of candidate tokens whose cumulative probability reaches approximately 90%.

Important distinction:

- Top-k uses a fixed maximum number of candidate tokens.
- Top-p uses a cumulative probability threshold.

GRC implications:

Organizations should document generation parameters when they materially affect output behavior or consistency.

### 4.9 Seed

Definition:

A Seed is an initial value used by a pseudorandom number generator to help reproduce a sequence of random choices.

In generative AI, a fixed seed may support more reproducible outputs under otherwise identical conditions.

GRC implications:

Seeds can support repeatability and testing, but they do not guarantee identical outputs across different model versions, software environments, hardware configurations, or inference implementations.

### 4.10 Deterministic vs. Non-Deterministic Generation

Deterministic Generation:

A generation process that produces the same output under identical conditions, including model version, inputs, configuration, and execution environment.

Non-Deterministic Generation:

A generation process that may produce different outputs from the same input because of sampling, randomness, infrastructure behavior, or implementation differences.

| Dimension          | Deterministic generation | Non-deterministic generation       |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Output consistency | More repeatable          | May vary                           |
| Testing            | Easier to reproduce      | Requires broader test coverage     |
| Typical use        | Standardized responses   | Creative or varied content         |
| Governance concern | Reproducibility          | Output variability and consistency |

GRC consideration:

For regulated processes, organizations should define the level of reproducibility required and maintain records of relevant model versions, inputs, configurations, and outputs.

## 5. Generative AI Modalities

Generative AI systems can produce different types of content, known as modalities.

A modality refers to a particular form or type of information processed or generated by an AI system.

### 5.1 Text Generation

Definition:

Text Generation is the process of producing written language using a generative AI model.

Examples:

- Emails.
- Reports.
- Policies.
- Summaries.
- Translations.
- Business documentation.
- Technical explanations.
- Software documentation.

Enterprise example:

A GRC team uses an AI assistant to draft an initial cybersecurity risk assessment based on approved project documentation.

GRC implications:

- Validate factual accuracy.
- Protect confidential source information.
- Review generated recommendations.
- Establish human approval.
- Maintain traceability to authoritative sources.

### 5.2 Image Generation

Definition:

Image Generation is the process of producing visual content using a generative AI model, often based on a text prompt, reference image, or other conditioning information.

Examples:

- Marketing images.
- Product illustrations.
- Conceptual diagrams.
- Synthetic training images.
- Visual design assets.

GRC implications:

- Copyright and intellectual property.
- Deepfake generation.
- Unauthorized likeness use.
- Misleading visual content.
- Provenance and attribution.
- Restricted content generation.

### 5.3 Audio Generation

Definition:

Audio Generation is the process of producing audio content using generative AI models.

Generated audio may include speech, sound effects, music, or other acoustic content.

Examples:

- Synthetic speech.
- Voice assistants.
- Audio narration.
- Generated sound effects.
- Language dubbing.

GRC implications:

- Voice impersonation.
- Fraudulent telephone calls.
- Unauthorized voice cloning.
- Consent and biometric privacy.
- Intellectual property.
- Disclosure of synthetic audio.

### 5.4 Video Generation

Definition:

Video Generation is the process of creating video content using generative AI models based on text prompts, images, video references, or other inputs.

Examples:

- Synthetic training videos.
- Marketing content.
- Animated scenes.
- Video editing.
- Simulated environments.

GRC implications:

- Deepfake creation.
- Identity impersonation.
- Misinformation.
- Copyright violations.
- Manipulated evidence.
- Misleading business communications.

### 5.5 Code Generation

Definition:

Code Generation is the process of producing computer program code using a generative AI model.

Examples:

- Python scripts.
- SQL queries.
- PowerShell scripts.
- Infrastructure configuration.
- Application code.
- Security automation scripts.

Example:

A cybersecurity engineer requests a script that analyzes firewall logs and identifies unusual connection attempts.

The AI model generates a proposed script.

GRC implications:

Generated code must not be assumed secure or production-ready.

Organizations should apply:

- Secure code review.
- Static application security testing.
- Dependency scanning.
- Secrets detection.
- Testing.
- Change management.
- Human approval.

### 5.6 Multimodal Generation

Definition:

Multimodal Generation refers to the generation of content involving more than one modality, such as text, images, audio, and video.

Examples:

- Generating an image and its descriptive caption.
- Producing speech from text.
- Generating a video from a written prompt.
- Creating a presentation containing text and images.
- Producing audio descriptions of visual information.

GRC implications:

Multimodal systems may combine several types of sensitive information.

An organization should assess privacy, data handling, content safety, provenance, and access controls across all supported modalities.

## 6. Generative AI Limitations and Reliability

Generative AI can produce convincing outputs without necessarily producing accurate or verified information.

This is a critical distinction for enterprise adoption.

The quality of generated content depends on the model, its training data, the prompt, the context, the generation configuration, and any external information sources used.

### 6.1 Hallucination

Definition:

A Hallucination is a generated output that contains information that is false, fabricated, unsupported, or inconsistent with available evidence, while potentially appearing plausible.

Example:

A GRC analyst asks an AI assistant to identify the requirements of a cybersecurity standard.

The model produces a convincing control reference that does not actually exist in the standard.

Potential consequences:

- Incorrect compliance assessments.
- Misleading management reports.
- Inaccurate legal or regulatory interpretations.
- Incorrect technical recommendations.
- Poor business decisions.

GRC controls:

- Source verification.
- Retrieval from authoritative documents.
- Human review.
- Evidence-based reporting.
- Clear limitations and disclaimers.
- Validation of regulatory references.

### 6.2 Grounding

Definition:

Grounding is the process of connecting a generative AI model's response to relevant information, evidence, or context from an identified source.

Grounding can reduce unsupported responses by providing the model with information relevant to the requested task.

Example:

An enterprise AI assistant retrieves an approved ISO/IEC 27001 policy document and uses its contents to answer an employee's question.

The response is grounded in the retrieved policy rather than relying exclusively on the model's pretrained knowledge.

GRC implications:

Organizations should assess:

- Source authority.
- Document currency.
- Access permissions.
- Retrieval accuracy.
- Source traceability.
- Whether generated claims are actually supported by retrieved information.

### 6.3 Factual Accuracy

Definition:

Factual Accuracy refers to the degree to which generated information corresponds to verifiable facts.

Example:

An AI system generates a summary of a security incident.

The summary must accurately represent:

- Incident dates.
- Affected systems.
- Observed events.
- Confirmed impact.
- Remediation activities.

GRC implications:

Factual accuracy should be validated according to the business impact of the generated content.

A generated marketing draft and a generated regulatory incident report should not have the same review requirements.

### 6.4 Output Consistency

Definition:

Output Consistency refers to the degree to which a generative AI system produces stable and coherent outputs when presented with equivalent or repeated inputs under comparable conditions.

Example:

A customer service assistant should provide consistent explanations of the same approved service policy.

GRC implications:

Organizations should test whether equivalent prompts produce materially inconsistent answers and establish acceptable output variation.

### 6.5 Toxicity

Definition:

Toxicity refers to harmful, abusive, offensive, or otherwise inappropriate content generated or reproduced by an AI system.

Examples:

- Hate speech.
- Harassment.
- Threatening language.
- Abusive content.
- Discriminatory statements.

GRC implications:

Content safety controls should reflect the organization's intended use, user population, applicable policies, and potential impact.

### 6.6 Bias in Generative AI

Definition:

Bias in Generative AI refers to systematic patterns in generated outputs that unfairly favor or disadvantage particular individuals, groups, viewpoints, or characteristics.

Bias may originate from training data, model architecture, fine-tuning, prompting, or deployment context.

Example:

An AI recruitment assistant consistently generates more favorable descriptions for candidates with certain demographic characteristics because of biased patterns in its training data or evaluation process.

GRC implications:

- Assess training data representativeness.
- Test outputs across relevant groups.
- Establish fairness criteria.
- Apply human review.
- Monitor complaints and outcomes.
- Document limitations and remediation.

### 6.7 Overreliance on Generative AI

Definition:

Overreliance occurs when users place excessive trust in AI-generated outputs without adequate verification, critical assessment, or human judgment.

Example:

A cybersecurity analyst accepts an AI-generated incident severity rating without reviewing the supporting evidence.

The incident is incorrectly classified as low severity.

GRC implications:

Organizations should establish human oversight requirements, user training, acceptable use policies, and review controls for high-impact outputs.

## 9. Summary — Part 1

This part established the foundations of Generative AI, its major model categories, generation mechanisms, modalities, limitations, and enterprise governance implications.

The most important distinctions are:

| Distinction                                      | What must be understood                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Generative vs. discriminative models             | Generative models produce new samples; discriminative models distinguish or predict outcomes                               |
| Generative AI vs. traditional predictive AI      | Generative AI produces content; predictive AI estimates outcomes or classifications                                        |
| Generator vs. discriminator                      | A GAN generator creates samples; its discriminator evaluates whether samples are real or generated                         |
| GAN vs. VAE                                      | GANs use adversarial training; VAEs learn probabilistic latent representations                                             |
| Diffusion vs. autoregressive generation          | Diffusion progressively denoises; autoregressive generation predicts sequential elements                                   |
| Prompt vs. prompt engineering                    | A prompt is the input; prompt engineering is the practice of designing it                                                  |
| Conditional vs. unconditional generation         | Conditional generation uses supplied conditions; unconditional generation does not depend on a specific external condition |
| Sampling vs. temperature                         | Sampling selects from a probability distribution; temperature modifies the distribution used for selection                 |
| Top-k vs. top-p                                  | Top-k limits candidate count; top-p limits cumulative candidate probability                                                |
| Grounding vs. factual accuracy                   | Grounding connects outputs to sources; factual accuracy concerns whether statements are correct                            |
| An output vs. verified information               | Generated content is not automatically evidence or an authoritative source                                                 |
| Generative capability vs. security authorization | The ability to generate content does not establish permission to access information or execute actions                     |

### Core Principle

Generative AI governance begins with understanding what the model can generate, what information influences its outputs, and how those outputs are used by people and enterprise systems. Generated content must never be assumed to be accurate, authorized, safe, or compliant merely because it is produced by an advanced AI model.

## Part 2 — Foundation Models, Model Training, Adaptation, and Generative AI Architectures

## 10. Foundation Models and Generative AI

Foundation models are among the most important developments in modern Generative AI. They provide reusable AI capabilities that can be adapted to different business applications without developing a completely new model for every use case.

A single foundation model may support text generation, summarization, translation, classification, reasoning-like tasks, and other capabilities, depending on its architecture and training.

Understanding foundation models is essential for enterprise GRC because organizations increasingly consume them through commercial AI services, cloud platforms, APIs, and internally deployed AI environments.

### 10.1 Foundation Model

Definition:

A Foundation Model is a model trained on broad and extensive datasets, typically using large-scale self-supervised or other learning techniques, that can be adapted to support a wide range of downstream tasks.

Foundation models provide reusable capabilities that can be applied to different applications through prompting, fine-tuning, additional training, or other adaptation techniques.

Examples of capabilities:

- Text generation.
- Summarization.
- Translation.
- Image understanding.
- Image generation.
- Code generation.
- Information extraction.
- Question answering.
- Multimodal processing.

Enterprise example:

A telecommunications operator adopts a foundation model to support several business applications:

| Enterprise application     | Foundation model capability         |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Customer service assistant | Generate customer responses         |
| SOC assistant              | Summarize security incidents        |
| GRC assistant              | Draft risk assessment documentation |
| HR assistant               | Answer employee policy questions    |
| Software engineering       | Generate and explain code           |
| Marketing                  | Produce campaign content            |

The organization may use the same foundation model across different applications while applying different prompts, integrations, access permissions, and governance controls.

GRC implications:

Foundation models introduce dependencies that must be assessed at both model and application levels.

The organization should establish:

- Model provider and ownership.
- Model provenance.
- Training data information, where available.
- Model version.
- Intended use and limitations.
- Licensing and contractual restrictions.
- Security and privacy requirements.
- Model update arrangements.
- Third-party assurance.
- Application-specific risk assessments.

Important distinction:

A foundation model is not necessarily a complete enterprise AI application. It is a reusable model capability that may be integrated into one or more applications.

### 10.2 Base Model

Definition:

A Base Model is a pretrained model before additional task-specific adaptation, such as instruction tuning or supervised fine-tuning.

It has learned general patterns from its training data but may not be optimized to follow user instructions or perform a particular business task reliably.

Example:

An organization obtains a pretrained language model and adapts it to support internal technical documentation.

The original pretrained model serves as the base model.

GRC implications:

The organization should assess the model's original training assumptions, capabilities, limitations, licensing conditions, and security properties before adapting it.

### 10.3 Pretrained Model

Definition:

A Pretrained Model is a model that has already undergone an initial training process using a dataset before being adapted or used for a specific downstream task.

Pretraining provides the model with learned representations or capabilities that can be reused.

Example:

A language model is pretrained on a large text corpus and subsequently used as the starting point for a customer support assistant.

GRC implications:

Organizations using pretrained models should understand:

- The source and quality of available training information.
- The model's intended purpose.
- Known limitations.
- Licensing conditions.
- Security evaluation results.
- Whether the model has been independently assessed.

### 10.4 Instruction-Tuned Model

Definition:

An Instruction-Tuned Model is a pretrained model that has undergone additional training to improve its ability to follow natural-language instructions and respond appropriately to user requests.

Example:

A pretrained language model is adapted using instruction-response examples covering:

- Summarization.
- Question answering.
- Translation.
- Explanation.
- Structured report generation.

The resulting model is better suited to interactive user instructions.

GRC implications:

Instruction tuning can improve usability, but it does not guarantee that a model will always follow instructions correctly or resist malicious inputs.

Security testing must consider instruction manipulation, prompt injection, inappropriate output, and policy bypass attempts.

### 10.5 Instruct Model

Definition:

An Instruct Model is a model designed or adapted to respond to user instructions and perform tasks expressed through natural language.

The term is commonly used in model naming and product descriptions to distinguish instruction-following variants from base models.

Example:

A provider offers two model variants:

- Base model: primarily pretrained to learn general patterns.
- Instruct model: further adapted to follow user instructions.

GRC implications:

Organizations should not assume that an instruct model has stronger security controls merely because it is designed to follow instructions.

Instruction-following capability and security enforcement are separate requirements.

### 10.6 Domain-Specific Model

Definition:

A Domain-Specific Model is a model adapted, trained, or optimized for a particular industry, business domain, or specialized task.

Examples:

- Healthcare language models.
- Financial analysis models.
- Legal document models.
- Telecommunications models.
- Cybersecurity models.
- Industrial maintenance models.

Enterprise example:

A telecom operator uses a domain-specific model that has been adapted to understand:

- Network architecture.
- Telecommunications protocols.
- Service provisioning.
- Billing terminology.
- Incident management.
- Customer service processes.

GRC implications:

Domain specialization does not automatically establish accuracy, compliance, or security.

Organizations should validate the model against the specific business environment and evaluate the quality and authorization of its domain-specific training information.

### 10.7 Multimodal Foundation Model

Definition:

A Multimodal Foundation Model is a foundation model designed to process or generate information across multiple modalities, such as text, images, audio, and video.

Example:

An enterprise assistant receives:

1. A written question.
2. A screenshot of a firewall configuration.
3. An audio explanation from an engineer.

The model processes the different inputs and generates a combined response.

GRC implications:

Multimodal models may process different categories of sensitive information within the same interaction.

Assessments should consider:

- Image and document confidentiality.
- Audio and voice privacy.
- Video and biometric implications.
- Cross-modal information leakage.
- Retention and processing locations.
- Access control.
- Output accuracy.

## 11. Generative AI Training and Adaptation Techniques

A generative model's capabilities can be adapted through several techniques.

These techniques have different effects on model parameters, operational architecture, cost, security exposure, and governance requirements.

### 11.1 Pretraining

Definition:

Pretraining is the initial training stage in which a model learns general representations, patterns, or relationships from a large dataset.

Depending on the architecture, pretraining may use self-supervised learning, supervised learning, or other training approaches.

Example:

A language model is trained using a large collection of text to learn:

- Language structure.
- Vocabulary.
- Grammar.
- Semantic relationships.
- Contextual patterns.
- General knowledge representations.

GRC implications:

Pretraining requires consideration of:

- Data provenance.
- Copyright and licensing.
- Personal information.
- Dataset quality.
- Data poisoning.
- Model security.
- Training infrastructure.
- Computational resource governance.

### 11.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

Definition:

Supervised Fine-Tuning is a training technique in which a pretrained model is further trained using labeled examples, commonly consisting of inputs and desired outputs.

The objective is to adapt the model to follow particular instructions or perform specific tasks.

Example:

A telecom operator fine-tunes a pretrained model using approved examples of customer support questions and corresponding responses.

| Input                             | Desired output                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| How can I reset my router?        | Provide approved router reset instructions      |
| How can I check my invoice?       | Explain the approved invoice access process     |
| How do I report a network outage? | Provide the official outage reporting procedure |

The model learns to produce responses more consistent with the examples.

GRC implications:

- Verify training data authorization.
- Review the quality of labeled examples.
- Protect customer information.
- Test for biased or inappropriate responses.
- Validate model behavior after fine-tuning.
- Maintain model version history.

### 11.3 Instruction Tuning

Definition:

Instruction Tuning is a training process that adapts a model to follow natural-language instructions using instruction-response examples.

It is commonly implemented through supervised fine-tuning, although instruction-following behavior can also be developed through other training approaches.

Example:

A model is trained using examples such as:

Instruction:

"Summarize the following cybersecurity incident in five bullet points."

Expected response:

A concise summary covering:

- Incident description.
- Affected systems.
- Detection method.
- Business impact.
- Recommended action.

GRC implications:

Instruction tuning can improve task consistency, but it does not replace application-level authorization, input validation, or output controls.

### 11.4 Continued Pretraining

Definition:

Continued Pretraining is the process of continuing the pretraining of an existing model using additional data to improve its knowledge or representations in a particular domain.

Unlike conventional fine-tuning using labeled task-specific examples, continued pretraining often uses an unsupervised or self-supervised objective.

Example:

A telecommunications company continues training an existing language model using an authorized corpus of technical telecommunications documentation.

The objective is to improve the model's familiarity with telecommunications terminology and domain-specific language.

GRC implications:

- Validate the additional training corpus.
- Confirm data usage rights.
- Assess confidential information exposure.
- Monitor changes in model behavior.
- Reassess security and performance.
- Document model lineage.

### 11.5 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

Definition:

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning is a family of techniques designed to adapt large pretrained models by updating only a small subset of parameters or introducing a relatively small number of additional trainable parameters.

The objective is to reduce computational and storage requirements compared with updating all model parameters.

Common techniques:

- LoRA.
- Adapters.
- Prefix Tuning.
- Prompt Tuning.

Example:

An organization wants to adapt a large language model to support internal GRC documentation.

Instead of updating all model parameters, it uses a parameter-efficient adaptation technique.

This can reduce the resources required to adapt and maintain the model.

GRC implications:

PEFT can reduce operational costs, but the resulting adaptation artifacts must still be protected, versioned, evaluated, and governed.

### 11.6 Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

Definition:

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that introduces trainable low-rank matrices into selected model components while keeping the original model weights largely frozen.

Instead of updating all original model parameters, LoRA learns a smaller set of additional parameters that modifies model behavior.

Conceptual explanation:

A pretrained model contains a large set of existing parameters.

LoRA introduces additional trainable matrices that modify selected transformations without requiring full-model retraining.

Example:

A company adapts an open-weight language model to produce cybersecurity risk assessments using LoRA.

The original model remains largely unchanged, while the LoRA adapter provides the task-specific adaptation.

GRC implications:

- Protect adapter files.
- Track adapter versions.
- Validate compatibility with the base model.
- Control adapter deployment.
- Assess whether adapters introduce harmful behavior.
- Maintain a record of the combined model configuration.

Important distinction:

A LoRA adapter is not necessarily a complete standalone model. Its behavior generally depends on the compatible base model and the way the adapter is applied.

### 11.7 Adapter-Based Fine-Tuning

Definition:

Adapter-Based Fine-Tuning is a parameter-efficient adaptation technique in which small trainable modules are inserted into or attached to an existing model architecture.

The original model parameters are often kept frozen while the adapters learn task-specific transformations.

Example:

An organization uses one foundation model for several business functions.

It maintains different adapters for:

- Customer service.
- Cybersecurity analysis.
- Internal policy assistance.

GRC implications:

Each adapter should have a defined owner, purpose, approval status, and security assessment.

The organization must also verify that an adapter cannot be applied to an unauthorized model version or application.

### 11.8 Prefix Tuning

Definition:

Prefix Tuning is a parameter-efficient adaptation technique in which trainable vectors are added to the model's attention context to influence its behavior without updating most or all of the original model parameters.

Example:

A language model is adapted for a document summarization task by learning a prefix that guides its processing toward the desired task.

GRC implications:

Prefix configurations should be version-controlled and tested because changes may affect output behavior and reliability.

### 11.9 Prompt Tuning

Definition:

Prompt Tuning is a parameter-efficient adaptation technique that learns continuous prompt embeddings, which are added to the model's input representation to influence model behavior.

Unlike ordinary prompt engineering, prompt tuning involves training or optimizing learned prompt representations.

Important distinction:

| Concept            | Meaning                                                             |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prompt Engineering | Human-designed instructions in natural language                     |
| Prompt Tuning      | Learned prompt representations optimized during training            |
| Fine-Tuning        | Additional model training that updates some or all model parameters |
| LoRA               | Parameter-efficient adaptation using low-rank parameter updates     |

GRC implications:

Learned prompt configurations should be treated as controlled model artifacts because they influence system behavior.

### 11.10 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

Definition:

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a model alignment approach in which human preferences or evaluations are used to guide model optimization.

A common RLHF process includes:

1. Collecting human preference data.
2. Training a reward model.
3. Optimizing the language model against the reward signal.

Example:

Human reviewers compare different answers generated by a language model.

They indicate which responses are more useful, accurate, safe, or aligned with the expected behavior.

The preference information is used to improve the model's responses.

GRC implications:

- Human feedback quality must be assessed.
- Reviewer instructions should be documented.
- Bias in preference data should be evaluated.
- Reward model limitations should be understood.
- Alignment outcomes should be tested.
- Security behavior should be independently assessed.

Important distinction:

RLHF can improve alignment with human preferences, but it does not guarantee that the model will always be truthful, unbiased, secure, or compliant.

### 11.11 Reward Model

Definition:

A Reward Model is a model trained to estimate the relative quality or preference of an output based on examples of human feedback or other defined evaluation signals.

In RLHF workflows, it can provide a reward signal used to optimize the generative model.

Example:

A reward model evaluates alternative responses based on preference data collected from human reviewers.

It estimates which response better satisfies the defined quality criteria.

GRC implications:

The organization should assess the reward model's training data, evaluation criteria, limitations, and potential bias.

### 11.12 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Definition:

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a model alignment technique that uses preference data to directly optimize a model's behavior without requiring the conventional separate reward-model and reinforcement-learning optimization pipeline used in standard RLHF implementations.

Example:

Human reviewers provide preferred and rejected answers for a set of prompts.

The model is trained to increase the relative likelihood of preferred responses compared with rejected responses.

GRC implications:

DPO does not eliminate the need for human feedback governance.

The organization must still assess preference data quality, reviewer bias, safety evaluation, and model behavior after optimization.

### 11.13 Distillation

Definition:

Knowledge Distillation is a model compression and transfer technique in which a smaller student model is trained to reproduce or approximate the behavior of a larger teacher model.

The objective is to create a more efficient model while retaining useful capabilities of the original.

Example:

An enterprise uses a large language model as a teacher to help develop a smaller model for internal document classification.

The smaller model is deployed where lower latency and reduced computational requirements are important.

GRC implications:

- Verify the legitimacy of the teacher model.
- Assess the data used to train the student.
- Validate the student's performance.
- Evaluate whether undesirable teacher behaviors are transferred.
- Maintain model lineage.
- Assess licensing and intellectual property restrictions.

### 11.14 Model Quantization

Definition:

Model Quantization is a technique that reduces the numerical precision used to represent model parameters or computations.

For example, a model may use lower-precision numerical formats rather than higher-precision floating-point representations.

Purpose:

- Reduce model memory requirements.
- Improve inference efficiency.
- Reduce computational costs.
- Support deployment on resource-constrained hardware.

Example:

A model originally stored using 16-bit floating-point parameters is converted to an 8-bit representation to reduce memory requirements.

GRC implications:

Quantization may affect model accuracy and output quality.

The organization should test the quantized model against defined performance, security, and reliability requirements before deployment.

### 11.15 Model Pruning

Definition:

Model Pruning is a model optimization technique that removes selected parameters, connections, or components that contribute relatively little to the model's behavior.

The objective is to reduce model complexity, storage requirements, or computational demands.

Example:

An organization prunes a neural network to reduce its size before deploying it to an edge device.

GRC implications:

The organization should verify that pruning does not materially degrade performance, robustness, or reliability.

## 12. Model Adaptation Strategy Comparison

Different adaptation approaches provide different benefits and introduce different governance requirements.

| Technique              | What changes?                             | Typical purpose              | Key governance concern              |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Pretraining            | Learns initial model parameters           | Develop general capabilities | Training data provenance            |
| Continued pretraining  | Further trains existing model             | Improve domain knowledge     | Additional data authorization       |
| Supervised fine-tuning | Updates model parameters                  | Adapt to specific tasks      | Training data quality               |
| Instruction tuning     | Adapts instruction-following behavior     | Improve task execution       | Instruction and output safety       |
| LoRA                   | Adds low-rank trainable parameters        | Efficient adaptation         | Adapter integrity                   |
| Adapter tuning         | Adds trainable modules                    | Task-specific adaptation     | Adapter governance                  |
| Prefix tuning          | Learns attention prefixes                 | Influence model behavior     | Configuration control               |
| Prompt tuning          | Learns prompt embeddings                  | Efficient task adaptation    | Learned prompt integrity            |
| RLHF                   | Optimizes using human preference feedback | Improve alignment            | Feedback quality and bias           |
| DPO                    | Optimizes directly using preferences      | Preference alignment         | Preference data governance          |
| Distillation           | Trains a smaller student model            | Model compression            | Model lineage and behavior transfer |
| Quantization           | Reduces numerical precision               | Improve efficiency           | Performance degradation             |
| Pruning                | Removes selected model components         | Reduce model complexity      | Capability and robustness loss      |

GRC recommendation:

The model adaptation technique should be recorded in the model inventory and technical documentation.

The organization should not use the generic term "fine-tuned model" as a substitute for documenting the actual adaptation method.

## 13. Generative AI Model Architecture and Deployment Concepts

Understanding model architecture alone is insufficient. An enterprise must also understand how generative models are packaged, accessed, deployed, and integrated into applications.

### 13.1 Open-Weight Model

Definition:

An Open-Weight Model is a model whose trained parameter weights are made available for access, subject to the applicable licensing and distribution conditions.

Open-weight models may allow organizations to download, host, adapt, or deploy model weights depending on the provider's terms.

Examples:

Organizations may obtain open-weight models for:

- On-premises deployment.
- Private cloud deployment.
- Model adaptation.
- Internal AI applications.
- Research and experimentation.

GRC implications:

Open weights do not automatically mean unrestricted use.

The organization must review:

- License conditions.
- Commercial use restrictions.
- Redistribution requirements.
- Acceptable use policies.
- Model provenance.
- Security vulnerabilities.
- Intellectual property obligations.
- Maintenance and update arrangements.

### 13.2 Open-Source AI Model

Definition:

An Open-Source AI Model refers to an AI model distributed under terms that provide specified freedoms to use, study, modify, and share the model and relevant materials.

The degree of openness depends on the license and the availability of the required components.

The term should not be used interchangeably with open-weight.

GRC implications:

Organizations should assess whether the model's:

- Source code.
- Model weights.
- Training information.
- Architecture documentation.
- Evaluation information.
- Licensing terms.

are available and sufficient for the intended use.

A model with publicly available weights is not automatically an open-source AI model under every definition or licensing framework.

### 13.3 Closed-Weight Model

Definition:

A Closed-Weight Model is a model whose trained parameters are not made publicly available for direct inspection or deployment by external users.

Users typically access it through a hosted service, API, or provider-controlled application.

Example:

An organization uses a commercial AI service through an API without receiving access to the underlying model weights.

GRC implications:

The organization may have limited visibility into:

- Model architecture.
- Training data.
- Internal parameters.
- Training methodology.
- Model update processes.
- Security testing.

This creates a need for stronger supplier assurance, contractual controls, transparency requirements, and independent assessment of available evidence.

### 13.4 Model Weights

Definition:

Model Weights are numerical parameters learned during training that determine how a neural network processes information and produces outputs.

Weights are part of the learned internal representation of a trained model.

GRC implications:

Model weights may represent valuable intellectual property and may expose information about training data or model behavior.

They should be protected through:

- Access control.
- Encryption.
- Secure storage.
- Integrity verification.
- Version management.
- Controlled distribution.

### 13.5 Model Checkpoint

Definition:

A Model Checkpoint is a saved state of a model during or after training, containing information needed to resume training or reproduce a particular model state.

A checkpoint may include:

- Model weights.
- Optimizer state.
- Training progress.
- Learning rate information.
- Other training metadata.

GRC implications:

Checkpoints should be treated as sensitive model artifacts.

Unauthorized access or modification may compromise model integrity or expose valuable information.

### 13.6 Model Artifact

Definition:

A Model Artifact is a file, package, or other digital object produced during model development or training that is required for model deployment, execution, or management.

Examples include:

- Model weights.
- Checkpoints.
- Tokenizers.
- Configuration files.
- Adapter files.
- Supporting metadata.
- Preprocessing components.

GRC implications:

Model artifacts should be included in software supply chain governance, asset inventory, integrity verification, and access management.

### 13.7 Model Serving

Definition:

Model Serving is the process of making a trained model available to applications or users so that it can receive requests and generate predictions or content.

Example:

A company deploys a language model behind an API endpoint.

Internal applications submit requests to the endpoint and receive generated responses.

GRC implications:

Model serving requires:

- Authentication.
- Authorization.
- Secure communication.
- Rate limiting.
- Logging.
- Availability management.
- Input and output controls.
- Monitoring.

### 13.8 Inference Endpoint

Definition:

An Inference Endpoint is an interface through which an application or user submits input data to a deployed model and receives its generated output.

Example:

A SOC application sends a security incident description to an inference endpoint and receives an AI-generated incident summary.

GRC implications:

The endpoint should be assessed as an application interface and an AI attack surface.

Security requirements should include authentication, authorization, input validation, request monitoring, abuse detection, and output handling.

### 13.9 Model API

Definition:

A Model API is an application programming interface that allows other systems or applications to interact with a deployed model.

It may support:

- Text generation.
- Embedding generation.
- Image generation.
- Audio processing.
- Model evaluation.
- Fine-tuning operations.

GRC implications:

API governance should address:

- API key protection.
- Identity management.
- Access permissions.
- Request limits.
- Data transmission.
- Logging.
- Provider dependencies.
- Cost management.
- Service availability.

### 13.10 Hosted Model vs. Self-Hosted Model

Hosted Model:

A model operated and maintained by a provider, typically accessed through a managed service or API.

Self-Hosted Model:

A model deployed and operated within infrastructure controlled by the consuming organization, such as its own data center or cloud environment.

| Dimension                  | Hosted model                                         | Self-hosted model                                  |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Infrastructure management  | Primarily provider-managed                           | Organization-managed                               |
| Model access               | Usually API or managed interface                     | Direct deployment interface                        |
| Infrastructure control     | More limited                                         | Greater control                                    |
| Operational responsibility | Shared with provider                                 | Primarily organization                             |
| Security responsibility    | Shared responsibility                                | Greater internal operational responsibility        |
| Data handling              | Governed by provider service terms and configuration | Governed by internal infrastructure and deployment |
| Model updates              | Often provider-controlled                            | Organization-controlled, depending on deployment   |

GRC consideration:

Self-hosting does not automatically make an AI system more secure or private.

The organization assumes additional responsibility for infrastructure security, patching, access control, monitoring, model updates, and operational resilience.

## 16. Summary — Part 2

This part covered foundation models, pretraining, model adaptation, alignment techniques, model artifacts, and deployment architectures.

The most important distinctions are:

| Distinction                                   | What must be understood                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Foundation model vs. application              | A foundation model provides reusable capabilities; an application implements a business use case                   |
| Base model vs. instruction-tuned model        | A base model is pretrained; an instruction-tuned model is further adapted to follow instructions                   |
| Pretraining vs. continued pretraining         | Pretraining establishes initial capabilities; continued pretraining extends them using additional data             |
| Supervised fine-tuning vs. instruction tuning | SFT is a training technique using labeled examples; instruction tuning is an adaptation objective that may use SFT |
| LoRA vs. full fine-tuning                     | LoRA updates additional low-rank parameters; full fine-tuning updates some or all original parameters              |
| Prompt engineering vs. prompt tuning          | Prompt engineering uses human-designed instructions; prompt tuning learns continuous prompt representations        |
| RLHF vs. DPO                                  | RLHF commonly uses a reward model and reinforcement learning; DPO directly optimizes from preference data          |
| Distillation vs. quantization                 | Distillation transfers behavior to a student model; quantization reduces numerical precision                       |
| Quantization vs. pruning                      | Quantization changes numerical representation; pruning removes selected model components                           |
| Open-weight vs. open-source                   | Open-weight provides access to parameters; open-source involves broader rights and access under qualifying terms   |
| Hosted vs. self-hosted                        | Hosted models are provider-operated; self-hosted models require greater organizational operational responsibility  |
| Model weights vs. model artifacts             | Weights are learned parameters; artifacts include the broader collection of deployment and training files          |
| Model serving vs. model training              | Serving provides inference access; training develops or modifies model capabilities                                |

### Core Principle

A foundation model's governance does not end with its initial development or procurement. Every adaptation, training process, model artifact, deployment method, and version change can influence its behavior and risk profile. Enterprise governance must maintain traceability from the original model through adaptation and production deployment, with clear ownership, security controls, and independent validation.
