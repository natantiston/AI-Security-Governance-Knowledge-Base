# 22.17 — AI Regulation Index

## Part 1 — International AI Regulatory Landscape, European Union AI Act, and Foundational Regulatory Concepts

### 1. Introduction

AI regulation is becoming a central component of enterprise governance, cybersecurity, privacy, and technology risk management.

Organizations deploying artificial intelligence must understand not only how AI systems operate, but also the legal obligations associated with their development, provision, deployment, distribution, and use.

Unlike voluntary standards and frameworks, regulations establish legally enforceable obligations within their applicable jurisdictions.

For an AI Security GRC professional, understanding AI regulation involves answering five fundamental questions:

1. Who is regulated? — Developers, providers, deployers, importers, distributors, and other relevant parties.
2. What is regulated? — AI systems, general-purpose AI models, personal data processing, automated decisions, and related activities.
3. When do obligations apply? — Depending on jurisdiction, system classification, role, risk, and implementation dates.
4. What must organizations implement? — Governance, risk management, transparency, security, documentation, human oversight, and accountability.
5. How is compliance demonstrated? — Through documented controls, technical evidence, assessments, monitoring, and regulatory reporting.

The objective of this chapter is to build a structured reference index of AI-related regulations and legal requirements, with particular attention to their practical implications for enterprise GRC.

### 2. Understanding the AI Regulatory Landscape

AI regulation does not exist as a single global legal framework.

Different jurisdictions regulate AI through different approaches:

- Comprehensive AI-specific legislation.
- Existing privacy and data protection laws.
- Consumer protection and product safety legislation.
- Sector-specific regulation.
- Cybersecurity and operational resilience requirements.
- Employment and discrimination legislation.
- Financial services and healthcare regulation.
- Government-issued mandatory rules and regulatory guidance.

An organization operating internationally may therefore be subject to several overlapping legal requirements.

#### 2.1 Regulation, Standard, Framework, and Guidance

These terms must not be confused.

| Instrument           | Nature                                                          | Legal status                                                 | Example                             |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Regulation           | Legally binding instrument                                      | Enforceable within its scope                                 | EU AI Act                           |
| Directive            | Legally binding EU instrument requiring national implementation | Binding on Member States as to the result                    | NIS2 Directive                      |
| National law         | Legislation enacted by a country                                | Legally enforceable                                          | Spanish data protection legislation |
| Technical standard   | Consensus-based technical requirements or specifications        | Generally voluntary unless incorporated into law or contract | ISO/IEC 42001                       |
| Governance framework | Structured approach to governance and risk management           | Generally voluntary                                          | NIST AI RMF                         |
| Regulatory guidance  | Explanation or interpretation issued by an authority            | Depends on legal context; may not itself be binding          | Regulatory guidance on AI           |
| Code of practice     | Defined set of recommended or agreed practices                  | Depends on legal basis and adoption                          | EU AI Act GPAI Code of Practice     |

Critical distinction: A standard may help demonstrate compliance, but adopting a standard does not automatically establish compliance with every applicable legal obligation.

For example, implementing ISO/IEC 42001 does not automatically establish compliance with the EU AI Act, GDPR, or sector-specific legislation.

EU Artificial Intelligence Act

Adopted legislation

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

The European Union's comprehensive legal framework establishing harmonized rules for artificial intelligence.

The EU AI Act is one of the most significant AI regulatory developments internationally.

It establishes a risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems and general-purpose AI models.

Its purpose includes:

- Protecting health, safety, and fundamental rights.
- Establishing harmonized rules for AI systems in the EU market.
- Promoting trustworthy AI development and deployment.
- Establishing obligations for providers and deployers.
- Addressing unacceptable AI practices.
- Establishing requirements for high-risk AI systems.
- Creating specific obligations for general-purpose AI models.
- Supporting market surveillance and regulatory enforcement.

#### 3.1 Regulatory Scope

The EU AI Act applies to specified actors and activities within its territorial and material scope.

Relevant actors include:

| Actor                     | Meaning                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Provider                  | Develops an AI system or general-purpose AI model, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts it into service under its name or trademark |
| Deployer                  | Uses an AI system under its authority, except where the use is a personal, non-professional activity                                                     |
| Importer                  | Places an AI system bearing the name or trademark of a third-country entity on the EU market                                                             |
| Distributor               | Makes an AI system available on the EU market without being the provider or importer                                                                     |
| Product manufacturer      | Places an AI system on the market or puts it into service together with a product under its name or trademark                                            |
| Authorized representative | Represents a provider established outside the EU under the relevant legal arrangements                                                                   |

An organization can occupy more than one role depending on the activity.

For example, a telecommunications company purchasing a third-party AI customer-service platform may initially be a deployer.

However, if it substantially modifies the system or markets it under its own name, it may assume additional provider responsibilities under the circumstances defined by the Act.

GRC implication: Organizations must identify their legal role for each AI system rather than assuming that purchasing an AI solution automatically limits their responsibilities to those of a deployer.

#### 3.2 Risk-Based Classification

The EU AI Act uses a risk-based regulatory approach.

Its structure distinguishes prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, AI systems subject to transparency obligations, and AI systems with limited or minimal regulatory requirements under the Act.

## EU AI Act — Risk-Based Regulatory Structure

Unacceptable risk

Prohibited practices

AI practices prohibited under Article 5, subject to the specific legal wording and exceptions.

High risk

Extensive obligations

High-risk AI systems subject to requirements concerning risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.

Specific transparency risk

Transparency obligations

Certain AI interactions and generated or manipulated content require disclosure or labeling under applicable provisions.

Minimal or no specific risk category

Limited obligations under the Act

Many ordinary AI applications are not classified as high-risk or prohibited, although other applicable legal obligations may still apply.

Conceptual overview. General-purpose AI model obligations form an additional regulatory dimension and should not be treated as a fifth mutually exclusive system-risk category.

Important: The classification is a legal assessment, not an internal organizational risk score.

An organization cannot simply label an AI system as low risk because its internal risk assessment produces a low numerical score.

#### 3.3 Prohibited AI Practices — Article 5

Article 5 establishes specific AI practices that are prohibited, subject to the precise conditions and exceptions set out in the legislation.

Major categories include:

| Prohibited practice                                | Description                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Harmful manipulation and deception                 | Certain AI systems using subliminal techniques or purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques to materially distort behavior and cause or be reasonably likely to cause significant harm |
| Exploitation of vulnerabilities                    | Certain exploitation of vulnerabilities related to age, disability, or specific social or economic situations                                                                                  |
| Social scoring                                     | Certain forms of social scoring resulting in detrimental or unfavorable treatment in unrelated contexts or unjustified or disproportionate treatment                                           |
| Criminal risk assessment based solely on profiling | Certain individual criminal risk assessments based solely on profiling or personality traits and characteristics, subject to the provision's wording                                           |
| Untargeted facial image scraping                   | Untargeted scraping of facial images from internet or CCTV footage to create or expand facial recognition databases                                                                            |
| Emotion inference in workplace and education       | Inferring emotions in workplace or educational settings, except for specified medical or safety reasons                                                                                        |
| Biometric categorization                           | Certain biometric categorization systems that infer sensitive characteristics                                                                                                                  |
| Real-time remote biometric identification          | Certain real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, subject to narrow statutory exceptions and safeguards                                     |

#### 3.4 GRC Assessment of Prohibited AI Practices

An enterprise should establish controls to prevent prohibited AI practices from entering its technology environment.

For example, a company considering an employee-monitoring AI system should evaluate:

- Whether the system infers employees' emotional states.
- Whether the system uses biometric information.
- Whether the processing falls within a prohibited practice.
- Whether a specific statutory exception applies.
- Whether the organization has sufficient documentation to justify its legal position.

Practical GRC controls:

1. Establish prohibited-use screening during AI procurement.
2. Include prohibited-practice checks in AI use-case approval.
3. Require legal and privacy review for sensitive AI applications.
4. Maintain an inventory of AI use cases.
5. Define escalation and rejection criteria.
6. Monitor changes in system functionality and intended purpose.

### 4. High-Risk AI Systems — Articles 6 and Annex III

High-risk AI systems are subject to the most extensive requirements under the EU AI Act.

Their classification is determined by the legal criteria in Article 6, including certain AI systems used as safety components of products covered by specified EU harmonization legislation and systems falling within the use cases listed in Annex III, subject to applicable conditions and exceptions.

#### 4.1 Annex III — High-Risk AI Use Cases

Annex III identifies several categories of high-risk AI applications.

| Category                                           | Examples                                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Biometrics                                         | Certain remote biometric identification and biometric categorization systems                                                                  |
| Critical infrastructure                            | AI safety components in the management and operation of specified critical infrastructure                                                     |
| Education and vocational training                  | Certain admission, assessment, and educational placement systems                                                                              |
| Employment                                         | Recruitment, candidate selection, promotion, termination, and employee performance evaluation                                                 |
| Essential private and public services              | Certain systems used to evaluate eligibility for essential services, including specified creditworthiness and emergency services applications |
| Law enforcement                                    | Certain systems used for risk assessment, evidence evaluation, and profiling                                                                  |
| Migration, asylum, and border control              | Certain systems used for risk assessment, application examination, and identification                                                         |
| Administration of justice and democratic processes | Certain systems assisting judicial authorities and influencing electoral or voting processes, subject to the defined scope                    |

Not every AI system used in these sectors automatically qualifies as high-risk.

The organization must assess the specific intended purpose and applicable legal conditions.

#### 4.2 Example — AI-Powered Recruitment System

A company deploys an AI application to screen job applicants and rank candidates based on CV content, professional experience, and interview assessments.

This system may fall within the employment-related high-risk category under Annex III.

The organization should assess:

- The system's intended purpose.
- The provider's regulatory responsibilities.
- The deployer's obligations.
- Whether the system's classification is correct.
- Whether human oversight is meaningful.
- Whether personal data is lawfully processed.
- Whether discrimination risks are adequately addressed.
- Whether appropriate documentation and monitoring exist.

#### 4.3 High-Risk AI System Requirements

Article 9 through Article 15 establish core requirements for high-risk AI systems.

| Article    | Requirement                                            | GRC interpretation                                                  |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Article 9  | Risk management system                                 | Establish a documented AI risk-management process                   |
| Article 10 | Data and data governance                               | Assess training, validation, and testing data governance            |
| Article 11 | Technical documentation                                | Maintain technical and regulatory documentation                     |
| Article 12 | Record-keeping                                         | Ensure appropriate automatic logging capabilities                   |
| Article 13 | Transparency and provision of information to deployers | Provide information necessary for proper use and interpretation     |
| Article 14 | Human oversight                                        | Establish effective human oversight measures                        |
| Article 15 | Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity                | Define and evaluate technical performance and security requirements |

GRC implication: These requirements should be translated into control objectives, control owners, implementation evidence, and ongoing testing.

### 5. General-Purpose AI Models — Chapter V

The EU AI Act establishes specific obligations for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models.

A GPAI model is a model that displays significant generality, is capable of competently performing a wide range of distinct tasks, and can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications, subject to the legal definition.

Examples may include general-purpose foundation models used for:

- Text generation.
- Summarization.
- Translation.
- Code generation.
- Question answering.
- Reasoning and other downstream tasks.

Not every GPAI model is automatically classified as a high-risk AI system.

The Act distinguishes general-purpose AI model obligations from the requirements applicable to high-risk AI systems.

#### 5.1 GPAI Provider Obligations

Under Article 53, providers of general-purpose AI models have specified obligations, including:

- Preparing and maintaining technical documentation.
- Providing information and documentation to downstream providers integrating the model.
- Establishing a policy to comply with applicable EU copyright law.
- Publishing a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training.

Certain exceptions and specific arrangements apply, including provisions concerning open-source models.

#### 5.2 GPAI Models with Systemic Risk

Some GPAI models may be classified as presenting systemic risk under the criteria established by the Act.

These models are subject to additional obligations.

Relevant areas include:

1. Model evaluation.
2. Adversarial testing.
3. Assessment and mitigation of systemic risks.
4. Serious incident reporting.
5. Cybersecurity protection.

The Act provides criteria for identifying GPAI models with systemic risk, including specified computational thresholds and the possibility of designation by the European Commission.

#### 5.3 GRC Assessment — GPAI Provider versus Deployer

| Assessment question                                  | GPAI provider                                             | Enterprise deployer                                          |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Is the organization providing the GPAI model?        | Yes, if it meets the legal provider definition            | Usually no when merely consuming a third-party model         |
| Technical documentation obligations under Article 53 | Applicable where the organization is an in-scope provider | Not automatically the provider's obligation                  |
| Downstream integration information                   | Provider must meet relevant information obligations       | Deployer should obtain and evaluate relevant documentation   |
| Training data copyright policy and summary           | Relevant provider obligations                             | Not automatically the deployer's obligation under Article 53 |
| Application-level security                           | Must address risks within its responsibilities            | Must secure the AI application and its integration           |
| AI use-case risk assessment                          | Relevant to the provider's responsibilities               | Necessary for the organization's deployment and use          |
| GDPR compliance                                      | Applies where personal data processing is within scope    | Applies where personal data processing is within scope       |

Key distinction: An enterprise consuming an external GPAI model is not automatically responsible for every obligation imposed on the GPAI provider. However, it remains accountable for its own applicable obligations as a deployer, provider of a downstream AI system, or another relevant regulated actor.

### 6. Transparency Obligations — Article 50

The EU AI Act establishes transparency obligations for certain AI systems and interactions.

These requirements are important because individuals should not be misled about whether they are interacting with AI or whether content has been artificially generated or manipulated.

Relevant cases include:

- AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons.
- AI-generated synthetic audio, image, video, or text content.
- Emotion recognition systems and biometric categorization systems within the relevant scope.
- Deepfakes.
- Certain AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest.

#### 6.1 Enterprise Examples

| Use case                             | Transparency consideration                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI chatbot in customer service       | Inform users that they are interacting with an AI system, subject to the applicable exception |
| AI-generated marketing image         | Apply relevant machine-readable marking and disclosure requirements                           |
| Synthetic executive video            | Assess deepfake disclosure obligations                                                        |
| AI-generated public-interest content | Assess the specific conditions for disclosure of AI-generated or manipulated text             |
| Emotion recognition application      | Inform affected individuals as required by applicable provisions                              |

#### 6.2 GRC Controls

- Define AI transparency requirements.
- Maintain an inventory of AI interactions with individuals.
- Implement disclosure mechanisms.
- Define labeling and content-marking requirements.
- Review third-party AI platform capabilities.
- Maintain evidence of transparency testing.
- Assess exceptions and applicable legal conditions.

### 7. EU AI Act Implementation Timeline

The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, with application dates phased over time.

The phased implementation is important because not all obligations became applicable simultaneously.

| Date            | Regulatory milestone                                                                                                                              |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 August 2024   | Regulation entered into force                                                                                                                     |
| 2 February 2025 | General provisions, AI literacy obligations, and prohibited AI practices began applying                                                           |
| 2 August 2025   | Governance provisions, GPAI-related provisions, and specified penalty provisions began applying                                                   |
| 2 August 2026   | General application date for most remaining provisions, subject to specific transitional rules                                                    |
| 2 August 2027   | Application date for specified high-risk AI systems covered by Article 6(1) and associated obligations under the relevant transitional provisions |

Legal currency matters

As of 31 October 2026, the general application date of 2 August 2026 has passed. However, organizations must still examine the specific transitional provisions, system categories, and any subsequent legislative amendments or implementation measures relevant to their circumstances.

The timeline should not be interpreted as meaning that every AI-related obligation applies to every organization from the same date.

### 8. AI Regulatory Compliance — Practical GRC Implementation

The AI Security GRC professional must translate the EU AI Act into a structured compliance assessment.

A regulatory compliance program should include the following activities.

#### 8.1 Regulatory Applicability Assessment

Determine whether the organization and its AI activities fall within the scope of the Act.

Questions include:

- Is the organization established in the EU?
- Is the organization placing AI systems on the EU market?
- Is the organization putting AI systems into service in the EU?
- Are AI system outputs used in the EU?
- What is the organization's role?
- Does the system fall into a prohibited or high-risk category?
- Does the organization provide a GPAI model?
- Does the organization deploy a third-party AI system?

### 9. Summary and Core Principle

The EU AI Act is a comprehensive legal framework that establishes differentiated obligations based on AI system characteristics, intended purpose, organizational role, and regulatory classification.

The key regulatory concepts introduced in this part are:

- Regulatory instruments differ in legal status and enforceability.
- The EU AI Act applies to defined actors and activities within its scope.
- Article 5 establishes prohibited AI practices.
- Articles 6 and Annex III establish the criteria for high-risk AI systems.
- Articles 9–15 establish requirements for high-risk AI systems.
- Chapter V establishes obligations for GPAI models and additional requirements for models with systemic risk.
- Article 50 establishes transparency obligations for specified AI interactions and content.
- Compliance requires documented classification, accountability, controls, evidence, and continuous oversight.

Core Principle: AI regulatory compliance begins with correctly determining the organization's legal role, the system's intended purpose, and the applicable regulatory classification. Without this foundation, an organization risks implementing the wrong controls, overlooking legal obligations, or claiming compliance without sufficient evidence.
