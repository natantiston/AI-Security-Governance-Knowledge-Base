# 22.07 — AI Risk Terminology

## Part 1 — AI Risk Foundations, Risk Concepts, Classification, and Assessment Methodology

### 1. Introduction to AI Risk

Artificial Intelligence introduces risks that extend beyond conventional IT and cybersecurity risk management. AI systems can make predictions, generate content, influence decisions, automate processes, and interact with external systems. Their behavior may depend on training data, model architecture, inference inputs, deployment environments, and operational context.

For GRC professionals, the challenge is to identify how these characteristics create uncertainty and potential harm, then translate that exposure into measurable risks, accountable ownership, appropriate controls, and documented management decisions.

AI risk management is not simply cybersecurity risk management applied to an AI model. It is a broader discipline covering technical, operational, legal, ethical, privacy, safety, security, and business risks throughout the AI lifecycle.

#### 1.1 Artificial Intelligence Risk (AI Risk)

AI risk refers to the possibility that an AI system, its development, deployment, operation, or use may produce adverse consequences for individuals, organizations, society, or the environment.

AI risks may arise from:

- Inaccurate or unreliable predictions.
- Biased or discriminatory outcomes.
- Unauthorized disclosure of information.
- Security vulnerabilities and adversarial attacks.
- Unsafe or inappropriate recommendations.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Regulatory non-compliance.
- Third-party and supply chain dependencies.
- Operational failures.
- Unintended or unexpected system behavior.

Example:

A telecom operator deploys an AI-powered credit eligibility model to determine whether customers qualify for installment-based mobile devices.

If the model produces systematically inaccurate decisions for a particular customer segment, the organization may face financial losses, customer complaints, discrimination concerns, reputational damage, and regulatory scrutiny.

The risk is not limited to model accuracy. It includes the consequences of using model outputs in a real business decision.

#### 1.2 AI Risk Management

AI risk management is the coordinated set of activities used to identify, analyze, evaluate, treat, monitor, and communicate risks associated with AI systems.

Its objectives include:

1. Understanding the intended purpose and operating context of AI systems.
2. Identifying potential adverse outcomes.
3. Evaluating likelihood and impact.
4. Establishing appropriate risk treatments.
5. Assigning accountability.
6. Monitoring changing risk conditions.
7. Maintaining evidence of risk management activities.

AI risk management should be integrated into enterprise risk management rather than operated as an isolated technical process.

#### 1.4 AI Risk Governance

AI risk governance refers to the organizational structures, policies, responsibilities, and oversight mechanisms used to direct and control AI-related risks.

It establishes:

- Risk ownership.
- Decision-making authority.
- Risk appetite and tolerance.
- AI risk classification.
- Risk assessment methodology.
- Risk escalation.
- Risk acceptance.
- Management reporting.
- Independent assurance.

AI risk governance ensures that technical teams do not make business risk decisions without appropriate authority.

#### 1.5 AI Risk Owner

An AI risk owner is the individual accountable for ensuring that an identified AI risk is appropriately assessed, managed, monitored, and escalated.

The risk owner is typically associated with the business function that owns the affected process or outcome.

Example:

A telecom operator uses an AI model to detect fraudulent customer transactions. The fraud operations manager may own the business risk associated with undetected fraudulent activity, while the AI engineering team is responsible for implementing technical controls.

Important distinction: A risk owner is not necessarily the same person as the control owner or technical system owner.

#### 1.6 AI System Owner

An AI system owner is the individual or function accountable for the overall management of a particular AI system.

Responsibilities may include:

- Maintaining system documentation.
- Coordinating lifecycle activities.
- Ensuring appropriate system access.
- Managing system changes.
- Coordinating risk assessments.
- Supporting audits.
- Maintaining operational oversight.

The AI system owner and risk owner may be the same person in smaller organizations, but their responsibilities should remain clearly defined.

#### 1.7 AI Risk Control Owner

An AI risk control owner is the individual or function accountable for implementing, operating, or maintaining a specific control designed to address an identified risk.

Example:

For the risk of unauthorized access to an AI model repository:

- Risk owner: Head of AI Platform.
- Control owner: IAM Operations Manager.
- Technical implementer: Cloud Engineering Team.
- Independent assurance: Internal Audit.

This distinction supports clear accountability and avoids the common GRC problem of assigning every responsibility to a single technical team.

### 2. Fundamental AI Risk Concepts

Before conducting AI risk assessments, GRC professionals must understand the terminology used to describe risk, uncertainty, exposure, and control effectiveness.

#### 2.1 Risk Event

A risk event is an occurrence that may result in an adverse consequence.

Example:

An AI model unintentionally exposes confidential customer information through its generated responses.

The risk event is the unauthorized disclosure, not merely the existence of the AI model.

#### 2.2 Risk Scenario

A risk scenario is a structured description of a possible event, including the circumstances, causes, and potential consequences.

A useful risk scenario can be structured as:

Threat or condition + Vulnerability or weakness + Risk event + Business impact

Example:

An attacker exploits insufficient access controls in an AI-powered customer-service application, obtains access to customer records, and causes unauthorized disclosure of personal information.

This formulation makes the risk actionable because it identifies the event and the business consequences.

#### 2.3 Threat

A threat is a potential cause of an unwanted incident that could result in harm to an organization, system, or individual.

AI-related threats may include:

- Malicious users.
- Cybercriminals.
- Malicious insiders.
- Compromised third-party providers.
- Adversarial inputs.
- Malicious datasets.
- Infrastructure failures.
- Unintended model behavior.

A threat does not automatically mean that a vulnerability exists or that an incident will occur.

#### 2.4 Vulnerability

A vulnerability is a weakness in an asset, system, process, or control that could be exploited or triggered by a threat.

Examples:

- Insufficient API authorization.
- Inadequate training data validation.
- Weak model artifact integrity protection.
- Unrestricted agent tool permissions.
- Missing security monitoring.
- Inadequate human review.

A vulnerability may be technical, procedural, organizational, or architectural.

#### 2.5 Threat Actor

A threat actor is an individual, group, organization, or other entity that may intentionally cause harm by exploiting weaknesses.

Potential AI threat actors include:

| Threat Actor           | Possible Motivation                               |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Cybercriminal          | Financial gain                                    |
| Malicious insider      | Fraud, sabotage, or data theft                    |
| Competitor             | Unauthorized acquisition of intellectual property |
| Nation-state actor     | Espionage or strategic disruption                 |
| Hacktivist             | Political or ideological objectives               |
| Malicious third party  | Supply chain compromise                           |
| Opportunistic attacker | Exploitation of exposed AI services               |

Risk assessments should consider both malicious and non-malicious sources of harm.

#### 2.6 Attack Vector

An attack vector is the method or path through which a threat actor attempts to exploit a weakness.

Examples:

- Malicious prompts.
- Compromised APIs.
- Poisoned datasets.
- Stolen credentials.
- Vulnerable software dependencies.
- Insecure model repositories.
- Unauthorized tool integrations.

#### 2.7 Attack Surface

An attack surface is the collection of exposed components, interfaces, and entry points that may be targeted by an attacker.

For an enterprise AI application, this may include:

- User interfaces.
- Model APIs.
- Retrieval systems.
- Vector databases.
- External integrations.
- Agent tools.
- Model repositories.
- Cloud infrastructure.

#### 2.8 Business Impact

Business impact refers to the consequences of a risk event for the organization and its stakeholders.

AI-related business impacts may include:

| Impact Category       | Example                                        |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Financial             | Fraud losses or financial penalties            |
| Operational           | Service disruption                             |
| Reputational          | Loss of customer confidence                    |
| Legal                 | Litigation or contractual disputes             |
| Regulatory            | Enforcement action                             |
| Privacy               | Exposure of personal data                      |
| Safety                | Physical harm arising from unsafe AI decisions |
| Strategic             | Loss of competitive advantage                  |
| Intellectual property | Unauthorized disclosure of proprietary models  |

A risk assessment should consider direct and indirect consequences.

#### 2.9 Likelihood

Likelihood refers to the possibility or probability that a particular risk event will occur within a defined period or set of conditions.

Factors may include:

- Threat capability.
- Threat motivation.
- Exposure.
- Vulnerability severity.
- Existing controls.
- Attack complexity.
- Historical incidents.
- Operational conditions.

Likelihood estimates should be based on an approved methodology rather than unsupported subjective assumptions.

#### 2.10 Impact Severity

Impact severity represents the magnitude of the consequences if a risk event occurs.

An organization may classify impact as:

- Negligible.
- Minor.
- Moderate.
- Major.
- Severe.

The actual definitions should be established in the enterprise risk methodology.

For AI systems, impact assessment should consider affected individuals, business processes, critical services, data sensitivity, and the scale of potential harm.

#### 2.11 Risk Exposure

Risk exposure describes the extent to which an organization, asset, process, or stakeholder is subject to a particular risk.

Example:

A publicly accessible AI inference endpoint that processes sensitive customer information may have significant exposure because it is reachable by external users and handles valuable data.

Risk exposure is not necessarily equivalent to a numerical risk score.

#### 2.12 Risk Concentration

Risk concentration occurs when multiple systems, processes, or business activities depend on the same underlying component, provider, technology, or control.

Example:

A telecom operator uses a single external foundation model provider across customer service, internal knowledge management, fraud analysis, and employee productivity applications.

An outage or compromise affecting that provider could impact several business functions simultaneously.

#### 2.13 Risk Interdependency

Risk interdependency refers to situations in which one risk may influence, increase, or trigger another risk.

Example:

A compromised dataset may lead to model poisoning, which may produce inaccurate predictions, which may cause incorrect business decisions and financial losses.

AI risk assessments should identify dependencies rather than treating every risk as an isolated event.

#### 2.14 Risk Velocity

Risk velocity refers to how quickly a risk event can develop and produce significant consequences after it occurs.

Example:

A compromised AI agent with access to customer databases and transaction systems may execute unauthorized actions rapidly.

This may require stronger preventive controls and immediate containment capabilities than a risk that develops gradually.

Risk velocity is a useful assessment dimension, but it should not be confused with likelihood or impact.

#### 2.15 Risk Persistence

Risk persistence describes how long the effects of a risk event may continue or remain unresolved.

Example:

A poisoned training dataset may continue influencing model behavior across multiple deployment cycles until the dataset, affected models, and downstream systems are identified and corrected.

This is particularly relevant when AI artifacts are reused across applications.

### 3. AI Risk Classification

AI risks should be classified into meaningful categories to support ownership, assessment, control mapping, reporting, and management decisions.

No single classification scheme covers every organization or AI use case. A practical framework should be adapted to the organization's AI portfolio and risk environment.

#### 3.1 AI Technical Risk

AI technical risk refers to the possibility that weaknesses in AI technologies, architectures, algorithms, models, or supporting components may result in adverse outcomes.

Examples:

- Model performance degradation.
- Inadequate generalization.
- Model instability.
- Data quality deficiencies.
- Model integrity compromise.
- Inference failures.
- Software dependency vulnerabilities.

#### 3.2 AI Data Risk

AI data risk refers to risks associated with the collection, acquisition, processing, storage, use, sharing, retention, and disposal of data used by AI systems.

Examples:

- Inaccurate datasets.
- Incomplete data.
- Data poisoning.
- Unauthorized data access.
- Privacy violations.
- Data leakage.
- Unlawful data acquisition.
- Inadequate data provenance.
- Excessive data retention.

#### 3.3 AI Model Risk

AI model risk refers to the possibility that a model's design, assumptions, data, implementation, behavior, or limitations may lead to incorrect or harmful outcomes.

Examples:

- Inaccurate predictions.
- Poor generalization.
- Model drift.
- Bias in model outputs.
- Overfitting.
- Inadequate validation.
- Unexplained model behavior.
- Model manipulation.

Model risk management is especially important when AI outputs influence financial, operational, regulatory, or customer-facing decisions.

#### 3.4 AI Cybersecurity Risk

AI cybersecurity risk refers to risks arising from threats that may compromise the confidentiality, integrity, availability, or secure operation of AI systems.

Examples:

- Prompt injection.
- Model extraction.
- Data poisoning.
- Adversarial evasion.
- Unauthorized access.
- Model theft.
- Infrastructure compromise.
- Supply chain attacks.

#### 3.5 AI Privacy Risk

AI privacy risk refers to the possibility that AI development or use may adversely affect individuals' privacy rights or result in inappropriate processing or disclosure of personal information.

Examples:

- Unauthorized use of personal data for training.
- Exposure of personal information through prompts.
- Membership inference.
- Reidentification of pseudonymized data.
- Excessive data retention.
- Inadequate transparency.
- Unlawful processing.

Privacy risk should be assessed independently of cybersecurity risk because a system can be technically secure while processing personal data unlawfully.

#### 3.6 AI Operational Risk

AI operational risk refers to the possibility of loss or disruption resulting from inadequate or failed processes, people, systems, or external events affecting AI operations.

Examples:

- AI service outages.
- Failed model deployments.
- Inadequate operational procedures.
- Incorrect configuration.
- Human operational errors.
- Insufficient monitoring.
- Failed recovery processes.
- Inadequate change management.

#### 3.7 AI Third-Party Risk

AI third-party risk refers to risks arising from the use of external AI providers, models, datasets, software components, platforms, infrastructure, and service providers.

Examples:

- Provider security failures.
- Subprocessor exposure.
- Model provenance uncertainty.
- Supplier service disruption.
- Unclear data processing arrangements.
- Contractual gaps.
- Provider model changes without adequate notification.

#### 3.8 AI Regulatory Risk

AI regulatory risk refers to the possibility that an organization's AI activities may violate applicable laws, regulations, or regulatory requirements.

Examples:

- Incorrect AI system classification.
- Failure to meet applicable transparency obligations.
- Inadequate documentation.
- Unlawful processing of personal data.
- Failure to meet applicable high-risk AI obligations.
- Insufficient oversight of third-party AI providers.

Regulatory risk should be evaluated against the actual applicability of legal requirements rather than assumed solely because an organization uses AI.

#### 3.9 AI Legal and Contractual Risk

AI legal and contractual risk refers to potential adverse consequences arising from legal disputes, intellectual property rights, licensing, contractual obligations, liability, or other legal considerations associated with AI.

Examples:

- Unauthorized use of copyrighted training material.
- Unclear ownership of generated content.
- Contractual restrictions on model use.
- Inadequate liability allocation.
- Disputes involving AI-generated decisions.
- Failure to meet customer security commitments.

#### 3.10 AI Ethical Risk

AI ethical risk refers to the possibility that AI development or use may conflict with accepted ethical principles, organizational values, or societal expectations.

Examples:

- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Unfair treatment.
- Manipulative AI behavior.
- Inappropriate surveillance.
- Lack of accountability.
- Misuse of AI-generated content.

Ethical concerns may overlap with legal and regulatory risks, but not every ethical issue automatically constitutes a legal violation.

#### 3.11 AI Safety Risk

AI safety risk refers to the possibility that AI system behavior or deployment may cause unintended physical, psychological, societal, or other forms of harm.

Examples:

- Incorrect recommendations in healthcare.
- Unsafe decisions in industrial automation.
- Unreliable autonomous system behavior.
- Failure to detect hazardous conditions.
- Inappropriate human reliance on AI outputs.

Safety risk is especially relevant when AI outputs influence physical systems, critical services, or decisions affecting human well-being.

#### 3.12 AI Reputational Risk

AI reputational risk refers to the possibility that AI-related failures, misuse, or controversial outcomes may damage the organization's reputation and stakeholder confidence.

Examples:

- Public exposure of customer data through an AI chatbot.
- Discriminatory AI-driven customer decisions.
- Misleading AI-generated communications.
- Publicized AI security incidents.
- Inadequate response to AI-related complaints.

Reputational consequences may arise even when a legal violation has not been established.

#### 3.13 AI Strategic Risk

AI strategic risk refers to the possibility that AI-related decisions, investments, dependencies, or capabilities may negatively affect the organization's long-term objectives.

Examples:

- Excessive dependence on one AI provider.
- Adoption of unsuitable AI technology.
- Failure to adapt to technological changes.
- Inadequate AI governance maturity.
- Loss of competitive advantage.
- Inability to scale AI securely.

#### 3.14 AI Financial Risk

AI financial risk refers to the possibility of financial loss arising from AI development, procurement, deployment, or operation.

Examples:

- Unexpected model inference costs.
- Denial-of-wallet attacks.
- Failed AI investments.
- Fraudulent AI-assisted transactions.
- Regulatory penalties.
- Legal claims.
- Service recovery costs.

#### 3.15 Consolidated AI Risk Classification Matrix

| Risk Category       | Primary Focus                        | Example                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Technical           | Technology and model limitations     | Model performance degradation                  |
| Data                | Data quality, integrity, and use     | Training data poisoning                        |
| Model               | Model design and behavior            | Biased predictions                             |
| Cybersecurity       | Malicious attacks and compromise     | Model extraction                               |
| Privacy             | Individual privacy and personal data | Personal data leakage                          |
| Operational         | Processes and service delivery       | Failed model deployment                        |
| Third-party         | External providers and dependencies  | Compromised AI supplier                        |
| Regulatory          | Legal and regulatory obligations     | Failure to meet applicable AI Act requirements |
| Legal / Contractual | Rights, liability, and agreements    | Unauthorized dataset licensing                 |
| Ethical             | Fairness and responsible use         | Discriminatory outcomes                        |
| Safety              | Harm arising from AI behavior        | Unsafe industrial recommendations              |
| Reputational        | Stakeholder trust                    | Public AI-related controversy                  |
| Strategic           | Long-term business objectives        | Excessive provider dependency                  |
| Financial           | Monetary loss                        | Uncontrolled inference expenditure             |

GRC principle: One AI scenario may belong to multiple risk categories. For example, a chatbot exposing personal data may simultaneously create cybersecurity, privacy, regulatory, operational, legal, and reputational risks. These should be connected without unnecessarily duplicating the underlying risk event.

### 4. AI Risk Assessment Methodology

An AI risk assessment methodology establishes a consistent approach for evaluating risks across different AI systems, business units, and deployment environments.

The methodology should be repeatable, proportionate to risk, and integrated with the organization's enterprise risk management processes.

#### 4.1 Risk Identification

Risk identification is the process of recognizing, describing, and documenting potential risk events that may affect an AI system or its stakeholders.

Methods include:

- AI system inventory reviews.
- Architecture reviews.
- Data flow assessments.
- Threat modeling.
- Stakeholder interviews.
- Security assessments.
- Privacy impact assessments.
- Vendor assessments.
- Adversarial testing.
- Incident history reviews.
- Regulatory applicability assessments.

Example:

During an assessment of a customer-service LLM, the GRC team identifies a scenario in which indirect prompt injection through retrieved documents could cause unauthorized disclosure of customer information.

The scenario is recorded as a potential AI cybersecurity and privacy risk.

#### 4.2 Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is the process of understanding the nature of identified risks and estimating their level based on defined criteria.

It may consider:

- Likelihood.
- Impact.
- Exposure.
- Threat capability.
- Vulnerability severity.
- Existing controls.
- Control effectiveness.
- Dependencies.
- Time horizon.

Risk analysis should distinguish facts, assumptions, and estimates.

#### 4.3 Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares analyzed risks against defined risk criteria to determine their significance and priority.

It supports decisions such as:

- Whether additional controls are required.
- Whether a risk requires escalation.
- Whether deployment should be delayed.
- Whether management approval is required.
- Whether residual risk can be accepted.

#### 4.4 Risk Prioritization

Risk prioritization ranks risks according to their significance and urgency so that limited resources are directed toward the most important exposures.

Prioritization may consider:

- Potential severity.
- Likelihood.
- Regulatory consequences.
- Criticality of affected services.
- Number of affected individuals.
- Exploitability.
- Risk velocity.
- Existing control weaknesses.

A low-likelihood scenario may still require immediate attention if its potential consequences are catastrophic.

#### 4.5 Qualitative Risk Assessment

A qualitative risk assessment evaluates risk using descriptive categories rather than precise numerical probability estimates.

Example:

| Likelihood     | Description                                |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Rare           | Highly unlikely under defined conditions   |
| Unlikely       | Not expected in normal circumstances       |
| Possible       | Could occur under certain conditions       |
| Likely         | Expected to occur in some circumstances    |
| Almost Certain | Expected to occur frequently or repeatedly |

Impact can also be classified using descriptive categories.

Qualitative assessment is useful when reliable numerical data is unavailable, but its definitions must be consistent across assessors.

#### 4.6 Quantitative Risk Assessment

A quantitative risk assessment uses numerical estimates to evaluate risk, such as probabilities, financial losses, frequencies, or expected costs.

Example:

An organization estimates that a particular AI service outage could result in an average financial loss of €20,000 per hour.

If the estimated outage duration is four hours, the direct estimated financial impact is €80,000, excluding additional consequences such as regulatory exposure or reputational damage.

Quantitative analysis can support investment and risk treatment decisions, but its accuracy depends on the quality of assumptions and underlying data.

#### 4.7 Semi-Quantitative Risk Assessment

A semi-quantitative risk assessment assigns numerical values to qualitative categories to support consistent comparison and prioritization.

Example:

- Likelihood: 1–5.
- Impact: 1–5.
- Risk score: Likelihood × Impact.

A resulting score may be used to assign a risk rating.

However, the numerical result is a prioritization aid, not an objectively measured probability of loss.

#### 4.8 Risk Scoring

A risk score is a value assigned to a risk based on an organization's defined scoring methodology.

A basic model is:

\\[ \text{Risk Score} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact} \\]

Example:

| Likelihood | Impact | Score | Illustrative Rating |
| ---------- | ------ | ----- | ------------------- |
| 1          | 5      | 5     | Medium              |
| 2          | 5      | 10    | High                |
| 3          | 4      | 12    | High                |
| 4          | 5      | 20    | Critical            |
| 5          | 5      | 25    | Critical            |

The rating thresholds are illustrative. Each organization must establish and approve its own risk matrix.

#### 4.10 Risk Inherent Rating

An inherent risk rating represents the risk level before accounting for the effect of existing controls.

Example:

A publicly accessible AI endpoint that processes sensitive customer information may have high inherent risk because of its exposure and potential consequences.

#### 4.11 Risk Residual Rating

A residual risk rating represents the remaining risk after evaluating the effectiveness of implemented controls.

Example:

The endpoint uses strong authentication, authorization, rate limiting, data loss prevention, and security monitoring.

These controls may reduce the residual risk, but their effectiveness must be verified rather than assumed.

#### 4.12 Risk Control Gap

A risk control gap is a deficiency between the controls required to manage a risk and the controls actually implemented or operating effectively.

Example:

The organization requires all production AI endpoints to use workload identity, but one production service still uses a static API key with excessive permissions.

The gap should be documented, risk-rated, assigned to an owner, and tracked through remediation.

#### 4.13 Risk Treatment Plan

A risk treatment plan documents the actions required to modify an identified risk.

It should include:

- Risk reference.
- Treatment decision.
- Required controls.
- Responsible owner.
- Supporting teams.
- Target completion date.
- Dependencies.
- Required evidence.
- Validation approach.
- Residual risk reassessment.

#### 4.14 Risk Monitoring

Risk monitoring is the continuous or periodic observation of risk conditions, indicators, control performance, and changes that may affect the risk profile.

AI risk monitoring may include:

- Model performance changes.
- Data drift.
- Model drift.
- Vulnerability disclosures.
- Provider changes.
- AI security incidents.
- Unauthorized access.
- New regulatory obligations.
- Changes in business purpose.
- Changes to model capabilities.

Risk monitoring is essential because AI risks can change after deployment.

#### 4.15 Risk Reassessment Trigger

A risk reassessment trigger is an event or condition requiring an AI risk assessment to be reviewed or repeated.

Examples:

| Trigger                       | Reason for Reassessment                                |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| New model version             | Model behavior or capabilities may change              |
| New training dataset          | Data quality, privacy, and provenance risks may change |
| New external provider         | Third-party exposure changes                           |
| New business purpose          | Intended use and impact may change                     |
| New tool integration          | Attack surface and permissions may increase            |
| Significant security incident | Existing risk assumptions may no longer be valid       |
| Major regulatory change       | Compliance obligations may change                      |
| Material architecture change  | Trust boundaries and attack paths may change           |

Reassessment should be based on materiality and defined organizational criteria, not only on an arbitrary annual review cycle.

### 5. Practical AI Risk Assessment — Telecom Operator Case Study

Consider a telecom operator implementing an AI-powered customer-service platform using an external LLM.

The platform enables customers to:

- Ask questions about billing.
- Retrieve account information.
- Check service availability.
- Request subscription changes.
- Submit service complaints.
- Receive personalized recommendations.

The solution integrates with Salesforce CRM, an e-commerce platform, billing systems, customer identity services, and external AI infrastructure.

#### 5.1 AI System Context

| Component           | Function                                 | Risk Consideration                                    |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer interface  | Receives customer requests               | Malicious or unauthorized input                       |
| API gateway         | Routes application requests              | API abuse and unauthorized access                     |
| LLM service         | Interprets and generates responses       | Prompt injection and sensitive information disclosure |
| RAG platform        | Retrieves enterprise information         | Unauthorized document retrieval                       |
| Salesforce CRM      | Provides customer records                | Customer data exposure                                |
| Billing system      | Provides account and invoice information | Incorrect financial information                       |
| E-commerce platform | Supports purchases and subscriptions     | Unauthorized transactions                             |
| Identity service    | Authenticates customers                  | Account takeover                                      |
| Monitoring platform | Records operational and security events  | Incomplete security visibility                        |

#### 5.2 Initial Risk Scenarios

| Risk ID | Risk Scenario                                                          | Risk Category             | Potential Business Impact                  |

| AIR-001 | AI produces inaccurate billing information                             | Model / Operational       | Customer complaints and financial disputes |
| AIR-002 | Customer information is exposed through AI responses                   | Privacy / Cybersecurity   | Data breach and regulatory consequences    |
| AIR-003 | Indirect prompt injection manipulates retrieved content                | Cybersecurity             | Unauthorized disclosure or actions         |

#### 5.3 Risk Assessment Questions

The GRC team should investigate:

Business and Governance

- Who owns the customer-service AI system?
- What decisions can the AI system make?
- Which decisions require human approval?
- Has the AI system been registered and classified?
- Has the intended purpose been formally documented?

Data and Privacy

- What customer information is sent to the external LLM provider?
- Is customer data used for model training?
- Where is customer data processed?
- Are retention and deletion requirements documented?
- Are document-level access restrictions enforced?

Security and Architecture

- Is the LLM endpoint protected by enterprise authentication?
- Are API permissions restricted?
- Can retrieved content manipulate model instructions?
- Are AI outputs validated before execution?
- Are customer transactions independently authorized?

Third-Party Risk

- Has the provider undergone a security assessment?
- Are subprocessors identified?
- Are security incident notification obligations contractual?
- Are model changes communicated to the organization?
- Is there a fallback arrangement if the provider becomes unavailable?

Monitoring and Assurance

- Are AI security events logged?
- Are unusual requests and usage patterns detected?
- Are security incidents covered by response procedures?
- Has adversarial testing been completed?
- Are residual risks formally approved?

### 6. Summary

AI risk management provides the structure for identifying, evaluating, treating, and monitoring risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

It requires a clear understanding of threats, vulnerabilities, risk events, business impacts, risk ownership, inherent and residual exposure, and control effectiveness.

AI risks span technical, data, model, cybersecurity, privacy, operational, third-party, regulatory, legal, ethical, safety, reputational, strategic, and financial domains. These categories can overlap and should be connected through a consistent enterprise risk methodology.

For GRC professionals, the most important discipline is to move beyond generic statements such as "AI may create security risks" and develop specific, assessable risk scenarios with identifiable causes, consequences, owners, controls, evidence, and treatment decisions.

### Core Principle

AI risk management is the discipline of translating uncertainty about AI systems into structured, measurable, and accountable management decisions. A defensible risk assessment must connect the AI system's intended purpose and architecture to credible risk scenarios, business impacts, control effectiveness, residual exposure, and authorized risk ownership.

## Part 2 — AI Risk Analysis, Model Risk, Data Risk, Privacy Risk, and Risk Measurement

### 7. AI Risk Analysis and Measurement

AI risk analysis requires more than identifying potential threats. It involves understanding how AI systems behave, how their components interact, how failures can occur, and how those failures may affect business objectives and stakeholders.

For GRC professionals, the objective is to translate technical and operational weaknesses into measurable business exposure.

#### 7.1 Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is the process of understanding the nature of a risk and determining its level by considering relevant risk factors.

In AI environments, risk analysis may consider:

- AI system complexity.
- Model characteristics.
- Data quality and sensitivity.
- Intended and unintended use.
- Threat exposure.
- Vulnerabilities.
- Existing controls.
- Business criticality.
- Potential impact on individuals.
- Third-party dependencies.

Risk analysis provides the foundation for risk evaluation and treatment decisions.

#### 7.2 Risk Criteria

Risk criteria are the terms of reference against which the significance of a risk is evaluated.

They establish how an organization determines whether a risk is acceptable, tolerable, or requires additional treatment.

AI risk criteria may include:

- Likelihood thresholds.
- Impact thresholds.
- Financial exposure limits.
- Privacy impact thresholds.
- Safety requirements.
- Regulatory obligations.
- Business continuity requirements.
- Risk appetite and tolerance.

Risk criteria should be approved, documented, and applied consistently.

#### 7.3 Risk Rating

Risk rating is the classification assigned to a risk based on the organization's assessment methodology.

Typical classifications include:

| Rating   | General Interpretation                                        | Management Response                     |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Critical | Unacceptable exposure requiring urgent attention              | Immediate escalation and treatment      |
| High     | Significant exposure requiring priority remediation           | Management oversight and action plan    |
| Medium   | Manageable exposure requiring planned treatment or monitoring | Defined remediation and periodic review |
| Low      | Limited exposure within established tolerance                 | Routine monitoring                      |

The organization must define specific thresholds and escalation rules.

#### 7.4 Risk Aggregation

Risk aggregation is the process of combining multiple risk assessments to understand the overall exposure of an organization, business unit, or AI portfolio.

Example:

A telecom operator operates 40 AI systems across customer service, fraud detection, network operations, marketing, and internal productivity.

Assessing each system independently may not reveal that several high-impact systems depend on the same external model provider.

Risk aggregation identifies this concentration and allows management to understand the broader exposure.

#### 7.5 Enterprise AI Risk Profile

An enterprise AI risk profile is a consolidated view of AI-related risks across an organization's systems, business processes, and third-party dependencies.

It may include:

- Number of AI systems by risk classification.
- Inherent and residual risk distribution.
- Critical risk concentrations.
- High-risk third-party dependencies.
- Unresolved control gaps.
- Open remediation actions.
- AI security incidents.
- Risk acceptance decisions.
- Regulatory exposure.

The risk profile supports executive oversight and investment prioritization.

#### 7.6 Risk Correlation

Risk correlation describes the relationship between two or more risks whose occurrence or severity may be related.

Example:

A third-party model provider changes its model behavior without adequate notice. This may increase model performance risk, operational risk, customer impact, and regulatory exposure simultaneously.

Correlated risks should be considered when assessing the organization's overall exposure.

#### 7.7 Risk Scenario Analysis

Risk scenario analysis evaluates plausible events and their potential consequences under specified conditions.

For AI systems, scenarios may involve:

- Malicious attacks.
- Model failures.
- Data quality degradation.
- Provider outages.
- Regulatory changes.
- Human misuse.
- Unexpected model behavior.

Example:

A telecom operator evaluates a scenario in which an AI fraud detection system incorrectly classifies legitimate transactions as fraudulent during a major promotional campaign.

The analysis should consider transaction volume, customer impact, operational capacity, financial exposure, and recovery requirements.

#### 7.8 Scenario-Based Risk Assessment

Scenario-based risk assessment uses defined situations to examine how threats, vulnerabilities, controls, and business impacts interact.

A scenario should identify:

1. The AI system and business process.
2. The triggering event or threat.
3. The vulnerability or contributing condition.
4. The sequence of events.
5. The potential consequences.
6. Existing controls.
7. Residual exposure.
8. Required treatment.

This approach is especially useful for AI agents, autonomous workflows, and complex enterprise integrations.

#### 7.9 Threat-Based Risk Assessment

Threat-based risk assessment identifies potential threat actors and attack techniques, then evaluates their ability to exploit weaknesses in an AI system.

Example:

A threat-based assessment of an LLM application may evaluate:

- Direct prompt injection.
- Indirect prompt injection.
- Model extraction.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- API abuse.
- Compromised dependencies.

Threat-based assessment is particularly relevant to AI cybersecurity but should not replace broader assessments of privacy, safety, operational, and regulatory risks.

#### 7.10 Impact Assessment

Impact assessment evaluates the potential consequences of an AI-related event for the organization, individuals, and other stakeholders.

For AI systems, impact assessment may cover:

| Impact Dimension | Example                                 |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Financial        | Fraudulent transactions                 |
| Operational      | Disruption of network services          |
| Customer         | Incorrect service decisions             |
| Privacy          | Exposure of personal data               |
| Regulatory       | Non-compliance with legal obligations   |
| Safety           | Harmful automated decisions             |
| Reputational     | Loss of public confidence               |
| Strategic        | Dependence on an unsuitable AI provider |

A single event may produce several simultaneous impacts.

#### 7.11 Cascading Risk

Cascading risk occurs when an initial failure or incident triggers additional failures or adverse consequences across interconnected systems.

Example:

```
       COMPROMISED AI DATASET
                |
                v
          MODEL POISONING
                |
                v
       INCORRECT PREDICTIONS
                |
                v
       AUTOMATED BAD DECISIONS
                |
                v
        CUSTOMER / BUSINESS
             IMPACT
                |
                v
       REGULATORY AND REPUTATIONAL
               EXPOSURE
```

Cascading risks are especially important when AI systems are integrated into critical business processes or interconnected infrastructure.

#### 7.12 Systemic AI Risk

Systemic AI risk refers to risk that may extend beyond an individual AI system or organization and affect broader interconnected systems, markets, institutions, or society.

Potential contributors include:

- Concentration of AI services among a small number of providers.
- Widespread reliance on shared models.
- Common software dependencies.
- Large-scale AI-enabled cyberattacks.
- Interconnected autonomous systems.
- Shared infrastructure failures.

Systemic risk differs from ordinary operational risk because its effects may propagate across multiple organizations or sectors.

### 8. AI Model Risk Management

AI model risk management focuses on the possibility that a model's design, assumptions, data, implementation, limitations, or use may produce incorrect or harmful outcomes.

It is particularly important when model outputs influence financial decisions, customer eligibility, fraud detection, critical infrastructure, or regulated business processes.

#### 8.1 Model Risk

Model risk is the potential for adverse consequences arising from decisions or actions based on incorrect, inappropriate, or improperly implemented model outputs.

Sources of model risk include:

- Incorrect model assumptions.
- Inadequate training data.
- Poor model design.
- Insufficient validation.
- Model implementation errors.
- Inappropriate use.
- Performance degradation.
- Inadequate monitoring.

Example:

A telecom operator uses a machine learning model to identify fraudulent customer transactions.

The model incorrectly classifies legitimate transactions as fraudulent because its training data does not adequately represent new customer behavior.

The resulting business impact may include blocked transactions, customer dissatisfaction, and revenue loss.

#### 8.2 Model Risk Management (MRM)

Model Risk Management (MRM) is the structured governance and control discipline used to identify, assess, validate, monitor, and manage risks arising from models.

An MRM framework may include:

- Model inventory.
- Model classification.
- Model ownership.
- Development standards.
- Independent validation.
- Performance monitoring.
- Change management.
- Model documentation.
- Issue management.
- Model retirement.

MRM is well established in financial services, but its principles can also be applied to other industries using AI and machine learning.

#### 8.3 Model Inventory

A model inventory is a centralized record of models used, developed, or managed by an organization.

It may contain:

| Field               | Description                                |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Model ID            | Unique identifier                          |
| Model Name          | Model name                                 |
| Business Purpose    | Intended business application              |
| Model Type          | ML, LLM, classification, forecasting, etc. |
| Model Owner         | Accountable model owner                    |
| Business Owner      | Owner of the affected business process     |
| Data Sources        | Training and operational data              |
| Provider            | Internal or external model provider        |
| Version             | Model version                              |
| Risk Classification | Organizational risk tier                   |
| Validation Status   | Validation outcome                         |
| Deployment Status   | Development, testing, production, retired  |
| Last Review         | Date of latest review                      |

A model inventory and an AI system inventory are related but not identical. One AI application may use multiple models, and one model may support multiple applications.

#### 8.4 Model Classification

Model classification is the categorization of models based on their purpose, complexity, criticality, impact, and risk exposure.

An organization may establish tiers such as:

| Tier              | Illustrative Characteristics                                             | Governance Expectations                                  |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tier 1 — Critical | Influences critical services, safety, or significant financial decisions | Strong validation, senior oversight, frequent monitoring |
| Tier 2 — High     | Influences important customer or operational decisions                   | Formal validation and documented approval                |
| Tier 3 — Moderate | Supports business processes with limited direct impact                   | Defined testing and periodic monitoring                  |
| Tier 4 — Low      | Limited-impact internal productivity use                                 | Proportionate governance and basic controls              |

These tiers are illustrative internal governance categories, not official EU AI Act classifications.

An organization's internal model risk tier must not be confused with a legal risk classification.

#### 8.5 Model Validation

Model validation is the process of evaluating whether a model is appropriate for its intended purpose and performs reliably within defined conditions.

Validation may assess:

- Conceptual soundness.
- Data suitability.
- Model assumptions.
- Performance metrics.
- Generalization.
- Bias and fairness.
- Robustness.
- Limitations.
- Intended use.
- Implementation correctness.

Model validation should be proportionate to the model's materiality and risk.

#### 8.6 Independent Model Validation

Independent model validation is an assessment performed by individuals or functions sufficiently independent from model development and implementation to provide objective challenge.

Its purpose is to reduce conflicts of interest and ensure that models are not approved solely on the basis of their developers' own assessments.

Example:

The AI engineering team develops a credit risk model. A separate model risk or validation function reviews the assumptions, training data, testing methodology, performance, and limitations before approval.

Independence requirements should be defined according to the organization's governance model and applicable regulatory expectations.

#### 8.7 Model Performance Risk

Model performance risk is the possibility that a model does not achieve the required level of predictive or generative performance.

Examples:

- High false-positive rates.
- High false-negative rates.
- Poor classification accuracy.
- Inaccurate forecasts.
- Low retrieval relevance.
- Unreliable generated responses.

Performance requirements must reflect the actual business purpose rather than relying on generic accuracy thresholds.

#### 8.8 Model Drift

Model drift is a broad term used to describe changes affecting model behavior or performance over time.

It may arise from:

- Changes in input data.
- Changes in underlying relationships.
- Changes in operating conditions.
- Changes in user behavior.
- Changes in model implementation.

Organizations should define the specific type of drift being monitored rather than use the term as a generic explanation for every model performance issue.

#### 8.9 Data Drift

Data drift refers to a change in the statistical properties or distribution of model input data over time.

Example:

A telecom fraud detection model was trained using historical transaction patterns. Customer behavior changes after the introduction of a new digital payment service, causing the incoming data distribution to differ from the training data.

This may affect model performance.

#### 8.10 Concept Drift

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between model inputs and the target variable changes over time.

Example:

Fraudsters adopt new techniques, changing the relationship between transaction characteristics and the probability of fraud.

Even if input data distributions appear relatively stable, the model's predictive relationship may no longer be valid.

#### 8.11 Model Degradation

Model degradation refers to a decline in model performance or suitability over time.

It may be caused by data drift, concept drift, changes in operational conditions, poor maintenance, or implementation issues.

Monitoring should identify degradation before it creates unacceptable business consequences.

#### 8.12 Model Explainability Risk

Model explainability risk arises when an organization cannot adequately understand or communicate how a model produces its outputs or why it produces particular results.

This may create difficulties in:

- Investigating incorrect decisions.
- Explaining customer outcomes.
- Demonstrating accountability.
- Supporting audit activities.
- Meeting applicable transparency requirements.
- Identifying unintended behavior.

Explainability requirements should be proportionate to the model's purpose, impact, and applicable obligations.

#### 8.13 Model Bias Risk

Model bias risk refers to the possibility that a model produces systematically skewed or unfair outcomes due to data, assumptions, design choices, or deployment conditions.

Bias may arise from:

- Unrepresentative training data.
- Historical discrimination.
- Incorrect labeling.
- Proxy variables.
- Sampling deficiencies.
- Model design.
- Deployment context.

Bias does not automatically establish unlawful discrimination. Legal assessment requires consideration of the applicable law, context, protected characteristics, and actual effects.

#### 8.14 Model Overfitting Risk

Model overfitting risk occurs when a model learns patterns specific to its training data and performs poorly on unseen data.

Example:

A customer churn model performs extremely well on historical training records but produces unreliable predictions when deployed to a new customer segment.

This can result in incorrect business decisions and wasted operational resources.

#### 8.15 Model Underfitting Risk

Model underfitting risk occurs when a model is insufficiently capable of learning the underlying patterns in the data.

It may result in poor performance across both training and unseen data.

Example:

A fraud detection model uses overly simplistic features and fails to identify complex fraudulent transaction patterns.

#### 8.16 Model Validation Evidence

A GRC assessment should request evidence demonstrating that the model has been appropriately evaluated.

Examples include:

- Model development documentation.
- Model inventory record.
- Training and validation dataset descriptions.
- Performance evaluation.
- Benchmark results.
- Bias and fairness assessments.
- Robustness testing.
- Explainability analysis.
- Independent validation report.
- Model limitations.
- Approval records.
- Monitoring thresholds.
- Change and retraining history.

GRC principle: A model should not be approved merely because it achieves a high accuracy score. Validation must establish whether the model is suitable for its intended use and whether its limitations are appropriately controlled.

### 9. AI Data Risk Management

Data is a fundamental dependency of many AI systems. Weaknesses in data quality, integrity, provenance, availability, or protection can directly affect model performance and organizational risk.

AI data risk management addresses the complete lifecycle of data used by AI systems.

#### 9.1 AI Data Risk

AI data risk is the possibility that weaknesses in the quality, integrity, provenance, availability, security, privacy, or use of AI-related data may cause adverse outcomes.

Examples:

- Incorrect training data.
- Incomplete datasets.
- Unauthorized data collection.
- Data poisoning.
- Inadequate labeling.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Inappropriate retention.
- Uncontrolled data sharing.

#### 9.2 Data Quality Risk

Data quality risk arises when data is inaccurate, incomplete, inconsistent, outdated, duplicated, or otherwise unsuitable for its intended purpose.

AI systems trained or operated using poor-quality data may produce unreliable results.

Example:

A telecom network anomaly detection model receives incomplete network telemetry from several regions. The model may fail to identify genuine service anomalies because the underlying data does not accurately represent network activity.

#### 9.3 Data Integrity Risk

Data integrity risk refers to the possibility that AI data may be modified, corrupted, manipulated, or otherwise altered without appropriate authorization or detection.

Consequences may include:

- Incorrect model training.
- Unreliable predictions.
- Compromised business decisions.
- Loss of evidence integrity.
- Reduced trust in AI outputs.

Controls may include integrity validation, access restrictions, audit logging, version control, cryptographic hashes, and controlled data pipelines.

#### 9.4 Data Provenance Risk

Data provenance risk arises when an organization cannot reliably establish where data originated, how it was collected, how it was transformed, or whether it is appropriate for its intended use.

Example:

An organization downloads a third-party dataset to fine-tune an LLM but cannot establish its original source, licensing conditions, collection methods, or privacy implications.

This may create intellectual property, privacy, compliance, and model integrity risks.

#### 9.5 Data Lineage Risk

Data lineage risk arises when an organization lacks sufficient visibility into how data moves through systems and how it is transformed or used.

A typical AI data lineage may include:

```
       ORIGINAL DATA SOURCE
                |
                v
          DATA COLLECTION
                |
                v
          DATA INGESTION
                |
                v
        DATA CLEANING / LABELING
                |
                v
        FEATURE ENGINEERING
                |
                v
          MODEL TRAINING
                |
                v
         MODEL EVALUATION
                |
                v
          MODEL DEPLOYMENT
                |
                v
          INFERENCE OUTPUT
```

Incomplete lineage makes it difficult to investigate model failures, identify affected datasets, demonstrate data governance, or reproduce model results.

#### 9.6 Data Poisoning Risk

Data poisoning risk is the possibility that malicious or unauthorized manipulation of training or learning data will influence model behavior.

Examples:

- Maliciously altered training records.
- Manipulated labels.
- Injected fraudulent transactions.
- Contaminated external datasets.
- Compromised data ingestion pipelines.

Data poisoning can affect model reliability, security, and business outcomes.

#### 9.7 Data Labeling Risk

Data labeling risk arises when training data is incorrectly, inconsistently, or maliciously labeled.

Example:

A fraud detection model is trained using transactions incorrectly labeled as legitimate. The model may learn misleading patterns and fail to detect fraudulent activity.

Controls include:

- Defined labeling standards.
- Quality assurance.
- Independent sampling.
- Label consistency checks.
- Controlled access.
- Documented labeling procedures.

#### 9.8 Data Leakage Risk

Data leakage risk refers to the unauthorized exposure of sensitive, confidential, or personal information through AI systems or their supporting processes.

Potential leakage channels include:

- Prompts and responses.
- Training datasets.
- Model outputs.
- Retrieval systems.
- Logs.
- Third-party APIs.
- Insecure storage.
- Development environments.

Data leakage may create privacy, confidentiality, intellectual property, and regulatory consequences.

#### 9.9 Data Retention Risk

Data retention risk arises when AI-related data is retained longer than necessary or without appropriate justification and governance.

Relevant data may include:

- Training data.
- Fine-tuning datasets.
- User prompts.
- Generated responses.
- Conversation histories.
- Inference logs.
- Agent memory.
- Evaluation records.

Retention requirements should reflect legal obligations, contractual commitments, business purposes, and operational needs.

#### 9.10 Data Minimization Risk

Data minimization risk arises when an AI system collects or processes more information than is necessary for its intended purpose.

Example:

A customer-service LLM receives complete customer identity documents even though the requested task only requires verifying a customer's subscription status.

The organization should limit the data provided to the minimum necessary for the intended operation.

#### 9.11 Data Classification

Data classification is the process of categorizing information according to sensitivity, criticality, business value, and protection requirements.

An illustrative classification scheme:

| Classification | Description                                            | AI Handling Requirement                                            |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Public         | Approved for unrestricted public distribution          | Standard integrity and usage controls                              |
| Internal       | Intended for authorized organizational use             | Enterprise access restrictions                                     |
| Confidential   | Sensitive business or customer information             | Strong access controls and encryption                              |
| Restricted     | Highly sensitive information requiring strict controls | Explicit authorization, enhanced monitoring, restricted processing |

Organizations should align these categories with their existing information classification policy.

#### 9.12 Data De-identification

Data de-identification refers to techniques used to reduce or remove identifying information from data.

Techniques may include:

- Masking.
- Tokenization.
- Generalization.
- Pseudonymization.
- Anonymization.
- Aggregation.
- Synthetic data generation.

The appropriate technique depends on the intended purpose, reidentification risk, data sensitivity, and applicable legal requirements.

#### 9.13 Pseudonymization Risk

Pseudonymization risk arises when pseudonymized data can be reidentified through access to additional information or through linkage with other datasets.

Under GDPR, pseudonymized data remains personal data where it can be attributed to an identifiable individual using additional information.

Replacing customer names with unique identifiers does not automatically make a dataset anonymous.

#### 9.14 Anonymization Risk

Anonymization risk refers to the possibility that supposedly anonymized data may still permit identification of individuals through direct or indirect means.

Removing names and email addresses alone does not guarantee effective anonymization.

Organizations should evaluate:

- Direct identifiers.
- Indirect identifiers.
- Uniqueness.
- Linkage attacks.
- Available external datasets.
- Reidentification methods.
- Residual identification risk.

#### 9.15 Synthetic Data Risk

Synthetic data risk arises when artificially generated data is used in AI development or testing but fails to adequately represent real-world conditions or inadvertently reveals information about source data.

Synthetic data can help reduce exposure to real personal information, but it does not automatically eliminate privacy, security, or data quality risks.

Potential weaknesses include:

- Poor statistical representativeness.
- Unrealistic edge cases.
- Bias reproduction.
- Memorization of source records.
- Disclosure of sensitive characteristics.

#### 9.16 Differential Privacy

Differential privacy is a mathematical framework designed to limit the influence that an individual record can have on the output of a data analysis or learning process.

It uses carefully calibrated randomness to provide a formal privacy guarantee.

Important concepts include:

- Privacy parameter epsilon (ε).
- Delta (δ), where applicable.
- Privacy budget.
- Composition.
- Noise calibration.

A smaller epsilon generally provides stronger privacy protection under comparable assumptions, although it may reduce analytical utility.

Differential privacy is not synonymous with anonymization and does not eliminate every privacy or security risk.

#### 9.17 Data Governance Evidence

GRC professionals should request evidence demonstrating that AI data is appropriately governed.

Examples include:

- Data classification policy.
- Data inventory.
- Dataset register.
- Data lineage diagrams.
- Data provenance documentation.
- Data quality reports.
- Data validation results.
- Dataset access reviews.
- Data retention schedules.
- Data deletion evidence.
- Privacy assessments.
- Data processing agreements.
- Data labeling standards.
- Data poisoning testing results.

### 10. AI Privacy Risk Management

AI privacy risk management addresses the potential effects of AI processing on individuals' privacy rights, personal information, and ability to exercise control over their data.

AI systems may process personal data during development, training, inference, monitoring, and deployment. Privacy risk must therefore be assessed throughout the AI lifecycle.

#### 10.1 AI Privacy Risk

AI privacy risk is the possibility that AI system development or use may result in inappropriate processing, exposure, inference, retention, or disclosure of information relating to identifiable individuals.

Examples:

- Personal data used without an appropriate legal basis.
- Personal information disclosed in model outputs.
- Excessive prompt logging.
- Reidentification of pseudonymized datasets.
- Unauthorized data sharing with external model providers.
- Inadequate transparency.
- Uncontrolled profiling.

#### 10.2 Personal Data

Personal data is information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person under GDPR.

Examples include:

- Names.
- Email addresses.
- Telephone numbers.
- Identification numbers.
- Location data.
- Online identifiers.
- Information associated with identifiable customer accounts.

Whether information qualifies as personal data depends on the circumstances and the possibility of identifying an individual.

#### 10.3 Special Categories of Personal Data

Special categories of personal data under GDPR include information revealing:

- Racial or ethnic origin.
- Political opinions.
- Religious or philosophical beliefs.
- Trade union membership.
- Genetic data.
- Biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person.
- Health data.
- Data concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation.

Processing these categories is subject to additional legal restrictions and conditions under GDPR.

An AI system processing such information requires careful legal and privacy assessment.

#### 10.4 Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)

A Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) is a GDPR assessment required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

It is particularly relevant to AI processing involving:

- Large-scale sensitive personal data.
- Systematic and extensive profiling.
- Automated decision-making with significant effects.
- Large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas.
- Other processing activities likely to create high privacy risk.

A DPIA evaluates:

- Processing operations and purposes.
- Necessity and proportionality.
- Risks to individuals.
- Measures addressing those risks.
- Safeguards and accountability.

A DPIA is not automatically required for every AI system. Applicability must be determined using GDPR Article 35 and relevant supervisory authority guidance.

#### 10.5 Privacy by Design

Privacy by Design means integrating privacy protection into the architecture, design, development, and operation of systems and processing activities.

For AI systems, this may include:

- Data minimization.
- Privacy-aware architecture.
- Restricted data access.
- Appropriate retention.
- Privacy-preserving techniques.
- Controlled data sharing.
- Transparency mechanisms.

#### 10.6 Privacy by Default

Privacy by Default means ensuring that, by default, only personal data necessary for each specific processing purpose is processed.

Example:

An AI chatbot should not automatically collect or retain complete customer conversation histories when only limited session context is necessary to complete the requested service.

#### 10.7 Automated Decision-Making Risk

Automated decision-making risk arises when decisions are made through automated processing, including profiling, and may affect individuals' rights or interests.

Under GDPR Article 22, individuals have protections concerning decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produce legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affect them, subject to specified conditions and exceptions.

GRC professionals must evaluate:

- Whether a decision is solely automated.
- Whether it produces legal or similarly significant effects.
- Whether an applicable exception is relied upon.
- What safeguards are required.
- Whether human intervention is meaningful.
- Whether individuals can express their views or contest decisions where required.

Human involvement should not be treated as meaningful merely because a person formally approves an AI-generated recommendation without exercising genuine judgment.

#### 10.8 Profiling Risk

Profiling under GDPR involves automated processing of personal data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person, particularly to analyze or predict aspects such as performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements.

AI systems may perform profiling for:

- Customer segmentation.
- Fraud detection.
- Creditworthiness assessment.
- Marketing personalization.
- Churn prediction.
- Risk scoring.

Profiling is not automatically prohibited, but the legal basis, transparency, purpose limitation, data protection requirements, and potential Article 22 implications must be assessed.

#### 10.9 Privacy Leakage Through Model Outputs

Privacy leakage through model outputs occurs when an AI system reveals personal information through generated content, predictions, or responses.

Potential causes include:

- Training data memorization.
- Inadequate retrieval authorization.
- Prompt manipulation.
- Weak output filtering.
- Inappropriate access to conversation history.

Example:

A customer asks an AI assistant for account information, but the system retrieves and displays another customer's personal details due to insufficient record-level authorization.

#### 10.10 Privacy Risk Assessment Matrix

| Privacy Risk                          | Potential Cause                  | Example Control                                 |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized personal data processing | Inadequate legal assessment      | Legal basis and purpose review                  |
| Excessive data collection             | Poor application design          | Data minimization                               |
| Personal data disclosure              | Insecure model outputs           | Output controls and access authorization        |
| Reidentification                      | Weak de-identification           | Reidentification testing                        |
| Excessive retention                   | Undefined retention requirements | Retention and deletion policy                   |
| Inappropriate profiling               | Uncontrolled model use           | Profiling assessment and governance             |
| Automated decision-making concerns    | Insufficient safeguards          | Article 22 assessment where applicable          |
| Third-party data exposure             | Inadequate provider controls     | Processor assessment and contractual safeguards |

### 12. GRC Assessment Questions and Model Answers

#### 12.1 What is the difference between AI risk and AI model risk?

AI risk covers the broader range of risks arising from AI development, deployment, and use, including cybersecurity, privacy, operational, regulatory, safety, and ethical concerns.

Model risk is a subset focused on the possibility that a model's design, assumptions, data, implementation, or use may lead to incorrect or harmful outcomes.

A model can be technically reliable while the broader AI application still creates significant privacy or cybersecurity risks.

#### 12.2 Why is model validation important?

Model validation establishes whether a model is suitable for its intended purpose and performs within defined requirements.

It evaluates conceptual soundness, data suitability, performance, limitations, robustness, and implementation.

Without appropriate validation, an organization may deploy a model that produces unreliable or harmful decisions.

#### 12.3 What is the difference between data drift and concept drift?

Data drift occurs when the statistical distribution of model input data changes over time.

Concept drift occurs when the relationship between input variables and the target outcome changes.

Both can affect model performance, but they represent different underlying changes and may require different monitoring and treatment approaches.

#### 12.4 What is inherent risk versus residual risk?

Inherent risk is assessed before considering existing controls.

Residual risk is the risk remaining after evaluating the controls implemented to manage the exposure.

Residual risk should be compared with approved organizational risk appetite and formally accepted by an authorized risk owner where appropriate.

#### 12.5 What is the difference between model inventory and AI system inventory?

An AI system inventory records the broader applications and systems that use AI capabilities, including their purpose, architecture, users, integrations, and ownership.

A model inventory records the individual models used or developed by the organization, including model versions, training information, validation, and deployment status.

One AI application may use several models, and one model may be reused across multiple applications.

#### 12.6 Why is data provenance important in AI risk management?

Data provenance establishes the origin, ownership, collection method, and transformation history of data.

Without it, an organization may be unable to establish whether data is trustworthy, lawfully obtained, appropriately licensed, or suitable for training and inference.

It also makes incident investigation and model reproducibility more difficult.

#### 12.7 Is pseudonymization equivalent to anonymization?

No.

Pseudonymization replaces identifying information with identifiers or other values, but the individual may still be identifiable using additional information.

Under GDPR, pseudonymized data remains personal data where it can be attributed to an identifiable individual.

Anonymization requires a substantially stronger assessment of whether identification is reasonably possible.

#### 12.8 When should an organization conduct a DPIA for an AI system?

A DPIA is required when the processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons.

The organization should assess the nature, scope, context, purposes, and potential impact of the processing, considering factors such as sensitive data, profiling, and automated decisions.

The fact that a system uses AI does not automatically mean that a DPIA is mandatory.

#### 12.9 What is the difference between a model owner and a risk owner?

The model owner is accountable for the management and governance of a particular model.

The risk owner is accountable for ensuring that a specific business risk is appropriately managed.

For example, an AI engineering manager may own the model, while a fraud operations manager owns the business risk associated with missed fraudulent transactions.

#### 12.10 How would you assess model risk before production deployment?

I would first confirm the model's intended purpose, business criticality, architecture, and data dependencies.

Then I would review its training data, assumptions, performance, limitations, robustness, fairness, and validation evidence.

I would also verify that the model has an assigned owner, an appropriate risk classification, defined monitoring thresholds, change controls, and documented approval.

Finally, I would evaluate residual risk and confirm that production deployment has been approved by the appropriate business and governance authorities.

### 13. Summary

AI risk analysis and measurement provide the methods needed to understand, prioritize, and manage exposure across an AI portfolio.

Model risk management focuses on the suitability, validation, performance, limitations, and ongoing reliability of models. Data risk management addresses quality, integrity, provenance, lineage, security, and appropriate data usage. Privacy risk management protects individuals against inappropriate processing, exposure, profiling, and automated decision-making consequences.

These disciplines are interconnected. A data quality failure may cause model degradation; model degradation may produce incorrect decisions; and those decisions may create operational, financial, privacy, regulatory, and reputational consequences.

For GRC professionals, the key is to maintain traceability between the underlying risk scenario, the affected AI asset, the accountable owner, the required controls, and the evidence demonstrating that risk is being managed.

### Core Principle

AI risk cannot be managed by assessing models, data, and privacy in isolation. Effective AI risk management requires understanding how these elements interact, how their weaknesses propagate into business consequences, and whether implemented controls reduce exposure to an acceptable level.

## Part 3 — AI Operational Risk, Third-Party Risk, Regulatory Risk, Ethical Risk, and Risk Treatment

### 14. AI Operational Risk Management

AI systems introduce operational risks because their performance and behavior depend on technology, data, people, processes, infrastructure, and external services.

Even when a model performs correctly during testing, operational weaknesses can cause failures after deployment.

AI operational risk management ensures that AI systems remain reliable, controlled, available, and suitable for their intended business purposes.

#### 14.1 AI Operational Risk

AI operational risk is the possibility of loss, disruption, or adverse outcomes resulting from inadequate or failed processes, people, systems, or external events affecting AI development, deployment, or operation.

Examples include:

- Incorrect model deployment.
- Inadequate change management.
- Failure of AI infrastructure.
- Poor operational monitoring.
- Human errors.
- Inadequate recovery procedures.
- Unauthorized configuration changes.
- Uncontrolled model updates.
- Failure of AI integrations.

#### 14.2 AI Service Availability Risk

AI service availability risk is the possibility that an AI service becomes unavailable or cannot perform its intended function when required.

Potential causes include:

- Cloud provider outages.
- Model-serving failures.
- Network disruptions.
- Infrastructure failures.
- API rate limits.
- Resource exhaustion.
- Denial-of-service attacks.
- External model provider disruption.

Example:

A telecom operator uses an AI chatbot to handle customer inquiries. An outage affecting its external LLM provider prevents the chatbot from processing customer requests.

The business impact may include increased call-center workload, customer dissatisfaction, and service disruption.

Controls include:

- Service availability monitoring.
- Redundant infrastructure where appropriate.
- Provider SLA monitoring.
- Capacity management.
- Business continuity arrangements.
- Fallback service procedures.

#### 14.3 AI Model Deployment Risk

AI model deployment risk refers to the possibility that a model is incorrectly, insecurely, or inadequately deployed into a production environment.

Potential causes include:

- Deployment of an unvalidated model.
- Incorrect model configuration.
- Incompatible dependencies.
- Unauthorized model versions.
- Inadequate production testing.
- Incorrect inference settings.
- Insufficient rollback arrangements.

Example:

An AI engineering team deploys a new fraud detection model without completing the required validation. The new model generates excessive false positives, causing legitimate customer transactions to be blocked.

Controls should include deployment approvals, validation gates, version control, release management, and rollback procedures.

#### 14.4 AI Change Management Risk

AI change management risk arises when changes to AI systems are introduced without appropriate assessment, approval, testing, or documentation.

Material changes may include:

- Model upgrades.
- Fine-tuning.
- Dataset modifications.
- Prompt changes.
- Retrieval configuration changes.
- API integrations.
- Tool permission changes.
- Infrastructure modifications.
- Provider changes.

Example:

A provider updates an external LLM. The organization does not reassess the model's behavior, and the updated model begins generating inaccurate customer-service responses.

A controlled change process should identify the change, assess its impact, determine required testing, obtain approval, and preserve implementation evidence.

#### 14.5 AI Configuration Risk

AI configuration risk is the possibility that incorrect or insecure configuration of an AI system results in vulnerabilities, operational failures, or unintended behavior.

Examples:

- Publicly exposed model endpoints.
- Excessive API permissions.
- Insecure model parameters.
- Incorrect data retention settings.
- Inadequate logging.
- Unrestricted tool execution.
- Misconfigured access policies.

Configuration management should establish approved baselines, controlled changes, periodic reviews, and configuration monitoring.

#### 14.6 AI Capacity Management Risk

AI capacity management risk occurs when an AI environment lacks sufficient computing, storage, network, or service capacity to meet operational requirements.

AI workloads may require significant resources, particularly for model training, inference, and high-volume processing.

Potential consequences include:

- Increased latency.
- Failed inference requests.
- Service degradation.
- Resource exhaustion.
- Unexpected operational costs.

Controls include capacity planning, workload monitoring, resource limits, scaling policies, and performance testing.

#### 14.7 AI Business Continuity Risk

AI business continuity risk is the possibility that an AI service disruption prevents critical business processes from continuing within acceptable limits.

Example:

A telecom operator depends on an AI platform for network anomaly detection. If the platform becomes unavailable, the organization may lose an important detection capability.

Business continuity planning should identify:

- Critical AI-dependent processes.
- Maximum tolerable disruption.
- Manual operating procedures.
- Alternative service arrangements.
- Recovery responsibilities.
- Communication requirements.

#### 14.8 Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is the targeted duration within which a service or system should be restored after a disruption.

Example:

An AI-powered network monitoring service has an RTO of two hours.

This means the organization targets restoration within two hours following a qualifying disruption.

The RTO should be based on business impact analysis and approved continuity requirements.

#### 14.9 Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is the maximum targeted amount of data loss measured in time that an organization can tolerate following a disruption.

Example:

An AI model registry has an RPO of 30 minutes.

The recovery arrangements should aim to ensure that no more than 30 minutes of relevant changes are lost.

For AI systems, recovery planning may need to consider datasets, model versions, configurations, prompts, embeddings, and associated metadata.

#### 14.10 AI Model Rollback

AI model rollback is the process of restoring a previously approved model version when a newer version introduces unacceptable performance, security, or operational problems.

Example:

A new AI model deployed for telecom fraud detection generates excessive false positives. The engineering team restores the previously validated model version while investigating the problem.

Rollback arrangements should include:

- Versioned model artifacts.
- Approved deployment records.
- Compatibility checks.
- Data and configuration dependencies.
- Defined rollback authority.
- Post-rollback validation.

#### 14.11 AI Human Dependency Risk

AI human dependency risk refers to risks arising from inadequate human involvement, excessive reliance on AI outputs, or insufficient human capability to supervise AI-assisted processes.

Examples:

- Employees blindly accepting AI recommendations.
- Inadequate human review of high-impact decisions.
- Overreliance on automated outputs.
- Lack of AI literacy.
- Insufficient escalation procedures.
- Inability to override incorrect recommendations.

Example:

A network operations analyst accepts an AI-generated recommendation to isolate a network segment without validating the operational consequences.

This may cause an avoidable service disruption.

Controls should establish clear human responsibilities, review requirements, escalation mechanisms, and appropriate training.

#### 14.12 Automation Bias

Automation bias is the tendency of individuals to favor or over-rely on recommendations produced by automated systems, even when contradictory evidence exists.

Example:

A fraud analyst approves an AI-generated fraud classification without examining the transaction evidence, resulting in an incorrect customer restriction.

Automation bias is particularly relevant when AI systems are perceived as more accurate or objective than human judgment.

Controls include human review, decision challenge mechanisms, training, explainability appropriate to the use case, and performance monitoring.

### 15. AI Third-Party and Supply Chain Risk Management

Organizations increasingly depend on external AI providers, foundation models, cloud platforms, datasets, software frameworks, and AI development services.

These dependencies introduce risks that may not be directly controlled by the organization.

Third-party AI risk management establishes a structured approach for identifying, assessing, monitoring, and managing these external dependencies.

#### 15.1 AI Third-Party Risk

AI third-party risk is the possibility of adverse consequences arising from the use of external AI products, models, services, providers, or supporting technologies.

Examples include:

- External LLM provider security failures.
- Compromised third-party models.
- Unauthorized use of customer data.
- Uncontrolled provider model updates.
- Cloud infrastructure outages.
- Inadequate supplier incident response.
- Unclear data processing responsibilities.
- Dependency on a single AI provider.

#### 15.2 AI Vendor Risk Management

AI Vendor Risk Management is the process of assessing and overseeing the security, privacy, operational, legal, and governance risks associated with external AI vendors.

It should be integrated into the organization's broader Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM) framework.

The assessment should consider the provider's:

- Security governance.
- AI development practices.
- Model management.
- Data protection.
- Access controls.
- Infrastructure security.
- Vulnerability management.
- Incident response.
- Business continuity.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Subprocessor arrangements.

#### 15.3 AI Vendor Due Diligence

AI vendor due diligence is the process of evaluating an AI provider before entering into a contractual or operational relationship.

Typical evidence includes:

- Security policies.
- Independent assurance reports.
- ISO/IEC 27001 certification, where relevant.
- SOC 2 reports, where relevant.
- Penetration testing summaries.
- Data processing agreements.
- Privacy documentation.
- AI governance documentation.
- Business continuity plans.
- Subprocessor lists.
- Incident response procedures.
- Model documentation.

A certification or assurance report is supporting evidence, not proof that every risk associated with the vendor is adequately controlled.

#### 15.4 AI Vendor Security Questionnaire

An AI vendor security questionnaire is a structured set of questions used to evaluate a provider's security and governance practices.

Example:

| Assessment Domain        | Questions                                                       |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI governance            | Does the provider maintain a formal AI governance framework?    |
| Data protection          | Is customer data used to train or improve shared models?        |
| Data residency           | Where is customer information processed and stored?             |
| Model security           | How are model weights and artifacts protected?                  |
| Access management        | How is privileged access controlled?                            |
| Encryption               | Is data encrypted in transit and at rest?                       |
| Vulnerability management | How are vulnerabilities identified and remediated?              |
| Security testing         | Are AI-specific security tests conducted?                       |
| Incident response        | How quickly are customers notified of relevant incidents?       |
| Subprocessors            | Which third parties can access or process customer information? |
| Business continuity      | What recovery arrangements support AI services?                 |
| Model changes            | How are material model updates communicated?                    |
| Data deletion            | Can customer data be securely deleted upon termination?         |
| Audit rights             | Can the customer obtain appropriate assurance evidence?         |

#### 15.5 AI Provider Concentration Risk

AI provider concentration risk occurs when an organization relies excessively on one AI provider, model platform, infrastructure provider, or technology ecosystem.

Example:

A telecom operator uses a single external AI provider for customer service, internal productivity, fraud analysis, and network operations.

A major provider outage or security incident could affect multiple business functions simultaneously.

Mitigation strategies may include:

- Alternative provider arrangements.
- Multi-model architecture.
- Portability requirements.
- Data export capabilities.
- Fallback operating procedures.
- Contractual exit provisions.
- Periodic concentration assessments.

Multi-provider strategies can reduce dependency but may introduce additional integration, security, and governance complexity.

#### 15.6 AI Fourth-Party Risk

AI fourth-party risk refers to risks arising from entities that provide services or components to an organization's direct AI supplier.

Example:

A telecom operator contracts an external LLM provider. The provider uses a third-party cloud infrastructure company to host its model and another supplier for data processing.

The telecom operator must consider relevant downstream dependencies even if it has no direct contract with those entities.

Controls include:

- Subprocessor transparency.
- Supplier dependency mapping.
- Contractual notification requirements.
- Security assurance.
- Incident escalation procedures.

#### 15.7 AI Outsourcing Risk

AI outsourcing risk arises when an organization transfers AI-related activities or capabilities to external service providers and becomes dependent on their performance, controls, or operational arrangements.

Examples:

- Outsourced model development.
- External AI model hosting.
- Managed AI platforms.
- Third-party data labeling.
- External AI-powered decision services.

Outsourcing does not automatically transfer the organization's legal or business accountability.

The organization should establish oversight, contractual obligations, performance monitoring, and exit arrangements.

#### 15.8 AI Vendor Lock-in

AI vendor lock-in occurs when an organization becomes excessively dependent on a provider because switching to an alternative is difficult, expensive, or operationally disruptive.

Potential causes include:

- Proprietary model interfaces.
- Provider-specific data formats.
- Embedded application integrations.
- Proprietary embeddings or vector services.
- Limited data portability.
- Specialized deployment dependencies.

Vendor lock-in can create strategic, financial, and operational risks.

#### 15.9 AI Exit Strategy

An AI exit strategy defines how an organization will transition away from an AI provider, platform, or service without unacceptable business disruption or loss of control.

It may address:

- Data export.
- Data deletion.
- Model artifact portability.
- Alternative providers.
- API replacement.
- Application migration.
- Credential revocation.
- Knowledge transfer.
- Contract termination.
- Service continuity.

An exit strategy should be evaluated before the provider becomes operationally critical.

#### 15.10 AI Supplier Risk Monitoring

AI supplier risk monitoring is the ongoing review of external provider security, performance, compliance, and operational conditions.

Monitoring activities may include:

- Reviewing security advisories.
- Tracking material model changes.
- Reviewing incidents.
- Monitoring service availability.
- Reassessing subprocessors.
- Reviewing assurance reports.
- Tracking contractual changes.
- Reassessing provider concentration.

### 16. AI Legal, Regulatory, and Compliance Risk

AI systems operate within a changing legal and regulatory environment. Organizations must understand which obligations apply to their AI activities and establish controls to demonstrate compliance.

Regulatory risk should not be assessed through generic assumptions. Applicability depends on the jurisdiction, system purpose, organizational role, data processing, and applicable sector requirements.

#### 16.1 AI Regulatory Risk

AI regulatory risk is the possibility that an organization fails to comply with applicable laws, regulations, or regulatory requirements governing its AI activities.

Potential consequences include:

- Regulatory enforcement.
- Administrative penalties.
- Operational restrictions.
- Mandatory remediation.
- Litigation.
- Reputational damage.
- Loss of customer confidence.

#### 16.2 AI Legal Risk

AI legal risk refers to potential adverse consequences arising from legal disputes, liability, intellectual property, data rights, contractual obligations, and other legal issues associated with AI systems.

Examples:

- Unauthorized use of copyrighted material.
- Disputes over AI-generated content.
- Inadequate liability allocation.
- Unlawful personal data processing.
- Misleading AI-generated claims.
- Breach of contractual obligations.

#### 16.3 AI Regulatory Applicability Risk

AI regulatory applicability risk arises when an organization incorrectly determines which legal or regulatory requirements apply to an AI system.

Example:

A company assumes that its customer-service chatbot is not subject to any specific AI-related transparency requirements because it is not a high-risk AI system.

This assumption may be incorrect. Certain transparency obligations can apply independently of high-risk classification.

Controls include:

- Legal applicability assessment.
- Intended-purpose documentation.
- AI system classification.
- Regulatory horizon scanning.
- Legal review.
- Compliance obligations register.

#### 16.4 AI Classification Risk

AI classification risk is the possibility that an AI system is incorrectly classified under an applicable legal or internal risk classification framework.

For example, under the EU AI Act, determining whether an AI system is high-risk requires evaluating the relevant legal provisions, intended purpose, and applicable conditions.

An organization's internal classification as Tier 1 or Tier 2 does not establish its legal classification under the EU AI Act.

Incorrect classification can result in missed obligations or unnecessary controls.

#### 16.5 Provider Compliance Risk

AI provider compliance risk concerns the possibility that an organization acting as an AI provider fails to satisfy applicable legal obligations.

Depending on the system and regulatory role, obligations may include:

- Risk management.
- Technical documentation.
- Quality management.
- Record-keeping.
- Conformity assessment.
- Post-market monitoring.
- Corrective actions.
- Transparency.

The applicable requirements depend on the AI Act classification and the provider's specific legal obligations.

#### 16.6 Deployer Compliance Risk

AI deployer compliance risk concerns the possibility that an organization using an AI system fails to satisfy obligations applicable to its role as a deployer.

Depending on the system and applicable provisions, requirements may include:

- Use according to instructions.
- Human oversight.
- Monitoring.
- Input data relevance.
- Record retention.
- Incident escalation.
- Workplace information and other transparency requirements where applicable.

An organization may have multiple legal roles depending on its activities and the AI system involved.

#### 16.7 General-Purpose AI (GPAI) Risk

General-Purpose AI (GPAI) risk refers to risks associated with models capable of performing a wide range of tasks and being integrated into different downstream systems and applications.

Examples include:

- Broad downstream deployment.
- Unanticipated use cases.
- Model misuse.
- Security vulnerabilities.
- Training data and copyright concerns.
- Insufficient downstream documentation.
- Systemic risk where applicable.

The EU AI Act establishes specific obligations for general-purpose AI model providers, including additional requirements for models with systemic risk.

A general-purpose model is not automatically classified as a high-risk AI system solely because it is general-purpose.

#### 16.8 AI Transparency Risk

AI transparency risk arises when individuals or organizations are not adequately informed about relevant AI system interactions, capabilities, limitations, or uses where transparency obligations apply.

Examples:

- Users are not informed that they are interacting with an AI system when required.
- AI-generated content is not appropriately identified where required.
- Users are not informed about relevant system limitations.
- Documentation does not explain intended use.

Transparency requirements differ according to the applicable legal provision and system context.

#### 16.9 AI Documentation Risk

AI documentation risk arises when required information about an AI system is incomplete, inaccurate, unavailable, or insufficiently maintained.

Documentation may include:

- Intended purpose.
- Architecture.
- Training and validation information.
- Data governance records.
- Risk assessments.
- Technical specifications.
- Human oversight procedures.
- Monitoring arrangements.
- Change records.
- Incident records.

Inadequate documentation can weaken compliance, auditability, and accountability.

#### 16.10 AI Intellectual Property Risk

AI intellectual property risk arises when AI development or use infringes intellectual property rights or creates uncertainty regarding ownership and permitted use.

Examples:

- Unauthorized use of copyrighted training material.
- Use of models outside license restrictions.
- Reproduction of protected content.
- Unclear ownership of fine-tuned models.
- Inappropriate disclosure of proprietary information.
- Contractual restrictions on generated outputs.

Controls should include legal review, license management, data provenance, supplier due diligence, and contractual safeguards.

#### 16.11 AI Contractual Risk

AI contractual risk arises when agreements with AI providers, customers, or partners fail to allocate responsibilities and obligations appropriately.

Relevant contractual provisions may address:

- Data ownership.
- Data processing.
- Data retention.
- Confidentiality.
- Security controls.
- Incident notification.
- Subprocessors.
- Intellectual property.
- Model changes.
- Service availability.
- Audit rights.
- Liability.
- Termination and exit.

### 17. AI Ethical Risk and Fairness Risk

AI systems can create ethical concerns when their design or deployment produces unfair, discriminatory, manipulative, or socially harmful outcomes.

Ethical risk management evaluates whether AI use is consistent with organizational values, accepted ethical principles, and applicable legal requirements.

#### 17.1 AI Ethical Risk

AI ethical risk is the possibility that AI development or use conflicts with accepted ethical principles, organizational values, or societal expectations.

Examples:

- Unfair treatment.
- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Manipulative behavior.
- Inappropriate surveillance.
- Lack of accountability.
- Misuse of AI-generated information.
- Inadequate transparency.

Ethical risk may exist even when the organization has not violated a specific law.

#### 17.2 AI Fairness Risk

AI fairness risk is the possibility that an AI system produces unjustified or systematically unequal outcomes for individuals or groups.

Example:

A telecom operator uses an AI model to determine customer eligibility for special offers. The model consistently excludes a particular customer segment because historical data reflects biased marketing practices.

Fairness must be evaluated in the context of the specific use case, affected population, relevant outcomes, and applicable requirements.

#### 17.3 Algorithmic Bias

Algorithmic bias refers to systematic patterns in an algorithm's outputs that may produce unfair or undesirable outcomes.

Bias may originate from:

- Historical data.
- Sampling methods.
- Labeling decisions.
- Feature selection.
- Model design.
- Proxy variables.
- Deployment conditions.

Not every statistical difference establishes unfairness or discrimination. The assessment must consider context, impact, and relevant fairness criteria.

#### 17.4 Discrimination Risk

AI discrimination risk is the possibility that AI-driven processing results in unlawful or unjustified discriminatory treatment.

Example:

An AI recruitment model systematically disadvantages qualified candidates based on characteristics that are legally protected in the relevant jurisdiction.

Controls include:

- Appropriate data governance.
- Bias testing.
- Fairness evaluation.
- Human oversight.
- Documented decision criteria.
- Monitoring.
- Complaint and appeal processes.

#### 17.5 Representativeness Risk

Representativeness risk arises when the data used to train or evaluate an AI system does not adequately represent the population or conditions in which the model will operate.

Example:

A telecom customer churn model is trained primarily on urban customers but deployed across both urban and rural regions.

The model may produce less reliable predictions for rural customers because their usage patterns and service conditions are inadequately represented.

#### 17.6 Proxy Discrimination Risk

Proxy discrimination risk occurs when seemingly neutral variables indirectly represent sensitive or protected characteristics and contribute to unfair outcomes.

Example:

An AI model uses geographic location as a predictor of customer eligibility. If location strongly correlates with a protected characteristic, the model may produce discriminatory effects even without directly using that characteristic.

#### 17.7 Explainability Risk

AI explainability risk arises when an organization cannot provide sufficient explanation of AI outputs or decisions for the needs of relevant stakeholders.

Potential consequences include:

- Inability to investigate incorrect decisions.
- Reduced accountability.
- Difficulty resolving customer complaints.
- Regulatory concerns.
- Lack of management confidence.

Explainability requirements should be proportionate to the system's intended purpose and impact.

#### 17.8 Transparency Risk

AI transparency risk concerns whether relevant stakeholders have sufficient information about AI system capabilities, limitations, intended purpose, and use.

Transparency is not identical to explainability:

- Transparency concerns making relevant information available.
- Explainability concerns understanding how or why an output or decision was produced.

A system may provide extensive documentation while remaining difficult to explain at the individual decision level.

#### 17.9 Human Oversight Risk

Human oversight risk arises when human supervision is absent, ineffective, insufficiently independent, or unable to intervene appropriately.

Example:

A telecom operator deploys an AI system to recommend network isolation actions. The operations team approves every recommendation automatically because it lacks the technical capacity to challenge the system.

The nominal presence of a human reviewer does not establish meaningful oversight.

Controls should define:

- Reviewer competence.
- Authority to intervene.
- Access to relevant information.
- Review criteria.
- Escalation paths.
- Override mechanisms.
- Evidence of actual review.

#### 17.10 Accountability Risk

AI accountability risk arises when responsibility for AI-related decisions, outcomes, or failures is unclear or cannot be demonstrated.

Example:

An organization cannot establish whether the business owner, AI engineering team, or external provider was responsible for approving a model used in customer eligibility decisions.

A defined RACI, decision log, system inventory, and risk ownership structure help reduce accountability gaps.

### 18. AI Safety and Societal Risk

AI safety risk concerns the potential for AI system behavior or deployment to cause harm, including physical, psychological, economic, or societal harm.

Safety risk is particularly important when AI systems influence critical infrastructure, industrial environments, healthcare, transportation, or other consequential activities.

#### 18.1 AI Safety Risk

AI safety risk is the possibility that an AI system's behavior, outputs, or deployment may result in unintended harm to individuals, organizations, or society.

Examples:

- Unsafe industrial recommendations.
- Incorrect medical decision support.
- Autonomous system failures.
- Misleading safety instructions.
- Failure to identify hazardous conditions.
- Inadequate safeguards for consequential decisions.

#### 18.2 Reliability Risk

AI reliability risk refers to the possibility that an AI system fails to perform consistently and dependably under its intended operating conditions.

Examples:

- Inconsistent model outputs.
- Unstable predictions.
- Unexpected failures.
- Inadequate performance under changing conditions.
- Unreliable integration with external systems.

Reliability is related to safety but is not identical to it. An unreliable system may create safety risk when its failures can cause harm.

#### 18.3 Robustness Risk

AI robustness risk arises when an AI system cannot maintain acceptable performance under variations, disturbances, or challenging operating conditions.

Examples:

- Poor performance on unusual inputs.
- Sensitivity to minor input changes.
- Failure under adversarial conditions.
- Poor generalization to new environments.
- Inadequate handling of unexpected situations.

#### 18.4 AI Misuse Risk

AI misuse risk refers to the possibility that AI capabilities are used for purposes that are unauthorized, harmful, unethical, or inconsistent with approved use.

Examples:

- AI-assisted fraud.
- Malicious content generation.
- Unauthorized surveillance.
- Automated social engineering.
- Misuse of agentic capabilities.
- Unauthorized decision automation.

Misuse may arise from external threat actors, insiders, or legitimate users acting outside approved boundaries.

#### 18.5 AI Misalignment Risk

AI misalignment risk refers to the possibility that an AI system's behavior does not adequately correspond to its intended objectives, constraints, or human expectations.

Example:

An AI optimization system is instructed to reduce customer-service handling time. It begins recommending premature case closures to improve the measured metric, even though customer issues remain unresolved.

This illustrates a mismatch between the measured objective and the actual business objective.

#### 18.6 Specification Gaming

Specification gaming occurs when an AI system achieves a formally defined objective through behavior that satisfies the specification but violates the intended purpose.

Example:

A network optimization model is rewarded for reducing reported congestion. It learns to suppress or ignore certain congestion alerts rather than improve actual network performance.

This may create misleading performance indicators and operational harm.

#### 18.7 Reward Hacking

Reward hacking occurs when an AI system exploits weaknesses in its reward function or evaluation mechanism to obtain a high reward without achieving the intended objective.

It is particularly relevant to reinforcement learning and systems optimized through feedback signals.

Reward hacking may produce behavior that appears successful according to a metric but is undesirable in the real operational environment.

#### 18.8 Societal Risk

AI societal risk refers to potential adverse consequences affecting communities, institutions, markets, or society more broadly.

Examples:

- Large-scale misinformation.
- Manipulation of public opinion.
- Discriminatory allocation of opportunities.
- Economic disruption.
- Widespread surveillance.
- Concentration of technological power.

Societal risk is broader than the risk associated with a single enterprise AI application.

#### 18.9 Catastrophic Risk

Catastrophic risk refers to the possibility of extremely severe harm, potentially affecting large populations, critical infrastructure, or society at scale.

The term should be used carefully and only where the severity and scope of the scenario justify it.

Not every AI failure, model error, or security incident represents a catastrophic risk.

### 21. GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

#### 21.1 How would you assess third-party AI risk?

I would begin by identifying the provider's role in the AI architecture and determining what data, models, services, and business processes depend on it.

I would assess security governance, data processing, model provenance, access controls, infrastructure security, incident response, subprocessors, resilience, contractual obligations, and regulatory responsibilities.

I would also evaluate provider concentration, model change notification, portability, and exit arrangements.

Finally, I would document the findings, assign owners, identify control gaps, and establish ongoing supplier monitoring.

#### 21.2 What is the difference between AI operational risk and AI model risk?

AI operational risk concerns failures in processes, people, systems, and external events affecting AI operations.

AI model risk concerns incorrect or inappropriate model behavior resulting from model design, assumptions, data, implementation, or use.

For example, an AI service outage is an operational risk, whereas a fraud detection model producing unreliable predictions is a model risk.

#### 21.3 How do you manage AI provider concentration risk?

I would first identify critical AI services and map their dependencies on providers, infrastructure, models, and supporting components.

Then I would evaluate the business impact of provider disruption and determine whether alternative models, providers, or fallback arrangements are available.

I would also assess portability, contractual exit rights, dependency concentration, and business continuity requirements.

The goal is to prevent a single provider failure from creating an unacceptable enterprise-wide disruption.

#### 21.4 What is the difference between AI transparency and explainability?

Transparency concerns whether relevant information about an AI system, its use, capabilities, limitations, and interactions is available to stakeholders.

Explainability concerns whether the reasons or mechanisms behind particular outputs or decisions can be understood.

A system may be transparent about its purpose and limitations while still being difficult to explain at the individual prediction level.

#### 21.5 What is automation bias, and how would you manage it?

Automation bias is the tendency of individuals to over-rely on automated recommendations, even when contradictory evidence exists.

I would establish meaningful human oversight, ensure reviewers have appropriate competence, define decision challenge procedures, and monitor whether human reviewers genuinely evaluate AI recommendations.

For high-impact decisions, I would also define mandatory approval and escalation requirements.

#### 21.6 What is the difference between AI risk appetite and risk tolerance?

Risk appetite represents the amount and type of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives.

Risk tolerance establishes more specific acceptable boundaries around that appetite.

For example, an organization may have a low appetite for customer data exposure and establish a tolerance that no unresolved critical access-control vulnerability can remain in a production AI system.

#### 21.7 How would you assess AI regulatory risk?

I would identify the applicable jurisdictions, intended purpose, AI system classification, organizational role, data processing activities, and sector-specific requirements.

Then I would map applicable obligations to controls, assign accountable owners, identify compliance gaps, and maintain evidence.

I would involve Legal and Compliance to validate legal interpretations rather than making regulatory classification decisions solely from a technical perspective.

#### 21.8 Why is a fallback strategy important for AI systems?

AI systems may depend on external providers, cloud infrastructure, models, and complex integrations.

A fallback strategy ensures that critical business processes can continue when the AI service becomes unavailable or unreliable.

Depending on the business process, this may involve a previous validated model, an alternative provider, manual processing, or a conventional non-AI workflow.

#### 21.9 How would you assess human oversight in an AI system?

I would examine whether human reviewers have the authority, competence, information, and time required to evaluate AI outputs.

I would also verify whether they can reject recommendations, intervene, escalate concerns, and document their decisions.

A human approval step that is routinely automatic does not demonstrate effective oversight.

#### 21.10 What is the difference between AI ethical risk and AI regulatory risk?

AI ethical risk concerns whether AI use aligns with accepted ethical principles, organizational values, and societal expectations.

AI regulatory risk concerns compliance with applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

An AI system may create ethical concerns without necessarily violating a specific law, although ethical issues may also create regulatory or legal exposure.

### 22. Summary

AI risk management must account for the complete environment in which AI systems operate.

Operational risks affect service availability, deployment, configuration, change management, resilience, and human oversight. Third-party risks arise from external models, providers, infrastructure, software dependencies, and subcontractors. Legal and regulatory risks concern the organization's applicable obligations, while ethical and safety risks address potential harm, unfairness, and unintended consequences.

These risks frequently interact. A provider outage may become an operational incident; a model update may create performance degradation; inadequate human oversight may allow incorrect decisions to affect customers; and those outcomes may create financial, legal, regulatory, and reputational consequences.

For GRC professionals, the objective is to establish an integrated risk management approach that connects these exposures to clear ownership, proportionate controls, evidence, treatment plans, and continuous monitoring.

### Core Principle

AI risk management must extend beyond the model and its technical controls. The organization must govern the operational environment, external dependencies, legal obligations, human decisions, and potential societal consequences that determine how AI systems affect the business and its stakeholders.

### 23. AI Risk Governance Frameworks and Standards

AI risk management requires a structured governance approach that connects organizational objectives, AI system characteristics, risk assessment, control implementation, and management oversight.

Several international standards and frameworks provide guidance for managing AI risks. They differ in purpose, scope, and implementation approach.

GRC professionals must understand how to use these references together without confusing a management-system standard, a risk management framework, a technical security resource, and a regulatory requirement.

#### 23.1 NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF)

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides a voluntary framework for incorporating trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, deployment, and use of AI systems.

It consists of four core functions:

| Function | Objective                                                                 | GRC Application                                           |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Govern   | Establish organizational policies, accountability, culture, and oversight | AI governance structure and accountability                |
| Map      | Understand context, intended purpose, stakeholders, and risks             | AI inventory, context assessment, and risk identification |
| Measure  | Analyze and evaluate AI risks                                             | Risk measurement, testing, and evaluation                 |
| Manage   | Prioritize and address identified risks                                   | Risk treatment, monitoring, and response                  |

The functions are interconnected and should operate continuously rather than as a strictly linear sequence.

GRC application: Use AI RMF to establish an enterprise AI risk management methodology and integrate AI risk into existing enterprise risk management processes.

#### 23.2 NIST AI RMF Trustworthiness Characteristics

NIST AI RMF identifies characteristics that contribute to trustworthy AI systems.

| Characteristic                 | Meaning                                                          | Example Risk                        |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Valid and Reliable             | Performs consistently and appropriately for its intended purpose | Incorrect model predictions         |
| Safe                           | Avoids causing unacceptable harm                                 | Unsafe automated recommendations    |
| Secure and Resilient           | Protects against threats and recovers from adverse events        | Model compromise                    |
| Accountable and Transparent    | Establishes responsibility and provides appropriate transparency | Unclear decision ownership          |
| Explainable and Interpretable  | Supports appropriate understanding of AI outputs and behavior    | Unexplained consequential decisions |
| Privacy-Enhanced               | Protects individuals' privacy                                    | Personal information disclosure     |
| Fair with Harmful Bias Managed | Addresses harmful bias and unjustified disparities               | Discriminatory outcomes             |

These characteristics should be considered in the context of the specific AI system and its intended use.

#### 23.3 NIST AI 600-1 — Generative AI Profile

The NIST AI 600-1 Generative AI Profile provides additional risk management guidance for generative AI.

It addresses risk considerations such as:

- Confabulation.
- Data privacy.
- Information security.
- Harmful bias.
- Human-AI configuration.
- Intellectual property.
- Information integrity.
- Value chain and component integration.

GRC application: Use this profile to supplement general AI risk assessments when evaluating LLMs, generative AI applications, RAG systems, and AI agents.

#### 23.4 ISO/IEC 42001 — Artificial Intelligence Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

It provides an organizational management-system approach to AI governance.

Relevant areas include:

- AI policy.

- AI risk management.
- AI system impact assessment.
- Operational planning and control.
- Performance evaluation.
- Internal audit.

- Continual improvement.

GRC application: Use ISO/IEC 42001 to establish an auditable AI governance management system, incorporating AI risk management and security requirements.

#### 23.5 ISO/IEC 23894 — AI Risk Management

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on managing risks associated with AI.

It supports the integration of AI risk management into organizational risk management activities.

Relevant concepts include:

- Context establishment.
- Risk identification.
- Risk analysis.
- Risk evaluation.
- Risk treatment.
- Risk monitoring.
- Risk communication.

GRC application: Use ISO/IEC 23894 to structure AI risk assessment and treatment methodologies.

#### 23.6 ISO/IEC 27001 — Information Security Management

ISO/IEC 27001 specifies requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS).

It provides an established foundation for managing information security risks affecting AI systems.

Relevant areas include:

- Information security risk assessment.
- Asset management.
- Access control.
- Supplier relationships.
- Secure development.
- Information protection.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Incident management.
- Business continuity.

GRC application: Integrate AI systems into the organization's ISMS scope and apply relevant security controls according to risk.

#### 23.7 ISO/IEC 27701 — Privacy Information Management

ISO/IEC 27701 specifies requirements and provides guidance for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a Privacy Information Management System (PIMS).

It is relevant when AI systems process personally identifiable information.

GRC application: Use privacy management controls to address AI data processing, privacy risks, information rights, and accountability.

#### 23.8 ISO/IEC 38507 — Governance Implications of the Use of AI by Organizations

ISO/IEC 38507 provides guidance to governing bodies on the governance implications of using AI within organizations.

It addresses how governing bodies should oversee AI use and integrate AI considerations into organizational governance.

GRC application: Use it to support board-level oversight, accountability, strategic alignment, and governance responsibilities relating to AI adoption.

#### 23.9 ISO/IEC 22989 — AI Concepts and Terminology

ISO/IEC 22989 establishes concepts and terminology related to AI.

It supports a common understanding of AI concepts across organizations, stakeholders, and technical disciplines.

GRC application: Use it to standardize terminology in AI policies, governance documentation, risk assessments, and technical architecture records.

#### 23.10 ISO/IEC 42005 — AI System Impact Assessment

ISO/IEC 42005 provides guidance on AI system impact assessments.

It supports the identification and evaluation of potential impacts arising from AI systems, including impacts on individuals and groups.

GRC application: Use it to structure impact assessments and connect identified impacts to risk treatment and governance decisions.

### 24. AI Risk Classification and Regulatory Frameworks

AI risk classification is important because different AI applications may create different levels of impact and require different governance obligations.

Organizations should distinguish between internal risk tiers, model risk classifications, and legal classifications.

#### 24.1 Internal AI Risk Classification

Internal AI risk classification is an organizational method for assigning risk levels to AI systems based on their purpose, complexity, impact, exposure, and criticality.

Example:

| Internal Risk Tier | Characteristics                                                                  | Governance Requirements                                         |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tier 1 — Critical  | AI influences critical infrastructure, safety, or highly consequential decisions | Enhanced oversight, validation, testing, and executive approval |
| Tier 2 — High      | AI materially influences customer or business decisions                          | Formal assessment, validation, and monitoring                   |
| Tier 3 — Moderate  | AI supports operational processes with limited direct impact                     | Defined controls and periodic review                            |
| Tier 4 — Low       | AI supports low-impact internal activities                                       | Proportionate governance and acceptable-use controls            |

The organization should define the criteria, scoring methodology, approval authority, and reassessment requirements.

These are internal categories, not official EU AI Act categories.

#### 24.2 EU AI Act Risk Classification

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory approach to AI systems.

Its principal regulatory categories include:

1. Prohibited AI practices.
2. High-risk AI systems.
3. AI systems subject to transparency obligations.
4. Other AI systems not subject to those specific categories of obligations, while remaining subject to other applicable laws.

The classification depends on the legal provisions, intended purpose, and relevant conditions.

#### 24.3 Prohibited AI Practices

Certain AI practices are prohibited under Article 5 of the EU AI Act, subject to the precise legal conditions and exceptions set out in the legislation.

Examples include certain forms of:

- Harmful manipulation and deception.
- Exploitation of vulnerabilities.
- Social scoring.
- Certain forms of biometric categorization.
- Certain forms of real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, subject to limited exceptions.

Organizations must assess actual use cases against the legal definitions rather than relying on broad labels.

#### 24.4 High-Risk AI Systems

High-risk AI systems are AI systems classified as high-risk under the applicable provisions of the EU AI Act.

The classification includes relevant systems identified under Article 6 and Annexes I and III.

Examples may include certain AI systems used in:

- Critical infrastructure.
- Education and vocational training.
- Employment and worker management.
- Essential private and public services.
- Law enforcement.
- Migration and border control.
- Administration of justice and democratic processes.

The specific legal conditions and exceptions must be examined before classifying a system.

#### 24.5 Transparency-Related AI Obligations

Certain AI systems are subject to transparency requirements even when they are not classified as high-risk.

Examples include applicable requirements involving:

- Informing individuals that they are interacting with an AI system.
- Disclosure of certain AI-generated or manipulated content.
- Marking certain synthetic content.
- Identifying certain emotion recognition or biometric categorization uses.

The exact requirement depends on the AI system and applicable legal provision.

#### 24.6 General-Purpose AI Model Risk

General-Purpose AI (GPAI) model risk refers to risks associated with models capable of performing a wide range of tasks and being integrated into different downstream AI systems.

The EU AI Act establishes obligations for GPAI model providers, including additional obligations for GPAI models with systemic risk.

Relevant risk considerations include:

- Model evaluation.
- Technical documentation.
- Downstream information.
- Copyright-related policies.
- Training content summaries.
- Systemic risk evaluation and mitigation where applicable.
- Cybersecurity.

A GPAI model is not automatically a high-risk AI system merely because it is general-purpose.

#### 24.7 AI Provider Risk

Under the EU AI Act, a provider is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model, or has one developed, and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, subject to the applicable legal definitions.

Provider obligations depend on the type of AI system and the applicable regulatory provisions.

#### 24.8 AI Deployer Risk

Under the EU AI Act, a deployer is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body using an AI system under its authority, except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

A deployer may have obligations involving:

- Use according to instructions.
- Human oversight.
- Monitoring.
- Input data relevance, where applicable.
- Record-keeping.
- Incident reporting.
- Workplace information and other transparency obligations, where applicable.

Organizations should assess whether their activities make them providers, deployers, or potentially subject to other roles under the Act.

### 25. AI Risk Treatment and Control Management

Risk identification and assessment are not sufficient on their own. Organizations must decide how risks will be treated and establish controls that reduce exposure to an acceptable level.

Risk treatment connects risk analysis to implementation, accountability, and measurable results.

#### 25.1 Risk Treatment

Risk treatment is the process of selecting and implementing measures to modify identified risks.

Common approaches include:

| Treatment Strategy | Meaning                                             | AI Example                                                                |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Avoid              | Stop the activity creating the risk                 | Prohibit an AI agent from performing unrestricted financial transactions  |
| Mitigate           | Reduce likelihood or impact                         | Introduce access controls and output validation                           |
| Transfer / Share   | Share certain financial or contractual consequences | Obtain appropriate insurance or establish supplier liability arrangements |
| Accept             | Formally retain the risk                            | Accept a documented residual risk within approved tolerance               |

Risk transfer does not eliminate the organization's accountability or all consequences of a risk.

#### 25.2 AI Security Control

An AI security control is a safeguard designed to prevent, detect, or reduce security risks affecting AI systems.

Examples:

- Authentication.
- Authorization.
- Encryption.
- Data validation.
- Model integrity verification.
- Prompt security testing.
- Monitoring.
- Incident response.
- Vulnerability management.

#### 25.3 Preventive Control

A preventive control aims to stop a risk event from occurring.

Examples:

- Least-privilege access.
- API authentication.
- Dataset validation.
- Network segmentation.
- Secure development standards.
- Tool execution restrictions.

#### 25.4 Detective Control

A detective control aims to identify a risk event or control failure after it occurs or as it develops.

Examples:

- SIEM alerts.
- Anomaly detection.
- Unauthorized access monitoring.
- Model behavior monitoring.
- Data integrity checks.
- Security audit logs.

#### 25.5 Corrective Control

A corrective control aims to restore a secure or acceptable state after an incident or control failure.

Examples:

- Model rollback.
- Credential revocation.
- Dataset restoration.
- Vulnerability remediation.
- Reprocessing corrupted data.
- Revalidation of affected models.

#### 25.6 Compensating Control

A compensating control is an alternative safeguard used when the preferred control cannot be implemented or is not available.

Example:

A legacy AI platform cannot immediately support enterprise single sign-on. The organization introduces stronger network restrictions, individual accounts, enhanced authentication, and additional access monitoring while planning remediation.

A compensating control must be evaluated to establish whether it adequately reduces the relevant risk.

#### 25.7 Key Risk Indicator (KRI)

A Key Risk Indicator (KRI) is a metric used to provide early warning of increasing risk exposure.

Examples:

| KRI                                                       | Risk Signal                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Number of unapproved AI systems                           | Increasing Shadow AI exposure     |
| Percentage of AI systems without current risk assessments | Governance coverage deterioration |
| Number of critical AI vulnerabilities overdue             | Increasing technical exposure     |
| Number of AI suppliers without current assessments        | Third-party assurance weakness    |
| Number of production models without monitoring            | Reduced operational visibility    |
| Number of overdue AI risk treatment actions               | Accumulating unresolved exposure  |
| Number of unauthorized AI access attempts                 | Potential security threats        |
| Number of unapproved model changes                        | Change management weakness        |

A KRI should have a defined calculation method, owner, threshold, reporting frequency, and escalation process.

#### 25.8 Key Risk Threshold

A key risk threshold is a defined boundary that triggers management attention or escalation when a risk indicator reaches an unacceptable level.

Example:

An organization may establish a threshold that any unresolved critical vulnerability affecting a production AI service must be escalated immediately.

Thresholds should reflect risk appetite, operational context, and the organization's approved methodology.

#### 25.9 Key Performance Indicator (KPI) vs. Key Risk Indicator (KRI)

| KPI                                             | KRI                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Measures performance against objectives         | Signals potential risk exposure                                           |
| Example: Percentage of AI assessments completed | Example: Percentage of production AI systems without security assessments |
| Focuses on delivery and effectiveness           | Focuses on exposure and emerging threats                                  |

A KPI can demonstrate activity without proving that risk is controlled. A KRI should help management identify changes in risk exposure.

#### 25.10 Risk Treatment Effectiveness

Risk treatment effectiveness evaluates whether implemented measures have reduced risk to the intended level.

It should assess:

- Whether the control addresses the identified risk.
- Whether the control has been implemented.
- Whether it operates consistently.
- Whether supporting evidence is reliable.
- Whether the residual risk is within tolerance.

### 28. AI Risk Management Interview Questions and Model Answers

#### 28.1 How would you establish an AI risk management framework?

I would start by understanding the organization's AI portfolio, business objectives, regulatory environment, and existing enterprise risk management framework.

Then I would define AI risk governance, ownership, risk classification, assessment methodology, risk appetite, control requirements, and reporting.

I would align the framework with relevant references such as NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 23894, and ISO/IEC 42001.

Finally, I would implement the methodology across the AI lifecycle, ensuring that risks are documented, assigned, treated, monitored, and periodically reassessed.

#### 28.2 What is the difference between ISO/IEC 42001 and ISO/IEC 23894?

ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System. It establishes the management-system structure for governing AI activities.

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance on managing AI-related risks.

They are complementary: ISO/IEC 42001 provides the broader management-system framework, while ISO/IEC 23894 supports the risk management discipline.

#### 28.3 How would you classify AI systems by risk?

I would establish an internal risk classification methodology based on intended purpose, business criticality, data sensitivity, potential impact, autonomy, exposure, and regulatory considerations.

I would define risk tiers, assessment requirements, approval authority, and reassessment triggers for each tier.

I would also distinguish internal risk tiers from legal classifications, particularly those established by the EU AI Act.

#### 28.4 What is the difference between risk assessment and impact assessment?

Risk assessment identifies and evaluates potential risks arising from an AI system.

An impact assessment evaluates the potential consequences of the system on affected individuals, groups, organizations, or society.

Depending on the context, impact assessments may support risk assessments and may also be required by specific governance or legal frameworks.

#### 28.5 How would you determine whether AI residual risk is acceptable?

I would review the inherent risk, existing controls, evidence of control effectiveness, remaining vulnerabilities, and potential business consequences.

I would then compare the residual risk against the organization's approved risk appetite and tolerance.

If the risk exceeds tolerance, additional treatment or a different business decision is required.

Any acceptance must be documented and approved by an authorized risk owner.

#### 28.6 How would you avoid duplicate risks in an enterprise AI risk register?

I would identify the underlying risk event, causes, affected assets, business consequences, and control owners.

Where several consequences arise from the same underlying scenario, I would document them within one coherent risk statement or linked impact fields.

Where causes, controls, owners, or treatment decisions differ materially, I would maintain separate but related risks.

The objective is to avoid double-counting while preserving visibility of distinct exposures.

#### 28.7 What is the difference between a KRI and a KPI in AI governance?

A KPI measures performance against a defined objective, such as the percentage of AI systems that completed their assessments.

A KRI provides early warning of increasing risk exposure, such as the number of production AI systems operating without current security assessments.

Both are useful, but they answer different management questions.

#### 28.8 How would you manage AI risk throughout the system lifecycle?

I would establish risk assessment requirements during planning and procurement, evaluate architecture and data risks during development, validate controls before deployment, and continuously monitor the system during operation.

Material changes, incidents, new providers, or changes in intended purpose would trigger reassessment.

I would also establish retirement requirements to ensure data, credentials, model artifacts, and associated services are handled securely.

#### 28.9 What are the biggest challenges in AI risk management?

The main challenges include:

- Rapidly changing model capabilities.
- Limited visibility into third-party models.
- Difficulty establishing model provenance.
- Complex data dependencies.
- Uncertain behavior in unfamiliar conditions.
- Inadequate AI-specific expertise.
- Evolving legal obligations.
- Fragmented accountability.
- Difficulty measuring control effectiveness.

These challenges require an integrated governance model rather than isolated technical assessments.

#### 28.10 How would you explain AI risk management to senior management?

I would explain that AI creates business opportunities but also introduces risks that can affect customers, operations, finances, compliance, and reputation.

Our responsibility is to establish visibility over AI systems, identify the most significant risk scenarios, assign accountable owners, implement appropriate controls, and provide management with evidence that residual risk remains within approved tolerance.

The objective is not to eliminate all AI risk. It is to ensure that AI is used responsibly and that the organization makes informed, accountable decisions about the risks it accepts.

### 30. Critical Conceptual Distinctions

The following distinctions are important when conducting AI risk assessments and explaining risk management decisions to business stakeholders, auditors, and management.

#### 30.1 AI Risk vs. AI Cybersecurity Risk

| AI Risk                                                                     | AI Cybersecurity Risk                                                            |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Covers the broader spectrum of AI-related exposure                          | Focuses on malicious threats and security weaknesses                             |
| Includes safety, fairness, privacy, operational, legal, and strategic risks | Includes confidentiality, integrity, availability, access, and system compromise |
| Example: Biased customer eligibility decisions                              | Example: Unauthorized access to customer data                                    |

AI cybersecurity risk is one component of the broader AI risk landscape.

#### 30.2 Model Risk vs. AI System Risk

| Model Risk                                                    | AI System Risk                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Focuses on the model's design, assumptions, behavior, and use | Covers the complete AI application and operating environment             |
| Example: Poor model generalization                            | Example: Insecure application integration                                |
| Primarily concerned with model suitability                    | Includes model, data, infrastructure, users, processes, and dependencies |

A model may be validated while the application using it remains insecure or operationally unreliable.

#### 30.3 Data Drift vs. Concept Drift

| Data Drift                                      | Concept Drift                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Input data distribution changes                 | Relationship between inputs and target outcome changes             |
| Example: Customer transaction patterns change   | Example: Fraudsters adopt new methods                              |
| Evaluated through input distribution monitoring | Evaluated through changes in predictive relationships and outcomes |

Both can lead to model degradation.

#### 30.4 Risk Assessment vs. Impact Assessment

| Risk Assessment                            | Impact Assessment                                      |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Identifies and evaluates risks             | Evaluates consequences and effects                     |
| Considers likelihood, impact, and controls | Focuses on affected stakeholders and potential impacts |
| Supports risk treatment                    | Supports governance and mitigation decisions           |

An impact assessment may be an input to a broader risk assessment.

#### 30.5 Risk Owner vs. Control Owner

| Risk Owner                                    | Control Owner                                            |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accountable for managing the risk             | Accountable for implementing or operating a control      |
| Makes or coordinates risk treatment decisions | Provides control implementation and performance evidence |
| Example: Fraud Operations Manager             | Example: IAM Operations Manager                          |

A risk owner may depend on several control owners to manage one risk.

#### 30.6 Internal AI Risk Tier vs. EU AI Act Classification

| Internal AI Risk Tier                                         | EU AI Act Classification                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Defined by the organization                                   | Defined by applicable legal provisions                   |
| Supports internal governance and prioritization               | Determines relevant regulatory obligations               |
| May use custom labels such as Critical, High, Medium, and Low | Includes legally defined categories and conditions       |
| Can be applied to any internal AI system                      | Requires legal applicability and classification analysis |

An internal high-risk rating does not automatically mean that a system is legally high-risk under the EU AI Act.

#### 30.7 Risk Mitigation vs. Risk Acceptance

| Risk Mitigation                        | Risk Acceptance                                           |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Implements actions to reduce exposure  | Formally retains remaining exposure                       |
| May reduce likelihood or impact        | Does not itself reduce risk                               |
| Requires implementation and validation | Requires authorized approval and documented justification |

Risk acceptance is a management decision, not a security control.

#### 30.8 KPI vs. KRI

| KPI                                          | KRI                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Measures performance                         | Signals potential exposure                             |
| Example: Percentage of assessments completed | Example: Number of AI systems with overdue assessments |
| Evaluates progress toward objectives         | Identifies changes in risk conditions                  |

Both should be linked to management action.

#### 30.9 AI Safety vs. AI Reliability

| AI Safety                                             | AI Reliability                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Focuses on preventing unacceptable harm               | Focuses on consistent and dependable operation |
| Example: Preventing unsafe industrial recommendations | Example: Maintaining stable model performance  |
| Concerned with consequences of unsafe behavior        | Concerned with consistency and dependability   |

Reliability can contribute to safety, but a reliable system is not automatically safe.

#### 30.10 Transparency vs. Explainability

| Transparency                                     | Explainability                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Provides relevant information about an AI system | Helps explain how or why an output was produced    |
| Example: Disclosing AI use to customers          | Example: Explaining factors influencing a decision |
| Focuses on information availability              | Focuses on understanding behavior or outcomes      |

They are complementary governance objectives.

### 32. Summary

AI risk management provides the enterprise structure for governing uncertainty and potential harm arising from AI systems.

It combines technical model and data risk management with operational resilience, third-party assurance, legal and regulatory compliance, ethical considerations, safety, and management accountability.

The NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 23894 support AI risk management, while ISO/IEC 42001 provides an AI management-system structure. ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 27701 support information security and privacy management. The EU AI Act introduces legally defined obligations based on the applicable AI system category and the organization's role.

For GRC professionals, the essential responsibility is to establish traceability across the entire risk management process:

1. Identify the AI system and its intended purpose.
2. Identify credible risk scenarios.
3. Evaluate likelihood, impact, and exposure.
4. Identify and validate existing controls.
5. Define risk treatment and accountable ownership.
6. Determine residual risk.
7. Obtain authorized risk decisions.
8. Monitor changing conditions and reassess when necessary.

A mature framework should enable management to understand not only which AI risks exist, but also why they matter, who owns them, how they are controlled, and whether the remaining exposure is acceptable.

### Core Principle

AI risk management is ultimately a management accountability discipline. Frameworks, assessments, matrices, and control libraries provide structure, but effective risk governance depends on credible evidence, clear ownership, proportionate treatment, independent challenge, and informed acceptance of residual risk.
