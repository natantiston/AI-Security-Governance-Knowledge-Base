# 22.15 — AI Standards Index

## Part 1 — International AI Standards, Foundational Terminology, and Management System Standards

### 1. Introduction to the AI Standards Landscape

AI governance requires more than internal policies and regulatory awareness. Organizations need structured standards and technical guidance to establish consistent approaches to AI management, risk assessment, security, data governance, transparency, and accountability.

International standards provide a common reference for organizations developing, procuring, integrating, deploying, and monitoring AI systems.

For cybersecurity GRC professionals, understanding the AI standards landscape is essential for three reasons:

1. Governance implementation: Standards provide structured requirements and guidance for establishing AI governance processes.
2. Risk management: Standards help organizations identify, assess, treat, and monitor AI-related risks.
3. Assurance and compliance: Standards provide reference criteria for evaluating management systems, controls, processes, and evidence.

However, standards must not be confused with legislation.

| Reference type          | Purpose                                                                                      | Example                 |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| International standard  | Establishes requirements, specifications, or guidance                                        | ISO/IEC 42001           |
| Technical specification | Defines technical requirements or implementation characteristics                             | ISO/IEC TS 4213         |
| Technical report        | Provides explanatory or research-oriented information                                        | ISO/IEC TR publications |
| Framework               | Provides a structured approach to managing a discipline                                      | NIST AI RMF             |
| Regulation              | Establishes legally binding requirements within its scope                                    | EU AI Act               |
| Directive               | Establishes legal objectives that EU Member States must implement through national law       | NIS2 Directive          |
| Code of practice        | Provides practices or commitments supporting a particular regulatory or governance objective | GPAI Code of Practice   |

Important distinction: ISO/IEC certification does not automatically establish compliance with the EU AI Act. Similarly, implementing the NIST AI RMF does not automatically satisfy every applicable regulatory obligation.

#### 2.1 ISO and IEC Standard Designations

ISO and IEC publications use different designations to communicate their publication type.

| Designation | Meaning                          | General purpose                                                                            |
| ----------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| ISO         | International Standard           | Requirements, specifications, or guidance                                                  |
| IEC         | International Standard           | Requirements or specifications within electrotechnical domains                             |
| ISO/IEC     | Joint International Standard     | Jointly developed international standard                                                   |
| ISO/IEC TS  | Technical Specification          | Technical requirements or specifications, often addressing developing or specialized areas |
| ISO/IEC TR  | Technical Report                 | Explanatory or informative technical publication                                           |
| ISO/IEC PAS | Publicly Available Specification | Specification made available through a defined standardization process                     |
| ISO Guide   | Guidance document                | Guidance supporting the application of standards or related practices                      |

Not every publication has the same normative status.

A standard containing requirements may support certification or conformity assessment, whereas a technical report is generally informative.

#### 2.2 Understanding Standard Numbers

An ISO/IEC standard number identifies a specific publication.

For example:

`ISO/IEC 42001:2023`

- ISO/IEC: Joint ISO and IEC publication.
- 42001: Standard identifier.
- 2023: Publication year of the edition.

A suffix such as `:2023` is important because standards are revised and amended over time.

When using standards professionally, always verify:

- The complete standard designation.
- The edition and publication year.
- Whether amendments or corrigenda exist.
- Whether a newer edition has been published.
- Whether the standard is applicable to the organization's scope.

#### 3.1 ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI Management System

ISO/IEC 42001 is one of the most important standards for enterprise AI governance.

It establishes requirements for organizations that provide or use AI systems.

Its purpose is to help organizations establish, implement, maintain, and continually improve an AI Management System.

The standard follows a management-system approach and uses the familiar structure found in several ISO management-system standards.

Its principal areas include:

| Management-system area      | Governance focus                                                                    |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Context of the organization | Understand organizational context, interested parties, and AIMS scope               |
| Leadership                  | Establish leadership commitment, AI policy, and organizational responsibilities     |
| Planning                    | Address AI risks and opportunities and establish objectives                         |
| Support                     | Provide resources, competence, awareness, communication, and documented information |
| Operation                   | Plan, implement, and control AI-related operational processes                       |
| Performance evaluation      | Monitor, measure, analyze, audit, and review AIMS performance                       |
| Improvement                 | Address nonconformities and continually improve the management system               |

Annex A — Reference controls

ISO/IEC 42001 includes a reference control framework in Annex A.

Its control themes include:

- Policies related to AI.
- Internal organization and responsibilities.
- Resources for AI systems.
- Impact assessment.
- AI system lifecycle.
- Data management.
- Information provided to interested parties.
- Use of AI systems.
- Third-party and customer relationships.

Annex A is particularly relevant to GRC professionals because it helps translate AI governance objectives into organizational controls.

Annex B — Implementation guidance

Annex B provides implementation guidance for the controls in Annex A.

It helps organizations understand how controls can be applied in practice.

Annex C — AI-related objectives and risk sources

Annex C provides examples of potential AI-related organizational objectives and risk sources.

Annex D — Use of the AI Management System across domains or sectors

Annex D addresses how the AIMS can be applied across different domains and sectors.

GRC application:

An organization pursuing ISO/IEC 42001 certification should establish:

1. A defined AIMS scope.
2. An approved AI policy.
3. Assigned roles and responsibilities.
4. AI risk-management processes.
5. AI impact assessment procedures.
6. AI lifecycle controls.
7. Documented operational evidence.
8. Internal audit and management review.
9. Corrective actions and continual improvement.

Critical distinction: ISO/IEC 42001 is a management-system standard. It does not prescribe a single technical architecture or guarantee that every AI system is secure, accurate, fair, or compliant with every applicable law.

#### 3.2 ISO/IEC 23894:2023 — AI Risk Management

ISO/IEC 23894 provides guidance for integrating AI risk management into organizational risk-management activities.

It is particularly relevant to professionals responsible for:

- AI risk assessments.
- AI risk registers.
- Risk treatment.
- Risk ownership.
- Risk monitoring.
- AI-related decision-making.

Its approach recognizes that AI risks may emerge from the technology itself, its development process, data, intended purpose, deployment context, and interactions with other systems.

Relationship with ISO/IEC 42001:

| ISO/IEC 42001                                         | ISO/IEC 23894                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Establishes AIMS requirements                         | Provides AI risk-management guidance                                     |
| Addresses the overall management system               | Focuses on risk-management activities                                    |
| Includes organizational governance and accountability | Supports identification, analysis, evaluation, and treatment of AI risks |
| Supports management-system certification              | Is guidance rather than a standalone certification requirement           |

A practical organization may use ISO/IEC 23894 to structure its AI risk methodology within the broader ISO/IEC 42001 management system.

#### 3.3 ISO/IEC 22989:2022 — AI Concepts and Terminology

This standard establishes common AI concepts and terminology.

It supports consistent communication between:

- AI developers.
- Enterprise architects.
- Cybersecurity professionals.

It is particularly useful when developing:

A governance professional should use consistent terminology when distinguishing AI systems, models, datasets, training, inference, and other technical concepts.

#### 3.4 ISO/IEC 23053:2022 — AI Systems Using Machine Learning

This standard provides a framework for describing AI systems that use machine learning.

It is relevant when assessing:

- ML system architecture.

It helps bridge the gap between AI governance and technical system architecture.

For GRC professionals, it provides useful context for understanding where governance and security controls should be implemented throughout the ML system.

### 6. ISO/IEC 42001 Certification and Assurance Concepts

ISO/IEC 42001 can be used as the basis for independent certification of an organization's AI Management System.

Certification evaluates whether the organization's management system conforms to the standard within the defined certification scope.

It is important to understand what certification demonstrates and what it does not demonstrate.

| Certification consideration | Explanation                                                                        |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Certification scope         | Defines which organizational activities, locations, and AIMS processes are covered |
| Certification audit         | Evaluates conformity against ISO/IEC 42001 requirements                            |
| Stage 1 audit               | Reviews management-system readiness and documentation                              |
| Stage 2 audit               | Evaluates implementation and effectiveness of the management system                |
| Surveillance audit          | Periodically evaluates continued conformity                                        |
| Recertification audit       | Evaluates the management system at the end of the certification cycle              |
| Nonconformity               | A failure to meet a specified requirement                                          |
| Corrective action           | Action addressing the cause of a detected nonconformity                            |
| Certification body          | An independent organization performing certification activities                    |
| Accreditation body          | An organization assessing the competence of certification bodies                   |

#### 6.1 ISO/IEC 42006 — Certification Body Requirements

ISO/IEC 42006 specifies requirements for bodies providing audit and certification of AI management systems.

It is relevant to the certification ecosystem rather than serving as the primary management-system implementation standard for organizations.

The distinction is:

- ISO/IEC 42001 defines requirements for the organization's AIMS.
- ISO/IEC 42006 defines requirements for bodies auditing and certifying AIMS.

An organization seeking certification should verify that its chosen certification body has appropriate competence, accreditation, and scope.

#### 6.2 Certification Does Not Guarantee Regulatory Compliance

Consider an organization that has achieved ISO/IEC 42001 certification.

It may have:

- An approved AI policy.
- Documented AI risk assessments.
- Defined governance roles.
- An AI system inventory.
- Lifecycle management processes.
- Internal audit and management review.

However, this does not automatically demonstrate that every AI system complies with:

- The EU AI Act.
- GDPR.
- NIS2.
- DORA.
- Applicable sector-specific requirements.
- Other relevant national or international legislation.

The organization must separately assess the applicable legal obligations and retain evidence of compliance.

Artificial intelligence — Guidance on risk management

Core risk reference

Provides guidance for integrating AI risk management into an organization's existing risk-management processes.

The standard recognizes that AI introduces risk characteristics that may require additional assessment beyond conventional IT risk management.

Examples include:

- Model performance degradation.
- Training-data poisoning.
- Bias and discriminatory outcomes.
- Lack of explainability.
- Unintended model behavior.
- Adversarial manipulation.
- Excessive reliance on AI-generated outputs.
- Third-party model dependencies.
- Unclear accountability for AI decisions.
- Unexpected interactions between AI components and business processes.

Practical risk-management lifecycle

```
        Establish Context
               |
               v
        Identify AI Risks
               |
               v
         Analyze Risks
               |
               v
        Evaluate Risks
               |
               v
         Treat Risks
               |
               v
       Monitor and Review
               |
               v
       Communicate and Consult
```

The organization should integrate these activities into its enterprise risk-management structure rather than operating AI risk management as an isolated technical exercise.

GRC application:

A telecom operator introduces a generative AI assistant into its Salesforce CRM environment. The assistant retrieves customer records, summarizes complaints, and recommends responses to customer-service agents.

An ISO/IEC 23894-aligned assessment could identify:

| Risk category     | Example risk                                            | Potential treatment                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Data security     | Unauthorized retrieval of customer information          | Identity-based authorization and retrieval filtering    |
| Privacy           | Exposure of personal information in generated responses | Data minimization and privacy controls                  |
| Model reliability | Incorrect customer-service recommendations              | Validation and human review                             |
| Prompt injection  | Malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content    | Input isolation and instruction hierarchy enforcement   |
| Operational       | Unavailability of the external AI provider              | Continuity and fallback procedures                      |
| Third-party       | Provider changes model behavior without adequate notice | Contractual notification and change-management controls |
| Accountability    | Unclear responsibility for AI-generated decisions       | Defined business and technical ownership                |

The risk register should identify risk owners, control owners, treatment actions, residual risk, and acceptance authority.

Important distinction: ISO/IEC 23894 provides risk-management guidance. It does not prescribe one universal AI risk-scoring formula or guarantee that every AI risk can be quantified.

### 3. AI Trustworthiness, Bias, and Fairness Standards

AI trustworthiness is multidimensional. A system that performs accurately may still be insecure, unfair, insufficiently transparent, or unsuitable for its intended purpose.

#### 3.1 ISO/IEC 24028:2020 — Overview of Trustworthiness in Artificial Intelligence

[ISO/IEC TR 24028:2020](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/SaRWeBwHeQ7IFV9QkaOE-sdtFZ0kfmOUQJo7ZzzDqgsehZAJwqLpnAOo9aZJm4zpdXaUwMvlcA8NXUO2jzZpwtGze7lQAdiAIflkkmlDYZYs2Oz_fP9SexKDJ_cus7WAe8uLayJVvcNOE8RzAtDlEyTw6xsnamr6Bx-2c8bk0FQ?purpose=inline)

ISO/IEC 24028:2020

Information technology — Artificial intelligence — Overview of trustworthiness in artificial intelligence

Provides an overview of AI trustworthiness concepts and related considerations.

Trustworthiness involves multiple characteristics that must be evaluated in the context of the AI system and its intended use.

Examples include:

- Reliability.
- Accuracy.
- Robustness.
- Resilience.
- Security.
- Safety.
- Explainability.
- Transparency.
- Privacy.
- Fairness.
- Accountability.

These characteristics are related but are not interchangeable.

| Characteristic | Governance question                                                                              |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Reliability    | Does the system consistently perform its intended function?                                      |
| Accuracy       | Are the outputs sufficiently correct for the intended use?                                       |
| Robustness     | Can the system maintain acceptable behavior under expected variations and disturbances?          |
| Resilience     | Can the system withstand, recover from, or adapt to disruptive conditions?                       |
| Security       | Is the system protected against malicious activity and unauthorized access?                      |
| Safety         | Does the system avoid unacceptable harm in its operational context?                              |
| Explainability | Can relevant stakeholders understand the basis of outputs or decisions to an appropriate degree? |
| Transparency   | Is relevant information about the system and its operation available to stakeholders?            |
| Privacy        | Are personal-data rights and privacy requirements appropriately protected?                       |
| Fairness       | Are outcomes assessed for unjustified disparities or discriminatory effects?                     |
| Accountability | Are responsibilities and decision ownership clearly established?                                 |

GRC application:

A financial institution deploys an AI-assisted credit assessment solution.

A trustworthiness assessment should examine more than prediction accuracy. It should also consider:

- Whether different customer groups experience unjustified disparities.
- Whether decisions can be explained to relevant stakeholders.
- Whether personal information is appropriately protected.
- Whether the system remains reliable under changing economic conditions.
- Whether human reviewers can challenge or override outputs.

[ISO/IEC TR 24027:2021](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/3Zlapr06h53ni74QY7hboW-oSQ0FcSAYBCkG6kJVfLDeAsU_1L5_pZZu2_zBQ_WUMSzemgrem19e0dnZT4kSMuw9lV537JHcULjZlwOEFJfRiejT12h9XyvVd6-LNoL01vdWK15ZgX1-DzSIYbGKq7xSmHSSSwZboWW1xmMpudI?purpose=inline)

ISO/IEC 24027:2021

Information technology — Artificial intelligence — Bias in AI systems and AI-aided decision making

Addresses bias considerations in AI systems and AI-assisted decision processes.

Bias may originate from multiple sources throughout the AI lifecycle.

| Bias source         | Explanation                                                                     | Example                                                                                 |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Historical bias     | Existing social or organizational inequalities are reflected in historical data | Historical hiring decisions favoring a particular group                                 |
| Representation bias | Certain populations are insufficiently represented                              | Facial recognition data dominated by one demographic                                    |
| Measurement bias    | Data collection or measurement methods produce systematic inaccuracies          | Inconsistent measurements across customer groups                                        |
| Labeling bias       | Labels reflect subjective or inconsistent human judgments                       | Subjective performance ratings used as training labels                                  |
| Aggregation bias    | A single model is applied to groups with materially different characteristics   | One prediction model used across distinct populations                                   |
| Evaluation bias     | Evaluation datasets do not represent the intended deployment population         | Model tested on a narrow demographic sample                                             |
| Deployment bias     | System use differs from the context for which it was designed                   | A model used for employee monitoring despite being designed for operational forecasting |

Bias management activities:

1. Identify potentially affected populations.
2. Assess data representativeness.
3. Examine feature selection and label quality.
4. Define appropriate fairness objectives.
5. Select suitable evaluation metrics.
6. Evaluate outcomes across relevant groups.
7. Investigate disparities.
8. Implement mitigation measures.
9. Monitor post-deployment performance.
10. Document residual limitations.

Important distinction: Removing protected attributes from a dataset does not necessarily eliminate discriminatory effects. Other variables may act as proxies for protected characteristics.

- Adversarial image inputs.
- Manipulated sensor readings.
- Unexpected input distributions.
- Malformed data.

GRC application:

- Identified limitations.
- Remediation activities.
- Retesting results.

## Part 3 — AI Security, Cybersecurity, Privacy, Software Engineering, and Technical Assurance Standards

### 1. Introduction

Parts 1 and 2 covered the foundational AI standards landscape, including AI management systems, risk management, trustworthiness, data quality, robustness, and lifecycle governance.

- Information security management.
- Application and software security.
- Cloud and infrastructure security.
- Data protection and privacy.
- Identity and access management.
- Supply-chain security.
- Vulnerability management.
- Security testing and assurance.

Establishes requirements and guidance for a Privacy Information Management System (PIMS).

ISO/IEC 27701 is relevant to organizations processing personal information through AI systems.

AI applications can introduce privacy concerns throughout the lifecycle:

- Collection of training data.
- Personal information in prompts.
- Retrieval of personal information.
- Model memorization.
- Inference of sensitive information.
- Disclosure through generated outputs.
- Data retention by external providers.
- Reuse of data for model improvement.

Example — AI-powered customer-service assistant

A telecommunications company uses a generative AI assistant to summarize customer interactions.

The privacy governance assessment should determine:

- What personal information is processed?
- What is the lawful basis for processing?
- Who determines the purposes and means of processing?
- Which service providers process the data?
- Is the data used for model training?
- How are data-subject requests handled?
- What retention and deletion arrangements apply?
- Are international transfers involved?

The organization must separately establish its applicable GDPR obligations.

#### 3.2 ISO/IEC 29100 — Privacy Framework

ISO/IEC 29100

Information technology — Security techniques — Privacy framework

This standard provides a privacy framework, including privacy principles and terminology.

It is useful when establishing a consistent privacy architecture for AI applications.

Its privacy principles include considerations such as:

- Consent and choice.
- Purpose legitimacy and specification.
- Collection limitation.
- Data minimization.
- Use, retention, and disclosure limitation.
- Accuracy and quality.
- Openness, transparency, and notice.
- Individual participation and access.
- Accountability.
- Information security.
- Privacy compliance.

AI governance application:

When deploying an AI system that processes personal data, the organization should establish controls ensuring that the data is collected and used for legitimate, defined purposes and that appropriate privacy safeguards remain effective throughout the system lifecycle.

### 4. Secure Software Development and Application Security Standards

AI applications are software systems. Their security must therefore incorporate established secure development and application security practices.

#### 4.1 ISO/IEC 27034 — Application Security

ISO/IEC 27034

Information technology — Security techniques — Application security

This series addresses application security and provides guidance for integrating security into application development and management.

For AI applications, relevant areas include:

- Application security requirements.
- Secure architecture.
- Security testing.
- Application security controls.
- Development governance.
- Security verification.
- Application maintenance.

Example:

An organization develops an internal AI application that connects an LLM to Salesforce.

The application security review should examine:

- Authentication and authorization.
- API security.
- Input validation.
- Output handling.
- Session management.
- Secrets management.
- Dependency vulnerabilities.
- Secure error handling.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Protection against prompt injection and unsafe tool execution.

#### 4.2 ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 — Software Life Cycle Processes

ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207

Systems and software engineering — Software life cycle processes

This standard establishes processes for software lifecycle management.

It can support governance of AI applications and their surrounding software components.

Relevant activities include:

- Acquisition.
- Development.
- Operation.
- Maintenance.
- Configuration management.
- Verification.
- Validation.
- Quality assurance.
- Risk management.

It is particularly useful when AI capabilities are embedded in enterprise software products or business applications.

#### 4.3 ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 — System Life Cycle Processes

ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288

Systems and software engineering — System life cycle processes

This standard addresses system lifecycle processes.

It is relevant when an AI solution consists of multiple interconnected components, including:

- AI models.
- Data platforms.
- APIs.
- Cloud infrastructure.
- Identity services.
- Business applications.
- Human operators.
- External service providers.

Distinction between 12207 and 15288:

| Standard           | Primary focus                |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207 | Software lifecycle processes |
| ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 | System lifecycle processes   |

An enterprise AI solution may require consideration of both software and system lifecycle governance.

### 5. AI Security Testing and Vulnerability Management References

Not every AI security reference is an ISO standard. Some of the most practical resources are technical frameworks, security guidance, and vulnerability classification systems.

These should be incorporated into the standards index but clearly identified by publication type.

OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications

AI application security reference

Identifies important security risks affecting applications that use large language models.

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a practical security reference for identifying and evaluating common LLM application vulnerabilities.

Common risk categories include:

| Risk                             | Description                                                                            |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prompt Injection                 | Malicious instructions manipulate model behavior                                       |
| Sensitive Information Disclosure | Sensitive information is exposed through model or application behavior                 |
| Supply Chain                     | Vulnerabilities or compromises arise from dependencies, models, datasets, or providers |
| Data and Model Poisoning         | Malicious or manipulated data affects model behavior                                   |
| Improper Output Handling         | Model outputs are used unsafely by downstream systems                                  |
| Excessive Agency                 | An AI application has excessive permissions or autonomy                                |
| System Prompt Leakage            | Sensitive system instructions are disclosed                                            |
| Vector and Embedding Weaknesses  | Retrieval and embedding components introduce security risks                            |
| Misinformation                   | Applications produce or propagate misleading information                               |
| Unbounded Consumption            | Excessive resource use creates cost, availability, or denial-of-service risks          |

The exact categories and ordering should be checked against the edition being used, because OWASP updates its guidance.

GRC application:

An AI security assessment should map relevant OWASP risks to:

- Application security controls.
- Identity and access management.
- Data protection.
- AI model governance.
- Security testing.
- Third-party risk management.
- Incident response.

Critical distinction: OWASP guidance is not an ISO certification standard and does not independently establish regulatory compliance.

#### 5.2 OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10

The OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10 project provides security risk guidance for machine-learning systems.

It is useful for assessing risks that may occur during:

- Data collection.
- Model training.
- Model deployment.
- Inference.
- Model integration.
- Operational monitoring.

It complements LLM-specific guidance because not all AI systems are generative AI applications.

#### 5.3 MITRE ATLAS

[Exploring MITRE ATLAS: A New Era in AI Security](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/CKj_54ZGzqPYcY9MHx5EbPXuLFoW-47qZ8fUSi7bedYbrweUOKdFGL990gynmPYNkDfgcUXWG2fU3-vlADrcWgvz83LwwhYOkWyI6IQoPEYjfWdPpgYkq8v0n2jaueqwAMJT4s_FEf4toQBDbIrKTwt8oF7viRBWKVe4P5YBdts?purpose=inline)

MITRE ATLAS

Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems

A knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques targeting AI-enabled systems.

MITRE ATLAS supports AI threat modeling, red teaming, and security architecture assessments.

It helps security teams understand how attackers may target:

- AI development environments.
- Training data.
- Model artifacts.
- Inference interfaces.
- AI applications.
- Supporting infrastructure.

Example attack scenarios:

- Poisoning training data.
- Extracting model information.
- Manipulating model inputs.
- Stealing model artifacts.
- Exploiting exposed AI services.
- Compromising AI development pipelines.

MITRE ATLAS should be used as a threat-informed reference, not as a prescriptive compliance standard.

#### 5.4 MITRE ATT&CK

MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations.

It is relevant to AI environments because attackers may compromise the conventional enterprise infrastructure supporting AI systems.

Examples include:

- Compromising cloud identities.
- Stealing credentials.
- Exploiting vulnerable applications.
- Establishing persistence.
- Exfiltrating data.
- Compromising development environments.

ATLAS versus ATT&CK:

| MITRE ATLAS                              | MITRE ATT&CK                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Focuses on threats to AI-enabled systems | Covers broader enterprise and platform attack behavior                   |
| Supports AI threat modeling              | Supports enterprise threat modeling and detection                        |
| Addresses AI-specific attack surfaces    | Addresses endpoints, networks, cloud, identities, and other technologies |
| Complements AI security assessments      | Complements SOC detection and incident response                          |

The two knowledge bases can be used together when assessing enterprise AI environments.

### 6. Software Supply Chain and Component Security Standards

AI systems rely on complex technology supply chains.

An organization may consume:

- Open-weight models.
- Commercial model APIs.
- Open-source libraries.
- Python packages.
- Container images.
- Model-serving frameworks.
- Vector databases.
- External datasets.
- Cloud infrastructure.

A compromised or vulnerable component can undermine the security of the entire AI solution.

#### 6.1 ISO/IEC 27036 Series — Information Security for Supplier Relationships

ISO/IEC 27036 series

Information security for supplier relationships

This series addresses information security in supplier relationships.

It is relevant to AI vendor and third-party governance.

GRC professionals should assess:

- AI model providers.
- Cloud service providers.
- Data suppliers.
- AI development partners.
- Managed AI service providers.
- Model hosting providers.
- External integration providers.

The assessment should consider security requirements throughout supplier selection, contracting, onboarding, service delivery, monitoring, and termination.

#### 6.2 ISO/IEC 20243 — Open Trusted Technology Provider Standard

ISO/IEC 20243

Open Trusted Technology Provider Standard (O-TTPS)

This standard addresses security and integrity considerations in the technology supply chain.

It can be relevant when evaluating technology providers and the integrity of products and components used in AI infrastructure.

#### 6.3 ISO/IEC 5230 — OpenChain Specification

ISO/IEC 5230

OpenChain Specification

This standard establishes requirements for an open-source license compliance program.

It is relevant to AI environments that use open-source software and libraries.

However, open-source license compliance and software security are separate concerns.

A component may be:

- Legally compliant with its open-source license.
- Technically vulnerable.
- Poorly maintained.
- Incompatible with organizational security requirements.

Organizations must assess these issues independently.

#### 6.4 Software Bill of Materials and AI Bill of Materials

A Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) identifies software components and dependencies within a software product.

An AI Bill of Materials (AI BOM) or model-oriented inventory may extend component transparency to AI-specific assets.

| Inventory           | Typical contents                                                                                 |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| SBOM                | Software packages, libraries, versions, dependencies, licenses                                   |
| AI BOM              | AI models, model versions, datasets, AI frameworks, model dependencies, relevant components      |
| Model inventory     | Models owned, developed, acquired, or deployed by the organization                               |
| AI system inventory | Complete AI-enabled systems, their business purposes, owners, integrations, and lifecycle status |

An AI BOM is not automatically a standardized or universally defined artifact. Organizations should define its scope and minimum required fields.

### 9. GRC Assessment Questions

A cybersecurity GRC professional should be able to evaluate whether the organization has translated these references into actual controls.

| Assessment domain      | Questions                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ISMS integration       | Are AI systems and supporting infrastructure included within the relevant ISMS scope?                         |
| Access control         | Are AI models, datasets, APIs, and administrative interfaces protected by appropriate access controls?        |
| Cloud security         | Are shared-responsibility arrangements defined and documented?                                                |
| Privacy                | Has the organization identified personal data processed by AI systems and established appropriate safeguards? |
| Application security   | Are AI applications subject to secure development and security testing?                                       |
| Lifecycle management   | Are AI components subject to controlled development, deployment, modification, and retirement?                |
| LLM security           | Has the organization assessed prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, and excessive agency?       |
| Threat modeling        | Are AI-specific threats considered in security architecture reviews?                                          |
| Supply chain           | Are external models, datasets, libraries, and providers subject to risk assessment?                           |
| Component transparency | Does the organization maintain sufficient inventories of software and AI components?                          |
| Incident response      | Are AI-related security incidents incorporated into incident response procedures?                             |
| Assurance              | Is there evidence that security controls operate effectively?                                                 |

### 10. Summary and Core Principle

This part covers the standards and security references required to protect AI systems, applications, data, infrastructure, and technology supply chains.

The principal references include:

- ISO/IEC 27001: Information security management.
- ISO/IEC 27002: Information security controls.
- ISO/IEC 27017: Cloud security.
- ISO/IEC 27018: Protection of PII in public clouds.
- ISO/IEC 27701: Privacy information management.
- ISO/IEC 29100: Privacy framework.
- ISO/IEC 27034: Application security.
- ISO/IEC/IEEE 12207: Software lifecycle processes.
- ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288: System lifecycle processes.
- OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications: LLM application security risks.
- OWASP Machine Learning Security Top 10: ML security risks.
- MITRE ATLAS: AI adversarial threat knowledge base.
- MITRE ATT&CK: Enterprise adversary tactics and techniques.
- ISO/IEC 27036: Supplier relationship security.
- ISO/IEC 20243: Technology supply-chain integrity.
- ISO/IEC 5230: Open-source license compliance.

Core Principle: AI security must be integrated into enterprise information security, privacy, application security, cloud governance, and supply-chain risk management. An AI system is only as secure as the models, data, applications, infrastructure, identities, and external dependencies that support it.
