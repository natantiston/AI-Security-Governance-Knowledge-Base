# 22.01 — AI Terminology
## Part 1 of 4 — Foundations of Artificial Intelligence Terminology

## 1. Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) governance requires more than familiarity with AI technologies. It requires a precise understanding of the terminology used to describe AI systems, their capabilities, limitations, risks, controls, and accountability structures.

In enterprise environments, terminology is not merely a technical concern. Definitions influence risk classification, security requirements, regulatory applicability, procurement decisions, contractual obligations, audit criteria, and management reporting.

Consider the following statements:

- “We use AI to automate customer support.”
- “We use a machine-learning model to classify customer requests.”
- “We use a large language model to generate customer responses.”
- “We use an AI agent that can retrieve customer records and execute account operations.”

These statements describe different levels of technical capability. Treating them as interchangeable can lead to inadequate security assessments, incomplete risk registers, incorrect control selection, and inappropriate governance decisions.

For example, a system that generates a response without taking action has a different operational risk profile from a system that can retrieve confidential records, invoke external tools, and modify customer accounts.

**The objective of this lesson is to establish a consistent AI vocabulary that can be used across cybersecurity, GRC, enterprise architecture, risk management, compliance, and AI governance.**

This part establishes the foundations of AI terminology and explains the principal distinctions necessary for sound governance decisions.

## 2. Why AI Terminology Matters in Security Governance

Terminology establishes the boundaries of what an organization believes it is assessing and controlling.

When stakeholders use the same word to describe different technologies, governance processes become inconsistent. Conversely, when stakeholders use different words for the same underlying capability, risks may be duplicated, overlooked, or incorrectly classified.

### 2.1 The relationship between terminology and governance

AI terminology affects several organizational processes.

| Governance activity | Why terminology matters | Example of a governance consequence |
|---|---|---|
| AI system inventory | Determines how systems and components are identified | An organization may fail to inventory an embedded AI feature because it does not consider the feature a separate AI system. |
| Risk assessment | Establishes the capabilities and limitations being assessed | A generative AI application may require assessments for hallucination, prompt injection, and sensitive-data disclosure. |
| Security architecture | Clarifies system components and trust boundaries | An AI application that calls external tools requires appropriate authorization and execution controls. |
| Data governance | Identifies how data is used throughout the AI lifecycle | Training data, inference inputs, retrieval sources, and generated outputs may require different controls. |
| Third-party risk management | Clarifies which components and services are externally supplied | An organization may rely on a third-party model provider even when the application itself is developed internally. |
| Regulatory assessment | Helps determine which legal definitions and obligations may apply | The legal classification of an AI system depends on the applicable legislation and the system's specific circumstances. |
| Audit and assurance | Establishes measurable control criteria | An auditor must understand what constitutes a model, an AI system, a control, and the relevant evidence. |
| Incident management | Enables consistent classification and escalation | A model producing an incorrect answer is not necessarily the same type of incident as an agent executing an unauthorized transaction. |

### 2.2 Terminology is a control-enabling mechanism

Consider an enterprise policy that states:

> All AI systems must undergo a security risk assessment before production deployment.

This requirement appears straightforward, but several questions immediately arise:

1. What qualifies as an AI system?
2. Does the requirement cover AI embedded in a third-party SaaS application?
3. Does it cover a model accessed through an external API?
4. Does it cover a retrieval-augmented generation application?
5. Does it cover an autonomous agent that uses enterprise tools?
6. What constitutes completion of the assessment?
7. Who determines whether the assessment is adequate?

Without defined terminology, different teams may interpret the same policy differently.

A more effective governance approach establishes an approved vocabulary, identifies the scope of relevant terms, assigns responsibilities, and connects definitions to operational procedures.

**GRC implication:** Terminology should not exist solely in a glossary. Where necessary, definitions should be incorporated into policies, standards, risk assessment methodologies, control descriptions, architecture documentation, procurement requirements, and audit procedures.

## 3. Foundational Artificial Intelligence Concepts

The following concepts establish the vocabulary needed to describe AI systems consistently.

### 3.1 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

**Definition:** Artificial intelligence is a broad field concerned with developing computational systems capable of performing tasks associated with capabilities such as perception, language processing, reasoning, prediction, planning, and decision-making.

AI is an umbrella term rather than a single technology or implementation method.

AI systems can use different approaches, including:

- Rule-based logic.
- Search and optimization.
- Knowledge representation.
- Machine learning.
- Deep learning.
- Generative models.
- Combinations of symbolic and statistical techniques.

The term *AI* alone does not establish whether a system learns from data, generates content, makes decisions autonomously, or presents a cybersecurity risk.

Those characteristics must be established through a more detailed assessment.

**Enterprise example**

A company uses three products:

1. A rules engine that flags transactions matching predefined fraud indicators.
2. A machine-learning model that estimates the probability of fraudulent activity.
3. A generative AI assistant that summarizes fraud investigation reports.

All three may be described as AI-related capabilities in an organizational inventory, but their technical behavior, failure modes, and security requirements differ.

**GRC consideration:** An AI inventory should capture enough information to distinguish system capabilities, implementation approaches, business purposes, and operational responsibilities. A generic label such as “AI application” is insufficient for meaningful risk analysis.

### 3.2 AI system

**Definition:** An AI system is a system that uses AI techniques to produce outputs such as predictions, recommendations, content, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

The exact definition depends on the framework, standard, or legislation being applied.

An AI system may include more than the underlying model. It can encompass application components, interfaces, data pipelines, orchestration logic, external services, and human interaction mechanisms.

A useful conceptual representation is:

```text
                 AI SYSTEM
                     |
       +-------------+-------------+
       |             |             |
   Data Inputs     AI Model    Application Logic
       |             |             |
       +-------------+-------------+
                     |
             Processing and
              Orchestration
                     |
              Output Interface
                     |
          Human or System Consumer
```

This diagram is conceptual. Actual architectures may contain additional components, feedback loops, external integrations, and control layers.

**Enterprise example**

A customer-service assistant may contain:

- A web-based user interface.
- An application backend.
- A third-party language model.
- A document retrieval service.
- An enterprise identity provider.
- A customer database.
- Logging and monitoring infrastructure.
- Human escalation workflows.

Assessing only the language model would leave significant parts of the system outside the assessment scope.

**GRC consideration:** Establish the assessment boundary before identifying risks. Determine which components are internally managed, externally supplied, shared with other systems, or operated by a third party.

### 3.3 AI model

**Definition:** An AI model is a computational representation that has been developed or configured to produce outputs from inputs according to learned patterns, mathematical relationships, encoded knowledge, or other computational structures.

The precise meaning depends on the type of AI technology.

For a machine-learning model, the model commonly contains parameters learned or adjusted during training. Other AI approaches may rely on explicitly encoded rules or representations.

A model is not necessarily a complete application.

For example:

- A language model can generate text.
- An application can provide a chat interface around that model.
- An orchestration layer can connect the application to databases and tools.
- An identity and authorization layer can determine which resources the application may access.

These components perform different functions and create different security considerations.

**GRC consideration:** Model-level assurance does not automatically establish system-level security. An appropriately evaluated model can still be deployed in an insecure application.

### 3.4 Algorithm

**Definition:** An algorithm is a defined sequence of computational steps used to solve a problem or perform a task.

Algorithms are not exclusive to AI. Conventional software, databases, encryption systems, and network protocols also use algorithms.

Within AI, algorithms can support tasks such as:

- Learning model parameters.
- Selecting actions.
- Optimizing an objective.
- Classifying observations.
- Searching for solutions.
- Generating outputs.

An algorithm describes a computational procedure; a model may be the resulting learned representation or another structure used by a system.

**Example**

A machine-learning training algorithm adjusts a model's parameters to reduce a specified loss function. Once training is complete, the resulting model can be used to generate predictions.

The training algorithm and the trained model are related, but they are not interchangeable concepts.

**GRC consideration:** When assessing a system, identify the relevant components rather than treating the algorithm, model, and application as synonymous.

### 3.5 Dataset

**Definition:** A dataset is an organized collection of data used for purposes such as training, validation, testing, evaluation, retrieval, or operational processing.

AI systems can interact with several categories of datasets.

| Dataset category | Primary purpose | Governance considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Training dataset | Supports model training or parameter adjustment | Data provenance, licensing, privacy, quality, representativeness, and poisoning risks |
| Validation dataset | Supports model selection and tuning decisions | Separation from training data, appropriate coverage, and evaluation integrity |
| Test dataset | Evaluates performance against selected criteria | Independence, representativeness, reproducibility, and resistance to leakage |
| Inference input data | Supplies information when the model is used | Confidentiality, personal data, access control, and input manipulation |
| Retrieval corpus | Supplies information retrieved during application execution | Source authorization, data freshness, document-level permissions, and prompt injection in retrieved content |
| Evaluation dataset | Measures behavior against defined tests or benchmarks | Test coverage, metric suitability, and benchmark limitations |
| Operational feedback data | Captures user feedback or observed system behavior | Consent, data retention, integrity, and appropriate use in future development |

These categories are functional distinctions. A dataset may serve more than one purpose, provided its use is appropriate and controlled.

**Enterprise example**

A financial-services organization develops an AI assistant using historical support conversations. During production, the assistant retrieves information from an internal knowledge base and receives new customer messages as inference inputs.

The historical conversations, knowledge-base documents, and incoming messages are all data sources, but they have different governance requirements.

The organization should not assume that approval to use one source automatically authorizes the others for training, retrieval, or operational processing.

**GRC consideration:** Maintain data lineage and document the intended purpose, ownership, classification, provenance, access permissions, retention requirements, and permitted uses of relevant datasets.

### 3.6 Inference

**Definition:** Inference is the process by which a trained or otherwise configured model processes an input to produce an output.

Depending on the system, the output may be a classification, prediction, recommendation, generated response, embedding, or other result.

A simplified model lifecycle is:

```text
Training or Configuration
           |
           v
     Model Available
           |
           v
     Input Supplied
           |
           v
        Inference
           |
           v
     Output Produced
           |
           v
  Application Processing
           |
           v
  Output Delivered or Used
```

Not every AI system requires model training by the deploying organization. For example, an enterprise may consume inference services from an externally hosted foundation model.

**GRC consideration:** Inference creates operational risks that may differ from development risks. Relevant concerns include unauthorized input data, prompt injection, sensitive-data exposure, inappropriate outputs, excessive resource consumption, and unsafe downstream actions.

### 3.7 AI output

**Definition:** An AI output is a result produced by an AI model or system.

Examples include:

- A predicted fraud probability.
- A classification label.
- A generated customer response.
- A summarized contract.
- A recommended security control.
- A synthetic image.
- A proposed sequence of actions.

An AI output is not automatically accurate, complete, unbiased, authorized, or suitable for its intended purpose.

Its reliability depends on factors such as system design, input quality, model capabilities, operating conditions, and the evaluation method.

**GRC consideration:** Define the permitted use of outputs, the level of human review required, the consequences of incorrect outputs, and the conditions under which an output must be rejected or escalated.

### 3.8 AI application

**Definition:** An AI application is a software application that incorporates or consumes AI capabilities to provide a user-facing or system-facing function.

An application may use an AI model through an API, host a model internally, or combine multiple models and conventional software components.

For example, a contract-review application may combine:

- A document upload interface.
- Optical character recognition.
- A language model.
- Retrieval from approved legal guidance.
- Rules that identify missing clauses.
- An approval workflow.
- Audit logging.

The application is the operational product. Its model is only one component.

**GRC consideration:** Application-level risk assessment must include conventional software security, identity and access management, API security, data protection, business logic, third-party dependencies, and the security characteristics of its AI components.

### 3.9 AI capability

**Definition:** An AI capability is a function that an AI model or system can perform under specified conditions.

Examples include:

- Classification.
- Prediction.
- Summarization.
- Translation.
- Content generation.
- Information extraction.
- Semantic search.
- Planning.
- Tool selection.

Capabilities should be described in operational terms rather than inferred from marketing labels.

For example, describing a system as an “AI assistant” does not establish whether it can access files, browse external websites, execute code, or modify business records.

**GRC consideration:** Document the system's actual capabilities, including those exposed through APIs, plugins, connectors, and tools. Capabilities influence the threat model and the controls required to constrain system behavior.

### 3.10 AI autonomy

**Definition:** AI autonomy describes the degree to which a system can perform tasks or select actions without direct human intervention at each step.

Autonomy is not a single universal technical measure. It is better assessed across the specific activities and permissions available to a system.

A conceptual progression is:

| Operational pattern | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Human-operated AI | A person initiates each interaction and decides what to do with the output | An analyst asks a model to summarize a report. |
| AI-assisted workflow | AI performs a defined task while a person retains responsibility for subsequent decisions | AI classifies a security alert for analyst review. |
| Human-approved execution | AI proposes an action, but a person must approve execution | AI prepares an access-change request that requires approval. |
| Bounded automated execution | AI can execute specified actions within predefined constraints | AI closes qualifying low-risk tickets under an approved policy. |
| Multi-step autonomous execution | AI can select and execute multiple actions toward an objective within its assigned permissions | An agent investigates an issue, queries authorized systems, and initiates permitted remediation steps. |

This progression is illustrative rather than a standardized maturity model.

A system may be autonomous in one dimension but tightly constrained in another. For example, it may independently select a sequence of steps while having read-only access to enterprise data.

**GRC consideration:** Assess autonomy in relation to available permissions, action scope, reversibility, impact, supervision, and the ability to interrupt execution. Do not infer risk solely from the label “autonomous.”

## 4. Distinguishing the AI System, Model, Algorithm, Data, and Application

One of the most important terminology distinctions in AI security governance is the difference between the system as a whole and its individual components.

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable.

### 4.1 Conceptual relationship

```text
                       AI APPLICATION
                              |
                              v
                        AI SYSTEM
                              |
              +---------------+---------------+
              |               |               |
          AI MODEL       DATA SOURCES     APPLICATION LOGIC
              |               |               |
              v               v               v
       Model inference   Input and context   Workflow execution
              |               |               |
              +---------------+---------------+
                              |
                              v
                        SYSTEM OUTPUT
                              |
                              v
                   Human or System Action
```

This is a simplified conceptual view. A real implementation may contain several models, data stores, orchestration services, and external dependencies.

### 4.2 Comparison table

| Concept | What it represents | Example | Typical security question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Algorithm | A computational procedure | A procedure for optimizing model parameters | Is the algorithm appropriate for the intended task? |
| Model | A computational representation used to produce outputs | A trained fraud-detection model | Has the model been evaluated for relevant failure modes? |
| Dataset | An organized collection of data | Historical transaction records | Is the data authorized, protected, and fit for purpose? |
| Inference | The process of producing an output from an input | Scoring a new transaction | Can inputs be manipulated or sensitive information exposed? |
| AI capability | A function the system can perform | Summarizing a contract | Is the capability reliable enough for its intended use? |
| AI application | Software incorporating AI functionality | A contract-review platform | Are the application and its integrations securely designed? |
| AI system | The complete system within the defined assessment boundary | A contract-review platform, including its model, retrieval service, identity controls, and integrations | Have all relevant components, interfaces, and dependencies been assessed? |
| AI output | A result produced by the model or system | A summary identifying contractual obligations | Is the output accurate enough, appropriately reviewed, and used within its permitted scope? |

### 4.3 Why the distinction matters during risk assessment

Suppose a company purchases a contract-analysis application that uses a third-party language model.

The company evaluates the model provider's published security documentation and concludes that the solution is secure.

That conclusion is not justified by the evidence alone.

The application may still have:

- Excessive permissions to the document repository.
- Weak authentication or session management.
- Inadequate tenant isolation.
- Poorly configured retrieval permissions.
- Insufficient logging.
- Vulnerable APIs.
- Inappropriate data-retention settings.
- An unsafe workflow for approving AI-generated legal recommendations.

The model provider's security measures may address some risks, but they do not automatically address the entire application or the organization's deployment.

The organization must distinguish **model assurance**, **application security**, **data governance**, **integration security**, and **operational governance**.

These are related assurance domains, not interchangeable evidence categories.

## 5. The AI Lifecycle: Foundational Terminology

AI governance also requires a common vocabulary for the stages through which AI systems are developed, deployed, used, maintained, and retired.

A simplified lifecycle is:

```text
Business Need and Intended Purpose
                 |
                 v
       Requirements and Design
                 |
                 v
       Data Preparation and
         Model Development
                 |
                 v
       Testing and Evaluation
                 |
                 v
       Deployment and Integration
                 |
                 v
       Production Operation
                 |
                 v
       Monitoring and Maintenance
                 |
                 v
          Modification or
             Retirement
```

This representation is illustrative. Lifecycle stages vary according to the AI technology, organizational operating model, and applicable framework.

### 5.1 Development

**Definition:** Development encompasses activities involved in creating or configuring an AI model or system.

Depending on the implementation, development may include:

- Selecting an AI approach.
- Preparing datasets.
- Training or fine-tuning a model.
- Configuring a foundation model.
- Developing prompts and orchestration logic.
- Integrating external services.
- Implementing application interfaces.

**Governance considerations:** Development activities require appropriate data controls, secure engineering practices, access restrictions, documented requirements, and protection of development environments.

### 5.2 Training

**Definition:** Training is the process of adjusting a model's parameters or internal representation using data and a specified learning procedure.

Training may involve large datasets and substantial computational resources.

Not every AI deployment includes training. An organization may use a pretrained model without modifying its parameters.

**Governance considerations:** Relevant concerns include data provenance, data quality, unauthorized data use, training-data poisoning, access control, reproducibility, and the security of the training environment.

### 5.3 Fine-tuning

**Definition:** Fine-tuning is a process in which a pretrained model is further trained on a more specific dataset or task to adapt its behavior.

Fine-tuning differs from simply providing additional instructions in a prompt. Depending on the method, fine-tuning changes model parameters, whereas prompting generally changes the information or instructions supplied at inference time.

**Governance considerations:** Assess the source and authorization of fine-tuning data, the security of the training pipeline, evaluation coverage, model versioning, and the consequences of changes in model behavior.

### 5.4 Testing and evaluation

**Definition:** Testing and evaluation are activities used to determine whether an AI model or system satisfies specified requirements and performs acceptably under relevant conditions.

Evaluation may address:

- Accuracy and task performance.
- Robustness.
- Safety.
- Security.
- Privacy.
- Fairness.
- Reliability.
- Interpretability or explainability, where relevant.
- Compliance with defined requirements.

A model's performance on a benchmark does not establish that the complete system is secure or suitable for every deployment.

**Governance considerations:** Define acceptance criteria, test scenarios, evidence requirements, accountable approvers, and the conditions under which the system must not be deployed.

### 5.5 Deployment

**Definition:** Deployment is the process of making an AI model or system available for use in a target environment.

Deployment may involve hosting, API configuration, application integration, identity configuration, data connectivity, and operational monitoring.

**Governance considerations:** Deployment should be subject to appropriate authorization, configuration review, security testing, change management, access controls, and readiness criteria.

### 5.6 Inference and production operation

Inference occurs when the model processes inputs to generate outputs. Production operation encompasses the broader activities involved in running the AI system in its intended environment.

These activities may include user interactions, data access, output handling, external tool calls, logging, and human review.

**Governance considerations:** Production controls should reflect the actual operating conditions, including changes in input patterns, data sensitivity, model behavior, usage volume, and the consequences of system errors.

### 5.7 Monitoring

**Definition:** Monitoring is the ongoing observation of system behavior, performance, security, and relevant operating conditions.

Monitoring may include:

- Availability and latency.
- Error rates.
- Output quality.
- Changes in data or input distributions.
- Security events.
- Policy violations.
- Unusual usage patterns.
- Evidence of unauthorized access or abuse.

Monitoring requirements depend on the system's purpose, risk, architecture, and operating environment.

**Governance considerations:** Establish monitoring responsibilities, thresholds, escalation procedures, evidence retention, and remediation workflows. Monitoring is not effective if identified issues have no defined response process.

### 5.8 Drift

**Definition:** Drift refers to a change that causes the relationship between a model, its inputs, its operating environment, or its expected outputs to differ from the conditions under which performance was previously assessed.

Two commonly discussed forms are:

- **Data drift:** A change in the distribution or characteristics of input data.
- **Concept drift:** A change in the relationship between relevant inputs and the outcome the model is intended to predict.

These terms are related but not identical.

For example, a fraud-detection model may encounter new transaction patterns after a payment platform changes. The change may affect model performance even if the model itself has not been modified.

**Governance considerations:** Define relevant performance indicators, acceptable thresholds, reassessment triggers, and escalation procedures. Drift should be evaluated in relation to the system's intended purpose rather than treated as an automatic indication of a security incident.

### 5.9 Retirement

**Definition:** Retirement is the controlled withdrawal of an AI model or system from operational use.

Retirement may involve disabling services, revoking credentials, removing integrations, retaining required evidence, managing data according to retention obligations, and updating system inventories.

**Governance considerations:** Confirm that decommissioning does not leave exposed endpoints, unused service accounts, unnecessary data copies, active API credentials, or undocumented dependencies.

## 6. AI Terminology from a GRC Perspective

Technical definitions become operationally valuable when connected to governance responsibilities and evidence requirements.

The following table translates foundational concepts into practical GRC questions.

| Term | Governance question | Potential evidence |
|---|---|---|
| AI system | Has the system and its assessment boundary been identified? | AI inventory, system architecture, system description |
| AI model | Which model or model version is used, and who is responsible for it? | Model inventory, provider documentation, model card where available |
| Algorithm | What computational approach is used, and what assumptions does it make? | Technical documentation, design records, evaluation reports |
| Dataset | Are data sources authorized and appropriate for their intended use? | Data lineage, data inventory, classification records, approvals |
| Inference | What information is processed during operation? | Data-flow diagrams, API specifications, processing records |
| AI output | How is the output validated and used? | Evaluation results, output-handling procedures, human-review records |
| AI capability | What can the system actually do? | Functional specifications, threat model, permission inventory |
| AI autonomy | Which actions can occur without direct human intervention? | Workflow diagrams, tool permissions, authorization rules |
| Development | Are development activities subject to appropriate security and governance controls? | Secure development procedures, access records, change records |
| Testing and evaluation | What evidence demonstrates that acceptance criteria have been met? | Test reports, evaluation results, documented approvals |
| Deployment | Was the system authorized for its intended production environment? | Deployment approval, security review, release records |
| Monitoring | Are material changes, failures, and security events detected and addressed? | Monitoring dashboards, alerts, incident records, remediation evidence |
| Drift | Are changes in operating conditions or performance evaluated? | Performance reports, reassessment records, monitoring thresholds |
| Retirement | Has the system been withdrawn without leaving unnecessary access or exposure? | Decommissioning checklist, credential revocation evidence, inventory updates |

The evidence listed above is illustrative. A specific framework, contractual requirement, or regulatory obligation may require different or additional records.

### 6.1 Questions for an AI governance review

When assessing an AI system, a GRC professional should be able to answer the following:

**Scope and ownership**
1. What is the intended purpose of the system?
2. Which components form part of the assessment boundary?
3. Who owns the business outcome?
4. Who is responsible for the model, application, data, and operational controls?

**Data and processing**
5. What data enters the system?
6. Which data sources are used for training, retrieval, or inference?
7. What information can be included in outputs?
8. Where are the relevant data and services hosted or processed?

**Capabilities and permissions**
9. What can the system generate, retrieve, recommend, or execute?
10. Which systems and resources can it access?
11. Which actions require human approval?
12. What prevents the system from exceeding its authorized scope?

**Assurance and operation**
13. What testing has been completed?
14. What evidence supports deployment approval?
15. How are failures, security events, and performance changes detected?
16. What conditions trigger reassessment, suspension, or retirement?

These questions help establish a consistent assessment baseline. They do not replace a formal risk assessment or the requirements of a particular standard.

## 7. Common Terminology Errors and Their Governance Consequences

Terminology mistakes often lead to practical control failures. The following examples illustrate why precise language matters.

| Incorrect assumption | Why it is incorrect | Potential consequence |
|---|---|---|
| “AI, machine learning, and generative AI are the same thing.” | These terms describe different levels or categories of technology. | Inadequate classification and inappropriate risk assessment. |
| “The model and the AI application are the same component.” | The application may include many components beyond the model. | Application-level vulnerabilities remain unassessed. |
| “A pretrained model does not need governance because we did not train it.” | Deployment, integration, data use, and operational behavior still create risks. | Third-party and operational risks are overlooked. |
| “An accurate model is necessarily a secure model.” | Accuracy and security are different properties. | Security weaknesses remain undetected despite good benchmark results. |
| “Inference does not involve sensitive data because the model is already trained.” | Inference inputs may contain confidential or personal information. | Sensitive information may be exposed through logs, providers, or outputs. |
| “All AI outputs are decisions.” | Outputs may be suggestions, classifications, generated content, or other results. | Governance requirements may be incorrectly assigned. |
| “An AI assistant is not autonomous because a human starts the interaction.” | A system may perform multiple actions after the initial request. | Tool permissions and execution risks are underestimated. |
| “A model card proves the entire system is compliant.” | Model documentation covers only selected aspects of the model and its intended use. | Incomplete assurance and unsupported compliance claims. |
| “Drift always means the system has been compromised.” | Drift can result from legitimate changes in data or operating conditions. | Incorrect incident classification and ineffective response. |
| “Retiring an application means deleting its user interface.” | Other components, credentials, integrations, and data may remain active. | Residual access, exposure, and incomplete decommissioning. |

### 7.1 Practical case study: An enterprise AI assistant

A telecommunications company deploys an AI assistant for customer-service representatives.

The assistant:

- Uses a third-party language model.
- Retrieves information from an internal knowledge base.
- Accesses customer records through an application API.
- Generates recommended responses.
- Can initiate selected customer-service workflows.

The project team describes the solution as “a chatbot.”

That description is not wrong, but it is insufficient for governance.

A more complete characterization identifies the application as an AI system that incorporates a generative model, retrieval functionality, enterprise data access, and workflow execution capabilities.

This description immediately exposes several assessment areas:

| Identified characteristic | Governance implication |
|---|---|
| Third-party language model | Assess provider risk, service terms, data handling, and relevant dependencies. |
| Internal knowledge-base retrieval | Assess document permissions, source integrity, and the risk of malicious instructions embedded in retrieved content. |
| Customer-record access | Assess authentication, authorization, data minimization, and protection of personal information. |
| Generated responses | Evaluate factual reliability, inappropriate disclosures, and required human review. |
| Workflow initiation | Assess action permissions, approval requirements, transaction integrity, and safeguards against unauthorized execution. |
| Production operation | Establish monitoring, incident handling, change management, and periodic reassessment. |

**Key lesson:** The label “chatbot” describes the interaction pattern, not the complete technical architecture, operational authority, or risk profile.

## 8. Part 1 Summary

This part established the foundational terminology needed to describe AI systems consistently in enterprise governance and cybersecurity contexts.

The central distinctions are:

- **Artificial intelligence** is the broad field; it does not identify a single implementation method.
- **An AI system** is the system under consideration and may include multiple technical and organizational components.
- **An AI model** is one component that produces outputs according to its design and configuration.
- **An algorithm** is a computational procedure, while a model may embody learned parameters or another representation.
- **A dataset** is an organized collection of information used for purposes such as training, evaluation, retrieval, or inference.
- **Inference** is the process of producing an output from an input.
- **An AI application** incorporates AI capabilities into a usable software product or service.
- **AI capability** describes what a system can do, while **AI autonomy** concerns the degree to which it can perform tasks or select actions without direct human intervention.
- **The AI lifecycle** includes development, testing, deployment, operation, monitoring, modification, and retirement, although its precise stages vary by system.

For GRC practitioners, the practical objective is to convert these definitions into consistent inventories, assessment boundaries, control requirements, evidence expectations, and accountability arrangements.

### Core Principle

**Define what the AI system is, what its components do, what data it processes, and what actions it can perform before deciding which risks and controls apply.**

Precise terminology is the foundation of defensible AI security governance.

## 9. Major Categories of Artificial Intelligence

Understanding AI terminology requires distinguishing between the broad concept of artificial intelligence, the approaches used to implement AI, and the capabilities exposed by a deployed system.

These categories are related, but they describe different dimensions of technology. A system can combine several approaches, and its classification may depend on whether the discussion concerns its underlying model, its application architecture, or its operational behavior.

### 9.1 Narrow AI (Artificial Narrow Intelligence)

**Definition:** Narrow AI refers to AI designed or configured to perform a particular task or a limited set of tasks rather than possessing unrestricted general-purpose capabilities across domains.

Examples include:
- Fraud detection systems.
- Facial recognition systems.
- Recommendation engines.
- Predictive maintenance models.
- AI-based malware classification.
- Document classification systems.
- Customer-service assistants.

Narrow AI can perform highly sophisticated tasks. The term does not imply that a system is simple, unreliable, or incapable of operating autonomously within a defined scope.

For example, an AI system may analyze millions of network events, identify suspicious patterns, correlate security alerts, and recommend remediation actions. Its operational capabilities may be substantial even though its purpose remains bounded.

**Enterprise example**

A telecommunications company uses an AI model to predict which network components are likely to fail. The model analyzes historical performance, environmental conditions, and operational telemetry.

The model may be highly effective at predicting equipment failures, but this does not establish that it can independently diagnose every network problem or safely perform every remediation action.

**GRC implications:**
- Define the intended purpose and operational boundaries.
- Identify the conditions under which the system is expected to operate.
- Document unsupported use cases.
- Evaluate performance against the intended task.
- Restrict the system from being used for purposes that have not been adequately assessed.

### 9.2 Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

**Definition:** Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is a term used to describe a hypothetical or proposed form of AI capable of performing a broad range of intellectual tasks at a level of generality comparable to human intelligence.

There is no universally accepted operational definition or universally agreed test that establishes when a system qualifies as AGI.

The term should therefore be used carefully in technical, governance, and regulatory discussions.

Current systems may demonstrate strong performance across multiple domains without establishing that they possess general intelligence in the stronger sense implied by some definitions of AGI.

**GRC implications:**

Organizations should not base their current security controls solely on speculative assumptions about AGI. Governance should focus on the system's demonstrated capabilities, tested limitations, access permissions, foreseeable failure modes, and potential impact.

Where future capabilities are relevant to strategic planning, assumptions should be documented separately from verified system characteristics.

### 9.3 Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)

**Definition:** Artificial Superintelligence (ASI) is a hypothetical concept referring to AI that substantially exceeds human intellectual capabilities across a broad range of relevant domains.

ASI is primarily discussed in connection with long-term AI research, strategic risk, and theoretical questions about advanced AI capabilities.

It is not a standardized operational classification for ordinary enterprise AI inventories.

**GRC implications:**

For present-day enterprise governance, the more actionable questions concern actual capabilities, the scope of authorized actions, the reliability of safeguards, and the consequences of failure.

Theoretical scenarios may inform strategic risk discussions, but they should not be confused with evidence about the behavior of a particular deployed system.

### 9.4 Reactive systems and systems with persistent state

Some AI discussions distinguish between systems that respond to the current input and systems that retain or use information about prior events.

This distinction is useful, but it should not be confused with a definitive classification of intelligence.

A conventional model may process an input without maintaining application-level memory. An application built around that model may nevertheless retain conversation history, user preferences, retrieved information, or workflow state.

**Example**

Two customer-service assistants use the same language model:

- **Assistant A:** Processes each request independently.
- **Assistant B:** Retains authorized conversation history and uses it to personalize subsequent responses.

The underlying model may be identical, while the application behavior and data-governance implications differ.

**GRC implications:**
- Identify what information persists between interactions.
- Establish retention and deletion requirements.
- Determine whether retained information contains personal or confidential data.
- Prevent unauthorized users from accessing another user's history.
- Define whether stored information can influence future outputs or actions.

**Important distinction:** Persistent state is an architectural property. It does not, by itself, establish that a system has advanced reasoning capabilities or human-like memory.

## 10. Machine Learning: Foundational Terminology

Machine learning (ML) is one of the principal approaches used to implement AI.

### 10.1 Machine Learning (ML)

**Definition:** Machine learning is a field of AI in which computational systems use data or experience to improve their performance on a task, typically through the adjustment of model parameters or the learning of patterns.

Instead of relying exclusively on explicitly programmed rules for every situation, an ML system can learn relationships from examples.

Consider two fraud-detection approaches.

**Rule-based approach:**

```text
IF transaction_amount > defined_threshold
AND transaction_country != customer's usual country
THEN flag transaction for review
```

**Machine-learning approach:**

```text
Historical Transaction Data
            |
            v
     ML Model Training
            |
            v
   Learned Model Parameters
            |
            v
    New Transaction Input
            |
            v
   Fraud Risk Prediction
            |
            v
       Review Decision
```

A rule-based system can be effective, and ML is not automatically superior. The appropriate approach depends on the problem, available data, operating conditions, explainability requirements, and consequences of errors.

**GRC implications:**
- Establish how the model was developed and evaluated.
- Assess the suitability and provenance of its training data.
- Identify relevant performance limitations.
- Determine how model changes are approved.
- Monitor whether the model remains suitable for its intended purpose.

### 10.2 Supervised learning

**Definition:** Supervised learning is a machine-learning approach in which a model learns from examples containing inputs and associated target labels or values.

The objective is to learn a relationship that can be used to predict labels or values for new inputs.

Common applications include:
- Email spam classification.
- Fraud detection.
- Customer churn prediction.
- Malware classification.
- Predictive maintenance.
- Credit-risk estimation.

**Example**

A security team trains a model using historical network events labelled as either malicious or benign.

The model learns patterns associated with the available labels and applies those patterns to new events.

However, labels can be inaccurate, incomplete, outdated, or biased. A model may also perform poorly when new attack techniques differ substantially from those represented in the training data.

**GRC implications:**

Assess label quality, dataset representativeness, class imbalance, false-positive and false-negative rates, and the consequences of incorrect predictions.

### 10.3 Unsupervised learning

**Definition:** Unsupervised learning is a machine-learning approach that identifies patterns, structures, or groupings in data without relying on predefined target labels in the same manner as supervised learning.

Common applications include:
- Clustering.
- Anomaly detection.
- Customer segmentation.
- Exploratory data analysis.
- Identification of unusual system behavior.

**Example**

A security analytics system analyzes authentication patterns and identifies activity that differs significantly from observed behavior.

The resulting anomaly is not necessarily malicious. It may reflect legitimate behavior, a change in operating conditions, or an error in the data.

**GRC implications:**
- Establish how anomalies are interpreted.
- Define thresholds and escalation procedures.
- Assess false-positive and false-negative consequences.
- Avoid treating model-generated anomalies as confirmed security incidents without appropriate investigation.

### 10.4 Semi-supervised learning

**Definition:** Semi-supervised learning combines a relatively small amount of labelled data with a larger amount of unlabelled data during model development.

This approach can be useful when obtaining reliable labels is expensive, time-consuming, or dependent on specialist review.

**Example**

An organization has a large collection of security events but only a small subset that has been manually classified by incident responders.

A semi-supervised approach may use both labelled and unlabelled data to develop a model.

**GRC implications:**

Document which data is labelled, how labels were obtained, what assumptions are made about unlabelled data, and how the resulting model is evaluated.

### 10.5 Self-supervised learning

**Definition:** Self-supervised learning is an approach in which a model learns from data by generating training objectives from the data itself, rather than relying entirely on manually supplied labels.

For example, a language model may learn to predict a missing or subsequent token based on surrounding text.

Self-supervised learning is important in the development of many foundation models.

It does not mean that the model independently decides what it should learn in an unrestricted sense. The learning objective, data, architecture, and training process remain determined by the development approach.

**GRC implications:**

Assess the provenance and permitted use of training data, the integrity of the training pipeline, model evaluation, and the limitations of the resulting model.

### 10.6 Reinforcement learning

**Definition:** Reinforcement learning (RL) is a machine-learning approach in which an agent learns a policy for selecting actions through interaction with an environment and feedback associated with those actions.

Feedback is often represented through rewards or penalties.

A simplified process is:

```text
        Agent
          |
          | Selects Action
          v
     Environment
          |
          | Returns Outcome
          v
    Reward or Penalty
          |
          v
   Policy Adjustment
          |
          v
   Subsequent Action
```

Reinforcement learning is used in areas such as robotics, simulation, control systems, and selected AI optimization problems.

Reinforcement learning is also associated with some methods used to refine the behavior of language models, although the complete training process may involve several other techniques.

**GRC implications:**
- Define the objective and reward structure.
- Assess whether the reward encourages unintended behavior.
- Establish boundaries for experimentation.
- Evaluate performance outside the training environment.
- Restrict access to production systems during testing.
- Assess the consequences of actions taken during learning or optimization.

### 10.7 Deep learning

**Definition:** Deep learning is a subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn representations and patterns from data.

Deep learning supports many modern capabilities, including image recognition, speech processing, natural language processing, and generative AI.

**Relationship:**

```text
Artificial Intelligence
          |
          v
   Machine Learning
          |
          v
     Deep Learning
          |
          v
    Neural Networks
   with Multiple Layers
```

This diagram illustrates the broad conceptual relationship, not a complete taxonomy of every AI technique.

**GRC implications:**

Deep learning systems may require substantial computational resources and large datasets. Their evaluation may involve complex performance characteristics, and some models may be difficult to interpret fully.

Governance should therefore address data quality, model evaluation, deployment security, operational monitoring, and the limitations of explanations produced by the system.

### 10.8 Neural network

**Definition:** A neural network is a computational model composed of interconnected processing units arranged in structures that transform inputs into outputs.

In modern machine learning, neural networks typically contain parameters that are adjusted during training.

Neural networks can be used for classification, prediction, generation, and other tasks.

Not all AI systems use neural networks, and not all neural networks are large language models.

**GRC implications:**

When a neural network is used in a high-impact or security-sensitive application, evaluate its behavior under relevant operating conditions rather than assuming that the model architecture alone establishes its reliability or security.

### 10.9 Model parameters

**Definition:** Model parameters are internal values adjusted during training or other model-development processes and used by the model to produce outputs.

In a neural network, these commonly include weights and biases.

Parameters are distinct from configuration settings, prompts, and input data, although all may influence system behavior.

**Example**

A language model may have billions of parameters. Its responses are influenced by those parameters, the supplied input, the model architecture, and the surrounding application.

A prompt can influence the output without changing the model's trained parameters.

**GRC implications:**

Model parameters may be commercially sensitive or security-relevant. Protect model artifacts against unauthorized modification or disclosure where appropriate, and maintain sufficient versioning and provenance information to support change management and investigation.

### 10.10 Model training versus inference

Training and inference are distinct operational activities.

| Dimension | Training | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Develop or adjust a model | Use a model to produce outputs |
| Typical input | Training examples and associated learning signals | Operational inputs or prompts |
| Main activity | Learning or adjusting model parameters | Processing an input using the available model |
| Common environment | Development or training infrastructure | Application or production infrastructure |
| Key security concerns | Training-data poisoning, unauthorized dataset access, model artifact integrity | Input manipulation, sensitive-data exposure, unsafe outputs, resource abuse |
| Typical governance evidence | Dataset records, training documentation, evaluation results | Input/output handling rules, access logs, operational monitoring |

The distinction is important because the security controls required for training infrastructure are not identical to those required for an inference service.

An organization using a third-party model may not perform the original training, but it still needs to govern inference, integration, and operational use.

## 11. Statistical Prediction, Reasoning, and Decision-Making

AI terminology frequently includes words such as *prediction*, *reasoning*, and *decision*. These terms should be used precisely because they imply different functions and levels of responsibility.

### 11.1 Prediction

**Definition:** Prediction is the estimation of an unknown value, outcome, class, or event based on available information.

Examples include:
- Estimating the probability of fraud.
- Predicting equipment failure.
- Forecasting customer demand.
- Classifying an email as potentially malicious.

A prediction is an estimate, not a guarantee.

**GRC implication:** Define acceptable error rates and determine how predictions will be validated before they influence consequential decisions.

### 11.2 Classification

**Definition:** Classification is the assignment of an input to one or more categories according to a defined classification scheme.

Examples include:
- Classifying an email as spam or legitimate.
- Categorizing a security alert by severity.
- Assigning a document a sensitivity label.
- Categorizing a support request by topic.

Classification systems can produce incorrect results, particularly when inputs are ambiguous, incomplete, or unlike the data used during development.

**GRC implication:** Establish the consequences of misclassification, define review or escalation requirements, and monitor error rates where appropriate.

### 11.3 Recommendation

**Definition:** A recommendation is a proposed option or course of action generated using information, rules, analysis, or model outputs.

Examples include:
- Recommending a security remediation.
- Suggesting a product.
- Proposing an access review.
- Identifying a potential incident response action.

A recommendation does not necessarily execute the proposed action.

**GRC implication:** Establish whether recommendations are advisory or binding, who is accountable for decisions based on them, and when independent validation is required.

### 11.4 Decision

**Definition:** A decision is a selection among available options or outcomes.

In an AI-enabled process, a decision may be produced by an automated rule, an AI model, a human operator, or a combination of these.

The term should not be used to imply that every AI-generated output constitutes an authoritative business decision.

**Example**

A credit-risk model produces a risk score. A separate decision engine applies business rules, eligibility requirements, and approval thresholds to determine the next step in the application process.

The model's prediction and the business decision are separate elements of the workflow.

**GRC implication:** Identify which component generates the output, which component determines the action, and who holds responsibility for the final outcome.

### 11.5 Reasoning

**Definition:** Reasoning refers to the process of deriving conclusions, evaluating relationships, or selecting steps based on information and assumptions.

In AI, the term is used in different ways depending on the technology and context. Some systems use explicit symbolic rules or search procedures; others produce outputs through learned statistical patterns.

A model's ability to generate a convincing explanation does not, by itself, prove that the explanation faithfully represents the internal processes that produced the answer.

**GRC implication:** Evaluate reasoning-related claims against the system's actual behavior and intended use. For consequential decisions, consider whether the evidence, assumptions, and conclusions can be independently verified.

### 11.6 Explainability and interpretability

These terms are related but should not be treated as universally interchangeable.

**Explainability** generally concerns the ability to provide explanations about a model's behavior, outputs, or relevant factors.

**Interpretability** generally concerns the extent to which a model's operation or behavior can be understood by people.

Terminology varies across research, standards, and implementation contexts. Organizations should document how these concepts are defined in their own governance framework.

For example, a simple decision tree may be relatively easy to inspect, whereas a complex neural network may require additional methods to investigate why it produced a particular result.

An explanation generated by an AI system may itself be incomplete or inaccurate. It should not automatically be treated as definitive evidence of the model's internal operation.

**GRC implication:** Determine what level of explanation is necessary for the use case, who needs the explanation, how its reliability will be assessed, and what supporting evidence must be retained.

Consider the following hypothetical system:

> A telecommunications operator uses an AI-enabled platform to analyze customer complaints, retrieve relevant internal procedures, generate recommended responses, and allow authorized employees to initiate selected service workflows.

> AI chatbot for customer support.

This description provides too little information for an effective initial risk assessment.

A stronger entry would distinguish the following:

| Attribute | Example description |
|---|---|
| Business purpose | Support customer-service employees in handling complaints and service requests |

| Outputs | Summaries, suggested responses, and workflow recommendations |
| Operational authority | Employees review outputs and authorize permitted service actions |
| External dependencies | Model provider and any third-party services used by the application |
| Primary assessment concerns | Data exposure, output reliability, access control, retrieval security, and unauthorized workflow execution |

1. Which components belong within the AI system's assessment boundary?
2. Which data sources contain customer or confidential information?
3. What can the model generate, and what can the application execute?
4. Which actions require explicit human authorization?
5. What evidence demonstrates that access permissions are correctly enforced?
6. How will the organization detect inappropriate outputs or unauthorized actions?
7. What changes would trigger a reassessment?

The goal is not to produce the longest possible system description. It is to establish an accurate, decision-useful description that supports risk identification, control selection, ownership assignment, and assurance.

## 14. Part 2 Summary

This part expanded the foundational vocabulary by examining major AI categories, machine-learning approaches, and concepts related to prediction and decision-making.

The principal distinctions are:

- **Narrow AI, AGI, and ASI** describe different concepts concerning the breadth of AI capabilities. AGI and ASI do not constitute universally standardized operational classifications.
- **Machine learning** is one approach within AI; **deep learning** is a subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers.

- **Explainability and interpretability** concern different aspects of understanding model behavior, although their precise definitions vary across contexts.
- A system's technical label does not establish its complete security or governance requirements.

For enterprise GRC, these distinctions help determine what must be assessed, which evidence is relevant, and where accountability resides.

### Core Principle

**Use terminology to describe observable capabilities, system boundaries, and operational responsibilities—not merely to repeat technology labels.**

A defensible AI governance assessment begins with an accurate description of what the system does, how it operates, and how its outputs influence business activities.

## 15. Generative Artificial Intelligence

### 15.1 Generative AI

**Definition:** Generative Artificial Intelligence (Generative AI or GenAI) refers to AI systems capable of generating new content, such as text, images, audio, video, software code, or structured data, based on learned patterns and the information supplied to them.

Generative AI differs from traditional predictive or classification systems in its primary output.

A classification model might determine whether an email is malicious. A generative model might explain why the email appears suspicious, summarize its contents, or produce a proposed incident report.

Generative AI systems can be used for:

- Document summarization.
- Content generation.
- Code assistance.
- Conversational interfaces.
- Synthetic data generation.
- Image and video generation.
- Information extraction.
- Knowledge assistance.
- Security analysis and reporting.

However, the ability to generate plausible content does not guarantee that the content is accurate, complete, safe, or appropriate.

**Enterprise example**

A cybersecurity team uses a generative AI application to summarize security incidents.

The application receives incident descriptions, correlates information from approved sources, and generates an executive summary.

Potential failure modes include:

- Incorrectly describing the root cause.
- Omitting a critical indicator of compromise.
- Inventing an event that did not occur.
- Including confidential information in the summary.
- Recommending a remediation action that is inappropriate for the environment.

The governance challenge is not simply whether the system can generate a summary. It is whether the summary is reliable enough for its intended use and whether the system operates within approved security and data-handling boundaries.

**GRC considerations:**
1. Define the permitted use cases.
2. Establish evaluation criteria for generated outputs.
3. Identify the consequences of inaccurate or misleading content.
4. Protect sensitive information supplied to the system.
5. Define human-review and approval requirements.
6. Establish monitoring and incident-escalation procedures.

### 15.2 Discriminative AI versus Generative AI

**Discriminative AI** generally focuses on distinguishing among categories or estimating relationships between inputs and target outcomes.

**Generative AI** focuses on producing new content or samples according to a learned model.

These are useful conceptual distinctions, but they are not mutually exclusive classifications for every AI system.

| Dimension | Discriminative approach | Generative approach |
|---|---|---|
| Typical objective | Classify inputs or predict target values | Generate content or data |
| Typical output | Label, score, probability, or prediction | Text, image, audio, code, or another generated artifact |
| Example | Classifying a network event as suspicious | Generating an explanation of a suspicious network event |
| Typical evaluation | Classification metrics, calibration, prediction error | Task quality, factual accuracy, relevance, safety, and other use-case-specific measures |
| Security concerns | Evasion, data poisoning, model manipulation, and inappropriate reliance on predictions | Prompt injection, sensitive-data disclosure, misleading content, unsafe outputs, and misuse |
| Governance focus | Reliability, decision impact, data quality, and error handling | Output quality, content safety, data handling, and the consequences of generated content |

**Important distinction:** A generative model can also support classification, and a discriminative model can be incorporated into a generative AI workflow. The classification describes a primary modeling objective, not necessarily every function of the complete system.

### 15.3 Synthetic data

**Definition:** Synthetic data is artificially generated data intended to reproduce selected characteristics or relationships of real-world data without necessarily reproducing the original records.

Synthetic data can be generated through statistical techniques, generative models, simulations, or other methods.

Potential applications include:
- Software testing.
- Model development.
- Privacy-conscious data analysis.
- Security testing.
- Scenario generation.
- Rare-event simulation.

Synthetic data is not automatically anonymous, unbiased, representative, or safe.

If synthetic data reproduces identifiable information from its source, it may retain privacy risks. It may also fail to represent important characteristics of the real population or environment.

**GRC considerations:**
- Document the generation method and intended use.
- Assess whether sensitive information can be reproduced.
- Evaluate representativeness and data quality.
- Identify limitations when using synthetic data for testing or model development.
- Avoid assuming that synthetic data eliminates privacy or regulatory obligations.

## 16. Foundation Models

### 16.1 Foundation model

**Definition:** A foundation model is a model trained on broad data, generally at substantial scale, that can be adapted to support a range of downstream tasks.

Foundation models are commonly associated with language, vision, audio, and multimodal applications.

They can be used through prompting, retrieval augmentation, fine-tuning, or other adaptation techniques.

A foundation model is not necessarily generative, although many widely used foundation models are generative.

**Example**

An enterprise adopts a pretrained foundation model for several applications:

- Customer-service assistance.
- Internal document search.
- Contract summarization.
- Security-report generation.

The same underlying model may be used in several applications, but each application can have different data sources, permissions, intended purposes, and risk profiles.

**GRC considerations:**
- Maintain an inventory of foundation models and their uses.
- Record provider, model identifier, version, and relevant contractual terms.
- Assess the provider's available documentation and assurance evidence.
- Determine whether the model is externally hosted or internally deployed.
- Evaluate the suitability of the model for each intended use.
- Reassess applications when material model or configuration changes occur.

**Key distinction:** Assessing a foundation model does not replace assessing the applications that use it.

### 16.2 Pretrained model

**Definition:** A pretrained model is a model that has already undergone a training process before being used or further adapted for a particular purpose.

Pretraining can establish general-purpose capabilities that can subsequently be adapted to specific tasks.

Organizations frequently consume pretrained models through APIs or deploy downloadable model artifacts without performing the original pretraining themselves.

**Example**

A company integrates a pretrained language model into an internal knowledge assistant.

The company may not control the original training process, but it controls or influences the application's prompts, retrieval sources, permissions, workflow design, and deployment configuration.

**GRC considerations:**
- Understand the provider's stated intended use and limitations.
- Review the available model documentation.
- Assess the organization's own deployment and integration.
- Verify whether the proposed use is permitted under applicable terms.
- Identify the evidence needed to support the organization's risk acceptance.

### 16.3 Base model

**Definition:** A base model is a model in its underlying pretrained form before additional task-specific instruction tuning or other adaptations that materially change its intended interaction pattern.

The term is particularly common in discussions of language models, although usage varies across model families and providers.

A base language model may be trained primarily to predict the next token in a sequence. It may not follow user instructions as reliably as a model that has undergone instruction tuning.

**GRC considerations:**

Do not assume that a base model is suitable for direct deployment in a user-facing application. Evaluate the model's actual behavior, safety characteristics, intended use, and the additional controls needed for the target environment.

### 16.4 Instruction-tuned model

**Definition:** An instruction-tuned model is a model that has undergone additional training to improve its ability to respond to instructions or perform specified tasks.

Instruction tuning can make a model more useful in conversational and task-oriented applications.

It does not guarantee that the model will follow every instruction correctly or safely.

**Example**

A base language model may continue a text sequence, while an instruction-tuned model is optimized to respond to requests such as summarizing a report or explaining a technical concept.

**GRC considerations:**

Evaluate whether the model follows approved instructions reliably, how it responds to conflicting instructions, and whether additional application-level safeguards are necessary.

Instruction tuning should not be treated as a substitute for access control, authorization, output validation, or other security controls.

### 16.5 Fine-tuned model

**Definition:** A fine-tuned model is a model that has undergone additional training using a task-specific or domain-specific dataset to adapt its behavior.

Fine-tuning may improve performance for a particular use case, but the result depends on the data, training method, evaluation process, and intended application.

**Example**

A company fine-tunes a model using approved examples of internal incident reports to improve the consistency of generated security documentation.

The resulting model must still be evaluated for accuracy, sensitive-data handling, robustness, and unintended changes in behavior.

**GRC considerations:**
- Approve and document the fine-tuning dataset.
- Control access to training pipelines and model artifacts.
- Record the resulting model version.
- Evaluate relevant security and performance properties.
- Establish change-management and rollback procedures.

### 16.6 Model adaptation: comparison

| Technique | What changes? | Typical purpose | Governance concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prompting | Instructions and context supplied to the model | Guide behavior for a specific task | Instruction integrity, prompt injection, and exposure of sensitive information |
| Retrieval augmentation | Information supplied from external data sources at inference time | Provide current or organization-specific context | Data permissions, retrieval integrity, and malicious instructions in retrieved content |
| Fine-tuning | Model parameters are further trained or adjusted | Adapt behavior to a domain or task | Training-data governance, model integrity, and evaluation |
| Instruction tuning | Model training emphasizes following instructions | Improve instruction-following behavior | Reliability, safety, and instruction-conflict handling |
| Continued pretraining | Additional pretraining is performed on further data | Adapt or extend learned representations | Data provenance, training integrity, and unintended changes in model behavior |

These techniques are not mutually exclusive. A single application may combine several of them.

## 17. Large Language Models

### 17.1 Large Language Model (LLM)

**Definition:** A Large Language Model (LLM) is a language model with a large number of learned parameters, typically trained on substantial text or text-associated data, that can process and generate language.

Modern LLMs are commonly based on transformer architectures, although the term itself does not prescribe one specific architecture.

LLMs can support:
- Text generation.
- Summarization.
- Question answering.
- Translation.
- Information extraction.
- Code generation.
- Conversational interaction.
- Reasoning-like task performance.

The word *large* does not have a single universal numerical threshold that defines every LLM.

An LLM's performance depends on more than parameter count. Architecture, training data, training methods, context length, inference configuration, and application design can all influence behavior.

**GRC considerations:**
- Identify the model and version in use.
- Assess its intended purpose and documented limitations.
- Evaluate the application that exposes the model.
- Establish controls for input data, outputs, and external integrations.
- Define how errors and security issues will be detected and handled.

### 17.2 Token

**Definition:** A token is a unit of text or other encoded input processed by a language model.

Depending on the tokenizer, a token may represent a word, part of a word, punctuation, whitespace-associated text, or another encoded unit.

A token is not necessarily equivalent to a word or a character.

For example, the sentence:

> The system detected suspicious activity.

may be represented as several tokens, depending on the tokenizer and model.

Tokenization affects:
- Context-window usage.
- Processing costs.
- Input and output limits.
- Model latency.
- Some forms of resource-consumption analysis.

**GRC considerations:**

For externally hosted models, token usage may affect service cost and resource consumption. Applications should also consider limits on input size, output size, request frequency, and processing time to reduce the risk of resource exhaustion or uncontrolled expenditure.

### 17.3 Context window

**Definition:** A context window is the amount of tokenized information a model can process within a single inference context, subject to the model's implementation and service limits.

The context may include:
- System instructions.
- Developer instructions.
- User messages.
- Conversation history.
- Retrieved documents.
- Tool results.
- Other supplied information.

A larger context window allows more information to be supplied in one interaction, but it does not guarantee that every detail will be used correctly.

**Example**

An enterprise assistant receives a lengthy contract and several related policies. Even if the combined material fits within the model's context window, the assistant may still overlook a relevant clause or misinterpret a conflict between documents.

**GRC considerations:**
- Establish input-size limits.
- Assess whether long-context processing affects reliability.
- Protect confidential information included in context.
- Consider how conversation history is retained and reused.
- Test performance on realistic document sizes and complexity.

### 17.4 Prompt

**Definition:** A prompt is the input supplied to a generative model to guide its response or behavior.

A prompt may contain:
- A task description.
- Questions.
- Instructions.
- Examples.
- Contextual information.
- Formatting requirements.
- Data to analyze.

Prompts may be supplied directly by a user or assembled by an application.

**Example**

A contract-review application might supply a prompt instructing the model to identify termination clauses, summarize obligations, and cite the relevant sections of the supplied document.

The prompt can influence the model's behavior, but it does not inherently establish an enforceable security boundary.

**GRC considerations:**
- Define the intended purpose of system-level instructions.
- Protect application-managed prompts against unauthorized modification.
- Test how user inputs affect model behavior.
- Avoid relying on prompts alone to enforce permissions or prevent unauthorized actions.
- Evaluate the treatment of confidential information included in prompts.

### 17.5 System prompt

**Definition:** A system prompt is an instruction layer used by an application or model interface to establish high-level behavioral guidance, constraints, or task context.

The precise meaning and priority of a system prompt depend on the model interface and implementation.

A system prompt might instruct an assistant to answer only from approved knowledge sources, avoid revealing confidential information, or request human review for certain tasks.

However, a system prompt is not equivalent to an authorization mechanism, a network security control, or a guaranteed policy-enforcement boundary.

**GRC considerations:**

Use prompts to guide behavior, but enforce critical restrictions through independently implemented controls wherever feasible. For example, an instruction not to access unauthorized customer records should be backed by actual access-control checks.

### 17.6 Prompt injection

**Definition:** Prompt injection is an attack in which malicious or manipulative instructions supplied through user input or external content attempt to influence a model or AI application in ways that conflict with its intended instructions or security requirements.

Prompt injection may occur through direct user input or indirectly through content processed by the application, such as retrieved documents, webpages, or other external data.

**Example**

An internal knowledge assistant retrieves a document containing instructions telling the model to disclose confidential information or disregard its normal operating rules.

If the application does not adequately distinguish trusted instructions from untrusted content, the model may be influenced by the malicious text.

**GRC considerations:**
- Treat externally sourced content as potentially untrusted.
- Restrict the permissions available to the application.
- Separate instructions from data where the architecture permits.
- Validate tool calls and downstream actions.
- Test direct and indirect prompt-injection scenarios.
- Monitor for suspicious behavior and unauthorized data access.

Prompt injection is not solved reliably by adding a single warning sentence to a prompt. It requires a broader defense-in-depth approach.

### 17.7 Hallucination

**Definition:** In generative AI, a hallucination is an output that presents information as plausible or factual even though it is incorrect, unsupported, or not adequately grounded in the available evidence.

The term is widely used, although precise definitions and evaluation methods vary across contexts.

Examples include:
- Inventing a legal citation.
- Describing a security event that did not occur.
- Producing a nonexistent API parameter.
- Attributing a statement to a document that does not contain it.

A hallucination is not necessarily a cybersecurity attack. It may result from ordinary model limitations, insufficient context, ambiguous instructions, or other conditions.

**GRC considerations:**
- Define the level of factual reliability required for each use case.
- Use authoritative sources where appropriate.
- Validate critical outputs against source evidence.
- Require human review for consequential decisions.
- Record limitations and escalation requirements.

### 17.8 Grounding

**Definition:** Grounding refers to connecting a model's output to specified information, evidence, or context intended to support the response.

Grounding may involve retrieved documents, structured data, verified records, or other sources.

Grounding can improve factual support, but it does not guarantee correctness. The model may misinterpret the source, omit important qualifications, or produce a conclusion that the evidence does not justify.

**GRC considerations:**

Where grounding is important, evaluate source quality, authorization, freshness, traceability, and the correspondence between generated claims and supporting evidence.

### 17.9 Embedding

**Definition:** An embedding is a numerical representation of an item, such as text, an image, or another input, in a vector space intended to capture relevant features or relationships.

Embeddings are commonly used for semantic search, similarity comparison, retrieval, clustering, and other AI applications.

For example, a document-search system can represent documents and user queries as vectors and retrieve documents whose representations are sufficiently similar.

An embedding is not the original document, nor is it necessarily a complete or human-readable explanation of the represented content.

**GRC considerations:**
- Protect embedding stores where they may reveal sensitive information or relationships.
- Apply appropriate access controls to the underlying records and retrieval results.
- Evaluate whether embeddings can expose information about their source data.
- Monitor for unauthorized access and manipulation of retrieval indexes.

### 17.10 Vector database

**Definition:** A vector database is a data-management system designed to store, index, and retrieve vector representations, often to support similarity-based search.

Vector databases are frequently used in retrieval-augmented generation architectures.

They may store embeddings alongside identifiers, metadata, and references to source documents.

**GRC considerations:**
- Enforce access controls at the appropriate record or document level.
- Preserve the relationship between retrieved content and its source.
- Protect stored vectors and associated metadata.
- Establish data deletion and retention procedures.
- Test whether retrieval can expose documents outside the user's authorization.

**GRC implication:** Map data flows, trust boundaries, identity boundaries, and action paths. Identify where controls are enforced and where the organization relies on model behavior rather than independently verifiable mechanisms.

## 19. Practical Terminology Exercise: Classifying an Enterprise AI Solution

### Scenario

A telecommunications operator introduces an AI-powered security assistant for its Security Operations Center (SOC).

The solution can:

1. Summarize security alerts.
2. Retrieve information from internal incident-response procedures.
3. Recommend investigation steps.
4. Query authorized security tools.
5. Draft incident reports.
6. Initiate selected response workflows when permitted.

The project team refers to the solution as an “LLM chatbot.”

That description identifies one technology and an interaction style, but it does not describe the complete system.

### 19.1 Correct terminology classification

| Characteristic | Appropriate terminology | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Core language-processing component | Large language model | Identifies the model technology and its associated limitations. |
| Overall solution | AI application or AI system, depending on the defined scope | Establishes the system-level assessment boundary. |
| Alert summarization | Generative AI capability | Requires evaluation of output accuracy and completeness. |
| Retrieval of incident procedures | Information retrieval; potentially retrieval-augmented generation | Introduces source-integrity and document-authorization requirements. |
| Investigation recommendations | AI-generated recommendations | Requires clear responsibility for decisions based on the recommendations. |
| Queries to security tools | Tool integration or tool use | Introduces API permissions, identity, and access-control concerns. |
| Draft incident reports | AI-generated content | Requires appropriate validation before reports become authoritative records. |
| Initiation of response workflows | Automated or agentic workflow behavior, depending on the implementation | Requires assessment of action permissions, approvals, and execution safeguards. |

### 19.2 Questions a GRC consultant should ask

**System scope**
- Which components are included in the assessment?
- Is the model hosted by the organization or a third-party provider?
- Which services and integrations are essential to the solution?

**Data and access**
- Which security records can the application retrieve?
- Are access permissions enforced independently of the model?
- Can retrieved content contain malicious instructions?
- Are prompts, responses, and tool results logged, and how is sensitive information protected?

**Actions and accountability**
- Can the system execute response actions or only recommend them?
- Which actions require human approval?
- Are tool permissions limited to the minimum required?
- Can an action be reversed or interrupted?
- Who is accountable for approving production use?

**Testing and assurance**
- Has the application been tested for prompt injection?
- Have generated summaries been evaluated for accuracy and omission?
- Have unauthorized tool calls and excessive permissions been tested?
- What evidence supports the decision to deploy?

### 19.3 Governance conclusion

The correct classification is not simply “an LLM.”

The solution is an AI-enabled security application incorporating a language model, retrieval capabilities, enterprise integrations, and potentially automated workflows.

The actual architecture and permissions determine which components and controls are relevant. The term *agentic* should be applied only when the system's behavior and architecture justify it, rather than merely because it can call a tool.

This distinction allows the organization to establish a defensible assessment boundary and identify the risks associated with data access, generated content, and operational actions.

## 20. Part 3 Summary

This part established terminology for generative AI, foundation models, large language models, and common components of modern AI applications.

The most important distinctions are:

- **Generative AI** describes systems that generate content; it does not guarantee that the generated content is correct.
- **A foundation model** is a broadly trained model that can support multiple downstream tasks.
- **A pretrained model** has already undergone training before its current use, while **fine-tuning** involves additional training to adapt the model.
- **An LLM** is a language model, not a complete enterprise application.
- **A prompt** influences model behavior but is not an enforceable authorization mechanism.
- **Prompt injection** attempts to manipulate model or application behavior through malicious instructions.
- **Hallucination** concerns incorrect or unsupported generated information, whereas **grounding** connects outputs to supporting information without guaranteeing correctness.
- **Embeddings** are numerical representations; **vector databases** support the storage and retrieval of such representations.
- **Tool integration** introduces security concerns beyond content generation, including authorization, execution permissions, and control of downstream actions.

For GRC practitioners, the critical task is to connect these technical concepts to system architecture, data governance, security controls, evaluation requirements, and operational accountability.

### Core Principle

**An AI model's capabilities, an application's permissions, and the system's operational authority are separate characteristics that must be assessed together.**

A model that generates content, an application that retrieves sensitive information, and a workflow that executes actions create different risks. Effective governance identifies those distinctions and ensures that the controls address the complete system rather than relying on the model's intended behavior alone.

## 21. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

### 21.1 Definition

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** is an approach that combines information retrieval with generative AI. Relevant information is retrieved from external sources and supplied to a generative model to help produce a response grounded in that information.

RAG is commonly used when an organization needs an AI application to answer questions using enterprise documents, internal policies, technical manuals, product information, or other knowledge sources.

Unlike a conventional model interaction that relies primarily on the model's learned parameters and supplied prompt, a RAG application retrieves relevant information at inference time.

A simplified RAG workflow is:

```text
                User Question
                      |
                      v
              Query Processing
                      |
                      v
              Information Search
                      |
                      v
             Retrieved Documents
                      |
                      v
          Relevance and Access Checks
                      |
                      v
              Prompt Construction
                      |
                      v
               Language Model
                      |
                      v
              Generated Response
                      |
                      v
           Validation and Presentation
```

Actual implementations may perform these operations in a different order or include additional processing stages.

### 21.2 Key RAG terminology

| Term | Definition | Enterprise example |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval | Selecting information relevant to a query | Finding the company's password-reset procedure |
| Retriever | Component that searches for relevant information | A service querying an enterprise document index |
| Knowledge base | A collection of information organized for reference or retrieval | Approved cybersecurity policies and procedures |
| Chunking | Dividing documents into smaller units for processing or retrieval | Splitting a lengthy security standard into sections |
| Embedding | Numerical representation used to capture features or relationships | Representing a policy section as a vector |
| Vector search | Searching for items using vector representations and a similarity measure | Finding passages semantically related to a question |
| Metadata filtering | Restricting retrieval based on attributes associated with records | Filtering documents by business unit or classification |
| Reranking | Reordering retrieved results using an additional relevance assessment | Prioritizing the passages most relevant to a query |
| Grounded response | A response intended to be supported by supplied evidence or retrieved information | An answer referencing an approved policy section |
| Source attribution | Identifying the sources supporting a response | Showing the document and section used to generate an answer |

### 21.3 RAG does not automatically guarantee security or accuracy

A common misconception is that supplying documents to a language model eliminates hallucinations.

RAG can improve access to relevant information, but it does not guarantee that the model will retrieve the correct document, interpret it correctly, or produce a faithful answer.

RAG also introduces risks associated with the retrieval system itself.

For example, an employee might ask an internal AI assistant about a confidential project. If the retrieval service searches across documents without enforcing the employee's permissions, the model may receive information that the employee should not access.

The security problem is not necessarily the model. It may be an authorization failure in the retrieval architecture.

**GRC considerations:**

- Enforce authorization when retrieving documents, not merely when presenting results.
- Preserve the relationship between retrieved content and its source.
- Validate source integrity and freshness.
- Treat retrieved content as potentially untrusted.
- Test for unauthorized cross-user and cross-tenant retrieval.
- Evaluate whether generated statements are supported by the retrieved evidence.
- Establish procedures for updating and removing information from retrieval indexes.

**Key distinction:** RAG supplies additional information to a model. It does not inherently retrain the model, guarantee factual accuracy, or enforce enterprise access-control policies.

## 22. Multimodal AI

### 22.1 Definition

**Multimodal AI** refers to AI systems that process, generate, or combine information from multiple modalities, such as text, images, audio, and video.

A modality is a type or form of information processed by a system.

Examples include:

- Text and images processed together.
- Speech converted into text and analyzed.
- Video frames analyzed alongside audio.
- Documents processed using text and visual layout.
- Generated text accompanied by synthesized audio.

Multimodal capability does not necessarily mean that every modality is processed by one unified model. A system may combine multiple specialized models and conventional processing components.

### 22.2 Common modalities

| Modality | Typical input | Potential output | Example application |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text | Documents, messages, prompts | Text or structured data | Contract analysis |
| Image | Photographs, diagrams, screenshots | Description, classification, extracted information | Inspection of equipment damage |
| Audio | Speech or recorded sound | Transcription, classification, generated audio | Call-center analysis |
| Video | Video frames and associated audio | Summaries, event descriptions, classifications | Security-video analysis |
| Structured data | Tables, database records, numerical features | Predictions, classifications, recommendations | Fraud-risk scoring |

### 22.3 Security and governance implications

Multimodal systems expand the range of information that can enter an AI workflow.

For example, a support application may receive an image of a damaged device containing a visible serial number, a customer's name, or other sensitive information.

The organization must assess not only the text prompt but also the image-processing pipeline, storage mechanisms, generated descriptions, and downstream integrations.

Relevant governance questions include:

1. Which modalities can the system accept?
2. What sensitive information can those modalities contain?
3. Are uploaded files scanned and handled securely?
4. Can embedded instructions in images or documents influence model behavior?
5. Are outputs reviewed before they influence business decisions?
6. What retention and deletion rules apply to uploaded media?
7. Are the system's capabilities and limitations documented for users?

**GRC principle:** Every supported input and output modality should be included in the system's data-flow analysis and threat model.

## 23. AI Agents and Agentic AI

### 23.1 AI agent

**Definition:** An AI agent is a system that uses information about its environment to select or perform actions toward a goal.

The term is used in several technical contexts. In contemporary generative AI applications, an AI agent commonly combines a model with instructions, state or memory, tools, and an execution mechanism.

Not every application called an agent has the same degree of autonomy or operational authority.

### 23.2 Agentic AI

**Definition:** Agentic AI refers to AI systems or architectures that exhibit agent-like behavior, such as planning steps, selecting tools, maintaining task state, and taking actions toward an objective.

Agentic behavior exists along a spectrum. A system that selects among a small number of approved tools is materially different from one that can independently plan and execute complex workflows across multiple enterprise systems.

The term does not, by itself, establish that a system is fully autonomous.

### 23.3 Typical AI agent architecture

```text
                 User Objective
                       |
                       v
                 Agent Runtime
                       |
                       v
                Model Processing
                       |
                       v
             Plan or Action Selection
                       |
                       v
                Policy Evaluation
                       |
             +---------+---------+
             |                   |
          Rejected            Approved
             |                   |
             v                   v
        Stop or Revise       Tool Execution
                                 |
                                 v
                         External System Result
                                 |
                                 v
                          State or Context
                                 |
                                 v
                       Further Processing
                                 |
                                 v
                         Completion Check
```

This is a conceptual architecture. In a secure implementation, authorization and policy enforcement must be implemented through reliable controls rather than depending exclusively on the model's willingness to follow instructions.

### 23.4 Important agent terminology

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Agent runtime | Environment responsible for coordinating an agent's execution | Service managing the agent's actions and tool calls |
| Tool use | The agent's use of an external function or service | Querying an approved security API |
| Tool calling | A model or application mechanism for requesting a tool invocation | Requesting execution of a ticket-search function |
| Planning | Selecting or organizing steps toward an objective | Breaking an incident investigation into subtasks |
| State | Information representing the current status of a task or process | Recording which investigation steps have completed |
| Memory | Information retained or made available for use across interactions or task steps | Storing approved task context |
| Orchestration | Coordinating models, tools, services, and workflow steps | Managing a multi-stage investigation |
| Human-in-the-loop | A process in which a human participates directly in a decision or action | An analyst approves a containment action |
| Human-on-the-loop | A process in which a human supervises system operation and can intervene | An operator monitors automated remediation |
| Human oversight | Arrangements intended to enable appropriate human supervision or intervention | Defined review authority and escalation procedures |

These terms are not interchangeable, and their precise use may vary across organizations and frameworks.

### 23.5 Agent versus conventional AI assistant

Consider two cybersecurity applications.

**Application A: AI assistant**

- Summarizes alerts.
- Explains security policies.
- Recommends investigation steps.
- Leaves execution to the analyst.

**Application B: AI agent**

- Receives an incident-investigation objective.
- Queries approved security tools.
- Retrieves relevant evidence.
- Selects additional investigative steps.
- Drafts a response plan.
- Initiates permitted actions under defined authorization rules.

Both applications may use the same language model. Their operational risk profiles differ because their permissions, workflows, and ability to act are different.

### 23.6 Why agentic systems require particular governance attention

Agentic systems may introduce risks beyond those associated with generating text.

These can include:

- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Excessive permissions.
- Compromised or manipulated task context.
- Inappropriate delegation to other agents or services.
- Repeated actions that create unintended consequences.
- Cascading failures across integrated systems.
- Inadequate action logging.
- Difficulty interrupting an ongoing workflow.

**Example**

An AI agent is authorized to investigate suspicious user accounts and initiate account suspension under specific conditions.

If the agent can suspend accounts without reliable authorization checks, an incorrect classification or manipulated input may result in an operational outage.

A safer architecture combines limited permissions, explicit policy checks, action validation, appropriate approval requirements, execution logging, and tested interruption mechanisms.

**GRC principle:** Assess an agent according to the actions it can perform, the resources it can access, the decisions it can influence, and the consequences of failure—not merely according to the model it uses.

## 24. Human Oversight and Automation Terminology

Human involvement is a major consideration in AI governance, particularly when outputs affect security, financial, legal, employment, or other consequential activities.

### 24.1 Human-in-the-loop (HITL)

**Definition:** Human-in-the-loop describes an arrangement in which a human participates directly in a defined stage of an AI-enabled process.

For example, an AI system may recommend a vulnerability remediation, but a security engineer must approve the proposed change before it is applied.

HITL is not automatically effective merely because a person is present in the workflow.

The human must have sufficient information, authority, time, and competence to evaluate the proposed action meaningfully.

**GRC considerations:**

- Define which decisions require human approval.
- Provide reviewers with relevant evidence and uncertainty information.
- Prevent approval from becoming an automatic rubber-stamping exercise.
- Record approvals and the identity of the responsible reviewer.
- Define escalation procedures for ambiguous or high-impact cases.

### 24.2 Human-on-the-loop (HOTL)

**Definition:** Human-on-the-loop describes an arrangement in which a human supervises an automated process and may intervene when required.

For example, a security automation platform may investigate selected alerts automatically while a security analyst monitors its activities and can suspend the workflow.

The effectiveness of this arrangement depends on whether intervention is practical and timely.

A supervisor who cannot detect a problem or interrupt an unsafe action does not provide meaningful operational oversight.

### 24.3 Human-out-of-the-loop

**Definition:** Human-out-of-the-loop describes an arrangement in which the relevant process operates without direct human participation or intervention at the individual action or decision stage.

This term describes a process configuration; it does not necessarily mean that the system has no human governance, supervision, or accountability arrangements at other levels.

**Example**

A system automatically classifies incoming support tickets and routes them according to predefined rules without requiring a person to approve each classification.

The appropriateness of this arrangement depends on the impact of errors, the effectiveness of safeguards, and the ability to detect and correct failures.

### 24.4 Comparison

| Dimension | Human-in-the-loop | Human-on-the-loop | Human-out-of-the-loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct human participation | At a defined stage of the process | Primarily through supervision and intervention | Not required for each relevant action |
| Typical pattern | Human approves a proposed action | Human monitors automated execution | System executes an approved routine automatically |
| Main dependency | Quality of human review | Monitoring and intervention effectiveness | Reliability of automated controls |
| Governance concern | Inadequate or superficial review | Delayed or ineffective intervention | Undetected errors and insufficient safeguards |

These categories are useful operational descriptions, not universal legal classifications. Applicable legislation may define human oversight or automated decision-making differently.

### 24.5 Human oversight is not the same as accountability

A person reviewing an AI output does not automatically become the sole party accountable for the system's behavior.

Accountability depends on the organization's governance model, delegated authority, applicable obligations, and the actual responsibilities assigned to individuals and functions.

For example, a security analyst may approve a specific remediation action, while the system owner remains responsible for ensuring that the application has been appropriately designed, tested, and authorized for its intended use.

**GRC principle:** Define oversight responsibilities, decision rights, escalation authority, and system ownership separately.

## 25. AI Safety, AI Security, and AI Ethics

These terms are often used together, but they address different questions.

### 25.1 AI safety

**Definition:** AI safety concerns preventing or reducing harmful outcomes arising from AI system behavior, including failures, unintended actions, and unsafe interactions with people or environments.

The scope of AI safety varies by application and framework.

Examples include preventing an AI-enabled industrial system from issuing unsafe operating instructions or ensuring that a medical-support system is not relied upon beyond its validated capabilities.

### 25.2 AI security

**Definition:** AI security concerns protecting AI models, data, systems, applications, and associated infrastructure against threats that compromise confidentiality, integrity, availability, or authorized behavior.

Relevant concerns include:

- Unauthorized access to models or data.
- Training-data poisoning.
- Adversarial manipulation.
- Prompt injection.
- Model or system abuse.
- Sensitive-information disclosure.
- Unauthorized tool execution.
- Compromise of AI infrastructure.

AI security includes both conventional cybersecurity concerns and threats arising from AI-specific characteristics.

### 25.3 AI ethics

**Definition:** AI ethics concerns the principles and values used to evaluate the appropriate development, deployment, and use of AI, including questions involving fairness, human dignity, accountability, transparency, privacy, and potential harm.

Ethical principles may inform organizational policies, impact assessments, design decisions, and governance requirements.

Ethical principles do not automatically constitute enforceable technical controls or legal obligations. Their operational implementation must be defined.

### 25.4 Comparison

| Dimension | AI safety | AI security | AI ethics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary concern | Preventing or reducing harmful system behavior | Protecting systems, data, and operations against threats | Evaluating whether development and use align with relevant values and principles |
| Typical question | Can the system cause unacceptable harm through unsafe behavior? | Can an attacker or unauthorized party compromise the system or its operation? | Is the system being developed and used appropriately? |
| Example | Preventing unsafe instructions in a critical process | Preventing unauthorized access to an AI-enabled service | Evaluating whether a system unfairly disadvantages a group |
| Typical governance mechanism | Safety analysis, testing, safeguards, escalation | Threat modeling, security controls, monitoring, incident response | Ethical review, impact assessment, policy requirements |
| Relationship to GRC | Informs risk acceptance and operational safeguards | Supports cybersecurity risk management and assurance | Informs governance principles, organizational obligations, and decision-making |

These areas overlap. A single failure may have safety, security, and ethical implications.

For example, a compromised AI-enabled industrial control application might cause physical harm, represent a cybersecurity incident, and violate the organization's safety obligations.

### 25.5 AI trustworthiness

**Definition:** AI trustworthiness refers to the extent to which an AI system and its use can be considered worthy of justified confidence in the relevant context.

Trustworthiness is not established by a single technical metric or certification. It may involve several properties, such as:

- Reliability.
- Safety.
- Security and resilience.
- Accountability.
- Transparency.
- Explainability.
- Privacy protection.
- Fairness.
- Appropriate human oversight.

The relevant properties and their relative importance depend on the intended purpose, operating environment, and applicable framework.

**GRC consideration:** Organizations should translate trustworthiness objectives into measurable requirements, verification activities, assigned responsibilities, and retained evidence. A broad statement that a system is “trustworthy” is not sufficient assurance on its own.

| AI ethics | Principles for evaluating appropriate AI development and use | Ethical assessment and governance requirements |
| AI trustworthiness | Justified confidence based on relevant properties and evidence | Assurance records and risk acceptance documentation |

### 26.1 From terminology to a control requirement

Consider the following statement:

> The organization shall implement appropriate human oversight for high-impact AI systems.

This is a useful policy objective, but it is not yet a complete operational control.

To make it actionable, the organization should define:

1. **Scope:** Which systems and use cases require oversight?
2. **Trigger:** Which decisions or actions require human involvement?
3. **Responsibility:** Who reviews, approves, or rejects the output?
4. **Competence:** What knowledge must the reviewer possess?
5. **Evidence:** What records demonstrate that the review occurred?
6. **Escalation:** What happens when the output is uncertain or potentially harmful?
7. **Effectiveness:** How is the quality of oversight assessed?

The terminology establishes what the policy means. The control design establishes how the organization implements and verifies it.

## 27. Common Terminology Misconceptions

The following misconceptions should be challenged during architecture reviews, risk assessments, and governance discussions.

| Misconception | Correct interpretation | Governance lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Every AI system uses machine learning. | AI can be implemented through different approaches, including rule-based and symbolic methods. | Identify the actual technology rather than assuming an implementation. |
| Every generative AI system is a large language model. | Generative AI includes systems that generate images, audio, video, and other outputs. | Assess all supported modalities and capabilities. |
| A foundation model is the complete AI application. | A foundation model is often one component of a larger application. | Assess the complete system boundary. |
| RAG eliminates hallucinations. | Retrieval can provide supporting information but does not guarantee correct interpretation or output. | Evaluate both retrieval quality and generated responses. |
| A prompt can enforce access control. | Prompts can guide behavior but do not replace enforceable authorization mechanisms. | Implement critical security restrictions independently. |
| An AI agent is necessarily fully autonomous. | Agentic behavior varies according to the system's design, permissions, and operating constraints. | Assess actual action authority rather than relying on labels. |
| Human approval automatically makes an AI process safe. | Approval can be ineffective if reviewers lack evidence, competence, or meaningful intervention authority. | Evaluate the effectiveness of oversight. |
| AI safety and AI security are interchangeable. | Safety focuses on harmful behavior and outcomes; security focuses on protecting systems and operations against threats. | Assess both where relevant. |
| Ethical principles automatically satisfy legal requirements. | Ethical objectives and legal obligations are related but distinct. | Map principles to applicable requirements and evidence. |
| Trustworthiness can be established by a model benchmark alone. | Trustworthiness depends on multiple properties and the context of use. | Require proportionate, system-level assurance. |

## 28. Practical GRC Exercise: Develop an AI Terminology Standard

### Scenario

An enterprise has adopted several AI-enabled products across cybersecurity, customer service, finance, and human resources.

Different departments use terms such as *AI application*, *AI model*, *AI assistant*, *AI agent*, and *automated decision system* inconsistently.

As a result, the central GRC team cannot reliably determine which systems require enhanced assessment, which components are externally supplied, or where human approval is mandatory.

### Task

Develop a basic AI terminology standard that supports consistent governance.

The standard should address the following elements.

| Element | Required outcome |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Explain why consistent terminology is necessary |
| Scope | Identify the systems, applications, and AI-related capabilities covered |
| Definitions | Establish approved meanings for relevant terms |
| Classification | Define how systems are categorized by capability and operating behavior |
| Ownership | Assign responsibility for maintaining system descriptions and classifications |
| Assessment | Specify how terminology informs risk assessment and control selection |
| Evidence | Identify documentation needed to support the classification |
| Review | Establish when definitions and classifications must be reassessed |

### Example policy statement

> The organization shall maintain an approved AI terminology standard and apply it consistently across AI inventories, architecture documentation, risk assessments, security reviews, procurement activities, and governance reporting. AI systems shall be classified according to their actual capabilities, components, data processing, external dependencies, and operational authority. Where a term has a specific meaning under an applicable standard or legal instrument, the organization shall use that definition when assessing compliance with the relevant requirement.

### Questions for the GRC reviewer

1. Are AI systems classified consistently across business units?
2. Does the inventory distinguish models from applications and complete systems?
3. Are generative AI and agentic capabilities explicitly identified where applicable?
4. Are tool permissions and autonomous actions documented?
5. Are human-oversight requirements defined for relevant workflows?
6. Are terminology-based classifications supported by technical evidence?
7. Are applicable standards and legal definitions referenced accurately?

### Expected outcome

A successful terminology standard should improve the consistency of system inventories, risk assessments, control selection, and reporting.

It should not create unnecessary bureaucracy by requiring every system to use every available AI classification. The standard should use definitions proportionately and focus on distinctions that affect governance decisions.

## 29. Part 4 Summary

This part consolidated terminology associated with modern AI architectures and the governance concepts used to assess their behavior.

The key distinctions are:

- **RAG** combines information retrieval with generative AI; it does not guarantee factual accuracy or enforce authorization automatically.
- **Multimodal AI** processes or generates information across multiple modalities, expanding the scope of data-flow and threat-model analysis.
- **AI agents and agentic AI** involve goal-directed behavior and may include planning, tool use, and action execution; the actual degree of autonomy depends on implementation.
- **Human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and human-out-of-the-loop** describe different arrangements for human participation and intervention.
- **AI safety, AI security, and AI ethics** address related but distinct concerns.
- **AI trustworthiness** requires justified confidence supported by evidence appropriate to the system's context and intended purpose.
- **Governance terminology** becomes operationally valuable when it is connected to system classification, accountability, controls, evidence, and review.

### Core Principle

**Standardize AI terminology around the distinctions that affect system boundaries, risk, permissions, accountability, and assurance.**

A mature AI governance program does not merely maintain a glossary. It uses precise definitions consistently to support architecture reviews, risk assessments, control implementation, regulatory analysis, and defensible management decisions.
