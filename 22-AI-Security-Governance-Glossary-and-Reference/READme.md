# Chapter 22 — AI Security Governance Glossary and Reference

## Overview

Chapter 22 provides a consolidated reference library for **AI Security Governance, Risk Management, Cybersecurity, Regulatory Compliance, and Enterprise AI Architecture**.

Its purpose is to establish a common vocabulary, clarify frequently misunderstood concepts, and provide structured reference material that can be used when designing, implementing, assessing, and governing enterprise AI systems.

As AI adoption expands across business operations, customer platforms, enterprise applications, cloud environments, and critical services, professionals need more than a general understanding of artificial intelligence. They must understand the terminology, standards, regulatory obligations, security threats, architectural patterns, control requirements, and governance practices associated with AI-enabled environments.

This chapter brings these areas together into a practical knowledge reference for cybersecurity professionals, GRC practitioners, AI governance specialists, risk managers, architects, auditors, and technology leaders.

By completing this chapter, readers should be able to:

1. Understand essential AI, machine learning, generative AI, LLM, and agentic AI terminology.

4. Navigate important AI security standards, frameworks, regulations, and professional resources.

7. Distinguish concepts that are frequently confused in AI engineering, cybersecurity, governance, and compliance.
8. Apply AI governance and security terminology consistently in enterprise risk assessments, architecture reviews, audits, and consulting engagements.
9. Identify relevant professional development resources, certifications, and learning pathways.
10. Use the chapter as a practical reference when developing AI governance documentation and security control libraries.

## Important Distinctions and Usage Principles

The following principles should guide the interpretation of concepts throughout this chapter:

1. **AI model is not the same as an AI system.** An AI system includes the model and the surrounding data, infrastructure, applications, interfaces, processes, and operational controls.
2. **AI governance is not the same as AI security.** Governance establishes accountability, decision rights, oversight, and organizational controls. Security protects AI systems and their supporting environments against threats.
3. **AI safety is not the same as AI security.** Safety concerns harmful outcomes and unacceptable behavior, while security focuses on protecting systems, data, models, and operations against malicious or unauthorized activity. They overlap but are not interchangeable.
4. **RAG does not guarantee factual accuracy.** Retrieval can improve source grounding, but incorrect, outdated, incomplete, or unauthorized source material can still produce unreliable outputs.
5. **Prompt instructions are not security controls.** Authentication, authorization, and execution permissions must be enforced by the application and underlying infrastructure.
6. **A policy is not evidence of control effectiveness.** Organizations must demonstrate that controls are implemented and operate as intended.
7. **AI risk is broader than cybersecurity risk.** AI risk assessments may include operational, legal, ethical, privacy, safety, financial, reputational, and societal impacts.
8. **Regulatory classification must be based on legal criteria.** Internal risk categories and organizational AI classifications must not be confused with formal regulatory classifications.
9. **Certification does not replace practical competence.** Professional capability must be demonstrated through applied knowledge, sound judgment, and effective implementation.
10. **Reference architectures are not automatically production designs.** They must be adapted to the organization's actual technology, data flows, threat model, regulatory environment, and operational requirements.
