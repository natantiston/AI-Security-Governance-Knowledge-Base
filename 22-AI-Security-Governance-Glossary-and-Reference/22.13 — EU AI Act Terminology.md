# 22.13 — EU AI Act Terminology

## Part 1 — EU AI Act Foundations, Regulatory Scope, Definitions, and Governance Principles

### 1. Introduction to the EU Artificial Intelligence Act

The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act) is the European Union's comprehensive legal framework for regulating artificial intelligence.

It establishes harmonized rules for the development, placing on the market, putting into service, and use of AI systems within the EU.

Its primary objective is to promote human-centric and trustworthy AI while protecting:

- Health and safety.
- Fundamental rights.
- Democracy and the rule of law.
- Environmental protection.
- Consumer interests.
- Innovation and the functioning of the internal market.

Unlike voluntary AI governance frameworks such as NIST AI RMF, the EU AI Act establishes legally binding obligations for organizations and individuals falling within its scope.

For cybersecurity and GRC professionals, the Act introduces important governance responsibilities involving AI risk classification, provider and deployer accountability, technical documentation, transparency, human oversight, conformity assessment, post-market monitoring, and regulatory reporting.

### 2. AI System — Article 3(1)

The EU AI Act defines an AI system in Article 3(1).

An AI system is a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

This definition is fundamental because the Act's obligations depend on whether a product or service falls within the regulatory definition of an AI system.

#### 2.1 Important Elements of the Definition

| Element                         | Explanation                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Machine-based system            | The system operates through computational processes                                                     |
| Varying levels of autonomy      | The system may operate with different degrees of independence from human intervention                   |
| Adaptiveness                    | The system may change its behavior after deployment                                                     |
| Explicit or implicit objectives | Objectives may be directly specified or arise from the system's design and operation                    |
| Inference                       | The system derives outputs from inputs rather than merely following exclusively predefined instructions |
| Inputs                          | Data or information received by the system                                                              |
| Outputs                         | Predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions                                                     |
| Influence on environments       | Outputs may affect physical or virtual environments                                                     |

#### 2.2 What Does Inference Mean?

Inference is the process through which an AI system generates outputs based on inputs, learned patterns, rules, or other computational mechanisms.

Examples:

- A fraud detection model evaluates transaction data and predicts suspicious activity.
- An LLM receives a user prompt and generates a response.
- A recommendation system analyzes user behavior and suggests products.
- A predictive maintenance system evaluates sensor data and predicts equipment failure.

Inference is an important distinction because conventional software that simply executes rules explicitly defined by human programmers does not automatically qualify as an AI system under the Act.

#### 2.3 AI System vs. Traditional Software

| Dimension    | Traditional software                                    | AI system                                                                    |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Processing   | Primarily predefined instructions and logic             | Uses inference to generate outputs                                           |
| Behavior     | Generally follows explicitly programmed logic           | May generate outputs based on learned patterns or other inference mechanisms |
| Adaptiveness | Usually changes through explicit software modifications | May exhibit adaptiveness after deployment                                    |
| Outputs      | Determined through programmed logic                     | May include predictions, generated content, recommendations, or decisions    |
| Governance   | Conventional software governance                        | May require additional AI-specific governance obligations                    |

This distinction is not absolute. Traditional software can contain AI components, and an AI-enabled product may combine conventional software with AI functionality.

#### 2.4 GRC Application

A telecom operator deploying AI-powered fraud detection should determine:

- Whether the system meets the Article 3(1) definition.
- Its intended purpose.
- Whether it falls into a regulated AI category.
- Whether it is high-risk under the Act.
- Which organization acts as provider or deployer.
- What obligations apply to the relevant lifecycle activities.

The organization should not assume that every system marketed as AI automatically falls into the same regulatory category.

### 3. Regulatory Scope — Article 2

Article 2 defines the scope of the EU AI Act.

The Regulation applies to specified actors and activities involving AI systems and general-purpose AI models.

#### 3.1 Organizations and Actors Within Scope

Depending on their activities and circumstances, the Act applies to:

- Providers placing AI systems on the EU market or putting them into service.
- Deployers of AI systems established or located within the EU.
- Providers and deployers established in third countries where the output produced by the AI system is used in the EU.
- Importers of AI systems.
- Distributors of AI systems.
- Product manufacturers placing AI systems on the market or putting them into service together with their products under their own name or trademark.
- Authorized representatives of providers.
- Certain providers and other actors of general-purpose AI models.

The Act also includes specific territorial and operational provisions that must be examined when determining applicability.

#### 3.2 Extraterritorial Application

The EU AI Act can apply to organizations established outside the European Union.

For example:

A US-based AI provider offers a generative AI service to customers in Spain. The service generates outputs that are used within the EU.

The provider may fall within the scope of the Regulation because Article 2 includes certain providers and deployers established or located in third countries where the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union.

The precise applicability must be assessed against the relevant provisions and circumstances.

#### 3.3 Territorial Scope vs. Organizational Location

| Concept                      | Meaning                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| EU establishment             | Organization established within the European Union                                                                             |
| EU market placement          | Making an AI system or model available on the EU market under the relevant legal conditions                                    |
| Putting into service         | Supplying an AI system for first use or using it for its intended purpose in the Union under the conditions defined by the Act |
| Extraterritorial application | Certain obligations may apply to organizations outside the EU                                                                  |
| Output used in the Union     | A relevant connecting factor under Article 2 for specified third-country actors                                                |

GRC implication: Organizations should assess the location of the provider, deployer, affected users, market activities, and use of AI outputs rather than relying solely on where the AI infrastructure is hosted.

### 4. Important EU AI Act Definitions — Article 3

Article 3 contains legal definitions that establish the terminology used throughout the Regulation.

The following terms are particularly important for AI governance and compliance professionals.

#### 4.1 Provider

A provider is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model, or that has an AI system or general-purpose AI model developed, and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge.

Example:

A software company develops an AI-powered recruitment platform and offers it to employers under its own brand.

The company may qualify as the provider.

GRC responsibility: Providers have significant obligations under the Act, particularly for high-risk AI systems and general-purpose AI models, depending on their classification and applicable provisions.

#### 4.2 Deployer

A deployer is a natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body using an AI system under its authority, except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

Example:

A telecom operator purchases an external AI customer service platform and uses it to support its customer operations.

The telecom operator is generally the deployer of that AI system.

GRC responsibility: Deployers have obligations that may include following instructions for use, assigning appropriate human oversight, monitoring operation, and fulfilling applicable requirements concerning data and incident handling.

#### 4.3 Importer

An importer is a natural or legal person located or established in the Union that places on the market an AI system bearing the name or trademark of a natural or legal person established in a third country.

Example:

A Spanish company imports an AI-enabled product from a manufacturer outside the EU and places it on the EU market.

The company may qualify as an importer.

#### 4.4 Distributor

A distributor is a natural or legal person in the supply chain, other than the provider or importer, that makes an AI system available on the Union market.

Example:

A technology reseller sells an AI software product supplied by an external provider to Spanish business customers.

The reseller may qualify as a distributor.

#### 4.5 Authorized Representative

An authorized representative is a natural or legal person located or established in the Union who has received and accepted a written mandate from a provider located or established outside the Union to perform and carry out specified obligations and procedures on its behalf.

This role is important for certain non-EU providers.

#### 4.6 Product Manufacturer

A product manufacturer is a manufacturer of a product that places an AI system on the market or puts it into service together with the product under its own name or trademark.

Example:

A manufacturer places an AI-enabled industrial machine on the EU market under its own brand.

Depending on the circumstances, the manufacturer may assume provider responsibilities under the Act.

#### 4.7 Operator

The EU AI Act uses the term operator as an umbrella term for specified economic operators, including:

- Providers.
- Product manufacturers.
- Deployers.
- Authorized representatives.
- Importers.
- Distributors.

This distinction is important because the obligations of an operator depend on its specific legal role.

### 5. Provider vs. Deployer — Critical Regulatory Distinction

One of the most important distinctions in EU AI Act governance is the difference between the provider and the deployer.

Organizations frequently misunderstand these roles, particularly when purchasing AI services from external vendors.

#### 5.1 Provider Responsibilities

Depending on the applicable AI category, provider obligations may include:

- Establishing a risk management system.
- Ensuring appropriate data governance.
- Preparing technical documentation.
- Maintaining logging capabilities.
- Designing appropriate transparency mechanisms.
- Providing instructions for use.
- Establishing human oversight measures.
- Ensuring accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.
- Conducting applicable conformity assessments.
- Registering relevant systems.
- Implementing post-market monitoring.

Not every obligation applies identically to every AI provider. The applicable legal requirements depend on the system classification and relevant provisions.

#### 5.2 Deployer Responsibilities

Depending on the AI system and its classification, deployer obligations may include:

- Using the AI system in accordance with instructions.
- Assigning competent human oversight personnel.
- Ensuring relevant input data is appropriate and sufficiently representative where the applicable provisions require it.
- Monitoring system operation.
- Keeping logs under its control where required.
- Informing relevant parties of serious risks or incidents.
- Suspending use when required.
- Complying with applicable transparency and information obligations.

#### 5.3 Comparison Table

| Dimension                 | Provider                                                                                                               | Deployer                                              |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary role              | Develops or commissions and places the AI system on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark | Uses the AI system under its authority                |
| AI system development     | May develop or commission development                                                                                  | May use an externally developed system                |
| Technical documentation   | Relevant provider obligation                                                                                           | May need to obtain and use provider information       |
| Risk management           | Relevant provider obligations                                                                                          | Must manage risks arising from its deployment and use |
| Human oversight           | Designs and provides relevant measures where required                                                                  | Assigns and implements appropriate oversight          |
| Monitoring                | Establishes applicable post-market monitoring                                                                          | Monitors operation within its responsibilities        |
| Conformity assessment     | Responsible where applicable                                                                                           | Must verify relevant conditions before deployment     |
| Regulatory responsibility | Depends on provider status and system category                                                                         | Depends on deployer status and system category        |

#### 5.4 Important: A Deployer Can Become a Provider

An organization that initially acts as a deployer may become a provider under Article 25 in specified circumstances.

These include situations where it:

- Places its name or trademark on a high-risk AI system already placed on the market or put into service, subject to the applicable conditions.
- Makes a substantial modification to a high-risk AI system that remains high-risk.
- Changes the intended purpose of an AI system, including a general-purpose AI system, in a way that causes it to become high-risk.

The legal consequences must be assessed against Article 25 and the relevant system classification.

Example:

A telecom operator purchases a third-party AI system for customer service. It substantially modifies the system and assumes responsibilities that cause it to qualify as a provider under the relevant legal conditions.

The operator should not automatically assume that the original supplier retains all provider obligations.

GRC principle: Regulatory role classification must be reassessed when organizations modify, rebrand, or change the intended purpose of AI systems.

### 6. Intended Purpose — Article 3

Intended purpose refers to the use for which an AI system is intended by the provider, including the specific context and conditions of use, as specified in the instructions for use, promotional or sales materials and statements, as well as technical documentation.

This concept is central to determining regulatory classification and obligations.

#### 6.1 Examples of Intended Purpose

| AI system                      | Intended purpose                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer service chatbot       | Respond to customer questions and provide service information |
| Fraud detection model          | Identify potentially fraudulent transactions                  |
| Recruitment screening system   | Assist in evaluating job applicants                           |
| Medical diagnostic AI          | Support medical professionals in diagnostic activities        |
| Network optimization AI        | Predict network congestion and recommend optimization         |
| Creditworthiness assessment AI | Evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons              |

#### 6.2 Why Intended Purpose Matters

The same underlying model may be used for different purposes, resulting in different regulatory implications.

Example:

A general-purpose language model is used in two applications:

- Application A: Generates internal meeting summaries.
- Application B: Evaluates candidates for employment selection.

The underlying model may be identical, but the intended purposes and potential impacts are materially different.

Application B may fall within the high-risk employment-related use cases listed in Annex III, depending on its specific function and circumstances.

#### 6.3 Intended Purpose vs. Actual Use

| Concept                  | Meaning                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intended purpose         | The purpose specified by the provider                                                                                                      |
| Actual use               | How the system is used in practice                                                                                                         |
| Foreseeable misuse       | Use outside the intended purpose that may nevertheless result from reasonably foreseeable human behavior or interaction with other systems |
| Changed intended purpose | A change in the intended use that may alter regulatory classification and obligations                                                      |

GRC implication: Organizations must monitor actual system usage and compare it against the documented intended purpose. Unauthorized or materially different usage may introduce new regulatory and operational risks.

### 7. Risk-Based Classification Under the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act adopts a risk-based regulatory model.

Different AI systems are subject to different legal obligations depending on their risk characteristics and intended purposes.

The commonly used classification model distinguishes:

1. Prohibited AI practices.
2. High-risk AI systems.
3. AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations.
4. Other AI systems, often described as minimal or limited-risk systems in explanatory materials.
5. General-purpose AI models, which have their own regulatory requirements.

The last category is particularly important: general-purpose AI models are not simply another risk tier in the same classification hierarchy.

#### 7.1 Prohibited AI Practices

Certain AI practices are prohibited under Article 5, subject to the precise scope, definitions, and exceptions established in the Regulation.

Examples include specified practices involving:

- Harmful manipulation or deception.
- Exploitation of vulnerabilities.
- Social scoring under defined conditions.
- Certain forms of individual criminal risk assessment based solely on profiling or personality traits.
- Untargeted scraping of facial images to create or expand facial recognition databases.
- Certain forms of emotion inference in workplaces and educational institutions.
- Certain forms of biometric categorization.
- Certain forms of real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement, subject to narrowly defined exceptions.

The prohibition analysis must be based on the exact legal wording of Article 5.

#### 7.2 High-Risk AI Systems

High-risk AI systems are subject to more extensive requirements under the Regulation.

They are identified through Article 6 and the relevant provisions and annexes.

High-risk classifications include specified categories involving:

- Biometric systems.
- Critical infrastructure.
- Education and vocational training.
- Employment and worker management.
- Access to essential private and public services.
- Law enforcement.
- Migration, asylum, and border control.
- Administration of justice and democratic processes.

Not every AI system used in these sectors is automatically high-risk. The relevant legal criteria and applicable exceptions must be examined.

#### 7.3 Specific Transparency Obligations

Certain AI systems are subject to transparency requirements, including specified systems that interact directly with natural persons or generate synthetic content.

Examples include:

- Chatbots that interact with people.
- Certain emotion recognition systems.
- Certain biometric categorization systems.
- AI-generated or manipulated content subject to applicable disclosure requirements.

#### 7.4 Other AI Systems

Many AI systems do not fall into the prohibited or high-risk categories and are not subject to the same extensive requirements.

However, they may still be subject to:

- Applicable transparency obligations.
- GDPR.
- Cybersecurity requirements.
- Consumer protection law.
- Intellectual property law.
- Other applicable EU or national legislation.

An organization should not interpret a lower AI Act risk category as an exemption from all legal and security obligations.

### 8. EU AI Act Regulatory Terminology: Risk Categories vs. Risk Management

The EU AI Act's legal classification system must be distinguished from an organization's internal AI risk assessment methodology.

#### 8.1 Regulatory Classification

Regulatory classification determines which legal category and obligations apply to an AI system or model.

Example:

An AI system used for recruitment may qualify as high-risk under the applicable conditions in Annex III.

#### 8.2 Organizational Risk Assessment

An internal AI risk assessment evaluates potential threats, vulnerabilities, impacts, and control weaknesses associated with the system.

Example:

A recruitment AI system may have risks involving:

- Discriminatory outcomes.
- Unauthorized access.
- Data poisoning.
- Inadequate human oversight.
- Privacy violations.
- Model performance degradation.

#### 8.3 Important Distinction

| Dimension         | EU AI Act classification                        | Internal AI risk assessment                       |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary objective | Determine legal regulatory category             | Evaluate organizational and system-specific risks |
| Basis             | Regulation's definitions, articles, and annexes | Organizational methodology and risk criteria      |
| Result            | Applicable legal obligations                    | Risk ratings and treatment decisions              |
| Authority         | EU legal framework                              | Organizational governance                         |
| Flexibility       | Must follow legal criteria                      | Can be tailored to organizational context         |

A system classified as high-risk under the Act may have a different internal risk rating depending on the organization's methodology.

Conversely, an AI system that is not legally classified as high-risk may still represent substantial cybersecurity, privacy, operational, or reputational risk.

### 9. EU AI Act Compliance Roles and Governance

Organizations should establish clear governance responsibilities for the implementation of EU AI Act requirements.

A typical enterprise model may involve:

| Function                     | Responsibility                                         |

| AI Governance Committee      | Coordination of AI governance decisions                |
| Legal and Regulatory Affairs | Interpretation of legal obligations                    |

| AI System Owner              | System lifecycle and operational management            |

The precise allocation of responsibilities should reflect the organization's legal role as provider, deployer, importer, distributor, or another relevant operator.

#### 9.1 Example: Telecom Operator Using External AI

A telecom operator purchases a third-party generative AI service and integrates it into its customer service platform.

A preliminary responsibility model could be:

### 10. Summary and Core Principle

The EU AI Act establishes a legally binding, risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems and general-purpose AI models within its scope.

The principal concepts covered in this part are:

1. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and its regulatory purpose.
2. The legal definition of an AI system under Article 3(1).
3. Regulatory scope and potential extraterritorial application under Article 2.
4. Economic operator definitions under Article 3.
5. Provider and deployer responsibilities.
6. Intended purpose and its impact on regulatory classification.
7. Prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, transparency obligations, and other AI systems.
8. The distinction between legal classification and internal organizational risk assessment.
9. Enterprise governance responsibilities for EU AI Act compliance.

Core Principle: EU AI Act compliance begins with correct legal classification. An organization must understand what constitutes an AI system, determine its regulatory role, establish the intended purpose, assess the applicable risk category, and identify the corresponding legal obligations before designing its compliance controls.

## Part 2 — EU AI Act Risk Classification, Prohibited Practices, and High-Risk AI System Requirements

### 11. Prohibited AI Practices Under Article 5

Article 5 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 identifies specific AI practices that are prohibited because they create unacceptable risks to fundamental rights, safety, or human dignity.

These prohibitions are not simply recommendations or internal governance preferences. Where a practice falls within the prohibition, an organization cannot make it permissible merely by implementing additional security controls, obtaining user consent, or accepting the associated risk.

For GRC professionals, the critical task is to determine whether an AI use case falls within the precise legal definition of a prohibited practice, including its conditions, scope, and applicable exceptions.

The main prohibited practices include:

| Prohibited practice                               | Regulatory concern                                                                                                                                           | Governance implication                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Harmful manipulation or deception                 | AI techniques that materially distort behavior and cause or are reasonably likely to cause significant harm                                                  | Assess how AI influences decisions, behavior, and user autonomy          |
| Exploitation of vulnerabilities                   | Exploiting vulnerabilities arising from age, disability, or specific social or economic situations to materially distort behavior and cause significant harm | Assess vulnerable populations and the design of AI-driven interactions   |
| Social scoring                                    | Evaluating or classifying people based on social behavior or personal characteristics in ways that lead to prohibited detrimental treatment                  | Prohibit unlawful scoring practices and assess cross-context data use    |
| Certain criminal-risk predictions                 | Individual risk assessments predicting criminal offending based solely on profiling or personality traits, subject to the statutory exception                | Review law-enforcement use cases and the basis of predictions            |
| Untargeted facial-image scraping                  | Creating or expanding facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage                            | Review biometric-data acquisition and dataset provenance                 |
| Emotion recognition in workplaces and education   | Inferring emotions in workplace or educational settings, except for medical or safety reasons                                                                | Review employee and student monitoring technologies                      |
| Certain biometric categorization                  | Inferring sensitive characteristics from biometric data to categorize individuals, subject to specified exceptions                                           | Review biometric processing purposes and prohibited inference categories |
| Certain real-time remote biometric identification | Real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces for law-enforcement purposes, subject to narrowly defined exceptions                 | Require strict legal review, authorization, and oversight                |

The table is a governance overview. Each prohibition has specific legal conditions that must be assessed against the actual system, intended purpose, deployment context, and actor involved.

### 12. Key Prohibited Practices: Definitions and Regulatory Boundaries

#### 12.1 Harmful Manipulation and Deception

The Act prohibits certain AI systems that deploy subliminal techniques beyond a person's consciousness or purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques to materially distort behavior.

The relevant conditions include:

- The system uses manipulative or deceptive techniques.
- The techniques materially distort the behavior of a person or group.
- The distortion causes, or is reasonably likely to cause, significant harm.

The prohibition is not a blanket ban on persuasive interfaces, recommendation engines, personalization, or advertising.

The specific statutory conditions matter.

Enterprise example:

An AI-powered customer engagement system analyzes customer behavior and generates highly personalized messages. It deliberately exploits a person's vulnerabilities to manipulate them into making financially harmful decisions.

The organization should assess whether the system's techniques, behavioral effects, and potential harm meet the legal threshold under Article 5.

GRC assessment questions:

1. Does the AI system intentionally influence user behavior through manipulative or deceptive techniques?
2. Does the system exploit psychological vulnerabilities?
3. Can its behavior materially distort user decisions?
4. Is significant harm caused or reasonably likely?
5. Have product, legal, privacy, and AI governance teams assessed the use case?

#### 12.2 Exploitation of Vulnerabilities

The Act prohibits AI systems that exploit vulnerabilities of individuals or specific groups due to their age, disability, or a specific social or economic situation.

The prohibition requires more than identifying a vulnerable person or tailoring a service to their needs. The statutory conditions concerning exploitation, material distortion of behavior, and significant harm must be considered.

Example:

An AI-driven lending platform identifies financially distressed customers and deliberately uses that information to influence them into accepting products that are harmful to their financial interests.

The GRC assessment should examine:

- Whether the system identifies vulnerable individuals or groups.
- Whether vulnerability information is used to exploit them.
- Whether behavior is materially distorted.
- Whether significant harm results or is reasonably likely.

#### 12.3 Social Scoring

Social scoring refers to certain AI-based evaluations or classifications of individuals or groups based on their social behavior or known, inferred, or predicted personal characteristics.

The prohibition concerns scoring that leads to either:

1. Detrimental or unfavorable treatment in social contexts unrelated to the contexts in which the data was originally generated or collected; or
2. Detrimental or unfavorable treatment that is unjustified or disproportionate to the person's social behavior or its gravity.

Example:

A service provider collects customer interaction data for customer support purposes and uses an AI-generated social score to deny unrelated services based on behavioral characteristics that are not relevant to the service decision.

The organization must assess whether this constitutes prohibited social scoring under Article 5.

Important distinction: Not every customer risk score, credit score, fraud score, or behavioral analytics model is automatically prohibited social scoring. The legal assessment depends on the scoring basis, purpose, context, and resulting treatment.

#### 12.4 Individual Criminal-Risk Prediction

The Act prohibits certain AI systems used to assess or predict the risk that an individual will commit a criminal offence where the assessment is based solely on profiling or assessing personality traits and characteristics.

There is a statutory exception where AI is used to support a human assessment of a person's involvement in criminal activity, based on objective and verifiable facts directly linked to criminal activity.

GRC implication:

Organizations must distinguish between:

- Risk analysis based on objective evidence and relevant facts.
- Predictions based solely on personal profiling or personality characteristics.

A human reviewer does not automatically make an otherwise prohibited system permissible. The legal basis and actual methodology remain important.

#### 12.5 Untargeted Facial-Image Scraping

The Act prohibits AI systems that create or expand facial-recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage.

The governance concern is the systematic collection of biometric information without an appropriately targeted and lawful basis.

Example:

An organization collects millions of facial images from publicly accessible websites and CCTV footage to create a facial-recognition database without a targeted collection process.

GRC controls should address:

- Dataset provenance.
- Biometric-data collection methods.
- Lawfulness of processing.
- Third-party dataset acquisition.
- Supplier due diligence.
- Technical and organizational restrictions on prohibited collection practices.

#### 12.6 Emotion Recognition in Workplaces and Educational Institutions

The Act prohibits AI systems intended to infer emotions of natural persons in workplaces and educational institutions, except where the system is intended to be put in place or placed on the market for medical or safety reasons.

Emotion recognition refers to AI systems intended to identify or infer emotions or intentions based on biometric data.

Example:

An employer deploys an AI-powered video analytics platform that analyzes employees' facial expressions to infer frustration, motivation, engagement, or satisfaction and uses the results for employee performance assessments.

This raises a direct Article 5 concern.

GRC assessment questions:

1. Does the system process biometric data?
2. Is its purpose to infer emotions or intentions?
3. Is it deployed in a workplace or educational environment?
4. Does a statutory medical or safety exception apply?
5. Has the organization documented the legal assessment and deployment purpose?

#### 12.7 Biometric Categorization Based on Sensitive Characteristics

The Act prohibits certain biometric categorization systems that individually categorize natural persons based on biometric data to deduce or infer sensitive characteristics.

The prohibition covers categorization based on:

- Race.
- Political opinions.
- Trade union membership.
- Religious or philosophical beliefs.
- Sex life.
- Sexual orientation.

The Act provides specific exceptions, including certain lawful dataset-filtering or labelling activities and categorization of biometric data in the area of law enforcement.

These exceptions must be assessed narrowly against their legal conditions.

Governance implication:

Organizations should not assume that biometric categorization is acceptable simply because the data is processed by a third-party AI provider or because the system does not directly display the inferred characteristic to the user.

#### 12.8 Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification in Publicly Accessible Spaces

The Act generally prohibits the use of real-time remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for law-enforcement purposes, subject to narrowly defined exceptions and strict conditions.

The exceptions concern specified objectives, including:

- Searching for certain victims, including missing persons.
- Preventing specific, substantial, and imminent threats to life or physical safety, or a genuine and present or foreseeable threat of a terrorist attack.
- Locating or identifying a person suspected of specified serious criminal offences, subject to applicable legal conditions.

The Act establishes additional requirements concerning necessity, proportionality, authorization, territorial and temporal limits, and fundamental-rights safeguards.

GRC implication:

A law-enforcement organization cannot rely on a general security justification to deploy real-time remote biometric identification. The specific statutory exception and its conditions must be established.

### 13. High-Risk AI Systems Under Article 6

High-risk AI systems are not prohibited by default. They are permitted subject to specific regulatory requirements and obligations.

The EU AI Act establishes two principal pathways for classifying AI systems as high-risk.

High-risk classification under Article 6

Pathway 1 — Article 6(1): AI systems used as safety components or products

An AI system may be high-risk where:

- It is intended to be used as a safety component of a product, or is itself a product.
- The product is covered by legislation listed in Annex I.
- The product is required to undergo a third-party conformity assessment under that legislation before being placed on the market or put into service.

Pathway 2 — Article 6(2): AI systems listed in Annex III

AI systems falling within the use cases specified in Annex III are generally high-risk, subject to the conditions and exception in Article 6(3).

The distinction is important because classification is based on the system's intended purpose and regulatory context, not simply on the technology used.

For example, an AI model that analyzes network traffic is not automatically high-risk under the EU AI Act. Its classification depends on the intended purpose and whether it falls within one of the statutory high-risk categories.

### 14. Annex III — High-Risk AI Use Cases

Annex III identifies specific areas where AI systems can create significant risks to health, safety, or fundamental rights.

| Annex III category                                    | Example AI application                                                                                                                                       | Governance considerations                                          |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. Biometrics                                         | Remote biometric identification, biometric categorization, emotion recognition where covered                                                                 | Biometric data, fundamental rights, accuracy, discrimination       |
| 2. Critical infrastructure                            | AI safety components in management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, and water, gas, heating, or electricity supply            | Operational resilience, safety, cybersecurity, human oversight     |
| 3. Education and vocational training                  | Admission, assessment, educational placement, examination monitoring                                                                                         | Fairness, transparency, impact on educational opportunities        |
| 4. Employment and worker management                   | Recruitment, candidate selection, promotion, termination, task allocation, worker monitoring and evaluation                                                  | Employment rights, discrimination, explainability, human oversight |
| 5. Essential private and public services              | Eligibility assessments for essential public assistance, creditworthiness, emergency-call evaluation, certain insurance risk assessments                     | Access to services, discrimination, financial and social impact    |
| 6. Law enforcement                                    | Certain individual risk assessments, evidence evaluation, profiling, and other specified applications                                                        | Fundamental rights, legal safeguards, accountability               |
| 7. Migration, asylum, and border control              | Certain assessments of migration-related risks, application examination, and identity verification                                                           | Rights protection, accuracy, human review                          |
| 8. Administration of justice and democratic processes | AI systems assisting judicial authorities in researching or interpreting facts and law, and certain systems influencing election outcomes or voting behavior | Judicial independence, democratic integrity, transparency          |

Important: The table summarizes Annex III categories. Not every AI system used in these sectors automatically qualifies as high-risk. The precise use case and applicable legal wording must be assessed.

#### 14.1 Critical Infrastructure

AI systems intended to be used as safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or the supply of water, gas, heating, or electricity can fall within Annex III.

Examples may include:

- AI systems controlling or supporting electricity-grid operations.
- AI safety components used in water-treatment operations.
- AI systems supporting road traffic management.
- AI systems used as safety components in critical digital infrastructure management.

A cybersecurity monitoring AI system used in a telecom environment is not automatically high-risk merely because the telecom operator is considered important or critical infrastructure. The specific intended purpose and statutory category must be evaluated.

#### 14.2 Education and Vocational Training

Relevant applications include AI systems used to:

- Determine access or admission to educational institutions.
- Evaluate learning outcomes.
- Assess educational levels.
- Determine appropriate education or training levels.
- Monitor or detect prohibited behavior during tests.

Governance concern: Automated assessments can affect educational access, opportunities, and individual rights.

#### 14.3 Employment and Worker Management

Relevant applications include AI systems used for:

- Recruitment and candidate selection.
- Targeted job advertisements.
- Filtering job applications.
- Evaluating candidates.
- Decisions affecting employment relationships.
- Promotion or termination decisions.
- Task allocation based on individual behavior or personal characteristics.
- Monitoring and evaluating worker performance or behavior.

Enterprise example:

An organization deploys an AI platform that evaluates job applicants using CV analysis, interview assessments, and behavioral indicators, then ranks candidates for recruitment decisions.

The organization must assess whether the system falls within Annex III and implement the applicable high-risk requirements where it does.

#### 14.4 Access to Essential Private and Public Services

This category includes certain AI systems used to evaluate eligibility for essential public assistance benefits and services, assess creditworthiness, evaluate emergency calls, and conduct specified life and health insurance risk assessments.

Enterprise example:

A financial institution uses an AI system to assess an individual's creditworthiness and determine access to credit.

The organization must assess whether the intended purpose falls within Annex III and whether the Article 6(3) exception applies.

#### 14.5 Law Enforcement

Annex III covers specified law-enforcement applications, including certain systems used for:

- Assessing the risk of an individual becoming a victim of criminal offences.
- Polygraphs and similar tools.
- Evaluating the reliability of evidence.
- Assessing the risk of offending or reoffending, subject to the relevant legal distinctions.
- Profiling individuals in the detection, investigation, or prosecution of criminal offences.

The interaction between Annex III and Article 5 is particularly important. A system can be high-risk where permitted, while a specific use may be prohibited under Article 5.

#### 14.6 Migration, Asylum, and Border Control

Relevant systems include specified applications used for:

- Polygraphs and similar tools.
- Assessing certain risks posed by individuals.
- Assisting with examination of applications for asylum, visas, or residence permits.
- Detecting, recognizing, or identifying individuals in certain border-control contexts.

Organizations must assess fundamental-rights impacts and ensure appropriate legal and operational safeguards.

#### 14.7 Administration of Justice and Democratic Processes

Relevant systems include AI intended to assist judicial authorities in researching and interpreting facts and law, and applying the law to concrete facts.

Annex III also covers certain AI systems intended to influence the outcome of an election or referendum, or the voting behavior of natural persons in the exercise of their vote.

There is an exception for AI systems whose output people are not directly exposed to, such as tools used to organize, optimize, or structure political campaigns from an administrative or logistical perspective.

### 15. Article 6(3) — Exceptions to Annex III High-Risk Classification

Article 6(3) provides a limited exception for certain AI systems listed in Annex III.

An Annex III system may not be considered high-risk if it does not pose a significant risk of harm to the health, safety, or fundamental rights of natural persons, including by not materially influencing the outcome of decision-making.

The Act identifies circumstances in which an AI system may qualify for this exception, including where it is intended to:

- Perform a narrow procedural task.
- Improve the result of a previously completed human activity.
- Detect decision-making patterns or deviations from prior decision-making patterns, without replacing or influencing the previously completed human assessment, without proper human review.
- Perform a preparatory task to an assessment relevant to an Annex III use case.

However, this exception is not available where the Annex III system performs profiling of natural persons.

#### 15.1 Examples of Potentially Narrow Procedural Tasks

Consider an AI system used in recruitment.

Scenario A — Administrative processing

The system checks whether an application contains all required documents and flags missing attachments.

This may constitute a narrow procedural task, depending on the actual system purpose and operation.

Scenario B — Candidate ranking

The system evaluates applicants' experience, personality, and suitability, then ranks candidates for interview selection.

This is materially different. It may influence recruitment decisions and fall within the high-risk classification.

The distinction is not whether AI is involved. It is whether the system's actual purpose and function satisfy the legal conditions for the exception.

#### 15.2 Profiling Limitation

An important restriction is that the Article 6(3) exception does not apply to Annex III AI systems that perform profiling of natural persons.

For example, an AI recruitment platform that profiles candidates based on personal characteristics cannot rely on the narrow-task exception simply because a human recruiter makes the final decision.

#### 15.3 Provider Assessment and Documentation

A provider concluding that an Annex III system is not high-risk under Article 6(3) must document the assessment before placing the system on the market or putting it into service.

The provider must also register the system as required under Article 49(2).

GRC evidence should include:

- Intended-purpose statement.
- Annex III applicability assessment.
- Article 6(3) analysis.
- Assessment of significant risk and decision-making influence.
- Profiling assessment.
- Supporting technical and operational evidence.
- Approval and registration evidence where applicable.

An organization should not treat an internal classification label as sufficient evidence of a valid legal exemption.

### 16. High-Risk AI System Requirements: Articles 8–15

High-risk AI systems must satisfy a set of regulatory requirements before and during their operation.

Articles 8–15 establish the core requirements for high-risk AI systems.

| Article    | Requirement                                            | Governance objective                                                 |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Article 8  | Compliance with requirements                           | Ensure the system satisfies applicable high-risk requirements        |
| Article 9  | Risk management system                                 | Identify, analyze, evaluate, and mitigate risks                      |
| Article 10 | Data and data governance                               | Ensure appropriate training, validation, and testing data governance |
| Article 11 | Technical documentation                                | Demonstrate compliance and provide necessary technical information   |
| Article 12 | Record-keeping                                         | Enable automatic logging and traceability                            |
| Article 13 | Transparency and provision of information to deployers | Enable deployers to understand and use the system appropriately      |
| Article 14 | Human oversight                                        | Enable effective human supervision and intervention                  |
| Article 15 | Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity                | Ensure appropriate technical performance and resilience              |

These requirements should be treated as an integrated compliance framework rather than isolated technical controls.

#### 16.1 Article 9 — Risk Management System

Providers of high-risk AI systems must establish, implement, document, and maintain a risk management system throughout the system lifecycle.

The risk management process includes:

1. Identifying and analyzing known and reasonably foreseeable risks.
2. Estimating and evaluating risks that may emerge during intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse.
3. Evaluating risks based on post-market monitoring information and other available data.
4. Adopting appropriate risk-management measures.

The risk-management process must be iterative and regularly reviewed.

GRC implementation:

A risk management system should connect:

- AI system inventory.
- Intended-purpose documentation.
- Risk assessments.
- Threat modeling.
- Fundamental-rights and safety considerations.
- Control selection.
- Residual-risk assessment.
- Change management.
- Post-market monitoring.

Risk management should not be limited to a one-time approval before deployment.

#### 16.2 Article 10 — Data and Data Governance

High-risk AI systems that use data for training must be developed using training, validation, and testing datasets that meet applicable quality criteria.

The requirements include appropriate data governance and management practices addressing:

- Data collection processes.
- Data-origin and provenance.
- Data preparation.
- Data annotation and labelling.
- Data cleaning.
- Data enrichment.
- Assumptions concerning the information represented.
- Data availability and suitability.
- Relevant biases.
- Appropriate measures to identify, prevent, and mitigate bias.

Where appropriate, datasets must take account of the characteristics of the persons or groups on which the system is intended to be used.

GRC example:

A high-risk recruitment AI system is trained using historical recruitment data.

If historical decisions contain discriminatory patterns, the model may reproduce or amplify those patterns.

The provider must establish appropriate data governance, assess relevant biases, and implement suitable mitigation measures.

Evidence examples:

- Dataset inventory.
- Data lineage and provenance records.
- Data quality assessments.
- Data preparation documentation.
- Bias assessment reports.
- Training and validation dataset separation.
- Data governance approvals.

#### 16.3 Article 11 — Technical Documentation

Providers must prepare technical documentation before placing a high-risk AI system on the market or putting it into service.

The documentation must demonstrate that the system complies with applicable requirements and provide relevant information to authorities and notified bodies where required.

The documentation should address relevant matters such as:

- General system description.
- Intended purpose.
- System architecture.
- Development processes.
- Algorithmic design choices.
- Training methodologies.
- Data governance.
- Performance and testing.
- Risk management.
- Monitoring arrangements.
- Compliance assessment.

The Act specifies technical documentation requirements in Annex IV.

GRC implication:

Technical documentation is a critical source of audit evidence. A provider should be able to demonstrate not only that controls exist but also how the system was designed, developed, tested, and assessed against regulatory requirements.

#### 16.4 Article 12 — Record-Keeping and Logging

High-risk AI systems must be designed and developed with capabilities that enable automatic recording of events, commonly referred to as logs, over the system's lifetime.

Logging capabilities must support traceability and monitoring appropriate to the system's intended purpose.

Relevant information may include:

- Periods of use.
- Reference databases used by the system.
- Input data that triggers a match.
- Identification of persons involved in verification of results, where applicable.

Logging requirements must be interpreted in conjunction with applicable data-protection and retention requirements.

GRC example:

A high-risk AI system used to support eligibility decisions should retain appropriate logs to help reconstruct how a decision was generated, what information was used, and whether required human oversight occurred.

#### 16.5 Article 13 — Transparency and Information to Deployers

High-risk AI systems must be sufficiently transparent to enable deployers to interpret the system's output and use it appropriately.

Providers must supply instructions for use containing relevant information, including:

- Provider identity and contact details.
- System characteristics and capabilities.
- Intended purpose.
- Level of accuracy and relevant performance limitations.
- Known or foreseeable circumstances that may affect performance.
- Human oversight measures.
- Required maintenance and monitoring.
- Relevant computational and technical requirements.

GRC implication:

An organization deploying a third-party high-risk AI system should not rely solely on vendor marketing material.

It should obtain and assess the provider's instructions, technical documentation where available, performance limitations, and operational requirements.

#### 16.6 Article 14 — Human Oversight

High-risk AI systems must be designed and developed so that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during use.

Human oversight measures must be proportionate to the risks, level of autonomy, and context of use.

Oversight should enable designated individuals to:

- Understand relevant system capabilities and limitations.
- Remain aware of automation bias.
- Interpret system outputs appropriately.
- Decide not to use the system or disregard, override, or reverse its output.
- Intervene in the system's operation or interrupt it through an appropriate mechanism.

Critical distinction:

Human oversight does not mean placing a person in the workflow merely to approve an AI recommendation.

A meaningful human oversight process requires:

- Competent and trained personnel.
- Access to relevant information.
- Sufficient time to evaluate outputs.
- Authority to challenge or override decisions.
- Defined escalation procedures.
- Evidence that oversight is actually performed.

A human approval step that routinely accepts AI recommendations without meaningful review is not a robust governance control.

#### 16.7 Article 15 — Accuracy, Robustness, and Cybersecurity

High-risk AI systems must achieve appropriate levels of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity throughout their lifecycle.

The requirements include:

- Appropriate accuracy levels and performance metrics.
- Resilience against errors, faults, and inconsistencies.
- Resilience against attempts by unauthorized third parties to alter system use or performance.
- Protection against vulnerabilities specific to AI systems.

The Act identifies relevant cybersecurity threats, including:

- Data poisoning.
- Model poisoning.
- Adversarial examples.
- Model evasion.
- Confidentiality attacks.
- Model-related vulnerabilities.

GRC implementation:

A cybersecurity assessment should consider more than conventional infrastructure security.

| Security area        | Example assessment                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Data poisoning       | Can an attacker manipulate training, fine-tuning, or other data sources?                            |
| Model poisoning      | Can unauthorized parties alter model parameters, weights, or training processes?                    |
| Adversarial examples | Can manipulated inputs cause incorrect or unsafe outputs?                                           |
| Model evasion        | Can an attacker craft inputs that bypass intended detection or classification?                      |
| Confidentiality      | Can sensitive information be extracted through model interactions or other attack techniques?       |
| Access control       | Are model endpoints, administrative interfaces, datasets, and model artifacts adequately protected? |
| Supply chain         | Are models, datasets, libraries, and external AI components assessed and controlled?                |
| Monitoring           | Are security events, anomalous behavior, and model-performance degradation monitored?               |

The AI Act's cybersecurity requirement should be integrated with the organization's wider security architecture, vulnerability management, incident response, and supplier-risk processes.

#### 17.1 Telecom Operator Example

Consider a telecom operator deploying several AI systems:

- Customer service chatbot.
- AI-based network anomaly detection.
- Predictive network maintenance.
- AI-based fraud detection.
- AI-powered recruitment screening.
- Employee productivity analytics.
- AI-driven customer creditworthiness assessment.

The organization should not classify all these systems under a single regulatory category.

Each use case requires a separate assessment of:

1. Intended purpose.
2. Provider and deployer roles.
3. Applicable Article 5 prohibitions.
4. Article 6 and Annex III classification.
5. Transparency requirements.
6. Other applicable legal obligations.
7. Required governance controls and evidence.

For example, a network anomaly detection system does not become high-risk simply because it supports a telecom operator. Its intended purpose and relationship to a safety component within a covered critical-infrastructure use case must be examined.

An AI recruitment screening system, however, may fall directly within Annex III's employment category.

This illustrates why AI governance must be use-case driven rather than based solely on technology names or organizational industry classifications.

#### 18.1 Regulatory Requirement vs. Internal Control

A GRC professional must distinguish between the legal requirement and the organization's chosen control implementation.

For example:

- Regulatory requirement: Article 9 requires a risk management system for high-risk AI systems.
- Internal control: The organization establishes a formal AI risk assessment procedure.
- Control implementation: Risk assessments are performed at defined lifecycle stages.
- Operating evidence: Completed assessments, approvals, remediation records, and periodic reviews.

A documented policy alone does not demonstrate that the organization has implemented an effective risk management system.

#### 18.2 Provider and Deployer Responsibilities

The AI Act allocates obligations differently depending on the actor's role.

A provider is generally responsible for ensuring that the high-risk AI system meets the applicable requirements and for fulfilling the relevant provider obligations.

A deployer has responsibilities concerning the use of the system under its authority, including following instructions, assigning appropriate human oversight, monitoring operation, and retaining logs where required.

The exact obligations depend on the relevant provisions and circumstances. Contracts may allocate operational responsibilities, but they cannot simply remove statutory obligations imposed on a regulated actor.

### 19. Key Regulatory Distinctions for AI Governance Professionals

| Concept                  | Correct interpretation                                                                                                            | Common mistake                                                            |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prohibited AI practice   | A use falling within Article 5's prohibition                                                                                      | Treating it as a high-risk use that can be approved through controls      |
| High-risk AI system      | A system classified under Article 6 and applicable Annexes                                                                        | Assuming every AI system used by a regulated organization is high-risk    |
| General-purpose AI model | A model subject to its own regulatory provisions                                                                                  | Treating all GPAI models as high-risk AI systems                          |
| Intended purpose         | The use intended by the provider, as specified in relevant information and documentation                                          | Classifying a system only by its technical name                           |
| Provider                 | The actor that develops or has developed a system and places it on the market or puts it into service under its name or trademark | Assuming the technology vendor is always the only provider                |
| Deployer                 | The actor using an AI system under its authority, subject to the statutory definition                                             | Assuming every customer is automatically a deployer in every circumstance |
| Article 6(3) exception   | A limited exception for certain Annex III systems meeting statutory conditions                                                    | Treating an internal low-risk label as sufficient evidence                |
| Human oversight          | Effective supervision with the ability to understand, challenge, and intervene                                                    | Treating a mandatory approval click as meaningful oversight               |

### 20. Summary and Core Principle

The EU AI Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework in which the legal classification of an AI system determines the applicable obligations.

Article 5 prohibits specific AI practices that create unacceptable risks. Article 6 establishes the pathways for identifying high-risk AI systems, including systems used as safety components in certain regulated products and systems falling within Annex III.

High-risk AI systems are subject to requirements covering risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity.

For GRC professionals, the essential responsibilities are to:

- Identify AI systems and document their intended purposes.
- Determine the organization's legal role.
- Assess prohibited practices before considering deployment.
- Apply Article 6 and Annex III classification criteria.
- Document any Article 6(3) exception.
- Translate applicable requirements into controls, accountability, and evidence.
- Monitor compliance throughout the AI system lifecycle.

Core Principle: EU AI Act compliance begins with accurate legal classification. An organization cannot establish the correct governance controls until it understands what the AI system is intended to do, how it is used, and which regulatory obligations apply.

## Part 3 — EU AI Act General-Purpose AI Models, Transparency Obligations, and Governance Responsibilities

### 21. General-Purpose AI Models (GPAI) Under the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act establishes specific regulatory requirements for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models. These requirements recognize that general-purpose models can be integrated into many different downstream systems and applications, creating risks that extend beyond the original model provider.

A GPAI model is generally characterized by its significant generality and ability to competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks. It can be integrated into multiple downstream systems or applications.

Examples include models capable of:

- Generating and summarizing text.
- Translating languages.
- Generating and interpreting code.
- Answering questions.
- Supporting reasoning tasks.
- Processing images, audio, or other modalities.
- Supporting downstream AI applications.

The regulatory distinction is important:

| Concept                       | Meaning                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| AI system                     | A machine-based system that infers how to generate outputs from inputs                                  |
| GPAI model                    | A model with significant generality that can competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks         |
| GPAI model with systemic risk | A GPAI model presenting systemic risks due to its high-impact capabilities, as determined under the Act |
| Downstream AI system          | An AI system incorporating or using a GPAI model as a component                                         |

A GPAI model is not automatically a high-risk AI system. The legal obligations applicable to GPAI models differ from the high-risk AI system requirements under Articles 8–15.

A GPAI model may be used as a component of a downstream high-risk AI system, but the downstream system's classification must be assessed separately.

Enterprise example:

A telecom operator purchases access to an external GPAI model through an API and integrates it into:

- Customer support applications.
- Internal knowledge assistants.
- Security operations workflows.
- Document analysis platforms.
- Employee productivity tools.

The model's GPAI classification does not automatically determine the regulatory classification of each application.

Each downstream application requires its own assessment based on intended purpose, deployment context, and applicable AI Act provisions.

### 22. GPAI Model Provider Obligations

Article 53 establishes obligations for providers of GPAI models.

These obligations are intended to support downstream integration, regulatory transparency, and copyright compliance.

The principal obligations include:

#### 22.1 Technical Documentation

Providers must prepare and maintain technical documentation concerning the GPAI model.

The documentation must include relevant information such as:

- General description of the model.
- Tasks the model is intended to perform.
- Types of AI systems into which the model can be integrated.
- Acceptable use policies.
- Model architecture and number of parameters.
- Input and output modalities.
- Relevant information about training, testing, and validation.
- Information concerning the computational resources used for training.
- Other information specified by the Act.

The technical documentation must be made available to the AI Office and national competent authorities upon request.

#### 22.2 Information for Downstream AI System Providers

GPAI providers must prepare and provide information and documentation to downstream providers who intend to integrate the model into their AI systems.

The information should enable downstream providers to understand:

- Model capabilities and limitations.
- Relevant technical characteristics.
- Integration requirements.
- Applicable use conditions.
- Known risks and limitations.
- Information necessary to comply with their obligations under the AI Act.

This is particularly important in enterprise environments where organizations use third-party models rather than developing their own.

GRC implication:

A company integrating an external GPAI model should establish a process to obtain, review, and retain relevant provider documentation.

Vendor marketing materials are not an adequate substitute for technical documentation and integration information.

#### 22.3 Copyright Compliance Policy

GPAI providers must establish a policy to comply with applicable Union copyright and related-rights law.

The policy must include measures to identify and comply with rights reservations expressed by rights holders under applicable EU copyright legislation.

This creates a governance responsibility concerning:

- Training-data acquisition.
- Copyright compliance.
- Data provenance.
- Rights reservations.
- Content-use restrictions.
- Legal accountability.

#### 22.4 Public Summary of Training Content

GPAI providers must make publicly available a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training the model, according to a template provided by the AI Office.

This requirement supports transparency concerning the broad categories and sources of training content.

It should not be confused with an obligation to publish the complete training dataset or disclose all confidential training information.

### 23. GPAI Models with Systemic Risk

The EU AI Act introduces additional requirements for GPAI models that present systemic risk.

A GPAI model may be classified as having systemic risk based on its high-impact capabilities, evaluated using appropriate technical tools and methodologies, or where the Commission designates it under the applicable criteria.

The Act establishes a presumption of systemic risk where the cumulative amount of computation used for training measured in floating-point operations exceeds the threshold specified in Article 51, currently set at \\(10^{25}\\) FLOPs.

The Commission may update the threshold and relevant criteria.

Systemic risk is not determined solely by the commercial popularity of a model or the number of customers using it.

The assessment considers the model's capabilities, potential impact, and relevant systemic-risk criteria.

#### 23.1 Examples of Systemic Risks

Potential systemic risks include:

| Risk category           | Example                                                                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cybersecurity           | Advanced capabilities that could materially increase the scale or sophistication of cyberattacks           |
| Biological and chemical | Capabilities that could facilitate the development or misuse of dangerous biological or chemical materials |
| Critical infrastructure | Capabilities that could contribute to significant disruption of essential infrastructure                   |
| Societal impact         | Large-scale manipulation or influence of individuals and groups                                            |
| Economic impact         | Significant disruption to economic activity or important market functions                                  |
| Information integrity   | Large-scale generation and dissemination of misleading or deceptive content                                |
| Autonomous capabilities | Model capabilities that could create risks through complex or poorly controlled downstream use             |

These are illustrative risk categories, not an exhaustive legal classification list.

#### 23.2 Additional Provider Obligations

Article 55 establishes additional obligations for providers of GPAI models with systemic risk.

These include:

1. Performing model evaluations in accordance with standardized protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art.
2. Assessing and mitigating possible systemic risks at Union level.
3. Tracking, documenting, and reporting serious incidents and possible corrective measures.
4. Ensuring an adequate level of cybersecurity protection for the GPAI model and its physical infrastructure.

GRC implication:

Systemic-risk governance requires more than conventional model documentation.

Providers need structured processes for capability evaluation, systemic-risk analysis, incident management, cybersecurity, and ongoing monitoring.

### 24. Open-Source GPAI Models and Regulatory Obligations

The EU AI Act provides certain limited exceptions for GPAI models released under a free and open-source licence.

However, open-source availability does not automatically exempt a provider from all GPAI obligations.

Article 53 provides that certain obligations concerning technical documentation, information for downstream providers, and copyright policy do not apply to providers of GPAI models released under a free and open-source licence that allows access, usage, modification, and distribution, provided that the model's parameters, including weights, information about the model architecture, and information about model usage are publicly available.

However, the exception does not apply to GPAI models with systemic risk.

The obligation to make a sufficiently detailed summary of training content publicly available also remains relevant.

Important distinction:

| Model release approach        | Governance consideration                                                                          |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Open-source model             | Examine the actual licence, availability of model components, and applicable statutory conditions |
| Open-weight model             | Public weights do not necessarily satisfy the statutory conditions for the open-source exception  |
| Proprietary model             | Provider obligations generally apply according to the Act                                         |
| GPAI model with systemic risk | Additional obligations apply; the open-source exception does not remove them                      |

For enterprise GRC teams, the key issue is not simply whether a model is advertised as open source.

The organization must understand the actual licensing terms, availability of components, provider identity, and regulatory obligations.

### 25. Transparency Obligations Under Article 50

Article 50 establishes transparency obligations for certain AI systems.

These requirements are separate from the obligations applicable to high-risk AI systems and GPAI models.

They are intended to ensure that individuals are appropriately informed when interacting with AI or encountering certain AI-generated or manipulated content.

#### 25.1 AI Interaction Disclosure

Providers must ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed so that individuals are informed that they are interacting with an AI system.

This obligation does not apply where it is obvious from the circumstances and context of use.

Example:

An organization deploys an AI chatbot on its customer support portal.

The user should be informed that the chatbot is an AI system unless the circumstances make that fact obvious.

GRC control examples:

- AI interaction disclosure standards.
- Customer-facing interface review.
- Approved chatbot disclosure wording.
- User-experience compliance testing.
- Records of transparency assessments.

#### 25.2 Synthetic Content Marking

Providers of AI systems, including GPAI systems, that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text content must ensure that outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated.

The requirement is subject to technical feasibility, taking into account the specific characteristics and limitations of the content types, implementation costs, and generally acknowledged state of the art.

This is not a requirement that every AI-generated output must display a visible watermark to every user.

Machine-readable marking and visible disclosure are distinct concepts.

#### 25.3 Emotion Recognition and Biometric Categorization Disclosure

Deployers of emotion-recognition systems or biometric categorization systems must inform the individuals exposed to the system's operation, subject to applicable legal conditions and exceptions.

This transparency obligation does not make an otherwise prohibited use permissible.

For example, informing employees that an emotion-recognition system is being used does not remove the Article 5 prohibition where the use falls within its scope.

#### 25.4 Deepfake Disclosure

Deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate image, audio, or video content constituting a deepfake must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated.

The Act defines deepfake content as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities, or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful.

There are specific considerations for artistic, creative, satirical, fictional, and analogous works.

Where the content forms part of an obviously artistic, creative, satirical, fictional, or analogous work, the disclosure obligation is subject to the Act's provision that disclosure must be made in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work.

#### 25.5 AI-Generated Text Published to Inform the Public

Deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate text published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest must disclose that the text has been artificially generated or manipulated.

The obligation does not apply where the AI-generated content has undergone a process of human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for publication.

This distinction matters for organizations publishing:

- Public announcements.
- News-like information.
- Public-interest reports.
- Other informational content directed at the public.

The applicability of the requirement must be assessed against the actual publication purpose and statutory conditions.

### 26. Transparency vs. Explainability vs. Interpretability

These concepts are related but should not be treated as interchangeable.

| Concept          | Definition                                                                                       | Example                                                              |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Transparency     | Providing relevant information about the AI system, its operation, purpose, limitations, and use | Informing customers that they are interacting with an AI chatbot     |
| Explainability   | Providing an understandable account of why an AI system produced a particular output             | Explaining which factors contributed to a credit-risk recommendation |
| Interpretability | The degree to which a human can understand how a model or its components operate                 | Understanding the influence of features in a simpler decision model  |

Transparency is a governance and disclosure concept. Explainability and interpretability concern the ability to understand system behavior and outputs.

An organization can provide transparency documentation without necessarily having a fully interpretable model.

Similarly, an explainable output does not automatically demonstrate regulatory compliance.

GRC assessment questions:

1. Is the individual informed when interacting with an AI system?
2. Are the system's intended purpose and limitations documented?
3. Can relevant users understand the meaning of AI-generated outputs?
4. Are explanations appropriate to the decision and its potential impact?
5. Are transparency obligations incorporated into system design and operational procedures?

### 27. AI Literacy Under Article 4

Article 4 requires providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level of AI literacy of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf.

AI literacy includes the skills, knowledge, and understanding that enable providers, deployers, and affected persons, taking account of their respective rights and obligations under the Act, to make an informed deployment of AI systems and gain awareness about the opportunities and risks of AI and possible harm it can cause.

The required level of AI literacy should consider:

- Technical knowledge.
- Experience.
- Education and training.
- Context in which AI systems are used.
- Persons or groups affected by the AI system.

AI literacy is not limited to AI engineers or data scientists.

It is relevant to:

- Senior management.
- Procurement teams.
- Legal and compliance teams.
- Cybersecurity professionals.
- Risk managers.
- HR personnel.
- Business owners.
- AI system operators.
- Employees using enterprise AI tools.

#### 27.1 Enterprise AI Literacy Programme

An organization can establish a role-based AI literacy programme.

| Audience                       | Required knowledge                                             | Example training                          |
| ------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Board and executive management | AI governance, strategic risk, regulatory accountability       | Executive AI governance briefing          |
| Legal and compliance           | AI Act, GDPR, contractual and regulatory responsibilities      | AI regulatory compliance training         |
| Cybersecurity teams            | AI threat landscape, model security, data protection           | AI security awareness and threat training |
| Procurement teams              | GPAI providers, third-party risk, contractual safeguards       | AI supplier due diligence                 |
| Developers and technical teams | Model limitations, data governance, secure development         | Secure AI development training            |
| Business users                 | Responsible use, limitations, confidentiality, human oversight | Enterprise AI acceptable-use training     |
| HR and recruitment             | Employment-related AI risks and human decision-making          | AI-supported recruitment governance       |

#### 27.2 Evidence of AI Literacy

Evidence may include:

- AI literacy policy.
- Training needs analysis.
- Role-based training matrix.
- Training materials.
- Attendance records.
- Assessment results.
- Refresher training.
- Awareness campaigns.
- Management review records.

Important: Article 4 does not prescribe a universal certification, a fixed number of training hours, or a single mandatory training programme for every organization.

The organization should determine appropriate measures based on its context, roles, and AI use cases.

### 28. AI Act Governance Roles and Accountability

The EU AI Act creates responsibilities for different actors across the AI value chain.

A practical enterprise governance model should clearly identify the responsible parties.

| Role                              | Primary responsibility                                                              | Example accountability                                           |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Board of Directors                | Strategic oversight and risk governance                                             | Approves AI governance direction and risk appetite               |
| Executive Management              | Implementation of organizational governance                                         | Allocates resources and establishes accountability               |
| AI Governance Committee           | Cross-functional oversight                                                          | Reviews AI classification, risks, and compliance                 |
| AI System Owner                   | Business ownership of an AI system                                                  | Ensures intended purpose and operational controls are documented |
| Provider                          | Regulatory responsibilities for systems or models it provides                       | Ensures applicable provider obligations are fulfilled            |
| Deployer                          | Regulatory responsibilities for use under its authority                             | Ensures appropriate deployment and operational controls          |
| Data Protection Officer           | Independent data-protection advice and monitoring within statutory responsibilities | Advises on GDPR obligations and DPIAs                            |
| CISO                              | Cybersecurity governance                                                            | Oversees security risks, controls, and incident management       |

| Internal Audit                    | Independent assurance                                                               | Evaluates governance and control effectiveness                   |

This is an illustrative operating model, not a mandatory organizational chart prescribed by the AI Act.

#### 28.1 Separation of Regulatory and Internal Responsibilities

An organization should distinguish between:

- Legal responsibility imposed on the provider or deployer.
- Internal accountability assigned to business and technical owners.
- Control ownership assigned to operational teams.
- Independent assurance performed by audit or other assurance functions.

For example, a business unit may own an AI application internally, while the organization is legally acting as the deployer.

The business owner does not automatically become the statutory deployer simply because it manages the system.

#### 28.2 Governance Committee Responsibilities

An AI Governance Committee may be responsible for:

1. Reviewing proposed AI use cases.
2. Confirming AI system inventory completeness.
3. Reviewing regulatory classification assessments.
4. Reviewing high-risk AI assessments.
5. Monitoring AI-related incidents.
6. Reviewing third-party AI providers.
7. Approving risk treatment plans within delegated authority.
8. Monitoring AI literacy.
9. Reviewing compliance reporting.
10. Escalating material risks to executive management.

The committee should not replace the legal responsibilities of providers, deployers, or other actors.

### 29. Integration with GDPR and Other Regulatory Requirements

The EU AI Act does not replace the GDPR or other applicable European legislation.

Organizations must assess overlapping legal requirements where AI systems process personal data or operate in regulated sectors.

| Regulatory area            | Main concern                                                                     | Relationship to AI governance                                     |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| EU AI Act                  | AI system and GPAI model obligations                                             | AI classification, risk management, transparency, oversight       |
| GDPR                       | Processing of personal data                                                      | Lawfulness, fairness, transparency, data subject rights, security |
| NIS2                       | Cybersecurity risk management and incident obligations for entities within scope | Organizational cybersecurity and resilience                       |
| DORA                       | Digital operational resilience for financial entities within scope               | ICT risk, third-party risk, testing, incident management          |
| Product safety legislation | Safety requirements for regulated products                                       | AI safety components and conformity assessment                    |
| EU copyright legislation   | Copyright and related rights                                                     | Training-data and content-use compliance                          |

#### 29.1 AI Act and GDPR Roles Are Different

The AI Act's provider and deployer roles must not be confused with GDPR controller and processor roles.

| EU AI Act   | GDPR                 |
| ----------- | -------------------- |
| Provider    | No direct equivalent |
| Deployer    | No direct equivalent |
| Importer    | No direct equivalent |
| Distributor | No direct equivalent |
| —           | Controller           |
| —           | Processor            |

A company can simultaneously be:

- A provider under the AI Act.
- A deployer under the AI Act for another AI system.
- A controller under the GDPR.
- A processor under the GDPR for a separate processing activity.

The roles must be determined independently based on the legal definitions and actual activities.

#### 29.2 AI Act and GDPR Article 22

GDPR Article 22 concerns decisions based solely on automated processing, including profiling, that produce legal effects concerning a person or similarly significantly affect them, subject to the provision's conditions and exceptions.

Not every AI-supported decision falls within Article 22.

For example, a human decision-maker who meaningfully evaluates an AI recommendation may produce a different legal analysis from a decision made solely through automated processing.

However, nominal human approval should not be assumed to constitute meaningful human involvement.

Organizations should assess:

- Whether the decision is solely automated.
- Whether it produces legal or similarly significant effects.
- Whether profiling is involved.
- Whether an applicable exception exists.
- Whether required safeguards are implemented.

### 30. Summary and Core Principle

The EU AI Act regulates GPAI models and certain AI system uses through distinct but interconnected obligations.

GPAI model providers must address technical documentation, downstream-provider information, copyright compliance, and training-content summaries. Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk face additional evaluation, mitigation, incident-reporting, and cybersecurity requirements.

Article 50 establishes transparency obligations for specified AI interactions and generated or manipulated content. Article 4 establishes AI literacy responsibilities for providers and deployers.

Effective enterprise governance requires clearly defined accountability across legal, business, technical, cybersecurity, procurement, privacy, and compliance functions. It must also integrate the AI Act with GDPR and other applicable regulatory requirements without confusing their respective legal roles.

Core Principle: AI governance is not limited to classifying systems as prohibited or high-risk. It must also address GPAI model responsibilities, transparency, AI literacy, downstream integration, and the accountability of every organization participating in the AI value chain.

## Part 4 — EU AI Act Transparency, Conformity Assessment, Accountability, and Compliance Operations

### 26. Transparency Obligations Under Article 50 (Continued)

Article 50 establishes transparency requirements for specific AI systems and use cases. These obligations are not limited to high-risk AI systems.

They address situations where people interact with AI or encounter synthetic or manipulated content that could otherwise be mistaken for authentic human-generated material.

#### 26.1 Synthetic Content Marking

Providers of AI systems, including systems with general-purpose AI capabilities, that generate synthetic audio, image, video, or text content must ensure that their outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated.

The marking must be effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable to the extent technically feasible, taking into account the specificities and limitations of different content types, implementation costs, and the generally acknowledged state of the art.

There are exceptions for certain systems performing an assistive function for standard editing or not substantially altering input data or its semantics, and for systems authorized by law for law-enforcement purposes.

Enterprise example:

An organization deploys a generative AI platform that creates marketing images, product videos, and synthetic audio.

The organization should establish controls to ensure that generated content is appropriately marked in accordance with applicable requirements.

GRC assessment questions:

1. Does the system generate synthetic audio, images, video, or text?
2. Does the provider implement machine-readable marking?
3. Is the marking detectable and sufficiently robust?
4. Are exceptions being relied upon documented and legally assessed?
5. Can the organization demonstrate that applicable transparency requirements are implemented?

#### 26.2 Deepfakes and AI-Generated or Manipulated Content

Deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate image, audio, or video content constituting a deepfake must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated.

A deepfake is defined under Article 3 as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content that resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities, or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful.

The disclosure requirement includes certain exceptions, such as where the content is part of an evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional, or analogous work, subject to the applicable conditions. Where relevant, disclosure should be made in an appropriate manner that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work.

Example:

A company generates a realistic video showing its chief executive announcing a new corporate policy, although the executive never recorded or authorized the statement.

The organization must assess the disclosure obligation and the risk of misleading employees, customers, investors, or the public.

GRC controls:

- Synthetic media disclosure policy.
- Approval of AI-generated executive communications.
- Content provenance and traceability.
- Deepfake detection and incident response procedures.
- Defined accountability for publishing manipulated media.

#### 26.3 AI-Generated Text on Matters of Public Interest

Deployers of AI systems that generate or manipulate text published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest must disclose that the text has been artificially generated or manipulated.

The disclosure obligation is subject to statutory exceptions, including where the content has undergone human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility for its publication.

There is also an exception for content authorized by law for the detection, prevention, investigation, or prosecution of criminal offences.

Example:

A media organization uses generative AI to produce an article about a public election or government policy.

The organization should assess whether Article 50 applies and whether the human-review and editorial-responsibility exception is satisfied.

#### 26.4 Emotion Recognition and Biometric Categorization Disclosure

Deployers of emotion recognition systems or biometric categorization systems must inform natural persons exposed to their operation and process personal data in accordance with applicable data-protection legislation.

This transparency obligation must be read alongside Article 5. Certain emotion-recognition and biometric-categorization practices are prohibited, so Article 50 disclosure does not make a prohibited practice lawful.

Key distinction: Transparency is not a substitute for legal authorization. Informing individuals that a system is being used does not automatically make the processing permissible.

### 27. Conformity Assessment Under the EU AI Act

Conformity assessment is the process used to demonstrate that an AI system satisfies the applicable regulatory requirements before it is placed on the market or put into service.

For high-risk AI systems, conformity assessment is a central compliance mechanism.

The assessment process depends on the category of AI system, applicable product legislation, and the specific requirements of the EU AI Act.

#### 27.1 Purpose of Conformity Assessment

The conformity assessment process aims to establish that:

- The AI system satisfies applicable legal requirements.
- Relevant risk-management measures are implemented.
- Data governance requirements are addressed.
- Technical documentation is complete.
- Human oversight mechanisms are appropriate.
- Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity requirements are met.
- Required quality-management processes are established.

#### 27.2 Conformity Assessment Procedures

The EU AI Act provides different conformity assessment routes.

| Assessment route                  | Description                                                                          | Governance consideration                                                       |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Internal control                  | Provider performs the applicable conformity assessment using its internal procedures | Requires documented evidence, accountability, and quality-management processes |
| Third-party conformity assessment | An independent notified body evaluates compliance where required                     | Requires coordination, technical documentation, and independent assessment     |
| Product legislation integration   | AI system conformity assessment is integrated with applicable product legislation    | Requires alignment between AI Act and sector-specific product compliance       |

Under Article 43, many Annex III high-risk AI systems follow the internal control procedure under Annex VI, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions.

Certain biometric systems listed in Annex III may require third-party conformity assessment under Annex VII, depending on the circumstances and applicable conditions.

For high-risk AI systems covered by Annex I product legislation, the applicable conformity assessment procedure depends on the relevant product legislation and the AI Act provisions.

Important: A provider should not assume that every high-risk AI system requires certification by an external notified body. The conformity assessment route must be established from the applicable legal provisions.

### 28. EU Declaration of Conformity and CE Marking

After completing the applicable conformity assessment procedure, the provider must prepare an EU declaration of conformity under Article 47.

The declaration confirms that the high-risk AI system complies with the applicable requirements of the EU AI Act.

Where applicable, the CE marking must be affixed to the high-risk AI system in accordance with Article 48.

#### 28.1 EU Declaration of Conformity

The declaration should contain the required information, including:

- Identification of the AI system.
- Name and address of the provider.
- A statement that the declaration is issued under the sole responsibility of the provider.
- Identification of the relevant EU legislation.
- References to applicable harmonized standards or common specifications, where relevant.
- Details of the notified body, where applicable.
- Place and date of issue.
- Identity and signature of the authorized person.

#### 28.2 CE Marking

CE marking indicates that the relevant product or AI system meets applicable EU harmonization legislation.

It is not:

- A general cybersecurity certification.
- Proof that an AI system is completely safe.
- A guarantee that an AI system will never produce harmful outputs.
- A substitute for ongoing monitoring and compliance.

GRC implication: Organizations should verify the applicability and availability of conformity documentation when procuring or deploying high-risk AI systems.

### 29. Registration of High-Risk AI Systems

Article 49 establishes registration obligations for certain high-risk AI systems in the EU database.

The registration requirements depend on the category of AI system and the relevant actor.

Providers must register applicable high-risk AI systems before placing them on the market or putting them into service, subject to the specific requirements and exceptions.

Deployers that are public authorities, EU institutions, bodies, or agencies must register their use of applicable Annex III high-risk AI systems in accordance with the Act.

Certain sensitive use cases may be subject to restricted registration arrangements rather than unrestricted public access.

GRC assessment questions:

1. Does the AI system fall within a category requiring registration?
2. Which actor is responsible for registration?
3. Has registration been completed before the relevant market placement or use?
4. Is the registration information accurate and maintained?
5. Are any restricted-access provisions applicable?

### 30. Post-Market Monitoring and Serious Incident Reporting

AI governance does not end when a system is approved, registered, or deployed.

The EU AI Act establishes requirements for monitoring AI systems after they have been placed on the market or put into service.

#### 30.1 Article 72 — Post-Market Monitoring

Providers of high-risk AI systems must establish and document a post-market monitoring system proportionate to the nature of the AI technology and the risks associated with the system.

The monitoring system must actively and systematically collect, document, and analyze relevant performance data throughout the system's lifetime.

The purpose is to evaluate whether the AI system continues to comply with applicable requirements and whether corrective or preventive action is necessary.

Monitoring should consider:

- Performance degradation.
- Accuracy changes.
- Unexpected system behavior.
- Emerging vulnerabilities.
- Changes in operating conditions.
- New reasonably foreseeable risks.
- Serious incidents.
- Corrective and preventive measures.

Enterprise example:

A telecom operator deploys a high-risk AI system supporting critical infrastructure operations.

After deployment, changes in network conditions cause the system's performance to deteriorate.

The provider's post-market monitoring process should identify and assess the issue and determine whether corrective action is necessary.

#### 30.2 Article 73 — Reporting of Serious Incidents

Providers of high-risk AI systems must report serious incidents to the relevant market surveillance authorities in accordance with Article 73.

A serious incident includes an incident or malfunction that directly or indirectly leads to:

- Death or serious damage to health.
- Serious and irreversible disruption of the management or operation of critical infrastructure.
- Infringement of obligations under EU law intended to protect fundamental rights.
- Serious damage to property or the environment.

Reporting timelines depend on the nature and severity of the incident. The Act establishes specific deadlines, including expedited reporting for the most serious incidents.

GRC implication:

Organizations should integrate AI incident management with their existing cybersecurity incident response, product safety, legal, and regulatory reporting processes.

An AI-related incident may trigger several reporting obligations under different legal frameworks. The organization must assess each independently.

#### 30.3 Corrective Actions

Where a provider identifies non-compliance or a risk associated with a high-risk AI system, it may need to take corrective actions, including:

- Bringing the system into compliance.
- Withdrawing the system from the market.
- Disabling the system.
- Recalling the system where applicable.
- Informing relevant authorities and affected actors.

Corrective action should be documented, tracked, approved, and validated.

### 31. Quality Management System Under Article 17

Providers of high-risk AI systems must establish a quality management system that ensures compliance with the EU AI Act.

The quality management system must be documented and implemented in a systematic and orderly manner.

It should address areas such as:

| Quality management area | Expected governance activity                                      |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Regulatory strategy     | Establish a compliance strategy and procedures                    |
| Design and development  | Control AI system design and development processes                |
| Testing and validation  | Define and document testing and validation activities             |
| Risk management         | Maintain lifecycle risk-management procedures                     |
| Data governance         | Establish dataset governance and management processes             |
| Technical documentation | Maintain required technical documentation                         |
| Post-market monitoring  | Monitor deployed-system performance                               |
| Incident handling       | Manage serious incidents and corrective actions                   |
| Communication           | Maintain communication with authorities and relevant stakeholders |
| Accountability          | Define management responsibilities and internal controls          |

The quality management system must be proportionate and appropriate to the provider's organization and the complexity of the AI system.

#### 31.1 Relationship with ISO Management Systems

Organizations may use existing management-system structures to support AI Act compliance.

Relevant standards and frameworks include:

- ISO/IEC 42001 — AI management systems.
- ISO/IEC 27001 — Information security management systems.
- ISO 9001 — Quality management systems.
- ISO/IEC 23894 — AI risk management guidance.
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework — AI risk governance and management.

These standards can support governance implementation, but certification against one of them does not automatically establish compliance with the EU AI Act.

For example, an organization certified to ISO/IEC 42001 may have a mature AI management system, but it must still demonstrate that the applicable legal requirements for each AI system have been satisfied.

### 32. Deployer Obligations Under Article 26

Deployers of high-risk AI systems have specific responsibilities when using those systems under their authority.

These responsibilities complement the provider's obligations.

Key deployer obligations include:

1. Using the system in accordance with the provider's instructions for use.
2. Assigning human oversight to appropriately competent, trained, and authorized individuals.
3. Ensuring that input data is relevant and sufficiently representative for the intended purpose, to the extent the deployer controls the input data.
4. Monitoring the operation of the AI system based on the instructions for use.
5. Informing the provider or distributor and relevant authorities when specified risks or serious incidents are identified.
6. Suspending use where appropriate under the statutory conditions.
7. Retaining automatically generated logs under their control for an appropriate period, generally at least six months unless otherwise provided by applicable law or other legal requirements.
8. Conducting a fundamental-rights impact assessment where required under Article 27.

Additional requirements apply to particular categories of deployers and systems.

#### 32.1 Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA)

Article 27 requires certain deployers to conduct a fundamental-rights impact assessment before deploying specified high-risk AI systems.

The obligation applies to:

- Bodies governed by public law.
- Private entities providing public services.
- Deployers of certain Annex III high-risk AI systems, including specified creditworthiness and life or health insurance risk assessment systems.

The assessment must include relevant information such as:

- The deployer's processes in which the AI system will be used.
- The intended purpose.
- The period and frequency of use.
- Categories of natural persons and groups likely to be affected.
- Specific risks of harm to those persons or groups.
- Human oversight arrangements.
- Measures to be taken if risks materialize.

The FRIA is not identical to a GDPR Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).

| FRIA                                                                                     | GDPR DPIA                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Based on EU AI Act Article 27                                                            | Based on GDPR Article 35                                                                             |
| Focuses on fundamental-rights impacts associated with specified high-risk AI deployments | Focuses on risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from personal-data processing |
| Applies to specified deployers and use cases                                             | Applies where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals                           |
| Considers AI deployment processes, affected groups, oversight, and mitigation            | Considers processing operations, necessity, proportionality, risks, and safeguards                   |

A single coordinated assessment process may help organizations avoid duplication, but it must cover the distinct legal requirements of both assessments where both apply.

### 33. AI Act Governance Operating Model

Organizations need a defined operating model to convert regulatory requirements into day-to-day accountability.

A practical governance structure should include the following roles.

| Role                          | Main responsibility                                                         |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Board or executive management | Strategic oversight, accountability, and risk acceptance                    |
| AI governance committee       | AI policy, regulatory oversight, and governance decisions                   |
| AI system owner               | Business accountability for the AI use case                                 |
| Provider                      | Compliance with applicable provider obligations                             |
| Deployer                      | Compliance with applicable deployer obligations                             |
| Legal and compliance          | Regulatory interpretation and legal assessments                             |
| CISO / cybersecurity          | AI security architecture, threats, vulnerabilities, and incident management |
| Data protection officer       | Data-protection advice and oversight where applicable                       |
| Data owner                    | Data quality, access, provenance, and governance                            |
| Model owner                   | Model lifecycle, validation, monitoring, and change management              |
| Internal audit                | Independent assurance over governance and control effectiveness             |

The exact structure will vary depending on whether the organization develops AI systems, integrates third-party models, or simply deploys externally provided applications.

#### 33.1 AI Governance Lifecycle

The following lifecycle provides a practical operating sequence for enterprise AI governance.

## EU AI Act governance lifecycle

1. AI system identification

   Identify the AI system, business owner, provider, deployer, and intended purpose.
2. Regulatory classification

   Assess Article 5 prohibitions, Article 6 high-risk classification, GPAI obligations, and Article 50 transparency requirements.
3. Risk and impact assessment

   Evaluate AI risks, fundamental-rights impacts, privacy implications, cybersecurity, and operational consequences.
4. Control implementation

   Establish risk management, data governance, human oversight, security, documentation, and transparency controls.
5. Conformity and deployment approval

   Complete applicable conformity assessment, documentation, registration, and deployment approvals.
6. Operational monitoring

   Monitor performance, risks, incidents, changes, and compliance throughout operation.
7. Periodic review and retirement

   Reassess regulatory classification and risks following material changes, and manage system withdrawal or retirement.

This lifecycle is an enterprise governance model, not a verbatim sequence prescribed by the EU AI Act. It organizes the regulatory obligations into a practical control process.

### 35. Common EU AI Act Compliance Failures

Organizations frequently make mistakes when translating regulatory requirements into operational controls.

| Common failure                                          | Why it is a problem                                                                              | Recommended governance response                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Classifying AI systems by technology name               | Technology alone does not determine legal classification                                         | Assess intended purpose and actual deployment context           |
| Treating all AI as high-risk                            | Creates inaccurate regulatory classifications and unnecessary control overhead                   | Apply Article 5, Article 6, and relevant Annexes                |
| Treating GPAI as automatically high-risk                | Confuses model-level obligations with downstream system classification                           | Assess GPAI and downstream AI system obligations separately     |

### 37. Summary and Core Principle

The EU AI Act establishes a comprehensive governance framework covering AI systems, general-purpose AI models, transparency, conformity assessment, operational monitoring, and accountability.

The main governance concepts covered in this lesson are:

- GPAI governance: Providers of general-purpose AI models have specific documentation, downstream information, copyright-policy, and training-content transparency obligations.
- Systemic-risk governance: GPAI models with systemic risk have additional evaluation, mitigation, incident-reporting, and cybersecurity obligations.
- Transparency: Article 50 addresses AI interaction disclosure, synthetic-content marking, deepfake disclosure, and certain AI-generated public-interest text.
- Conformity assessment: Applicable high-risk AI systems must undergo the relevant conformity assessment procedure.
- Accountability: Providers and deployers have distinct statutory responsibilities.
- Operational governance: Risk management, human oversight, monitoring, incident response, and evidence management must operate throughout the system lifecycle.
- Fundamental rights: Certain deployers must conduct a fundamental-rights impact assessment under Article 27.

For cybersecurity and GRC professionals, the EU AI Act should be treated as an operational compliance framework, not merely a legal document. Its requirements must be translated into accountable roles, defined processes, technical controls, monitoring mechanisms, and verifiable evidence.

Core Principle: Effective EU AI Act compliance requires continuous accountability across the AI lifecycle. Regulatory classification establishes which obligations apply, but operational evidence demonstrates whether the organization actually fulfils them.
