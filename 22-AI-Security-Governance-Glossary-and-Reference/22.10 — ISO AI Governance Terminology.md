# 21.10 — AI Security Governance Tools and Templates

## Part 1 — AI Security Governance Toolkit Foundations, Tool Categories, and Enterprise Implementation Architecture

### 1. Introduction to AI Security Governance Tools and Templates

AI security governance requires more than policies, standards, frameworks, and risk assessments. Organizations need operational tools that translate governance requirements into repeatable activities, measurable controls, documented decisions, and auditable evidence.

An AI governance toolkit is a structured collection of documents, registers, assessment instruments, workflows, control mechanisms, and reporting templates used to manage AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

Its purpose is to make AI governance operational.

For example, an organization may establish an AI acceptable-use policy prohibiting employees from entering confidential customer information into unauthorized generative AI services. However, the policy alone does not demonstrate that the organization can enforce the requirement.

The organization also needs operational mechanisms such as:

- An AI system inventory identifying approved and unauthorized AI services.
- An AI acceptable-use assessment for employees and business units.
- A third-party AI assessment for externally hosted AI services.
- A data classification and information-handling assessment.
- An access-control review covering AI applications and integrations.
- Monitoring mechanisms to identify unauthorized AI usage.
- An exception register documenting approved deviations.
- An audit evidence repository demonstrating that controls operate effectively.

These components collectively transform a governance requirement into an operational control environment.

The central distinction is between defining governance expectations and providing the mechanisms to implement, monitor, and demonstrate them.

### 2. What Constitutes an AI Security Governance Toolkit?

An AI Security Governance Toolkit is an integrated set of governance instruments designed to support the identification, assessment, treatment, monitoring, and assurance of AI-related risks.

It generally contains six major categories.

| Toolkit category              | Purpose                                                     | Typical artifacts                                            |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Governance documentation      | Establish organizational expectations and accountability    | AI policy, AI governance charter, standards                  |
| Inventories and registers     | Maintain visibility over AI assets, risks, and dependencies | AI inventory, model inventory, AI risk register              |
| Assessment templates          | Evaluate risks, compliance, and control requirements        | AI impact assessment, security assessment, vendor assessment |
| Operational control templates | Standardize control implementation and execution            | Access reviews, change approvals, monitoring checklists      |
| Assurance and audit templates | Evaluate control effectiveness and compliance               | Audit checklists, evidence registers, control testing        |
| Reporting and metrics         | Measure governance performance and risk exposure            | KPI dashboards, KRI reports, management scorecards           |

These categories should not operate independently. They should form an interconnected governance environment.

#### 2.1 Governance documentation

Governance documentation establishes the organization's formal requirements for AI development, procurement, deployment, operation, and retirement.

Examples include:

- AI governance policy.
- AI security policy.
- Responsible AI principles.
- AI acceptable-use policy.
- AI development and deployment standard.
- AI third-party risk management standard.
- AI data governance standard.
- AI incident management procedure.
- AI model lifecycle management procedure.
- AI risk acceptance procedure.

The documentation hierarchy should be clear.

```
Corporate Governance
        |
        v
AI Governance Policy
        |
        v
AI Security and Responsible AI Standards
        |
        v
AI Governance Procedures
        |
        v
Operational Control Activities
        |
        v
Evidence and Management Reporting
```

A policy establishes what the organization requires. A standard defines mandatory implementation expectations. A procedure describes how activities are performed. Evidence demonstrates whether the required activities actually occurred.

#### 2.2 Inventories and registers

Inventories and registers provide the organization's authoritative records of AI-related assets, activities, risks, and decisions.

Examples:

| Register                | Information maintained                                                         |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| AI System Inventory     | AI applications, business purposes, owners, providers, lifecycle status        |
| AI Model Inventory      | Models, versions, model owners, training information, validation status        |
| AI Risk Register        | Risk events, causes, impacts, inherent and residual risk, treatment            |
| AI Third-Party Register | AI suppliers, service dependencies, contractual obligations, assessment status |
| AI Data Register        | Datasets, data owners, classifications, processing purposes, retention         |
| AI Exception Register   | Approved policy deviations, justifications, compensating controls, expiry      |
| AI Incident Register    | AI-related incidents, impact, root cause, corrective actions                   |
| AI Control Register     | Control objectives, owners, implementation status, evidence                    |

A critical distinction is that an AI system inventory and an AI model inventory are not interchangeable.

An AI application may use multiple models, and one model may support multiple applications.

For example:

```
Customer Service AI Platform
        |
        +---- LLM for customer conversations
        |
        +---- Classification Model for ticket routing
        |
        +---- Fraud Detection Model
        |
        +---- Embedding Model for semantic search
```

The AI system inventory records the overall business application and its operational context. The model inventory records the individual models and their technical and governance characteristics.

A mature organization maintains relationships between these records rather than creating isolated spreadsheets.

### 3. The AI Security Governance Toolkit Lifecycle

Governance tools should support the entire AI lifecycle rather than focusing exclusively on initial approval.

A typical lifecycle contains seven stages.

```
1. Identification
       |
       v
2. Registration and Classification
       |
       v
3. Risk and Impact Assessment
       |
       v
4. Design and Security Review
       |
       v
5. Approval and Deployment
       |
       v
6. Monitoring and Assurance
       |
       v
7. Change Management and Retirement
```

Each stage requires specific governance artifacts.

| Lifecycle stage       | Governance activities                                                         | Required tools and templates                             |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identification        | Identify proposed, purchased, developed, and discovered AI systems            | AI intake form, AI discovery checklist                   |
| Registration          | Record ownership, purpose, provider, architecture, and dependencies           | AI system inventory, model inventory                     |
| Classification        | Determine organizational risk tier and applicable legal classification        | AI classification assessment                             |
| Assessment            | Evaluate cybersecurity, privacy, operational, legal, and ethical risks        | AI risk assessment, DPIA screening, AI impact assessment |
| Design                | Review architecture, data flows, integrations, access, and security controls  | AI architecture review, security assessment              |
| Approval              | Document risk decisions and authorization to proceed                          | AI approval checklist, risk acceptance form              |
| Monitoring            | Evaluate performance, incidents, drift, compliance, and control effectiveness | Monitoring checklist, KPI/KRI dashboard                  |
| Change and retirement | Reassess material changes and manage decommissioning                          | AI change assessment, retirement checklist               |

The organization should establish governance gates at appropriate lifecycle stages.

A governance gate is a formal decision point at which the responsible authority determines whether an AI system can proceed, requires remediation, or must be rejected.

For example, an AI application should not automatically proceed to production simply because its technical testing has passed. The organization may still need to resolve outstanding privacy, security, contractual, regulatory, or operational risks.

A successful technical deployment is not equivalent to governance approval.

### 4. Core AI Security Governance Tool Categories

A practical enterprise toolkit should cover the following operational domains.

#### 4.1 AI governance and accountability tools

These tools establish ownership, decision-making authority, oversight, and governance responsibilities.

Examples:

- AI governance charter.
- AI governance committee terms of reference.
- AI governance RACI matrix.
- AI system ownership register.
- AI approval and escalation matrix.
- AI policy exception register.
- AI governance maturity assessment.

Primary governance questions:

1. Who is accountable for the organization's AI governance program?
2. Who owns each AI system and its associated risks?
3. Who approves the deployment of high-impact AI applications?
4. Who can accept residual AI risks?
5. How are disagreements between business, technology, security, and legal functions resolved?
6. How are governance decisions documented and retained?

#### 4.2 AI risk management tools

These tools support systematic identification, analysis, evaluation, treatment, and monitoring of AI risks.

Examples:

- AI risk assessment template.
- AI risk register.
- AI risk scoring methodology.
- AI risk treatment plan.
- AI risk acceptance form.
- AI risk monitoring dashboard.
- AI risk reassessment checklist.

Risk assessments should consider more than cybersecurity.

| Risk domain            | Example risk                                                          |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cybersecurity          | Prompt injection exposes confidential information                     |
| Privacy                | Personal data is processed without an appropriate lawful basis        |
| Data governance        | Training data is inaccurate, incomplete, or improperly sourced        |
| Model risk             | Model performance deteriorates because of data or concept drift       |
| Operational            | AI service failure disrupts customer-facing operations                |
| Third-party            | External AI provider changes its service or data-processing practices |
| Regulatory             | AI system is deployed without satisfying applicable legal obligations |
| Safety and reliability | AI output causes an unsafe or materially incorrect decision           |
| Ethical and fairness   | AI-assisted decisions produce unjustified discriminatory outcomes     |

The risk assessment must distinguish between:

- Inherent risk: exposure before considering relevant controls.
- Control design: whether the proposed controls are appropriately designed.
- Control implementation: whether those controls have actually been implemented.
- Control operating effectiveness: whether implemented controls operate as intended.
- Residual risk: remaining exposure after evaluating controls.

A risk register that records only likelihood, impact, and a final score is insufficient for enterprise risk management. It must also document ownership, treatment, evidence, and decision authority.

#### 4.3 AI cybersecurity assessment tools

These tools evaluate the technical and security characteristics of AI systems.

Examples:

- AI security assessment questionnaire.
- AI threat modeling template.
- AI attack surface assessment.
- AI architecture security review.
- AI penetration testing checklist.
- LLM security assessment.
- Agentic AI security assessment.
- AI supply chain security checklist.
- AI vulnerability remediation tracker.

Typical assessment areas include:

| Security domain                | Assessment focus                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identity and access management | Authentication, authorization, workload identity, least privilege |
| Data security                  | Classification, encryption, retention, access, data leakage       |
| Model security                 | Model integrity, poisoning, extraction, version control           |
| Application security           | Input validation, output handling, API security                   |
| LLM security                   | Prompt injection, jailbreaks, sensitive information disclosure    |
| Agent security                 | Tool authorization, autonomy boundaries, approval gates           |
| Infrastructure security        | Network segmentation, containers, secrets, cloud configuration    |
| Supply chain security          | Model provenance, dependencies, datasets, third-party components  |
| Security monitoring            | Logging, anomaly detection, incident response                     |
| Resilience                     | Availability, fallback, recovery, service continuity              |

The assessment must reflect the actual architecture. A conventional web application questionnaire cannot adequately assess every AI-specific threat.

#### 4.4 AI privacy and data governance tools

These tools address the collection, processing, use, storage, sharing, and disposal of information used by AI systems.

Examples:

- AI data inventory.
- AI data classification checklist.
- Privacy impact assessment screening.
- Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) template.
- AI data lineage register.
- Data retention assessment.
- Data provenance checklist.
- Training data governance assessment.
- Data sharing and international transfer assessment.

Important assessment questions include:

1. What data is collected, generated, or processed?
2. Does the data contain personal or special-category personal data?
3. What is the legal basis for processing?
4. Is the data used for training, fine-tuning, inference, or retrieval?
5. Is data transmitted to an external AI provider?
6. Are retention and deletion requirements defined?
7. Can the organization demonstrate data provenance?
8. Are data subjects' rights supported where applicable?
9. Are anonymization or pseudonymization techniques appropriately implemented?
10. Are cross-border data transfers assessed?

Removing direct identifiers does not automatically anonymize personal data. Pseudonymized data remains personal data under GDPR where an individual can still be identified using additional information.

#### 4.5 AI third-party and supplier governance tools

Organizations frequently consume AI capabilities through cloud platforms, SaaS products, external foundation models, APIs, and managed AI services.

Consequently, AI supplier governance must extend beyond conventional IT supplier assessments.

Examples:

- AI third-party risk assessment.
- AI supplier due diligence questionnaire.
- AI contractual security review.
- AI provider model transparency assessment.
- AI subprocessor register.
- AI supplier monitoring checklist.
- AI supplier exit and portability assessment.

The assessment should consider:

- Data processing and data ownership.
- Use of customer data for model training.
- Model and service transparency.
- Security architecture.
- Model provenance and update practices.
- Vulnerability disclosure and remediation.
- Incident notification obligations.
- Subprocessors and fourth-party dependencies.
- Data location and international transfers.
- Service availability and resilience.
- Contract termination and data deletion.
- Portability and exit strategy.

A supplier's ISO 27001 certificate does not independently demonstrate that its AI services satisfy all AI-specific security, privacy, governance, or regulatory requirements.

### 5. Governance Templates Versus Operational Security Controls

One of the most important distinctions in AI governance is the difference between a document that describes a control and a mechanism that actually enforces it.

Consider the following example.

Governance requirement: Only authorized users may access an enterprise LLM application.

| Layer                | Example                                                                      |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Policy               | AI applications must be protected by enterprise identity controls            |
| Standard             | AI applications must integrate with approved identity providers              |
| Procedure            | IT administrators must configure and periodically review access              |
| Technical control    | SSO, MFA, RBAC, conditional access, and privileged access controls           |
| Operational evidence | Access logs, configuration records, periodic access review results           |
| Assurance            | Internal audit or control testing validates implementation and effectiveness |

The governance toolkit should connect all these layers.

```
Governance Requirement
        |
        v
Control Objective
        |
        v
Control Design
        |
        v
Control Implementation
        |
        v
Operational Execution
        |
        v
Evidence Collection
        |
        v
Control Effectiveness Testing
        |
        v
Management Reporting
```

A common governance failure occurs when an organization produces extensive policy documentation but cannot demonstrate that the required controls are implemented or operating.

For example, an AI policy may prohibit employees from uploading confidential information to public LLM services. If the organization has no approved AI inventory, no DLP enforcement, no monitoring, and no exception process, the requirement may exist only on paper.

A documented control is not necessarily an implemented control, and an implemented control is not necessarily an effective control.

### 6. Standards and Frameworks That Inform the Toolkit

An enterprise AI governance toolkit should be aligned with recognized standards and frameworks. However, the organization should not blindly copy every framework into its control library.

Each framework serves a different purpose.

| Standard or framework             | Primary contribution to the toolkit                          |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| ISO/IEC 42001                     | AI management system requirements and governance structure   |
| ISO/IEC 23894                     | AI risk management guidance                                  |
| ISO/IEC 22989                     | AI concepts and terminology                                  |
| ISO/IEC 23053                     | Framework for AI systems using machine learning              |
| ISO/IEC 27001                     | Information security management system requirements          |
| ISO/IEC 27701                     | Privacy information management system requirements           |
| NIST AI RMF                       | AI risk management framework                                 |
| NIST AI 600-1                     | Generative AI risk management profile                        |
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework      | Cybersecurity governance and risk management outcomes        |
| OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications | Common LLM application security risks                        |
| MITRE ATLAS                       | AI and machine learning threat knowledge base                |
| EU AI Act                         | Applicable legal requirements for AI systems and GPAI models |

These references should be used according to their intended scope.

For example:

- ISO/IEC 42001 provides management system requirements; it is not a technical AI penetration testing methodology.
- ISO/IEC 23894 provides risk management guidance; it is not a substitute for an organization's risk acceptance policy.
- NIST AI RMF provides a risk management structure; it is not legislation.
- OWASP and MITRE ATLAS provide useful security knowledge; neither automatically establishes legal compliance.
- The EU AI Act creates legal obligations where its scope and requirements apply.

The toolkit should establish a traceable relationship between external requirements, internal controls, responsible owners, and evidence.

#### 7.1 Centralized governance repository

The organization should maintain a controlled repository containing:

- Approved policies and standards.
- Current assessment templates.
- AI system and model inventories.
- Risk and control registers.
- Assessment results.
- Approval records.
- Exception decisions.
- Audit evidence.
- Corrective action plans.
- Management reports.

Each artifact should have an owner, version, approval status, review frequency, and retention requirement.

#### 7.2 Traceability between governance artifacts

The most important architectural characteristic is traceability.

For example:

```
AI System ID: AI-CRM-001
        |
        +---- Business Owner
        |
        +---- System Owner
        |
        +---- AI Provider
        |
        +---- Model Inventory Records
        |
        +---- Risk Assessments
        |
        +---- Applicable Controls
        |
        +---- Assessment Findings
        |
        +---- Risk Treatment Actions
        |
        +---- Approval Decisions
        |
        +---- Monitoring Evidence
        |
        +---- Audit Records
```

This traceability allows the organization to answer fundamental governance questions:

- Which AI systems are exposed to a particular risk?
- Which controls mitigate that risk?
- Which controls have failed or are overdue?
- Which systems have outstanding high-risk findings?
- Who accepted the remaining risk?
- What evidence supports the approval decision?
- Which systems must be reassessed following a material change?

Without traceability, governance reporting becomes a manual exercise involving disconnected spreadsheets and inconsistent information.

### 8. Common Failures in AI Governance Toolkit Implementation

Organizations can invest considerable effort in developing templates while still failing to establish an effective governance capability.

The following failures are particularly common.

| Failure                    | Why it happens                                               | Consequence                                                   |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Excessive documentation    | Governance is treated as a document-production exercise      | High administrative burden with limited control effectiveness |
| Duplicate registers        | Teams develop independent spreadsheets                       | Inconsistent records and conflicting ownership                |
| No accountable owners      | Responsibilities are unclear                                 | Risks and remediation actions remain unresolved               |
| Generic assessments        | Conventional IT questionnaires are reused without adaptation | AI-specific threats are missed                                |
| No evidence requirements   | Assessments capture answers but not supporting proof         | Weak auditability                                             |
| No lifecycle integration   | Assessments occur only before initial deployment             | Material changes and operational risks are overlooked         |
| No governance gates        | Business teams can bypass formal approval                    | Unauthorized AI deployment                                    |
| Static risk assessments    | Risks are not reassessed after changes                       | Outdated risk decisions                                       |
| Weak third-party oversight | Supplier certificates are treated as sufficient assurance    | Unidentified provider and supply chain exposure               |
| No management reporting    | Governance data is not translated into decisions             | Senior management cannot evaluate AI exposure                 |

A further mistake is attempting to introduce every possible governance artifact before understanding the organization's AI landscape.

The correct starting point is to identify the organization's AI use cases, business exposure, regulatory obligations, operating model, and existing governance capabilities.

The toolkit should be proportionate to the organization's risk profile and operational maturity.

### 9. Practical Enterprise Scenario: AI Governance Toolkit for a Telecom Operator

Consider a telecommunications operator implementing an enterprise AI customer-service platform.

The platform uses:

- Salesforce Service Cloud for customer relationship management.
- An enterprise LLM for customer interactions.
- Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for accessing internal knowledge.
- A vector database for retrieving relevant information.
- APIs connecting to billing and provisioning systems.
- An external cloud AI provider.
- Enterprise identity management for employee authentication.

The operator must establish governance across the application, models, data, integrations, suppliers, and business processes.

| TKG-002     | AI Model Inventory           | Record the LLM and embedding model                            |
| TKG-003     | AI Classification Assessment | Determine internal risk tier and assess legal applicability   |
| TKG-004     | AI Risk Assessment           | Identify business, operational, privacy, and security risks   |
| TKG-005     | AI Security Assessment       | Evaluate application, model, API, and infrastructure security |
| TKG-006     | AI Privacy Assessment        | Assess customer data processing and GDPR implications         |
| TKG-007     | AI Third-Party Assessment    | Evaluate the external AI provider                             |
| TKG-008     | AI Architecture Review       | Review integrations, trust boundaries, and data flows         |
| TKG-009     | AI Control Register          | Record security and governance controls                       |
| TKG-010     | AI Approval Checklist        | Document formal production authorization                      |

#### 9.2 Example governance traceability

Scenario: The LLM retrieves customer account information through an API connected to Salesforce.

| Governance element | Example                                                                                         |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Risk ID            | AIR-CRM-001                                                                                     |
| Risk event         | Unauthorized disclosure of customer account information                                         |
| Threat scenario    | Prompt injection or misuse of an AI tool retrieves information outside the user's authorization |
| Business impact    | Customer privacy breach, regulatory exposure, reputational damage                               |
| Control objective  | Ensure customer data access is authorized and restricted                                        |
| Preventive control | Enforce authorization at the API and backend data layers                                        |
| Detective control  | Log and monitor customer data access and unusual retrieval activity                             |
| Corrective control | Revoke access, contain the incident, investigate, and remediate                                 |
| Control owner      | Application Security / CRM Technology Owner                                                     |
| Risk owner         | Customer Service Business Owner                                                                 |
| Evidence           | API authorization configuration, access logs, security test results                             |
| Assurance activity | Periodic access-control testing and AI security assessment                                      |

The key design principle is that the LLM must not become the authority responsible for determining whether a user can access customer information.

Authorization must be enforced by trusted application and backend controls.

A prompt instruction such as "only retrieve information belonging to the authenticated customer" is not an access-control mechanism.

#### 9.3 Governance operating principle

The operator should maintain a single authoritative record for each AI system and link the associated models, risks, controls, findings, decisions, and evidence.

This makes it possible to move from individual questionnaire responses to an auditable enterprise governance process.

### 10. Summary and Core Principle

An AI Security Governance Toolkit provides the operational foundation for implementing AI governance across the enterprise.

Its main components include:

1. Governance policies, standards, and procedures.
2. AI system and model inventories.
3. AI risk and control registers.
4. Cybersecurity, privacy, and third-party assessments.
5. Lifecycle approval and change-management mechanisms.
6. Operational monitoring and incident management.
7. Assurance, audit evidence, and management reporting.

The toolkit must establish traceability between governance requirements, control objectives, implementation activities, risk decisions, and supporting evidence.

Core Principle: An effective AI governance toolkit is not measured by the number of templates an organization possesses. It is measured by whether those tools enable accountable decisions, enforceable controls, continuous risk management, and demonstrable assurance throughout the AI lifecycle.

### 11. AI Governance Intake and Registration Templates

The first operational requirement in AI governance is to establish a consistent process for identifying and registering AI initiatives.

Organizations cannot effectively govern AI systems that they do not know exist.

AI initiatives may originate from several sources:

- Business units procuring third-party AI solutions.
- IT departments implementing AI-enabled SaaS platforms.
- Development teams building internal machine learning applications.
- Employees using public generative AI services.
- Vendors embedding AI capabilities into existing products.
- Autonomous agents introduced into enterprise workflows.
- Existing applications receiving new AI capabilities through software updates.

An AI governance intake process should capture these initiatives before they become operational.

#### 11.1 AI Governance Intake Form

The intake form is the initial mechanism for collecting information about a proposed or discovered AI use case.

Template: AI Governance Intake Form

| Field                    | Description                                       | Example                                        |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Intake ID                | Unique request identifier                         | AI-INT-001                                     |
| Request date             | Date of submission                                | 2026-10-31                                     |
| Business unit            | Requesting department                             | Customer Operations                            |
| Requestor                | Person initiating the request                     | Customer Service Manager                       |
| Proposed AI system       | Name of the solution                              | AI Customer Assistant                          |
| Business purpose         | Intended business outcome                         | Automate customer inquiries                    |
| AI capability            | Type of AI functionality                          | LLM with RAG                                   |
| Deployment model         | Hosting arrangement                               | External SaaS                                  |
| AI provider              | Supplier or model provider                        | External AI provider                           |
| Data categories          | Information processed                             | Customer records, service history              |
| Personal data            | Whether personal data is processed                | Yes                                            |
| Business criticality     | Operational importance                            | High                                           |
| External integrations    | Connected systems                                 | Salesforce, Billing API                        |

The intake process should not assume that the requestor understands AI architecture or regulatory classifications. The form should allow preliminary information to be submitted while requiring the governance team to validate technical and legal details.

#### 11.2 AI Discovery and Shadow AI Register

An organization should not rely exclusively on voluntary AI registration.

Employees may use AI functionality without formally notifying IT, cybersecurity, procurement, or compliance.

A separate discovery process should identify unauthorized or previously unregistered AI usage.

Template: AI Discovery Register

| Field                | Description                                                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Discovery ID         | Unique discovery reference                                    |
| Discovery source     | CASB, network monitoring, procurement, audit, employee report |
| AI service           | Identified application or provider                            |
| Business unit        | Department associated with usage                              |

| Data exposure        | Potentially processed data categories                         |
| Authorization status | Approved, restricted, unapproved, under review                |
| Risk rating          | Preliminary organizational risk rating                        |
| Investigation owner  | Assigned responsible person                                   |
| Required action      | Approve, restrict, investigate, or block                      |
| Closure evidence     | Proof that the issue has been resolved                        |

Discovery mechanisms may include:

- Cloud Access Security Broker (CASB).
- Secure Web Gateway (SWG).
- Data Loss Prevention (DLP).
- Software asset management.
- Procurement and expense reviews.
- Identity and access management logs.
- Endpoint and browser telemetry.
- Employee awareness reporting.

A discovery result is not automatically proof of a policy violation. The organization should establish the actual usage context, data exposure, authorization status, and applicable internal requirements before deciding on corrective action.

### 22. From Governance Templates to Operational Implementation

Having policies, registers, assessment questionnaires, and control templates does not automatically establish an effective AI governance program.

The organization must integrate these artifacts into its existing business and technology processes.

For example, completing an AI risk assessment is only one activity in the governance lifecycle. The organization must also ensure that:

- The assessment is initiated at the appropriate time.
- The correct stakeholders participate.
- Risks are evaluated consistently.
- Findings are assigned to accountable owners.
- Required controls are implemented.
- Remediation is validated.
- Residual risks are formally accepted where appropriate.
- The decision is recorded.
- The system is monitored after deployment.

This requires an operational workflow supported by clearly defined responsibilities, decision gates, and evidence requirements.

### 23. AI Governance Workflow and Decision Gates

A governance workflow establishes how an AI initiative moves from proposal to production and eventual retirement.

Each stage should have defined entry criteria, activities, decision authority, and exit criteria.

#### 23.1 Governance Workflow Definition

| Stage                  | Activities                                                                     | Responsible function                           | Exit criteria                    |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1. Intake              | Capture business purpose, proposed solution, provider, and initial information | Business owner                                 | Intake submitted                 |
| 2. Registration        | Create AI system and model inventory records                                   | AI Governance Office                           | Records established              |
| 3. Classification      | Determine internal risk tier and assess legal applicability                    | AI Governance, Legal, Privacy                  | Classification documented        |
| 4. Assessment          | Evaluate security, privacy, operational, and third-party risks                 | GRC, Cybersecurity, Privacy                    | Assessments completed            |
| 5. Architecture review | Examine data flows, integrations, trust boundaries, and dependencies           | Enterprise Architecture, Security Architecture | Architecture findings documented |
| 6. Remediation         | Address control gaps and assessment findings                                   | Technology and business owners                 | Required remediation validated   |
| 7. Approval            | Review outstanding risks and authorize deployment                              | Authorized governance authority                | Approval decision recorded       |
| 8. Deployment          | Implement approved configuration and operational controls                      | IT and application teams                       | Production readiness confirmed   |
| 9. Monitoring          | Track performance, security, compliance, and operational indicators            | Service owner, SOC, AI Governance              | Monitoring operational           |
| 10. Reassessment       | Review material changes, incidents, and periodic risk exposure                 | Relevant control and risk owners               | Updated risk decision            |
| 11. Retirement         | Decommission the system, revoke access, and manage data and supplier exit      | Business and technology owners                 | Retirement evidence recorded     |

#### 23.2 Governance Decision Gates

A decision gate is a formal checkpoint at which an authorized person or committee determines whether an initiative may proceed.

| Gate                         | Decision                                                                    | Possible outcome                          |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Gate 1 — Intake              | Is this an AI initiative requiring governance oversight?                    | Register, redirect, or reject             |
| Gate 2 — Classification      | What internal scrutiny and legal assessments are required?                  | Assign risk tier and assessment scope     |
| Gate 3 — Assessment          | Have relevant risks and obligations been identified?                        | Proceed, remediate, or escalate           |
| Gate 4 — Design              | Does the proposed architecture provide appropriate safeguards?              | Accept design, request changes, or reject |
| Gate 5 — Production approval | Are mandatory requirements satisfied and remaining risks authorized?        | Approve, conditionally approve, or reject |
| Gate 6 — Operational review  | Does the production system remain within approved conditions?               | Continue, restrict, suspend, or reassess  |
| Gate 7 — Retirement          | Have operational, data, access, and contractual obligations been addressed? | Close system record                       |

A governance gate must not become a rubber-stamping exercise.

The approver should receive sufficient evidence to understand the system, its material risks, outstanding findings, and the implications of the decision.

#### 23.3 Conditional Approval

Some organizations permit conditional production approval where remaining findings are manageable and do not violate mandatory requirements.

For example:

- A medium-priority monitoring enhancement may be completed within an agreed period.
- Additional reporting may be required after deployment.
- A restricted feature may remain disabled until its assessment is completed.
- A compensating control may be required while a permanent solution is implemented.

Conditional approval should specify:

1. The unresolved finding.
2. The associated residual risk.
3. The compensating control.
4. The accountable risk owner.
5. The remediation owner.
6. The deadline.
7. The escalation condition.
8. The approval authority.

Conditional approval must not be used to bypass applicable legal requirements or to deploy systems with unacceptable unresolved exposure.

#### 24.3 Three Lines Model

AI governance should integrate with the organization's wider risk management and assurance model.

```
                 BOARD / AUDIT COMMITTEE
                          |
                          v
                  EXECUTIVE MANAGEMENT
                          |
        +-----------------+-----------------+
        |                 |                 |
        v                 v                 v
    FIRST LINE        SECOND LINE       THIRD LINE
    Operations        Risk / GRC        Internal Audit
    Business          Compliance        Independent
    Technology        AI Governance     Assurance
    AI Owners         Security Oversight
        |                 |                 |
        +-----------------+-----------------+
                          |
                          v
                  GOVERNANCE REPORTING
```

First Line — Business and Technology

- Owns AI systems and operational risks.
- Implements controls.
- Executes procedures.
- Maintains operational evidence.
- Escalates incidents and control failures.

Second Line — Risk, GRC, Compliance, and Oversight

- Establishes governance methodologies.
- Defines risk assessment expectations.
- Monitors compliance and control implementation.
- Challenges risk decisions.
- Reports material exposure.
- Provides oversight and guidance.

Third Line — Internal Audit

- Independently assesses governance design and operating effectiveness.
- Evaluates whether risk management and controls are adequate.
- Reports assurance findings to the appropriate governance authority.

The Three Lines Model does not mean that the second line owns every AI risk. Operational risk ownership remains with the relevant business and technology functions.

### 25. AI Control Testing and Effectiveness Assessment

An organization must test whether its AI controls are appropriately designed, implemented, and operating effectively.

A completed questionnaire or a control owner's statement is not sufficient evidence of control effectiveness.

#### 25.1 Three Dimensions of Control Evaluation

| Dimension                    | Key question                                                                     | Example                                                                           |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Design effectiveness         | Would the control adequately address the intended risk if implemented correctly? | Does the API authorization design prevent unauthorized customer record retrieval? |
| Implementation effectiveness | Has the control actually been implemented?                                       | Is the authorization policy configured in production?                             |
| Operating effectiveness      | Does the control operate consistently as intended?                               | Do test results demonstrate that unauthorized access attempts are denied?         |

These dimensions should be assessed separately.

A technically well-designed control may not be implemented. An implemented control may also fail in operation.

#### 25.2 AI Control Testing Plan

| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |

| Test period       | Period under examination                                           |
| Expected result   | Required control behavior                                          |

#### 25.3 Example: API Authorization Control Testing

Control objective: Ensure that the AI assistant cannot retrieve or modify customer information without authorization.

Testing procedure:

1. Identify the AI application's API endpoints.
2. Identify the user and service identities involved.
3. Review the authorization model.
4. Verify that permissions are enforced by the backend service.
5. Test authorized access to the correct customer record.
6. Test unauthorized access to another customer's record.
7. Test access using an expired or invalid identity.
8. Test privilege escalation attempts.
9. Review relevant access and denial logs.
10. Document exceptions and remediation requirements.

Expected results:

| Test case                             | Expected result                                               |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Authorized customer record request    | Access permitted within approved scope                        |
| Request for another customer's record | Access denied                                                 |
| Expired identity                      | Request rejected                                              |
| Unauthorized API operation            | Request rejected                                              |
| Attempt to increase privileges        | Request rejected and appropriately logged                     |
| Repeated suspicious requests          | Detection and escalation according to monitoring requirements |

The test should validate enforcement at the trusted backend, not merely whether the LLM follows an instruction not to disclose customer information.

#### 25.4 Control Testing Frequency

Testing frequency should be risk-based.

| Control category           | Illustrative testing frequency                       |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Critical API authorization | Continuous monitoring and periodic technical testing |
| Privileged access review   | Quarterly or according to risk and policy            |
| AI supplier assessment     | Before onboarding and periodically thereafter        |
| AI model change approval   | Every material change                                |
| Prompt injection testing   | Before production and after material changes         |
| AI incident response       | Periodic exercises and after relevant incidents      |
| AI governance inventory    | Periodic reconciliation and change-triggered updates |
| AI control effectiveness   | According to the risk-based assurance plan           |

These are illustrative frequencies, not universal requirements imposed by the listed standards.

### 26. AI Security Red Teaming and Adversarial Assessment Templates

#### 26.1 AI Red Teaming Scope

- Unauthorized tool invocation.

### 28. AI Governance Maturity Assessment

A maturity assessment evaluates how consistently an organization implements AI governance across its business and technology environment.

It should measure actual capabilities rather than the number of policies or completed documents.

#### 28.1 AI Governance Maturity Model

An organization may establish a five-level internal maturity model.

| Level   | Maturity   | Characteristics                                                                                      |
| ------- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Level 1 | Initial    | Ad hoc AI usage, limited ownership, inconsistent risk assessment                                     |
| Level 2 | Developing | Basic AI inventory, draft policies, preliminary assessments                                          |
| Level 3 | Defined    | Approved governance framework, documented processes, assigned owners                                 |
| Level 4 | Managed    | Integrated workflows, measurable controls, periodic assurance, management reporting                  |
| Level 5 | Optimized  | Continuous improvement, automated evidence collection, advanced monitoring, risk-informed governance |

This is an illustrative organizational maturity model, not an official maturity scale prescribed by ISO/IEC 42001 or NIST AI RMF.

#### 28.2 Maturity Assessment Questionnaire

| Assessment area     | Example question                                                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Governance strategy | Is there an approved enterprise AI governance strategy?                  |
| Accountability      | Are AI system, model, risk, and control owners formally assigned?        |

| Third-party         | Are AI suppliers assessed and monitored?                                 |
| Lifecycle           | Are governance gates integrated into AI lifecycle processes?             |
| Monitoring          | Are AI systems continuously or periodically monitored according to risk? |
| Assurance           | Are AI controls independently tested where appropriate?                  |
| Reporting           | Does management receive meaningful AI risk and governance reports?       |

#### 28.3 Maturity Assessment Scoring

Example scoring:

- 0 — Not implemented.
- 1 — Ad hoc.
- 2 — Partially implemented.
- 3 — Defined and implemented.
- 4 — Measured and managed.
- 5 — Continuously improved.

The organization may calculate an average score for each domain, but the average must not conceal critical gaps.

For example, an organization could score highly in documentation and training while having no effective access controls for an agent connected to financial systems.

A maturity score should therefore be accompanied by:

- Critical control exceptions.
- High-risk findings.
- Evidence quality.
- Scope and coverage limitations.
- Governance dependencies.
- Remediation priorities.

Maturity is an indicator of capability, not proof of regulatory compliance or security.

#### 30.2 Example Governance Escalation

During security testing, the red team discovers that an agent can invoke a billing API without checking whether the authenticated employee is authorized to modify the relevant customer account.

The finding is classified as critical because it creates a potential unauthorized transaction path.

The governance response should be:

1. Record the finding in the security assessment.
2. Link it to the relevant AI system and risk register.
3. Identify the business and technical risk owners.
4. Restrict or disable the affected tool capability.
5. Implement backend authorization enforcement.
6. Conduct negative authorization testing.
7. Review logs for potential previous exploitation.
8. Validate remediation independently.
9. Update residual risk.
10. Reconsider production approval.

The business sponsor should not override this issue simply because the launch date is approaching.

### 31. GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. How would you operationalize an AI governance framework?

I would begin by establishing visibility through an AI system inventory, assigning accountable owners, and defining an internal risk classification methodology. I would then integrate AI risk, security, privacy, and third-party assessments into existing procurement, architecture, and project lifecycle processes. Finally, I would establish governance approval gates, control testing, continuous monitoring, and management reporting. The objective is to make governance part of normal enterprise operations rather than a separate documentation exercise.

Q2. What is the difference between an AI governance workflow and an AI governance policy?

A policy establishes organizational requirements and expectations. A workflow translates those requirements into operational activities, responsibilities, decision gates, and evidence. A policy may require every production AI system to undergo security assessment, while the workflow determines who initiates the assessment, who performs it, how findings are remediated, and who authorizes deployment.

Q3. How do you determine whether an AI system is ready for production?

I evaluate whether the system is registered, classified, assessed, and reviewed against its relevant legal, security, privacy, operational, and third-party requirements. I verify that mandatory controls are implemented, material findings are resolved or appropriately handled, monitoring is operational, and residual risks are accepted by authorized individuals. Production readiness requires evidence, not merely confirmation from the project team.

Q4. What is the difference between control design and operating effectiveness?

Control design assesses whether a control is appropriately structured to address a risk. Operating effectiveness assesses whether that control actually operates as intended over the relevant period. For example, a well-designed API authorization policy may exist, but operating effectiveness requires evidence that unauthorized access attempts are consistently rejected.

Q5. Why is AI red teaming important?

AI red teaming evaluates how an AI system behaves under adversarial conditions. It helps identify weaknesses such as prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, unauthorized tool invocation, and excessive agency. The results provide evidence for risk assessment and remediation, but testing cannot establish that a system is immune to every possible attack.

Q6. How would you manage an AI supplier after initial approval?

I would establish ongoing monitoring based on the supplier's criticality and risk profile. This includes reviewing material model changes, security incidents, subprocessors, data processing practices, vulnerabilities, service availability, and contractual changes. Material changes should trigger reassessment and, where necessary, updated contractual or technical safeguards.

Q7. How do you establish an AI governance maturity baseline?

I would assess governance across accountability, inventory, classification, risk management, cybersecurity, privacy, third-party oversight, lifecycle management, monitoring, assurance, and reporting. Each domain should be scored against defined maturity criteria and supported by evidence. I would also identify critical gaps separately so that high average scores do not conceal serious control weaknesses.

Q8. What is the role of the Three Lines Model in AI governance?

The first line owns AI systems and implements operational controls. The second line establishes risk methodologies, provides oversight, challenges decisions, and monitors exposure. The third line independently assesses governance and control effectiveness. This model reinforces accountability while maintaining appropriate separation between operational execution and independent assurance.

Q9. What would you do if a business unit wanted to deploy an AI solution before completing the required assessment?

I would explain the governance requirements and the potential business, security, privacy, and regulatory consequences. I would determine whether the outstanding requirements are mandatory, identify any acceptable alternatives, and escalate material issues to the authorized decision-maker. I would not treat a commercial deadline as justification for bypassing mandatory controls or accepting risks without proper authority.

Q10. How do you demonstrate that an AI governance program is effective?

I would use multiple sources of evidence: inventory completeness, assessment coverage, control testing results, risk remediation performance, incident trends, supplier assurance, monitoring effectiveness, and independent audit findings. I would also report material exceptions and residual risks to management. A successful program demonstrates that risks are identified, decisions are accountable, controls operate, and weaknesses are addressed.

### 32. Summary and Core Principle

Operational AI governance requires more than a collection of templates. It requires an integrated operating model that connects business processes, technical controls, risk ownership, decision authority, assurance, and reporting.

The principal capabilities covered in this part are:

1. AI lifecycle workflows and governance decision gates.
2. Governance stakeholders, RACI, and the Three Lines Model.
3. Control design, implementation, and operating effectiveness testing.
4. AI security red teaming and adversarial assessment.
5. Continuous AI supplier monitoring.
6. AI governance maturity assessment.
7. Phased enterprise implementation roadmap.
8. Governance execution within a telecom enterprise scenario.

Core Principle: AI governance becomes operationally effective when every material AI activity has a defined owner, every significant risk has an accountable decision-maker, every required control has verifiable evidence, and every governance decision is integrated into the organization's normal operating processes.

#### 35.2 Framework Mapping Principles

When mapping internal controls to external frameworks, use the following principles:

1. Identify the exact requirement or outcome being addressed.
2. Confirm that the requirement applies to the organization's AI system and role.
3. Map the requirement to an internal control objective.
4. Identify the actual implementation mechanism.
5. Define the evidence required to demonstrate implementation.
6. Test control effectiveness where appropriate.
7. Document gaps and remediation actions.
8. Maintain the mapping when frameworks, regulations, or internal controls change.

Avoid treating a broad mapping as proof of compliance.

For example, mapping an internal access-control policy to an ISO/IEC 42001 requirement does not independently establish that the AI management system conforms to ISO/IEC 42001.

Likewise, mapping an internal control to an EU AI Act requirement does not establish legal compliance without evaluating applicability, the responsible legal actor, and the full statutory obligation.

- Internal risk classification is documented.
- Legal applicability has been assessed.
- Security and privacy reviews are completed.
- Supplier risks are assessed.
- Material security findings are remediated or appropriately addressed.
- Required controls are implemented.
- Monitoring and incident response are operational.
- Residual risks are accepted by authorized owners.
- Approval evidence is retained.

Production authorization should be conditional on satisfying applicable mandatory requirements.

#### 39.5 Post-Deployment Governance

After production deployment, the operator should:

1. Monitor AI service performance.
2. Monitor access to customer data and enterprise APIs.
3. Detect prompt injection and suspicious usage patterns.
4. Review model and knowledge-base changes.
5. Monitor provider security and availability.
6. Investigate incidents.
7. Reassess material changes.
8. Review risk treatment actions.
9. Test controls according to the assurance plan.
10. Report significant exposure to management.

The AI system should remain within its approved purpose, architecture, permissions, and operational boundaries.

### 40. Common AI Governance Toolkit Implementation Mistakes

| Mistake                                                 | Why it is problematic                                | Corrective approach                                  |

### 42. GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. How would you build an enterprise AI governance toolkit from scratch?

I would begin by understanding the organization's AI landscape, business priorities, regulatory exposure, and existing governance capabilities. I would establish the governance charter, accountability model, AI inventory, and classification methodology. I would then develop the risk, security, privacy, supplier, and control assessment instruments. Finally, I would integrate these artifacts into lifecycle approval, monitoring, assurance, and reporting processes. I would prioritize practical implementation over creating unnecessary documentation.

Q2. How do you ensure traceability between regulations, controls, and audit evidence?

I establish a structured mapping from applicable external requirements to internal policies, control objectives, implemented controls, AI systems, and supporting evidence. Each relationship should use identifiable references and assigned owners. This allows the organization to demonstrate not only which requirements apply but also how they are implemented and validated.

Q3. What is the difference between an AI governance toolkit and an AI governance platform?

A toolkit is the collection of methodologies, templates, registers, procedures, and control specifications used to implement governance. A governance platform is the technology environment that may automate or manage these processes. A toolkit can exist without a dedicated platform, but a platform still requires well-defined governance methodologies and accountable decision-making.

Q4. How would you reduce administrative overhead in an AI governance program?

I would consolidate overlapping assessments, establish reusable control libraries, integrate AI governance into existing procurement and project workflows, and automate repetitive tasks such as reminders, inventory updates, and evidence collection. I would also apply risk-based assessment depth so that low-impact AI systems do not receive the same administrative treatment as systems with significant security, privacy, or business consequences.

Q5. How do you demonstrate AI governance effectiveness to senior management?

I would present a risk-based dashboard covering AI inventory completeness, assessment coverage, high-risk findings, remediation performance, control effectiveness, incidents, supplier exposure, and material exceptions. I would focus on trends, business impact, accountability, and decisions required rather than reporting activity counts alone.

Q6. How would you approach an organization with no AI inventory?

I would establish an AI discovery initiative using business engagement, procurement records, cloud service information, software inventories, security telemetry, and employee reporting. I would register identified systems, assign owners, classify their use cases, and prioritize high-impact applications for assessment. I would also establish an ongoing discovery process so that the inventory remains accurate.

Q7. What is the most important artifact in an AI governance toolkit?

There is no single artifact that independently establishes effective governance. However, the AI system inventory is foundational because it establishes visibility and supports ownership, classification, risk assessment, supplier oversight, and monitoring. Without an accurate inventory, the organization cannot reliably determine which AI systems require governance attention.

Q8. How would you handle a disagreement between business stakeholders and cybersecurity regarding AI deployment?

I would translate the technical concern into a clearly defined business risk, supported by the architecture, threat scenario, potential impact, and control evidence. I would identify practical mitigation options and determine whether the remaining exposure falls within approved risk appetite. If the disagreement remains unresolved, I would escalate it to the formally authorized risk acceptance authority. The decision must be documented and must not bypass mandatory legal or security requirements.

Q9. How do you prevent AI governance from becoming a documentation exercise?

I would connect every important artifact to a real operational activity. For example, the AI inventory should trigger assessments, assessment findings should generate remediation actions, control registers should drive testing, and dashboards should support management decisions. I would measure actual control implementation and operating effectiveness rather than simply counting completed forms.

Q10. What would be your first priority when implementing AI governance in a telecom operator?

My first priority would be visibility and accountability: identify AI systems, their business purposes, owners, models, data sources, providers, and integrations. I would then prioritize systems with customer data, critical operational dependencies, external exposure, or autonomous capabilities. This provides a defensible basis for risk prioritization and the implementation of proportionate controls.

### 44. Summary and Core Principle

An enterprise AI Security Governance Toolkit must provide a complete operational structure for governing AI systems from initial identification through retirement.

The consolidated toolkit supports:

1. Governance documentation and accountability.
2. AI system and model inventories.
3. Risk classification and assessment.
4. Cybersecurity, privacy, and third-party assurance.
5. Control design, implementation, and testing.
6. Lifecycle approval and change management.
7. Monitoring, incident response, and remediation.
8. Audit evidence and management reporting.
9. GRC platform integration and automation.
10. Enterprise consulting and continuous improvement.

The most important outcome is a traceable governance environment in which requirements, risks, controls, ownership, decisions, and evidence are connected.

Core Principle: An enterprise AI Security Governance Toolkit must enable the organization to demonstrate control over its AI environment—not merely document its intentions. Effective governance is established through accountable ownership, risk-informed decisions, enforceable controls, continuous assurance, and reliable evidence throughout the AI lifecycle.
