# 22.11 — NIST AI Terminology

## Part 1 — NIST AI Concepts, Risk Management Terminology, Governance Functions, and Foundational Definitions

### 1. Introduction to NIST AI Terminology

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) is a United States government agency that develops technical standards, measurement science, cybersecurity guidance, and risk management frameworks.

NIST has become an important reference point for enterprise AI governance, particularly through the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) and its associated resources.

NIST AI terminology provides a common vocabulary for discussing AI systems, their characteristics, potential risks, trustworthiness, and governance requirements.

For cybersecurity and GRC professionals, understanding this terminology is important because it enables consistent communication between:

- Executive management.
- AI governance committees.
- Cybersecurity teams.
- Enterprise risk management.
- Data governance.
- Privacy and legal functions.
- AI developers and engineers.
- Internal and external auditors.
- Regulatory and compliance teams.

NIST terminology is especially useful when an organization wants to establish an AI risk management methodology that integrates with existing enterprise risk management and cybersecurity processes.

#### 1.1 Key NIST AI Publications

The following publications provide important terminology and conceptual foundations.

| Publication     | Title                                                                                         | Primary purpose                                                          |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| NIST AI RMF 1.0 | Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework                                             | Establish a framework for managing AI risks and promoting trustworthy AI |
| NIST AI 100-1   | Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework                                             | Official publication identifier for AI RMF 1.0                           |
| NIST AI 100-2   | Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations           | Establish terminology for adversarial machine learning                   |
| NIST AI 600-1   | Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile | Identify and manage generative AI-specific risks                         |

The publications have different purposes. They should not be treated as interchangeable frameworks or as a single mandatory compliance standard.

### 2. Artificial Intelligence Terminology in the NIST Context

NIST AI terminology provides a foundation for understanding the systems being governed.

#### 2.1 Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Artificial Intelligence refers to a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments.

The concept encompasses systems with different levels of autonomy and adaptability.

Examples include:

- Machine learning systems.
- Natural language processing applications.
- Computer vision systems.
- Recommendation engines.
- Generative AI applications.
- Autonomous systems.

From a GRC perspective, the important consideration is not simply whether an application uses AI. It is how the system functions, what decisions or outputs it produces, and what consequences may arise from its use.

#### 2.2 AI System

An AI system is a machine-based system that can make predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments, based on human-defined objectives.

An AI system may consist of multiple technical and organizational components.

For example, an enterprise AI customer service application may include:

```
Enterprise AI System
        |
        +---- User Interface
        |
        +---- AI Application
        |
        +---- Foundation Model
        |
        +---- Retrieval Engine
        |
        +---- Enterprise Data Sources
        |
        +---- API Integrations
        |
        +---- Identity and Access Management
        |
        +---- Monitoring and Logging
```

The AI system is therefore broader than the model itself.

This distinction matters because governance assessments must examine the complete application, including its data, infrastructure, integrations, users, and operating environment.

#### 2.3 Machine Learning (ML)

Machine Learning is a field of AI concerned with systems that learn patterns from data to perform tasks such as prediction, classification, or decision-making.

Examples include:

- Fraud detection.
- Customer churn prediction.
- Network anomaly detection.
- Credit risk modeling.
- Predictive maintenance.

From a governance perspective, ML introduces risks related to training data quality, model performance, bias, drift, validation, and security.

#### 2.4 Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative AI refers to AI systems capable of generating synthetic content, including:

- Text.
- Images.
- Audio.
- Video.
- Software code.
- Structured outputs.

Examples include LLM-based assistants, image generation systems, and audio synthesis applications.

NIST AI 600-1 provides a risk-management perspective specifically addressing generative AI.

#### 2.5 Foundation Model

A foundation model is a model trained on broad data at scale that can be adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks.

Foundation models may support generative and non-generative applications.

Examples of downstream applications include:

- Text generation.
- Classification.
- Semantic search.
- Summarization.
- Question answering.
- Multimodal analysis.

Foundation models introduce governance considerations involving training data provenance, model transparency, evaluation, security, limitations, and downstream deployment.

### 3. NIST AI RMF — Foundational Concepts

The NIST AI RMF provides a voluntary framework to help organizations manage risks associated with AI systems.

Its purpose is to support the development and use of trustworthy and responsible AI.

The framework is designed to be:

- Voluntary.
- Rights-preserving.
- Non-sector-specific.
- Use-case agnostic.
- Adaptable to different organizational contexts.

The framework provides a structured approach to identifying, assessing, managing, and monitoring AI risks.

#### 3.1 AI Risk

AI risk refers to the potential for an AI system to create adverse consequences for individuals, organizations, society, or the environment.

AI risks can arise from:

- Technical failures.
- Poor data quality.
- Model limitations.
- Security vulnerabilities.
- Privacy violations.
- Unintended system behavior.
- Inappropriate human reliance.
- Unclear accountability.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Misuse or malicious exploitation.

AI risk is not limited to cybersecurity.

For example, a customer recommendation model may create discriminatory outcomes without suffering a conventional cybersecurity attack.

Similarly, a generative AI system may disclose confidential information because of inadequate access controls.

#### 3.2 AI Risk Management

AI risk management is the coordinated set of activities used to identify, assess, prioritize, respond to, and monitor risks associated with AI systems.

The objective is to reduce unacceptable risks while enabling organizations to obtain the intended benefits of AI.

Typical activities include:

1. Identifying AI systems and their intended purposes.
2. Understanding the operating context.
3. Identifying affected stakeholders.
4. Evaluating potential harms.
5. Assessing likelihood and impact.
6. Selecting risk treatment measures.
7. Monitoring residual risks.
8. Reviewing effectiveness and emerging risks.

AI risk management should be integrated with enterprise risk management rather than treated as an isolated technical exercise.

#### 3.3 Trustworthy AI

NIST identifies characteristics associated with trustworthy AI systems.

The seven characteristics in NIST AI RMF 1.0 are:

1. Valid and reliable.
2. Safe.
3. Secure and resilient.
4. Accountable and transparent.
5. Explainable and interpretable.
6. Privacy-enhanced.
7. Fair, with harmful bias managed.

These characteristics are related but distinct.

For example:

- A model may be accurate but not secure.
- A system may be secure but not fair.
- A system may be explainable but not privacy-enhanced.
- A system may be transparent but still unreliable.

Trustworthiness should therefore be evaluated through multiple dimensions rather than a single overall AI quality score.

### 4. The Seven Trustworthy AI Characteristics

#### 4.1 Valid and Reliable

Validity concerns whether an AI system is appropriate for its intended purpose.

Reliability concerns whether the system performs consistently as expected under specified conditions.

Example:

A telecom fraud detection model must reliably identify suspicious transactions while maintaining acceptable false-positive and false-negative rates.

Governance questions:

- Has the system been validated against its intended purpose?
- Are evaluation datasets representative?
- Are performance limitations documented?
- Is performance monitored after deployment?
- Are changes subject to reassessment?

#### 4.2 Safe

Safety concerns whether an AI system avoids creating unacceptable risks of harm to people, property, or the environment.

Example:

An AI system used to support emergency network operations must not generate recommendations that create unacceptable safety consequences.

Governance questions:

- Have foreseeable hazards been identified?
- Are safety requirements defined?
- Are failure scenarios tested?
- Are human intervention mechanisms available?
- Are unsafe behaviors monitored?

#### 4.3 Secure and Resilient

Security concerns protecting AI systems against unauthorized access, manipulation, and compromise.

Resilience concerns the ability to withstand adverse events and recover while maintaining appropriate functionality.

Examples of security threats:

- Data poisoning.
- Model extraction.
- Prompt injection.
- Unauthorized access.
- Model tampering.
- Supply chain compromise.

Examples of resilience requirements:

- Backup and recovery.
- Redundancy.
- Incident response.
- Fallback mechanisms.
- Service continuity.

Governance questions:

- Are AI-specific threats assessed?
- Are models and datasets protected?
- Are critical AI dependencies identified?
- Are recovery procedures tested?
- Can the system operate safely during degraded conditions?

#### 4.4 Accountable and Transparent

Accountability concerns assigning responsibility for AI system decisions, outcomes, and governance.

Transparency concerns making appropriate information about the system available to relevant stakeholders.

Transparency may include information about:

- System purpose.
- Intended users.
- Limitations.
- Capabilities.
- Data sources.
- Governance decisions.
- Human oversight.
- Risk management.

Example:

A company deploying an AI-based customer service assistant should establish who owns the system, who approves its use, and how users are informed that they are interacting with AI.

Governance questions:

- Is system ownership documented?
- Are decision-making responsibilities defined?
- Are relevant stakeholders informed?
- Are system limitations communicated?
- Are governance decisions traceable?

#### 4.5 Explainable and Interpretable

These concepts are related but should not be treated as synonyms.

Explainability concerns providing information about the mechanisms or factors contributing to an AI system's outputs.

Interpretability concerns the extent to which a human can understand the system's operation or behavior.

Example:

A credit-risk model may provide information about the factors contributing to a particular risk score.

Governance questions:

- Can relevant stakeholders understand important model outputs?
- Are explanations appropriate for the intended audience?
- Are limitations of explanations documented?
- Can decisions be reviewed or challenged where appropriate?

An explanation is not necessarily proof that a model is correct or that its decision is fair.

#### 4.6 Privacy-Enhanced

Privacy-enhanced AI involves protecting individuals' privacy and personal information throughout AI system design, development, deployment, and operation.

Relevant considerations include:

- Data minimization.
- Purpose limitation.
- Access restrictions.
- Data protection.
- Retention and deletion.
- Privacy-enhancing technologies.
- Privacy impact assessments.

Examples of privacy-enhancing techniques include:

- Differential privacy.
- Federated learning.
- Secure multiparty computation.
- Homomorphic encryption.

These techniques have different security properties and implementation constraints. Their use does not automatically establish GDPR compliance.

Governance questions:

- Is personal data processed lawfully?
- Are privacy risks assessed?
- Is data minimization implemented?
- Are retention and deletion requirements enforced?
- Are appropriate technical and organizational safeguards implemented?

#### 4.7 Fair, with Harmful Bias Managed

Fairness concerns whether an AI system produces outcomes that are appropriate and do not create unjustified harmful bias.

Bias can originate from:

- Training data.
- Data collection processes.
- Historical decisions.
- Feature selection.
- Model design.
- Evaluation methods.
- Deployment context.
- Human interpretation and use.

Example:

A recruitment model trained on historically biased hiring data may reproduce or amplify discriminatory patterns.

Governance questions:

- Has the organization identified relevant fairness considerations?
- Are evaluation datasets sufficiently representative?
- Are appropriate subgroup performance assessments performed?
- Are potential discriminatory impacts investigated?
- Are remediation actions documented?

Fairness is context-dependent. A single mathematical fairness metric cannot establish fairness for every AI application.

### 5. NIST AI RMF Core Functions

The NIST AI RMF 1.0 is structured around four core functions:

## NIST AI RMF — Four Core Functions

# GOVERN

Cross-cutting governance

Policies, accountability, culture and oversight.

# MAP

Context and risk identification

Understand the system, context and impacts.

# MEASURE

Analysis and evaluation

Assess, test and monitor AI risks.

# MANAGE

Risk response

Prioritize, treat and monitor risks.

GOVERN is cross-cutting and informs the other three functions. The functions are not necessarily executed as a rigid linear sequence.

#### 5.1 GOVERN

GOVERN establishes the organizational structures, policies, processes, and accountability mechanisms necessary for AI risk management.

It is a cross-cutting function that informs and supports MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE.

Its objectives include:

- Establishing AI risk management policies.
- Defining roles and responsibilities.
- Integrating AI risk into enterprise risk management.
- Establishing accountability.
- Promoting organizational AI risk awareness.
- Managing third-party AI risks.
- Establishing governance oversight.
- Supporting continuous improvement.

Example:

A telecommunications operator establishes an AI Governance Committee responsible for overseeing AI risk exposure, approving governance policies, and escalating significant issues to executive management.

#### 5.2 MAP

MAP establishes the context in which an AI system operates and identifies risks, impacts, and relevant stakeholders.

Its activities include:

- Understanding the business purpose.
- Identifying intended users.
- Documenting system characteristics.
- Identifying stakeholders.
- Understanding operating environments.
- Identifying potential impacts.
- Mapping dependencies and third-party relationships.

Example:

Before deploying a customer service RAG assistant, the organization identifies the connected CRM platform, external LLM provider, customer data sources, intended users, and potential consequences of incorrect responses.

#### 5.3 MEASURE

MEASURE focuses on assessing and analyzing AI risks using appropriate methods, metrics, testing, and evaluation.

Its activities include:

- Selecting appropriate measurement methods.
- Evaluating model performance.
- Assessing trustworthiness characteristics.
- Testing security and robustness.
- Evaluating privacy and fairness considerations.
- Documenting limitations.
- Monitoring AI behavior.

Example:

A security team performs prompt injection testing, unauthorized retrieval testing, and sensitive information disclosure assessments against an enterprise RAG system.

#### 5.4 MANAGE

MANAGE focuses on prioritizing and responding to identified AI risks.

Its activities include:

- Prioritizing risks.
- Selecting risk treatment measures.
- Assigning responsibility.
- Implementing mitigation controls.
- Managing residual risk.
- Monitoring risk exposure.
- Responding to incidents.
- Decommissioning systems when necessary.

Example:

An organization discovers that an AI agent can access customer billing records beyond the permissions of its intended users. The organization restricts the agent's API permissions, implements backend authorization, tests the remediation, and updates the risk register.

### 6. NIST AI RMF Function Categories

Each core function contains categories and subcategories that provide more specific outcomes and activities.

The four functions should be understood as a connected management structure.

| Function | Category  | Primary focus                                        |

| GOVERN   | GOVERN 1  | Policies, processes, procedures, and practices       |
| GOVERN   | GOVERN 2  | Accountability and roles                             |

| GOVERN   | GOVERN 4  | Organizational risk culture                          |
| GOVERN   | GOVERN 5  | Stakeholder engagement                               |
| GOVERN   | GOVERN 6  | Third-party and supply chain risk                    |
| MAP      | MAP 1     | Context and intended purpose                         |
| MAP      | MAP 2     | Categorization of AI systems                         |
| MAP      | MAP 3     | Benefits, costs, and impacts                         |
| MAP      | MAP 4     | Risks and impacts to individuals and communities     |

These are category-level summaries. The full framework includes more detailed subcategories and outcomes.

#### 6.1 Why GOVERN Is Cross-Cutting

GOVERN differs from the other three functions because it establishes the organizational conditions under which AI risk management takes place.

Without governance:

- Risk ownership may be unclear.
- Assessments may be inconsistent.
- Controls may not be implemented.
- Third-party responsibilities may be overlooked.
- Risk acceptance may occur without authority.
- Monitoring may lack management oversight.

For example, MAP may identify a serious privacy risk, MEASURE may evaluate its likelihood and impact, and MANAGE may recommend additional controls.

However, GOVERN establishes who has authority to approve the treatment decision and who remains accountable for the residual risk.

This is why GOVERN is not simply another sequential assessment stage.

### 7. NIST AI RMF Profiles and Implementation Context

NIST AI RMF supports different organizational and use-case contexts through profiles.

A profile helps an organization apply the framework to a particular operating environment, technology, sector, or risk context.

#### 7.1 AI RMF Current Profile Concepts

Two useful concepts are:

- Cross-sectoral profile: Applies the AI RMF to a particular risk area across multiple sectors.
- Use-case profile: Applies the AI RMF to a specific AI application or use case.

A profile can help an organization identify which risks, outcomes, and activities are particularly relevant to its environment.

#### 7.2 Generative AI Profile

NIST AI 600-1 provides a Generative AI Profile associated with the AI RMF.

It addresses risks specific to generative AI, including:

- Confabulation.
- Harmful bias and homogenization.
- Information integrity.
- Information security.
- Intellectual property.
- Privacy.
- Human-AI configuration.
- Value chain and component integration.
- Environmental impacts.

The profile helps organizations adapt the broader AI RMF to generative AI systems.

For example, a conventional predictive ML model and an enterprise LLM may share general governance requirements but have different security and operational risk characteristics.

The generative AI profile helps identify additional considerations relevant to the LLM deployment.

### 8. Applying NIST AI RMF to Enterprise GRC

NIST AI RMF can be integrated into an enterprise GRC operating model.

The framework should not operate as a separate compliance exercise.

Instead, its functions can be connected to existing organizational processes.

| NIST function | Enterprise GRC process             | Example deliverable                  |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| GOVERN        | Governance and accountability      | AI governance policy and RACI        |
| GOVERN        | Third-party risk management        | AI supplier assessment               |
| MAP           | AI inventory and classification    | AI system inventory                  |
| MAP           | Business and impact assessment     | AI impact assessment                 |
| MEASURE       | Risk and control assessment        | AI risk assessment                   |
| MEASURE       | Security testing                   | AI red-team report                   |
| MANAGE        | Risk treatment                     | AI risk treatment plan               |
| MANAGE        | Risk acceptance                    | Risk acceptance record               |
| MANAGE        | Monitoring and incident management | AI monitoring and incident registers |

#### 8.1 Telecom Enterprise Example

Consider a telecom operator implementing an AI customer service assistant using Salesforce, an external LLM, RAG, and billing APIs.

GOVERN

- Establish an AI governance policy.
- Assign business, system, and model owners.
- Define risk acceptance authority.
- Establish supplier governance requirements.

MAP

- Register the AI system.
- Document its intended purpose.
- Identify customer data processing.
- Map Salesforce, billing, and external provider dependencies.
- Identify affected customers and employees.

MEASURE

- Assess prompt injection exposure.
- Test unauthorized customer data retrieval.
- Evaluate response accuracy.
- Assess privacy and supplier risks.
- Validate access-control implementation.

MANAGE

- Restrict unauthorized API access.
- Implement monitoring and logging.
- Remediate security findings.
- Establish fallback mechanisms.
- Approve residual risks through authorized governance channels.

This approach connects the framework's concepts to practical enterprise risk management activities.

### 9. NIST AI Terminology — Important Conceptual Distinctions

| Term               | Meaning                                                | Important distinction                      |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| AI                 | Broad field of machine-based intelligent capabilities  | Not limited to machine learning            |
| AI system          | Complete machine-based system                          | Broader than the model                     |
| AI model           | Computational representation used to produce outputs   | One component of an AI system              |
| Machine learning   | AI approach involving learning from data               | Not all AI uses ML                         |
| Generative AI      | AI capable of generating synthetic content             | Not all AI is generative                   |
| Foundation model   | Broadly trained model adaptable to downstream tasks    | Not synonymous with LLM                    |
| AI risk            | Potential adverse consequences associated with AI      | Broader than cybersecurity risk            |
| Trustworthy AI     | AI characterized by defined trustworthiness properties | Not a single technical certification       |
| AI risk management | Coordinated activities for managing AI risk            | Integrated with enterprise risk management |
| AI profile         | Context-specific application of a framework            | Not necessarily a separate standard        |

#### 9.1 AI System Versus AI Model

An AI model is a component. An AI system includes the model and the broader environment in which it operates.

A model may be technically secure while the application using it has excessive permissions, insecure APIs, or insufficient monitoring.

Consequently, AI governance must assess the complete system.

#### 9.2 AI Risk Versus Cybersecurity Risk

Cybersecurity risk focuses on threats and vulnerabilities affecting the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information systems and associated assets.

AI risk includes cybersecurity risks but also extends to fairness, reliability, safety, privacy, transparency, accountability, and other potential adverse impacts.

#### 9.3 Trustworthiness Versus Compliance

Trustworthy AI is a set of characteristics and objectives used to evaluate AI systems.

Regulatory compliance concerns whether applicable legal obligations are satisfied.

An organization cannot automatically claim legal compliance merely because it has evaluated the seven NIST trustworthy AI characteristics.

Likewise, compliance with a particular legal requirement does not automatically establish that an AI system is trustworthy across every dimension.

### 10. Summary and Core Principle

NIST AI terminology provides a foundation for understanding AI systems, AI risks, and the characteristics associated with trustworthy AI.

The key concepts introduced in this part are:

1. AI systems, machine learning, generative AI, and foundation models.
2. AI risk and enterprise AI risk management.
3. The seven trustworthy AI characteristics.
4. The four NIST AI RMF core functions: GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE.
5. The categories supporting each core function.
6. AI RMF profiles and the generative AI risk profile.
7. Integration of NIST AI RMF into enterprise GRC.
8. The distinction between AI systems, models, risks, trustworthiness, and compliance.

Core Principle: NIST AI RMF provides a structured approach to managing AI risks through organizational governance, contextual understanding, measurement, and risk response. Its value lies in integrating trustworthy AI considerations into enterprise decision-making and lifecycle management rather than treating AI risk as an isolated technical problem.

# 22.11 — NIST AI Terminology

## Part 2 — NIST AI Risk Assessment, Measurement, Evaluation, and Risk Treatment Terminology

### 11. NIST AI Risk Assessment Concepts

AI risk assessment is a structured process for identifying, analyzing, and evaluating potential adverse consequences associated with an AI system.

Within the NIST AI RMF, risk assessment is not limited to determining whether a model performs accurately. It requires understanding the system's intended purpose, operating environment, stakeholders, potential impacts, and the effectiveness of relevant safeguards.

An AI risk assessment should consider:

- Technical performance.
- Reliability and robustness.
- Cybersecurity.
- Privacy.
- Safety.
- Fairness and harmful bias.
- Transparency and explainability.
- Human oversight.
- Third-party dependencies.
- Organizational and societal impacts.

The assessment should be proportionate to the system's intended use and potential consequences.

#### 11.1 AI Risk Identification

AI risk identification is the process of determining potential risk scenarios associated with the design, development, deployment, operation, and retirement of an AI system.

Examples:

| Risk category     | Example risk scenario                                            |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Model performance | Model produces inaccurate predictions                            |
| Data quality      | Training data contains incomplete or inaccurate records          |
| Security          | Attacker manipulates inputs to influence model behavior          |
| Privacy           | Personal information is disclosed through model outputs          |
| Fairness          | Model produces discriminatory outcomes                           |
| Safety            | AI recommendations create unacceptable harm                      |
| Transparency      | Stakeholders cannot understand relevant system limitations       |
| Operational       | AI service failure disrupts business operations                  |
| Third-party       | External provider changes model behavior without adequate notice |

A risk scenario should describe what could happen, the conditions that could enable it, and its potential consequences.

#### 11.2 AI Risk Analysis

Risk analysis examines the characteristics and potential consequences of identified risks.

Typical considerations include:

- Likelihood of occurrence.
- Severity of impact.
- Exposure.
- Vulnerabilities.
- Existing safeguards.
- Threat conditions.
- Affected stakeholders.
- Uncertainty.
- Potential cascading effects.

Organizations may use qualitative, quantitative, or hybrid methodologies.

NIST AI RMF does not prescribe one universal risk-scoring formula.

For example, a company may use a likelihood-and-impact matrix, but the resulting score is an organizational prioritization mechanism, not necessarily a statistically established probability of harm.

#### 11.3 AI Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares analyzed risks against organizational criteria to determine whether they are acceptable, require treatment, or must be escalated.

Example:

| Risk                                       | Analysis                                   | Evaluation                                      |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized retrieval of customer records | High potential privacy and security impact | Requires remediation before production          |
| Minor response formatting errors           | Limited business impact                    | May be monitored and accepted                   |
| Incorrect billing recommendations          | Potential financial and customer impact    | Requires additional validation and human review |
| External provider outage                   | Potential service disruption               | Requires fallback and continuity controls       |

The organization should define its risk appetite, risk tolerance, acceptance authority, and escalation thresholds.

### 12. AI Risk Measurement Terminology

NIST AI RMF emphasizes the importance of measuring AI risks using appropriate methods and metrics.

Measurement provides evidence about AI system characteristics, performance, limitations, and potential impacts.

However, not every AI risk can be represented by a single numerical metric.

#### 12.1 Measurement Method

A measurement method is the approach used to collect and evaluate information about an AI system or its risks.

Examples include:

- Statistical evaluation.
- Benchmark testing.
- Adversarial testing.
- Security testing.
- Human evaluation.
- Surveys and stakeholder feedback.
- Expert review.
- Operational monitoring.
- Simulation.
- Independent assessment.

The selected method should be appropriate to the risk being evaluated.

For example, accuracy testing alone cannot establish whether a RAG application enforces document-level authorization.

#### 12.2 Measurement Metric

A measurement metric is a defined indicator used to evaluate a particular system characteristic or risk.

Examples:

| Metric                    | Measurement purpose                                           |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accuracy                  | Proportion of correct predictions                             |
| Precision                 | Proportion of positive predictions that are correct           |
| Recall                    | Proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified      |
| False-positive rate       | Frequency of incorrect positive classifications               |
| False-negative rate       | Frequency of missed positive cases                            |
| Latency                   | Time required to produce a response                           |
| Availability              | Proportion of time a service is available                     |
| Robustness                | Performance under defined perturbations or adverse conditions |
| Fairness metric           | Measurement of a defined fairness property                    |
| Privacy leakage indicator | Evidence of unintended information disclosure                 |

Metrics must be interpreted in context.

For example, a high accuracy score may conceal poor performance for a particular population or operational condition.

#### 12.3 Measurement Uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty concerns limitations or uncertainty associated with measurement results.

Potential sources include:

- Incomplete datasets.
- Sampling limitations.
- Measurement errors.
- Unrepresentative evaluation data.
- Changing operating environments.
- Inconsistent human evaluations.
- Limited testing coverage.
- Unknown model behavior.

Organizations should document uncertainty rather than presenting evaluation results as absolute guarantees.

#### 12.4 Measurement Validity

Measurement validity concerns whether a measurement approach actually evaluates the characteristic it claims to measure.

Example:

A benchmark designed to measure general language understanding may not adequately evaluate an LLM's resistance to prompt injection.

Similarly, a conventional penetration test may not adequately cover retrieval authorization failures or indirect prompt injection.

A measurement method must match the risk objective.

#### 12.5 Measurement Reliability

Measurement reliability concerns whether a measurement process produces sufficiently consistent results under comparable conditions.

For example, repeated model evaluations may produce different results because of:

- Sampling behavior.
- Model version changes.
- Dataset variations.
- Evaluation configuration.
- Human evaluator differences.

The organization should establish reproducible testing conditions and document relevant limitations.

### 13. AI Evaluation and Testing Terminology

Evaluation determines whether an AI system satisfies defined requirements and performs appropriately within its intended context.

NIST AI RMF encourages organizations to evaluate systems across relevant trustworthiness characteristics.

#### 13.1 AI System Evaluation

AI system evaluation examines whether the complete system behaves as intended.

It may cover:

- Functional performance.
- Reliability.
- Security.
- Privacy.
- Safety.
- Fairness.
- Explainability.
- Operational resilience.
- Human oversight.

The evaluation should consider the application, model, data, infrastructure, integrations, and users.

#### 13.2 Model Evaluation

Model evaluation examines the performance and characteristics of an individual AI model.

Examples:

- Classification accuracy.
- Prediction error.
- Precision and recall.
- Model robustness.
- Generalization performance.
- Calibration.
- Fairness indicators.
- Model limitations.

Model evaluation is only one component of complete AI system evaluation.

#### 13.3 Benchmark

A benchmark is a defined dataset, task, or evaluation procedure used to compare or measure AI system performance.

Examples include:

- Language understanding benchmarks.
- Image classification benchmarks.
- Reasoning benchmarks.
- Security evaluation datasets.
- Domain-specific performance tests.

Governance considerations:

- Is the benchmark relevant to the intended use?
- Does it represent actual operating conditions?
- Is the benchmark susceptible to data contamination?
- Does it measure the risks that matter to the organization?
- Are benchmark limitations documented?

A strong benchmark result does not guarantee production performance.

#### 13.4 Red Teaming

Red teaming is an adversarial evaluation approach designed to identify weaknesses through simulated attack or misuse scenarios.

For AI systems, testing may include:

- Prompt injection.
- Jailbreaking.
- Sensitive information disclosure.
- Model extraction.
- Unauthorized tool invocation.
- Data poisoning.
- Adversarial examples.
- Retrieval manipulation.

Red teaming provides evidence about vulnerabilities under tested conditions. It does not prove that a system is immune to all attacks.

#### 13.5 Independent Evaluation

Independent evaluation involves assessment by individuals or teams that are sufficiently separate from the activities being evaluated.

Its purpose is to reduce conflicts of interest and strengthen assurance.

Example:

An AI development team performs its own model testing, while an independent cybersecurity assessment team evaluates security controls and adversarial behavior before production approval.

Independence requirements should be proportionate to system risk and organizational structure.

### 14. AI Risk Tracking and Monitoring Terminology

AI risks may change throughout the system lifecycle.

A model that performs adequately during initial validation may deteriorate after deployment because of changing data, user behavior, operating conditions, or external dependencies.

Risk tracking provides visibility into these changes.

#### 14.1 Risk Tracking

Risk tracking is the ongoing recording and monitoring of identified AI risks, their status, treatment actions, and changes in exposure.

Typical tracking information includes:

- Risk identifier.
- Risk description.
- Risk owner.
- Current rating.
- Treatment decision.
- Remediation status.
- Residual risk.
- Target date.
- Supporting evidence.

#### 14.2 Risk Monitoring

Risk monitoring involves observing relevant indicators and conditions to identify changes that may affect AI risk.

Examples:

- Model performance deterioration.
- Data drift.
- Increasing false-positive rates.
- Unauthorized retrieval attempts.
- Prompt injection activity.
- Changes in model providers.
- Security incidents.
- Unusual API consumption.
- Changes in user population.
- New regulatory obligations.

Risk monitoring should have defined thresholds, ownership, and escalation procedures.

#### 14.3 Feedback

Feedback refers to information obtained from relevant stakeholders, users, evaluators, and operational processes that helps improve AI system performance and risk management.

Examples include:

- User complaints.
- Human reviewer corrections.
- Customer feedback.
- Incident reports.
- Security findings.
- Model evaluation results.
- Audit observations.
- Supplier notifications.

Feedback should be assessed before being incorporated into model development, retraining, or operational changes.

Unverified feedback should not automatically be treated as authoritative training data.

### 15. NIST AI RMF MEASURE Function Terminology

The MEASURE function focuses on evaluating AI system characteristics, assessing trustworthiness, and tracking AI risks.

It contains four categories.

| Category  | Primary objective                                      |

| MEASURE 1 | Select and apply appropriate measurement methods       |

| MEASURE 4 | Obtain feedback and input from relevant stakeholders   |

- Security assessment.

### 16. NIST AI RMF MANAGE Function Terminology

MANAGE focuses on prioritizing and responding to AI risks identified through governance, contextual analysis, and measurement.

The organization should select appropriate responses based on risk severity, business objectives, legal requirements, and available controls.

#### 16.1 Risk Prioritization

Risk prioritization determines which AI risks require attention first.

Prioritization may consider:

- Severity of potential harm.
- Likelihood of occurrence.
- Exposure.
- Number of affected individuals.
- Business criticality.
- Legal and regulatory obligations.
- Control effectiveness.
- Risk appetite.
- Uncertainty.

For example, unauthorized retrieval of customer financial information may require immediate treatment even if no confirmed incident has occurred.

#### 16.2 Risk Treatment

Risk treatment refers to selecting and implementing measures to modify risk exposure.

Common enterprise treatment options include:

| Treatment      | Meaning                                                                     | Example                                                     |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mitigate       | Implement controls to reduce risk                                           | Enforce backend authorization                               |
| Avoid          | Stop the activity creating the exposure                                     | Do not deploy an unsafe agent capability                    |
| Transfer/share | Allocate or share aspects of risk through contractual or other arrangements | Contractual allocation of defined supplier responsibilities |
| Accept         | Formally retain the remaining risk                                          | Approve a limited residual operational risk                 |

These are general enterprise risk management treatment concepts used to operationalize AI risk decisions.

Risk acceptance does not eliminate risk and cannot waive applicable legal obligations.

#### 16.3 Risk Response

Risk response refers to the actions taken to address an identified risk.

Responses may include:

- Technical remediation.
- Additional testing.
- Human oversight.
- Restricted functionality.
- Supplier changes.
- Additional monitoring.
- Deployment delay.
- System suspension.
- Decommissioning.

The response should be proportionate to the nature and potential impact of the risk.

#### 16.4 Residual Risk

Residual risk is the exposure remaining after relevant risk treatment measures have been considered.

Example:

An AI assistant initially presents a high risk of unauthorized customer data retrieval.

The organization implements:

- Backend authorization.
- Role-based access controls.
- Data minimization.
- Retrieval filtering.
- Security monitoring.
- Adversarial testing.

The remaining exposure is reassessed and documented.

Residual risk must be evaluated using evidence about the actual implementation and effectiveness of controls.

A control that is merely planned should not be treated as an implemented safeguard.

#### 16.5 Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance is an authorized decision to retain a defined level of residual risk.

An appropriate decision should document:

- Risk description.
- Business justification.
- Potential impact.
- Existing controls.
- Residual exposure.
- Risk owner.
- Approval authority.
- Expiry or review conditions.
- Supporting evidence.

A project manager or system administrator cannot automatically accept enterprise risk simply because that person is responsible for delivery.

Acceptance authority must follow the organization's governance and delegation arrangements.

### 19. Practical GRC Case Study: Measuring and Managing RAG Risks

Consider a telecom operator deploying an enterprise RAG application that retrieves internal cybersecurity policies and customer service procedures.

The application uses an external LLM and a vector database connected to approved enterprise repositories.

The organization identifies three material risks.

#### 19.1 Risk Scenario 1 — Unauthorized Document Retrieval

Risk: Employees retrieve confidential information belonging to another department.

MAP

- Identify source repositories.
- Understand document classifications.
- Identify user groups.
- Map authorization boundaries.

MEASURE

- Test document-level authorization.
- Test revoked permissions.
- Examine cross-department retrieval.
- Review access logs.

MANAGE

- Implement retrieval-time authorization.
- Synchronize source permissions.
- Restrict service identities.
- Monitor denied and suspicious requests.

#### 19.2 Risk Scenario 2 — Indirect Prompt Injection

Risk: A malicious document influences the LLM to disclose sensitive information or perform an unauthorized action.

MAP

- Identify untrusted document sources.
- Map the ingestion pipeline.
- Identify the LLM's available capabilities.

MEASURE

- Conduct adversarial document testing.
- Evaluate prompt injection resistance.
- Test whether retrieved content can trigger unauthorized tools.

MANAGE

- Isolate untrusted retrieved content.
- Restrict tool permissions.
- Validate sensitive operations independently.
- Introduce human approval for consequential actions.

#### 19.3 Risk Scenario 3 — Obsolete Enterprise Information

Risk: The RAG system retrieves superseded policies and provides incorrect operational guidance.

MAP

- Identify authoritative document owners.
- Establish document approval and versioning requirements.

MEASURE

- Test retrieval of superseded documents.
- Evaluate source freshness.
- Review retrieval accuracy.

MANAGE

- Remove or supersede outdated content.
- Implement document lifecycle synchronization.
- Monitor indexing failures.
- Establish periodic source validation.

| Risk scenario             | GOVERN                               | MAP                                   | MEASURE                              | MANAGE                                        |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Unauthorized retrieval    | Assign access-control ownership      | Map users, documents, and permissions | Test authorization boundaries        | Enforce permissions and remediate             |
| Indirect prompt injection | Establish AI security accountability | Identify untrusted content and tools  | Perform adversarial testing          | Restrict execution and monitor                |
| Obsolete information      | Establish document governance        | Identify authoritative sources        | Test freshness and retrieval quality | Update indexes and enforce lifecycle controls |

This example demonstrates how the four NIST functions can be applied to actual enterprise risks.

### 20. GRC Interview Questions and Model Answers

Q1. What is the difference between the NIST AI RMF MEASURE and MANAGE functions?

MEASURE focuses on evaluating AI system characteristics and risks through appropriate methods, metrics, testing, and feedback. MANAGE focuses on prioritizing those risks and implementing appropriate responses. In simple terms, MEASURE establishes what the organization understands about its AI risk exposure, while MANAGE determines what actions should be taken.

Q2. Does NIST AI RMF prescribe a universal AI risk-scoring methodology?

No. NIST AI RMF provides a structured approach to identifying, assessing, and managing AI risks, but it does not prescribe one universal likelihood-and-impact scoring formula. Organizations should establish risk criteria appropriate to their operating environment and enterprise risk appetite.

Q3. What is the difference between AI system evaluation and model evaluation?

Model evaluation examines the characteristics and performance of an individual model. AI system evaluation considers the complete application, including the model, data, infrastructure, integrations, users, and operating environment. A model can perform well while the overall application remains insecure or unreliable.

Q4. Why is measurement uncertainty important in AI governance?

AI evaluation results can be affected by incomplete datasets, sampling limitations, changing environments, measurement errors, and model variability. Documenting uncertainty helps management understand the limitations of evaluation results and prevents unsupported claims about AI reliability or safety.

Q5. How would you apply the MEASURE function to an enterprise LLM?

I would establish evaluation criteria covering response quality, reliability, security, privacy, and robustness. I would then perform representative testing, adversarial assessments, access-control validation, and operational monitoring. Results would be documented with their limitations and linked to the relevant risk register.

Q6. What is the purpose of the MANAGE function?

MANAGE ensures that identified AI risks lead to appropriate organizational action. This includes prioritization, risk treatment, ownership assignment, implementation of controls, residual risk decisions, and continuous monitoring.

Q7. Is risk acceptance equivalent to risk mitigation?

No. Risk acceptance is a decision to retain exposure. Risk mitigation involves implementing measures to reduce the likelihood or impact of a risk. Accepting a risk does not reduce its underlying exposure and does not eliminate applicable legal obligations.

Q8. How do NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 complement each other?

NIST AI RMF provides a structured approach to AI risk management through GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE. ISO/IEC 42001 provides requirements for establishing and maintaining an AI management system. An organization can use both to combine risk management practices with formal management system governance.

Q9. How do you demonstrate that an AI risk has been effectively managed?

I would examine the original risk scenario, the implemented treatment measures, supporting technical and operational evidence, control testing results, and the reassessed residual risk. I would also verify that an authorized risk owner has approved the remaining exposure where required.

Q10. Why should AI risk management be integrated into enterprise GRC?

AI risks often overlap with cybersecurity, privacy, operational, legal, supplier, and business risks. Integrating AI risk management with enterprise GRC reduces duplication, improves accountability, and enables management to evaluate AI exposure within the organization's broader risk environment.

### 21. Summary and Core Principle

NIST AI RMF provides terminology and structured activities for evaluating and managing AI risk.

The principal concepts covered in this part are:

1. AI risk identification, analysis, and evaluation.
2. Measurement methods, metrics, uncertainty, validity, and reliability.
3. AI system evaluation, model evaluation, benchmarking, and red teaming.
4. Risk tracking, monitoring, and stakeholder feedback.
5. MEASURE function categories and their governance applications.
6. MANAGE function categories, risk treatment, and residual risk.
7. Integration of AI risk management with enterprise GRC.
8. Application of NIST AI RMF to enterprise RAG risks.

Core Principle: Measuring AI risk without taking action does not establish effective risk management. NIST AI RMF connects evaluation with accountable risk treatment, requiring organizations to use evidence to prioritize exposure, implement safeguards, monitor residual risk, and continually improve their AI governance practices.
