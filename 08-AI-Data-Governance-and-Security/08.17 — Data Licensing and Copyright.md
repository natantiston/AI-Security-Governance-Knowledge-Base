# 08.17 — Data Licensing and Copyright

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to Data Licensing and Copyright

AI systems depend heavily on data.

Training datasets, fine-tuning datasets, evaluation datasets, documents, images, audio, software code, databases, and other digital content may be subject to **copyright, licensing, contractual, or other legal restrictions**.

For AI governance, the key principle is:

> **The fact that data is publicly accessible does not automatically mean that it is legally free to use for AI development.**

An organization should understand the rights and restrictions associated with data before using it to develop, train, test, deploy, or improve an AI system.

---

### 2. Why Data Licensing Matters in AI Governance

AI systems can consume enormous quantities of data.

For example:

```text
Websites
   +
Books
   +
Images
   +
Videos
   +
Research Papers
   +
Customer Data
   +
Software Code
   ↓
AI Training Dataset
   ↓
AI Model
```

Each source may have different legal rights and restrictions.

Therefore, AI governance must establish controls around:

* Data ownership.
* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* Terms of use.
* Permitted purposes.
* Attribution.
* Redistribution.
* Derivative works.
* Commercial use.
* Data retention.
* Third-party restrictions.

---

### 3. Copyright

Copyright generally protects original creative works.

Examples relevant to AI include:

* Books.
* Articles.
* Photographs.
* Illustrations.
* Music.
* Videos.
* Software code.
* Graphics.
* Databases or compilations where applicable.

The existence of copyright does not necessarily mean AI use is prohibited, but it means the organization needs to understand the applicable rights and legal basis.

---

### 4. Copyright vs Data Ownership

These concepts should not be treated as identical.

A dataset may contain information that is not itself protected by copyright while the **selection, arrangement, compilation, or underlying materials** may have legal protection.

Therefore:

```text
Data
  ≠
Automatically Copyright-Free
```

A governance assessment should determine what rights actually apply.

---

### 5. License

A license is permission to use a work under specified conditions.

For AI governance, a license may define:

* Who can use the data.
* What they can use it for.
* Whether commercial use is allowed.
* Whether modification is allowed.
* Whether redistribution is allowed.
* Whether attribution is required.
* Whether additional restrictions apply.

---

### 6. License Is Not Ownership

Receiving permission to use a dataset does not necessarily mean acquiring ownership of it.

For example:

```text
Licensor
   ↓
Grants Permission
   ↓
Organization
   ↓
Uses Dataset
```

The organization may have a **right to use** the data without owning the underlying intellectual property.

---

### 7. Publicly Available Data

One of the most important AI governance misconceptions is:

> **“It is on the internet, so we can use it.”**

That is not a sufficient governance determination.

Public availability and legal permission are separate questions.

```text
Publicly Accessible
        ↓
Can We Access It?
        ↓
Can We Legally Use It?
        ↓
For What Purpose?
        ↓
Under What Conditions?
```

---

### 8. Website Terms of Use

Websites may establish terms governing how their content may be accessed or used.

AI projects involving web data should therefore consider:

* Terms of service.
* API terms.
* Robots directives where relevant.
* Copyright notices.
* Licensing statements.
* Restrictions on automated access.
* Restrictions on commercial use.

---

### 9. Web Scraping and AI

Web scraping can create governance questions involving:

* Copyright.
* Contractual terms.
* Privacy.
* Database rights.
* Computer misuse laws.
* Rate limits.
* Authentication requirements.

Therefore:

> **Technical ability to scrape data does not establish legal permission to use it.**

---

### 10. Data Provenance

Every significant AI dataset should have identifiable provenance.

A basic lineage model is:

```text
Source
  ↓
Collection
  ↓
Transformation
  ↓
Storage
  ↓
AI Dataset
  ↓
Training / Evaluation
```

Provenance helps determine whether the organization has the necessary rights to use the data.

---

### 11. Data Provenance Questions

A GRC professional should be able to ask:

1. Where did the data come from?
2. Who provided it?
3. Who owns it?
4. Under what license was it obtained?
5. What restrictions apply?
6. Was it modified?
7. Can it be used commercially?
8. Can it be used for AI training?
9. Can the resulting model be commercialized?
10. Can the dataset be redistributed?

---

### 12. Data Inventory

Organizations should maintain an inventory of important AI datasets.

Example:

| Dataset   | Source      | License          | AI Training | Commercial Use | Owner        |
| --------- | ----------- | ---------------- | ----------- | -------------- | ------------ |
| Dataset A | Vendor      | Commercial       | Yes         | Yes            | Data Office  |
| Dataset B | Open Source | Specific License | Review      | Review         | Engineering  |
| Dataset C | Internal    | Corporate        | Yes         | Yes            | Data Owner   |
| Dataset D | Public Web  | Unclear          | Unknown     | Unknown        | Legal Review |

An inventory helps prevent unauthorized data use.

---

### 13. Dataset Classification

Licensing governance should be combined with data classification.

Possible categories include:

```text
Public
Internal
Confidential
Restricted
Third-Party Licensed
Regulated
```

Classification determines the level of governance and access control required.

---

### 14. License Metadata

A dataset catalog should capture license information.

Example:

```text
Dataset ID: AI-DATA-021
Source: External Provider
License: Commercial
Permitted Use: AI Development
Commercial Use: Yes
Redistribution: Prohibited
Attribution: Required
Expiration: 31 Dec 2027
Owner: Data Governance
```

This makes licensing conditions operational rather than merely legal documentation.

---

### 15. License Compatibility

AI systems may combine data from multiple sources.

For example:

```text
Dataset A
   +
Dataset B
   +
Dataset C
   ↓
Training Dataset
```

The organization should determine whether the applicable licenses are compatible with the intended combined use.

A license that permits one type of use may impose conditions that conflict with another dataset or downstream activity.

---

### 16. License Restrictions

Common restrictions may include:

* Non-commercial use only.
* Attribution required.
* No redistribution.
* No modification.
* Share-alike requirements.
* Geographic restrictions.
* Field-of-use restrictions.
* Time-limited access.
* Restrictions on sublicensing.

These restrictions should be captured before data enters an AI pipeline.

---

### 17. Commercial Use

An organization intending to commercialize an AI system should specifically assess whether its data licenses permit commercial use.

Example:

```text
Data License
     ↓
Non-Commercial Only
     ↓
Commercial AI Product
     ↓
Potential License Conflict
```

Commercialization should therefore trigger an appropriate legal and licensing review.

---

### 18. AI Training Rights

A license may permit general use of content but not clearly address AI training.

Where AI training is a material use, governance should determine whether:

* The license expressly permits it.
* The license is sufficiently broad.
* Additional permission is required.
* Legal review is necessary.

Do not assume that a general data-use license automatically covers every AI use case.

---

### 19. Fine-Tuning Data

The same governance principles apply to data used for:

* Fine-tuning.
* Instruction tuning.
* Preference optimization.
* Reinforcement learning.
* Retrieval-augmented generation.
* Evaluation.

The organization should understand the rights associated with each dataset.

---

### 20. Evaluation Data

Evaluation datasets are also governed assets.

For example:

```text
Training Data
     +
Validation Data
     +
Test Data
     +
Benchmark Data
```

The organization should verify the permitted use of each category.

---

### 21. Benchmark Datasets

Public benchmarks may have specific licenses or terms.

Before incorporating a benchmark into a commercial AI workflow, verify:

* License.
* Attribution.
* Redistribution rights.
* Commercial restrictions.
* Modification rights.
* Dataset-specific conditions.

---

### 22. Research Data

Research datasets may be subject to:

* Publisher restrictions.
* Institutional terms.
* Data-use agreements.
* Research licenses.
* Privacy requirements.
* Ethical restrictions.

Academic availability does not automatically mean unrestricted commercial AI use.

---

### 23. Open Data

Open data does not necessarily mean unrestricted data.

Different open-data licenses may impose different obligations.

Therefore:

```text
Open Data
   ≠
No Restrictions
```

The applicable license must still be reviewed.

---

### 24. Open-Source Data

Open-source concepts are commonly associated with software, but similar licensing principles can apply to datasets.

Organizations should distinguish between:

* Open access.
* Open license.
* Public domain.
* Free-to-use.
* Commercially licensed.
* Restricted access.

These terms should not be treated as interchangeable.

---

### 25. Public Domain

Public-domain works generally are not protected by copyright in the same way as copyrighted works.

However, governance should still consider:

* Jurisdiction.
* Database rights.
* Trademarks.
* Privacy.
* Contractual restrictions.
* Other applicable rights.

Therefore, public-domain status should not automatically end the entire legal assessment.

---

### 26. Creative Commons

Creative Commons licenses provide standardized permissions with different conditions.

Examples of conditions can include:

* Attribution.
* Non-commercial use.
* No derivatives.
* Share-alike.

An AI governance program should verify the **specific license version and conditions** rather than treating all Creative Commons material as equivalent.

---

### 27. Attribution

Some licenses require attribution.

A governance process should determine:

* What attribution is required.
* Where it must appear.
* What information must be included.
* Whether attribution must be preserved downstream.

Attribution requirements should be captured as part of dataset metadata.

---

### 28. Share-Alike Requirements

Some licenses require derivative works to be distributed under similar licensing conditions.

This can create complex questions when licensed material becomes part of:

* Training datasets.
* AI-generated outputs.
* Software.
* Commercial services.

Legal review may be appropriate when the licensing implications are unclear.

---

### 29. No-Derivatives Restrictions

Some licenses restrict modification or creation of derivative works.

AI processing may raise questions about whether a particular use constitutes a modification or derivative use under the applicable law and license.

Therefore, organizations should not make assumptions about license interpretation.

---

### 30. Dataset Redistribution

An organization may have permission to use a dataset internally without having permission to redistribute it.

Example:

```text
Vendor
  ↓
Licensed Dataset
  ↓
Internal AI Training
      ✓

Publish Dataset Publicly
      ✗
```

Distribution rights should therefore be separately assessed.

---

### 31. Model Distribution

Licensing questions can also arise when an AI model trained using third-party data is distributed.

Governance should consider:

```text
Licensed Data
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Model
      ↓
Model Distribution
```

The organization should determine whether the underlying licenses impose relevant downstream obligations.

---

### 32. Model Outputs

Data licensing can also become relevant to AI-generated outputs.

Governance questions include:

* Could the output reproduce protected material?
* Does the output contain substantial similarity to source content?
* What contractual restrictions apply?
* What organizational policies govern publication?

Output governance should therefore complement training-data governance.

---

### 33. Memorization and Copyright Risk

Generative AI systems may sometimes reproduce training examples or closely resemble source material.

A governance program should consider:

```text
Training Data
      ↓
Model Learning
      ↓
Generated Output
      ↓
Potential Reproduction
      ↓
Legal / IP Review
```

This is one reason output testing and monitoring can be important for certain AI applications.

---

### 34. Code and Software Licensing

AI systems may be trained or fine-tuned using source code.

Software licenses can impose specific conditions.

Potential license types include:

* Permissive licenses.
* Copyleft licenses.
* Commercial licenses.
* Proprietary licenses.

The organization should understand the relevant license obligations before incorporating generated or derived code into products.

---

### 35. AI Coding Assistants

Where AI coding tools are used, governance should address:

* Source-code provenance.
* Third-party code similarity.
* License obligations.
* Security.
* Intellectual property.
* Developer review.

AI-generated code should not automatically be assumed to be free from licensing concerns.

---

### 36. Image and Multimedia Data

The same principles apply to:

* Photographs.
* Illustrations.
* Videos.
* Audio.
* Graphics.
* Design assets.

An AI project should document the rights associated with multimedia training data.

---

### 37. Copyright Risk Register

Licensing risks should be recorded in the AI risk register.

Example:

| Risk                        | Impact      | Treatment            |
| --------------------------- | ----------- | -------------------- |
| Unlicensed training data    | High        | License review       |
| Unknown provenance          | High        | Block until verified |
| Attribution failure         | Medium      | Metadata + process   |
| Unauthorized redistribution | High        | Access controls      |
| Output reproduction         | Medium/High | Output testing       |

---

### 38. Legal Review Trigger

Legal review should be considered when:

* License terms are unclear.
* Data provenance is uncertain.
* Commercial use is planned.
* Dataset redistribution is planned.
* Third-party rights may be involved.
* AI-specific rights are ambiguous.
* The project involves high-value intellectual property.

---

### 39. GRC Control Objective

A useful control objective is:

> **Ensure that AI systems use data only where the organization has established appropriate rights, licenses, permissions, or other lawful authorization for the intended purpose.**

This can become a formal control within an AI governance framework.

---

### 40. Part 1 Summary

The foundational principle of **Data Licensing and Copyright** is:

> **AI governance must establish where data came from, what rights apply to it, what the license permits, and whether those permissions cover the organization's intended AI use.**

The governance chain is:

```text
DATA SOURCE
     ↓
PROVENANCE
     ↓
OWNERSHIP / RIGHTS
     ↓
LICENSE
     ↓
PERMITTED USE
     ↓
AI TRAINING / FINE-TUNING / TESTING
     ↓
MODEL / OUTPUT
     ↓
DISTRIBUTION / COMMERCIALIZATION
```

The key GRC mindset is:

> **“Accessible does not automatically mean authorized. Before using data in an AI system, I would establish provenance, identify the applicable rights and license, verify that the intended AI use is permitted, document restrictions, and escalate ambiguous or high-risk cases for legal review.”**

# 08.17 — Data Licensing and Copyright

## Part 2

### 41. Data Licensing Due Diligence

Before data is incorporated into an AI lifecycle, the organization should perform appropriate licensing due diligence.

A practical process is:

```text
Identify Dataset
      ↓
Identify Source
      ↓
Establish Provenance
      ↓
Identify Rights Holder
      ↓
Identify License / Permission
      ↓
Review Restrictions
      ↓
Match Against Intended AI Use
      ↓
Approve / Reject / Escalate
```

The objective is to ensure that the organization does not unknowingly introduce unlicensed or improperly sourced data into an AI system.

---

### 42. Dataset Provenance Record

Each material external dataset should have a provenance record.

A basic record may contain:

| Field            | Example            |
| ---------------- | ------------------ |
| Dataset ID       | AI-DATA-042        |
| Source           | External Provider  |
| Acquisition Date | 2026-08-01         |
| Rights Holder    | Identified         |
| License          | Commercial         |
| AI Training      | Permitted / Review |
| Commercial Use   | Permitted          |
| Redistribution   | Restricted         |
| Owner            | Data Governance    |
| Legal Review     | Completed          |

This creates an auditable trail.

---

### 43. Provenance Confidence

Not every dataset will have the same level of provenance certainty.

A useful classification is:

```text
Verified
   ↓
Documented
   ↓
Partially Verified
   ↓
Unknown
```

Datasets with unknown provenance should receive additional scrutiny before being used in material AI systems.

---

### 44. Unknown Data Provenance

A common governance red flag is:

> **“We don't know where the dataset came from.”**

This should not be treated as a minor documentation issue.

Unknown provenance can create uncertainty around:

* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* Privacy.
* Confidentiality.
* Intellectual property.
* Contractual restrictions.

---

### 45. Dataset Chain of Custody

Organizations should understand how data entered their environment.

```text id="dl401"

      ↓

      ↓
Acquisition
      ↓

      ↓
Transformation
      ↓
```

Each transition should be documented where material to the risk.

### 46. Data Acquisition Methods

Different acquisition methods create different governance considerations.

Examples include:

* Direct purchase.
* Vendor API.
* Public download.
* Internal database.
* Web collection.
* Data-sharing agreement.

The acquisition mechanism should be documented.

### 47. Directly Licensed Data

For example:

```text id="dl402"
Organization
  ↓
```

Even here, the organization should verify the exact scope of the license.

### 48. API-Based Data

Organizations may obtain data through APIs.

Governance should examine:

* API terms.
* Permitted uses.
* Storage rights.
* Caching restrictions.
* Retention.
* Redistribution.
* Rate limits.
* Commercial use.

The API itself does not necessarily grant unrestricted ownership of retrieved data.

---

### 49. Web-Collected Data

Data collected from websites requires additional consideration.

Potential issues include:

* Website terms.
* Copyright.
* Database rights.
* Privacy.
* Automated-access restrictions.
* Authentication controls.
* Geographic requirements.

A web collection process should therefore undergo appropriate legal and compliance review.

---

### 50. Data Scraping Governance

A controlled scraping process can include:


The goal is to ensure that technical collection is aligned with legal and governance requirements.

---

### 51. Robots.txt

Robots.txt files can communicate website preferences regarding automated crawlers.

However:

> **Robots.txt should not be treated as a universal legal authorization or prohibition mechanism.**

Organizations should consider it as one technical signal within a broader legal and governance assessment.

---

### 52. Terms of Service

Terms of service may contain restrictions concerning:

* Automated collection.
* Data reuse.
* Commercial use.
* Redistribution.
* Data mining.
* Account use.

AI teams should not assume that a website's content is unrestricted merely because it can be accessed through a browser.

---

### 53. Database Rights

In some jurisdictions, databases may receive legal protection separate from the copyright status of individual data elements.

Therefore, a governance review may need to consider:

```text
Individual Data
      +
Database Structure / Investment
      ↓
Potential Database Rights
```

The applicable jurisdiction should be considered.

---

### 54. Jurisdiction

Copyright and related rights can vary by jurisdiction.

An international AI system may involve:

```text
Source Country
      ↓
Processing Country
      ↓
Model Development Country
      ↓
Deployment Country
      ↓
Customer Country
```

Legal and licensing requirements may differ across these locations.

---

### 55. Cross-Border Data Licensing

Cross-border use can introduce additional considerations involving:

* Contractual restrictions.
* Data-transfer requirements.
* Local intellectual-property law.
* Privacy regulations.
* Data residency.
* Export restrictions where applicable.

GRC should coordinate with legal and privacy teams when these issues are material.

---

### 56. Internal Data

Internal organizational data can also be subject to intellectual-property or contractual restrictions.

Examples:

* Proprietary documents.
* Customer-provided information.
* Licensed third-party content.
* Vendor manuals.
* Commercial databases.

“Internal” does not necessarily mean “owned without restrictions.”

---

### 57. Customer-Provided Data

If customers provide data to an organization, the organization should determine:

* What rights the customer retains.
* What processing is authorized.
* Whether AI training is permitted.
* Whether secondary use is allowed.
* Whether data may be retained.
* Whether data may be used to improve a shared model.

Contractual language can be particularly important.

---

### 58. Employee-Provided Data

Organizations should similarly evaluate data supplied by employees.

Examples include:

* Documents.
* Code.
* Research.
* Designs.
* Communications.

Ownership and usage rights may depend on employment agreements, policies, jurisdiction, and applicable law.

---

### 59. Third-Party Licensed Content

An organization may legitimately possess content while lacking permission to use it for AI training.

Example:

```text id="dl403"
Organization Purchases Document
        ↓
Can Read Document
        ≠
Automatically Authorized to Train AI
```

The permitted purpose must be established.

---

### 60. License Scope

When reviewing a license, identify:

* **Who** may use the data.
* **What** may be done with it.
* **Where** it may be used.
* **When** it may be used.
* **How** it may be distributed.
* **For what purpose** it may be processed.

This is effectively a **license-scope matrix**.

---

### 61. License-Scope Matrix

Example:

| Requirement    | Status   |
| -------------- | -------- |
| Internal Use   | ✓        |
| AI Training    | ✓        |
| Fine-Tuning    | Review   |
| Commercial Use | ✓        |
| Redistribution | ✗        |
| Public Release | ✗        |
| Modification   | ✓        |
| Attribution    | Required |

This makes legal terms actionable for technical teams.

---

### 62. Field-of-Use Restrictions

Some licenses may limit the use of data to a specific field or purpose.

Example:

```text id="dl404"
Licensed For:
Research

Proposed Use:
Commercial AI Product
```

The proposed use may fall outside the licensed scope.

---

### 63. Time-Limited Licenses

Some licenses expire.

Example:

```text id="dl405"
License Valid
01 Jan 2026
      ↓
31 Dec 2026
```

If the organization continues using the dataset after expiration, it may create a governance issue depending on the agreement.

---

### 64. License Renewal

For time-limited datasets, governance should track:

* Expiration date.
* Renewal owner.
* Renewal decision.
* Continued use.
* Required deletion.

Automated reminders can prevent expired licenses from being overlooked.

---

### 65. Geographic Restrictions

Some agreements may restrict where data can be processed or used.

Example:

```text id="dl406"
License
   ↓
European Economic Area Only
```

An AI workload deployed globally may therefore require additional assessment.

---

### 66. User Restrictions

A license may limit access to specific categories of users.

For example:

```text id="dl407"
Licensed Users:
Employees

External Contractors:
Not Included
```

Access management should enforce the applicable license conditions.

---

### 67. Attribution Governance

Where attribution is required, the requirement should become an operational control.

```text id="dl408"

          ↓
Metadata Captures Attribution
          ↓
Publication Process
          ↓
Attribution Included
```

Attribution should not depend entirely on individual employee memory.

### 68. License Obligation Register

### 69. License Compliance

License compliance requires more than storing the license document.

The organization should verify:

```text
License Requirement
       ↓
Control
       ↓
Implementation
       ↓
Evidence
       ↓
Testing
```

This is where GRC becomes particularly important.

---

### 70. Control Example

**Requirement:**

Data may not be redistributed externally.

**Control:**

External downloads are restricted.

**Evidence:**

* IAM policy.
* DLP rule.
* Access logs.
* Distribution approvals.

**Testing:**

Attempt to download or share the dataset through controlled testing.

---

### 71. Copyright Compliance Control

A formal control could state:

> **AI training and development datasets must have documented provenance and an approved legal basis, license, permission, or other applicable authorization before being incorporated into controlled AI workflows.**

Possible evidence:

* Dataset inventory.
* License document.
* Legal approval.
* Data-use agreement.
* Provenance record.

---

### 72. License Compliance Monitoring

Monitoring may identify:

* Expired licenses.
* Unauthorized datasets.
* Unauthorized users.
* External sharing.
* Unapproved AI use.
* Missing attribution.

These findings can feed into the GRC risk register.

### 74. AI Dataset Approval Gate

Before a dataset enters training:

```text id="dl410"

      ↓

      ↓
```

### 75. Exception Management

Sometimes a business may need to use a dataset despite an unresolved licensing issue.

This should be handled through a formal exception process.

```text id="dl411"
Exception Requested
       ↓
Risk Assessed
       ↓
Compensating Controls
       ↓
Risk Acceptance
       ↓
Time-Limited Approval
       ↓

```

Exceptions should not become permanent workarounds.

---

### 76. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance should be performed by an authorized risk owner.

The AI team should not necessarily be able to self-approve a material copyright or licensing risk.

Governance should establish:

* Approval authority.
* Risk thresholds.
* Documentation.
* Expiration.
* Review requirements.

---

### 77. Legal Hold

Where litigation or a formal investigation exists, relevant data may need to be preserved.

Therefore, normal deletion processes may need to be suspended according to applicable legal requirements.

This should be coordinated with legal counsel.

---

### 78. Data Retention vs License Retention

Two different concepts should be distinguished:

**Data retention**

How long the organization keeps the dataset.

**License documentation retention**

How long the organization keeps evidence of the rights and permissions associated with the dataset.

Even after deleting a dataset, retaining appropriate licensing records may be necessary for audit or contractual evidence.

---

```text id="dl412"
      ↓
```

The legal treatment of embeddings can depend on the specific circumstances and jurisdiction.

---

### 84. RAG Systems

Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems can introduce licensing considerations even without conventional model training.

```text id="dl416"

      ↓
Vector Database
      ↓
Retriever
      ↓
LLM
      ↓
Response
```

The organization should verify that the retrieval, processing, storage, and output use are consistent with applicable rights and agreements.

---

### 85. Fine-Tuning and RAG Distinction

These should not be treated as identical:

**Fine-tuning**

Data influences model parameters.

**RAG**

Data is typically retrieved at runtime and supplied as context.

Both can involve licensing questions, but the relevant contractual and legal analysis may differ.

* Data portability.
* Audit rights.
* Warranty of authority.
* Indemnification where appropriate.

Legal counsel should determine appropriate language.

### 91. Indemnification

### 99. GRC Interview Answer

If asked:

**“How would you govern copyright and licensing for AI data?”**

# 08.17 — Data Licensing and Copyright

## Part 3

### 101. Copyright and AI Training

The use of copyrighted material to train AI systems is one of the most complex areas of AI governance.

The governance question should not simply be:

> **“Is the data copyrighted?”**

Instead, the organization should examine:

```text
Copyright Status
      ↓
Source
      ↓
Acquisition Method
      ↓
License / Permission
      ↓
Intended AI Processing
      ↓
Jurisdiction
      ↓
Commercial Purpose
      ↓
Legal / Compliance Assessment
```

The applicable legal analysis can vary significantly by jurisdiction and by the facts of the use case.

---

### 102. Copyrighted Does Not Automatically Mean Prohibited

Copyright protection does not mean that every use of the material is automatically unlawful.

Depending on the jurisdiction and circumstances, there may be:

* Licensed use.
* Statutory exceptions.
* Limitations and exceptions.
* Fair-use or fair-dealing doctrines.
* Other legal bases.

However, organizations should not assume that an exception applies merely because data is being used for AI.

---

### 103. Fair Use and Fair Dealing

Different jurisdictions use different legal concepts.

For example, some jurisdictions recognize **fair use**, while others use **fair dealing** or specific statutory exceptions.

Therefore:

```text
AI Use
  ↓
Applicable Jurisdiction
  ↓
Applicable Legal Doctrine
  ↓
Fact-Specific Assessment
```

This is an area where Legal should determine the organization's position when material risk exists.

---

### 104. Purpose of AI Processing

The purpose of processing can influence the legal analysis.

Potential purposes include:

* Research.
* Internal experimentation.
* Product development.
* Commercial AI services.
* Security testing.
* Education.
* Model evaluation.

The same dataset may present different governance considerations depending on its intended use.

---

### 105. Commercial vs Non-Commercial Use

A non-commercial research project and a commercial AI product may have different licensing implications.

For example:

```text id="q4av1h"
Dataset
  ↓
Research Prototype
  ↓
Commercial Product
```

The transition to commercial deployment should trigger a licensing reassessment.

---

### 106. Training vs Inference

Organizations should distinguish between:

**Training**

Data is used to develop or modify model parameters.

**Inference**

Data is submitted to an existing model to produce an output.

Both can involve legal and contractual considerations, but the relevant rights and obligations may differ.

---

### 107. Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning introduces another use of third-party content.

```text id="j7x6nq"
Base Model
    +
Licensed Dataset
    ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
```

The organization should verify that the dataset license and other applicable rights support the intended fine-tuning activity.

---

### 108. Retrieval-Augmented Generation

RAG can use copyrighted or licensed documents without necessarily training the model on them.

```text id="8y9l0n"
Licensed Content
      ↓
Document Repository
      ↓
Retriever
      ↓
LLM Context
      ↓
Generated Response
```

Governance should still evaluate the rights to:

* Store.
* Process.
* Retrieve.
* Display.
* Share.

---

### 109. AI Outputs

Copyright governance should not stop at the training dataset.

Organizations should also consider outputs.

Potential issues include:

* Reproduction of protected material.
* Substantial similarity.
* Third-party trademarks.
* Confidential content.
* Licensed content appearing in outputs.

---

### 110. Output Similarity Testing

For higher-risk applications, organizations can establish testing for potential reproduction.

Example:

```text id="c3i3sn"
Prompt
  ↓
AI Output
  ↓
Similarity Check
  ↓
Potential Protected Content?
  ↓
Review / Block / Escalate
```

This is particularly relevant for systems designed to generate content at scale.

---

### 111. Memorization Risk

Large models can sometimes memorize portions of their training data.

Potential governance risks include:

* Verbatim reproduction.
* Near-verbatim reproduction.
* Leakage of proprietary information.
* Leakage of licensed content.

Testing should be proportionate to the AI system's risk.

---

### 112. Membership Inference vs Copyright

These concepts should be distinguished.

**Membership inference**

Attempts to determine whether particular information or records were included in training.

**Copyright/output reproduction**

Concerns whether generated output reproduces protected content or otherwise creates an IP issue.

They can overlap but are not the same risk.

---

### 113. Data Leakage

If an organization trains a model using proprietary content, there is a potential concern that sensitive information could appear in outputs.

Therefore:

```text id="2n7a0v"
Proprietary Data
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Model
      ↓
User Prompt
      ↓
Output
      ↓
Potential Disclosure
```

Security, privacy, and IP governance should work together.

---

### 114. Confidentiality vs Copyright

Confidentiality and copyright are different legal concepts.

A document may be:

* Copyrighted.
* Confidential.
* Both.
* Neither.

Governance should therefore avoid treating copyright review as a substitute for confidentiality review.

---

### 115. Trade Secrets

Trade-secret information may require strong protection even if copyright is not the primary concern.

Examples may include:

* Proprietary algorithms.
* Manufacturing processes.
* Business strategies.
* Internal methodologies.
* Customer lists.

Using such information in an AI system can create significant governance risks.

---

### 116. Proprietary Enterprise Data

Enterprise AI projects may use:

* Internal procedures.
* Contracts.
* Engineering documents.
* Technical manuals.
* Source code.
* Business reports.

The organization should establish whether it owns the material and whether third-party restrictions apply.

---

### 117. Third-Party Content Inside Internal Documents

An internal document may contain content licensed from another party.

Example:

```text id="k0m2xq"
Internal Report
   +
Third-Party Images
   +
Licensed Market Data
   ↓
AI Knowledge Base
```

Internal possession does not necessarily grant unrestricted AI-use rights.

---

### 118. Contractual Restrictions

Contracts can restrict data use even where copyright is not the central issue.

Examples:

* “Internal use only.”
* “No automated processing.”
* “No resale.”
* “No derivative use.”
* “No model training.”

Contractual restrictions should be captured in the AI data governance process.

---

### 119. Terms vs Copyright

An important GRC distinction is:

```text
Copyright
    ≠
Contract
    ≠
Privacy Law
    ≠
Database Rights
```

A dataset can raise several independent legal questions simultaneously.

---

### 120. Data Rights Matrix

A useful governance artifact is a data-rights matrix.

| Dataset | Copyright | Contract | Privacy | Database Rights | AI Use     |
| ------- | --------- | -------- | ------- | --------------- | ---------- |
| A       | Yes       | Yes      | No      | Possible        | Review     |
| B       | No        | Yes      | Yes     | No              | Restricted |
| C       | Yes       | No       | No      | No              | Licensed   |
| D       | Unknown   | Unknown  | Unknown | Unknown         | Escalate   |

This prevents teams from reducing all legal analysis to copyright alone.

---

### 121. Rights Stack

For complex datasets, governance can think in terms of a **rights stack**:

```text
Copyright
   +
Database Rights
   +
Contract
   +
Privacy
   +
Confidentiality
   +
Trademark
   +
Other Applicable Rights
```

The organization must understand the relevant layers before using the data.

---

### 122. Trademark Considerations

AI datasets may contain:

* Company names.
* Logos.
* Brand identifiers.
* Product names.

Trademark concerns are distinct from copyright.

If AI outputs are commercially distributed, trademark considerations may become relevant.

---

### 123. Personality / Publicity Rights

Images, voices, names, or likenesses of individuals can raise additional rights issues depending on jurisdiction.

For example:

```text id="p7y8n4"
Person's Image
      ↓
AI Training
      ↓
Generated Image
      ↓
Commercial Publication
```

This may require legal review beyond copyright analysis.

---

### 124. Voice Data

AI systems involving speech or voice cloning can introduce additional concerns involving:

* Copyright.
* Personality rights.
* Publicity rights.
* Contractual rights.
* Consent.

Organizations should therefore conduct broader rights assessments.

---

### 125. Image Generation

Image-generation systems can involve multiple rights layers:

```text id="0k7w1c"
Training Images
      ↓
Model
      ↓
Generated Image
      ↓
Commercial Use
```

Governance should consider both the training-data question and the downstream-output question.

---

### 126. Music and Audio

Music datasets can involve multiple rights, including:

* Composition rights.
* Sound recording rights.
* Performance rights.
* Licensing agreements.

AI projects using music therefore require appropriate rights analysis.

---

### 127. Video Data

Video may contain:

* Copyrighted audiovisual works.
* Music.
* Images.
* Performances.
* Personal likenesses.
* Trademarks.

A single video dataset can therefore contain multiple rights layers.

---

### 128. Software Code

Code introduces a particularly important licensing dimension.

A repository may contain code under:

* MIT.
* Apache.
* BSD.
* GPL.
* LGPL.
* Proprietary licenses.
* Other terms.

The organization should evaluate the applicable license before incorporating generated or derived code into products.

---

### 129. Permissive vs Copyleft Licenses

Broadly:

**Permissive licenses**

Often impose relatively limited conditions, such as attribution or preservation of notices.

**Copyleft licenses**

May impose additional conditions on distribution of certain derivative works.

The exact obligations depend on the specific license and circumstances.

---

### 130. AI-Generated Code Governance

A secure AI coding process may include:

```text
AI-Generated Code
       ↓
Security Scan
       ↓
License / Similarity Check
       ↓
Developer Review
       ↓
Repository Approval
       ↓
Production
```

This integrates IP governance with secure software development.

---

### 131. Data License Compatibility

When combining datasets:

```text id="f0f3ra"
Dataset A
   +
Dataset B
   +
Dataset C
   ↓
Combined Dataset
```

The organization should assess whether the resulting use remains compatible with all applicable licensing conditions.

---

### 132. License Inheritance

Some licenses may impose downstream obligations under particular circumstances.

Therefore, when datasets are transformed or combined, the organization should determine:

* Which obligations remain.
* Which obligations apply only to certain components.
* Whether distribution triggers additional conditions.

Legal interpretation may be required.

---

### 133. Dataset Contamination

A training dataset may accidentally contain content that the organization did not intend to include.

Example:

```text id="5e4c0h"
Approved Data
     +
Accidental Third-Party Content
     ↓
Training Dataset
```

Automated and manual data-quality processes can help identify unexpected content.

---

### 134. Data Scanning

Organizations can use automated scanning to identify:

* Known restricted datasets.
* Copyright notices.
* License files.
* Source-code repositories.
* Proprietary identifiers.
* Sensitive information.

Automation should support—not replace—legal assessment.

---

### 135. License Detection

For software repositories, license-detection tools can help identify declared licenses.

For general datasets, organizations may use metadata and source records.

However:

> **Automated license detection is evidence, not definitive legal interpretation.**

---

### 136. Content Provenance

Provenance should ideally be preserved through transformations.

Example:

```text id="j4q7r0"
Original Source
      ↓
Extracted Data
      ↓
Cleaned Data
      ↓
Filtered Data
      ↓
Training Dataset
```

Each stage should retain sufficient metadata to reconstruct lineage.

---

### 137. Data Lineage

A mature AI platform should be able to answer:

> “Which datasets contributed to this model?”

Example:

```text id="8g6m3w"
Model M-017
    ↓
Training Run TR-221
    ↓
Dataset D-042
    ↓
Dataset D-042 Sources
    ↓
Original Providers
```

This is critical for audit and remediation.

---

### 138. Model Retraining

If a dataset is later determined to be improperly licensed, governance should consider whether affected models require:

* Retraining.
* Replacement.
* Restricted deployment.
* Additional mitigation.
* Legal assessment.

This is a difficult operational problem and should be addressed in advance for material AI systems.

---

### 139. Data Removal Requests

Where an organization receives a valid request or legal requirement concerning particular content, it may need to determine:

* Where the content exists.
* Which datasets contain it.
* Which models were trained on it.
* Whether it appears in outputs.
* What remediation is technically possible.

The exact legal obligation depends on the applicable law and circumstances.

---

### 140. Machine Unlearning

Some AI architectures support techniques intended to remove the influence of particular data.

Conceptually:

```text id="4w2m8s"
Original Training Data
        ↓
Model
        ↓
Removal Requirement
        ↓
Unlearning / Retraining
        ↓
Updated Model
```

However, unlearning capabilities and legal requirements are separate questions.

---

### 141. Data Deletion vs Model Deletion

Deleting a dataset does not necessarily mean that information has been removed from a trained model.

```text id="9b3k1x"
Delete Dataset
      ≠
Automatically Delete Model Knowledge
```

This distinction is important for AI governance and incident response.

---

### 142. Governance Implication

If licensing rights are withdrawn, the organization should have a documented process for determining the impact on:

* Source datasets.
* Derived datasets.
* Training runs.
* Models.
* Applications.
* Outputs.
* Backups.

---

### 143. Copyright Risk Assessment

A practical assessment can score:

| Factor          | Low      | Medium    | High    |
| --------------- | -------- | --------- | ------- |
| Provenance      | Verified | Partial   | Unknown |
| License         | Clear    | Ambiguous | None    |
| Commercial Use  | No       | Possible  | Yes     |
| Distribution    | Internal | Limited   | Public  |
| Output Exposure | Low      | Medium    | High    |

Higher-risk combinations should trigger enhanced review.

---

### 144. Three-Line Governance Test

A simple GRC test is:

```text id="7c5x2k"
WHERE did the data come from?
WHAT rights do we have?
HOW are we using it?
```

If any answer is unclear, the dataset should receive additional review.

---

### 145. Four-Line Governance Test

For higher-risk AI systems, extend it to:

```text id="4y7q2v"
WHERE did it come from?
WHO owns / controls the rights?
WHAT does the license permit?
HOW will the AI system use it?
```

This provides a simple interview-ready framework.

---

### 146. Copyright Risk Treatment

Possible treatments include:

* Obtain a license.
* Obtain explicit permission.
* Replace the dataset.
* Restrict the use case.
* Remove problematic content.
* Apply technical controls.
* Seek legal advice.
* Accept the risk through authorized governance.

The appropriate treatment depends on the actual legal and business circumstances.

---

### 147. Avoidance

If licensing cannot be established and the dataset is not essential, the lowest-risk option may be to avoid using it.

```text id="9f0p2h"
Unknown Rights
      ↓
Can We Replace Dataset?
      ↓
YES
      ↓
Use Lower-Risk Alternative
```

Risk avoidance can be more efficient than attempting to remediate an uncertain dataset later.

---

### 148. Risk Reduction

Where the dataset is necessary, controls may reduce exposure.

Examples:

* Limit access.
* Restrict use to research.
* Prevent redistribution.
* Remove restricted content.
* Use a licensed alternative.
* Implement output controls.

These controls do not necessarily resolve an underlying legal prohibition, so legal review remains important where appropriate.

---

### 149. Risk Transfer

Organizations may use contractual mechanisms such as:

* Vendor warranties.
* Representations.
* Indemnification.
* Insurance.

These mechanisms can help manage residual commercial risk but should not replace due diligence and compliance controls.

---

### 150. Part 3 Summary

The key lesson of Part 3 is:

> **Copyright is only one component of AI data rights. Effective governance must consider copyright, licensing, contracts, confidentiality, database rights, privacy, trademarks, personality rights, and other applicable legal restrictions.**

The complete decision path is:

```text id="k3d8x2"
DATA
 ↓
PROVENANCE
 ↓
RIGHTS
 ↓
LICENSE / CONTRACT
 ↓
INTENDED AI USE
 ↓
JURISDICTION
 ↓
RISK ASSESSMENT
 ↓
CONTROL / LICENSE / PERMISSION
 ↓
APPROVAL
 ↓
MONITORING
```

For a GRC interview, the key statement is:

> **“I would not treat copyright as a binary yes-or-no question. I would establish provenance, identify the rights and contractual restrictions, determine the intended AI use and jurisdiction, assess the risk, and ensure that the organization has an appropriate license, permission, legal basis, or approved risk treatment before the data enters the AI lifecycle.”**

### 189. Licensing Governance Maturity

**Level 1 — Ad Hoc**

Teams independently acquire and use data.

**Level 2 — Defined**

Policies and basic ownership exist.

**Level 3 — Controlled**

Dataset registration, licensing review, and approval gates exist.

**Level 4 — Measured**

Compliance metrics, control testing, and risk reporting exist.

**Level 5 — Optimized**

Licensing metadata, lineage, policy-as-code, and AI pipelines are integrated.

### 197. Example: Enterprise AI Project

Suppose an organization wants to build an AI assistant using thousands of internal and third-party documents.

A GRC approach would be:

**Step 1 — Identify sources**

Determine which documents are internal and which originate from third parties.

**Step 2 — Establish rights**

Determine ownership, licensing, contractual restrictions, and permitted AI use.

**Step 3 — Classify**

Determine confidentiality and licensing sensitivity.

**Step 4 — Assess**

Evaluate copyright, privacy, security, and contractual risk.

**Step 5 — Control**

Implement access, storage, retention, and distribution controls.

**Step 6 — Approve**

Obtain appropriate business, Legal, Privacy, and Security approvals.

**Step 7 — Monitor**

Track changes, access, license expiration, and downstream use.

### 199. Interview-Ready Answer

If asked:

**“How would you establish data licensing governance for an enterprise AI program?”**

A strong answer is:

> **“I would start with provenance and establish what data we are using, where it came from, who owns or controls the rights, and what license or contractual terms apply. Then I would map those permissions against the intended AI use—training, fine-tuning, RAG, evaluation, commercial deployment, or redistribution. I would document the result in the data catalog, assign ownership, establish approval gates, monitor license expiration and changes, and integrate the requirements into the AI and data pipelines where possible. From a GRC perspective, I would also establish controls, collect evidence, perform periodic testing, manage exceptions, and maintain traceability from the AI system back to the underlying datasets.”**

### 200. Part 4 Summary

**Data Licensing and Copyright** should ultimately be governed as a **lifecycle and enterprise-risk process**, not simply as a Legal checklist.

The complete governance model is:

```text
DATA SOURCE
     ↓
PROVENANCE
     ↓
OWNERSHIP / RIGHTS
     ↓
LICENSE / CONTRACT
     ↓
PERMITTED AI USE
     ↓
RISK ASSESSMENT
     ↓
APPROVAL
     ↓
TECHNICAL ENFORCEMENT
     ↓
MODEL / APPLICATION
     ↓
MONITORING
     ↓
REASSESSMENT
     ↓
AUDIT / EVIDENCE
```

The most important GRC principle is:

> **“I need to know not only whether we can access the data, but whether we are authorized to use it for the specific AI purpose, what restrictions apply, how those restrictions are enforced, and how we can demonstrate compliance.”**

### Final Interview Anchor

**Remember:**

> **PROVENANCE → RIGHTS → LICENSE → PURPOSE → RISK → CONTROL → EVIDENCE**

That sequence gives you a practical GRC framework for answering almost any question about **AI data licensing, copyright, intellectual property, and third-party data governance**.
