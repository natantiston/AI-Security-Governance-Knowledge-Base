# 08.15 — Data Retention and Data Minimization

## Part 1

### 1. Data Retention and Data Minimization Foundations

Data retention and data minimization are governance principles that determine:

* **What data an AI system should keep**
* **How much data it should collect or process**
* **How long the data should be retained**
* **When the data should be deleted or anonymized**

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI systems should process and retain only the data that is necessary for an authorized and defined purpose, for no longer than necessary.**

---

### 2. Why Data Minimization Matters in AI

AI systems can create significant amounts of data during their operation.

For example:

```text id="dm01"
User Input
   ↓
Prompt
   ↓
AI Processing
   ↓
Retrieved Data
   ↓
Model Output
   ↓
Conversation History
   ↓
Logs
   ↓
Analytics
   ↓
Backups
```

If every component retains information indefinitely, the organization's data exposure increases.

---

### 3. The Data Minimization Principle

Data minimization means limiting data to what is:

1. Necessary.
2. Relevant.
3. Proportionate.
4. Appropriate for the defined purpose.

For example:

```text id="dm02"
Business Purpose:
Customer Support

Required:
Customer ID
Issue Category
Relevant Case Information

Not Required:
Entire Customer History
Unrelated Financial Records
Unnecessary Personal Information
```

---

### 4. Data Minimization vs Data Deletion

These are related but different concepts.

**Data minimization**

> Prevent unnecessary data from being collected or processed in the first place.

**Data deletion**

> Remove data that is no longer required.

The preferred approach is:

```text id="dm03"
Collect Less
     ↓
Process Less
     ↓
Retain Less
     ↓
Delete When No Longer Required
```

---

### 5. Data Retention

Data retention defines the period for which information remains available.

A retention policy should establish:

* What data is retained.
* Why it is retained.
* Where it is retained.
* Who can access it.
* How long it is retained.
* When it must be deleted.
* What happens when retention expires.

---

### 6. AI Data Retention Scope

Retention governance should consider more than the original dataset.

AI environments may retain:

* Training datasets.
* Validation datasets.
* Test datasets.
* Prompts.
* Model outputs.
* Conversation histories.
* RAG documents.
* Embeddings.
* Vector-store records.
* Agent memory.
* Application logs.
* Audit logs.
* Security logs.
* Backups.
* Temporary files.
* Cached information.

---

### 7. Retention Lifecycle

A basic lifecycle is:

```text id="dm04"
Collect
   ↓
Use
   ↓
Store
   ↓
Retain
   ↓
Review
   ↓
Archive / Delete
```

Retention should be an intentional decision rather than the default result of indefinite storage.

---

### 8. Purpose Limitation

Data should be retained for a defined purpose.

For example:

```text id="dm05"
Purpose:
Fraud Detection

Data:
Transaction Information

Retention:
Defined by Business + Legal + Regulatory Requirements
```

The organization should be able to explain why the data continues to exist.

---

### 9. Purpose-Based Retention

Different purposes may require different retention periods.

Example:

| Data                      | Purpose             | Retention                  |
| ------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------- |
| AI prompts                | Service operation   | Defined operational period |
| Security logs             | Security monitoring | Defined security period    |
| Training dataset          | Model development   | Based on model lifecycle   |
| Temporary processing data | Processing only     | Delete after processing    |
| Audit evidence            | Assurance           | Defined audit requirement  |

The actual period should be established by applicable business, legal, regulatory, contractual, and security requirements.

---

### 10. Retention Schedule

An enterprise retention schedule should define approved periods for different categories of information.

Example:

```text id="dm06"
Data Category
     ↓
Business Purpose
     ↓
Retention Requirement
     ↓
Retention Period
     ↓
Disposal Method
```

This creates an auditable retention decision.

---

### 11. Retention Ownership

Retention decisions should have accountable owners.

Potential responsibilities include:

* Data Owner.
* Records Management.
* Legal.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* AI Governance.
* Business Owner.

No single technical team should necessarily determine retention requirements independently.

---

### 12. Data Owner Responsibilities

The data owner should help determine:

* Business purpose.
* Required data.
* Authorized users.
* Retention requirements.
* Appropriate disposal conditions.

The owner should also review whether continued retention remains justified.

---

### 13. Legal and Regulatory Requirements

Retention requirements may be influenced by:

* Laws.
* Regulations.
* Court requirements.
* Contracts.
* Industry obligations.
* Records-management policies.
* Security requirements.

An organization should therefore avoid establishing retention periods based solely on technical convenience.

---

### 14. Privacy and Retention

Where personal information is involved, retention should be consistent with applicable privacy requirements.

The governance question is:

> **Why does the organization still need this personal information?**

If the original purpose has ended and no valid retention requirement applies, continued retention should be reassessed.

---

### 15. Security and Retention

Security risk generally increases as the quantity and duration of stored sensitive information increase.

Conceptually:

```text id="dm07"
More Data
   +
Longer Retention
   +
More Copies
   =
Larger Exposure
```

Minimization can therefore reduce the organization's attack surface.

---

### 16. Attack Surface Reduction

Reducing unnecessary AI data can reduce:

* Potential breach impact.
* Unauthorized-access exposure.
* Insider risk.
* DLP exposure.
* Storage exposure.
* Backup exposure.
* Third-party exposure.

Data minimization is therefore both a **privacy principle and a security control strategy**.

---

### 17. Data Inventory for Retention

Retention governance requires visibility into where data exists.

An AI data inventory may identify:

```text id="dm08"
Dataset
   ↓
Repository
   ↓
Copies
   ↓
AI Systems
   ↓
Logs
   ↓
Backups
   ↓
Archives
```

Without an inventory, deletion and retention enforcement become difficult.

---

### 18. AI Data Discovery

Data discovery can identify:

* Sensitive information.
* Personal information.
* Duplicated datasets.
* Old datasets.
* Unused datasets.
* Unclassified information.
* Data outside approved repositories.

Discovery helps identify unnecessary retained information.

---

### 19. Data Classification and Retention

Classification should influence retention requirements.

For example:

```text id="dm09"
Public
→ Lower Security Risk

Internal
→ Controlled Retention

Confidential
→ Enhanced Retention Governance

Restricted
→ Strict Retention + Disposal Controls
```

Classification alone does not determine the retention period, but it can influence the required governance and security controls.

---

### 20. Data Lifecycle Management

AI data should have an explicit lifecycle:

```text id="dm10"
Creation
   ↓
Collection
   ↓
Processing
   ↓
Active Use
   ↓
Retention
   ↓
Archive
   ↓
Deletion
```

Each stage should have defined ownership and controls.

---

### 21. Active Data

Active data is currently required for an approved business or AI purpose.

Examples:

* Current training datasets.
* Active RAG knowledge.
* Current evaluation datasets.
* Operational inference data.

Active data requires appropriate security and access controls.

---

### 22. Inactive Data

Inactive data is no longer actively used but may still have a valid retention requirement.

Examples:

* Historical audit records.
* Archived model-training datasets.
* Historical evaluation results.
* Required business records.

Inactive data should not automatically remain in highly accessible production environments.

---

### 23. Archived Data

Archiving moves information from active systems into controlled long-term storage.

The objective may be:

* Reduce production storage.
* Preserve required records.
* Reduce active attack surface.
* Maintain historical evidence.

Archived data still requires appropriate protection.

---

### 24. Data Deletion

Deletion should occur when:

* The purpose ends.
* Retention expires.
* The dataset becomes obsolete.
* The information is no longer required.
* A valid governance requirement requires removal.

Deletion should be authorized and traceable.

---

### 25. Secure Deletion

Deleting a database record does not necessarily mean all copies are gone.

Potential copies may exist in:

```text id="dm11"
Primary Storage
      ↓
Replica
      ↓
Cache
      ↓
Backup
      ↓
Log
      ↓
Archive
      ↓
Local Copy
```

Retention governance must therefore consider the complete data environment.

---

### 26. Deletion Verification

A mature process should verify that deletion occurred.

Evidence may include:

* Dataset identifier.
* Deletion date.
* Authorized requester.
* Deletion system.
* Deletion status.
* Affected repositories.

Where appropriate, deletion verification should include dependent systems.

---

### 27. Retention Exceptions

Exceptions may be required when data must be retained beyond its normal period.

Examples include:

* Legal hold.
* Active investigation.
* Regulatory requirement.
* Litigation.
* Security investigation.
* Business continuity requirement.

Exceptions should be formally documented.

---

### 28. Legal Hold

A legal hold can suspend normal deletion requirements for relevant information.

Conceptually:

```text id="dm12"
Retention Expired
       ↓
Legal Hold Active
       ↓
Deletion Suspended
       ↓
Hold Released
       ↓
Normal Disposal Resumes
```

The hold should be appropriately documented and controlled.

---

### 29. Security Investigation Hold

Security investigations may also require temporary preservation.

For example:

```text id="dm13"
Suspicious AI Activity
       ↓
Investigation
       ↓
Relevant Logs / Data Preserved
       ↓
Investigation Completed
       ↓
Retention Reassessed
```

Preservation should be limited to information relevant to the investigation.

---

### 30. Data Minimization in AI Development

AI development teams often collect more data than necessary because storage is relatively inexpensive.

This can lead to:

* Excessive personal information.
* Unnecessary sensitive data.
* Larger training datasets.
* Greater breach impact.
* More complex governance.

Development teams should therefore justify the data they require.

---

### 31. Minimum Necessary Dataset

The preferred design is:

```text id="dm14"
Business Objective
       ↓
Required Information
       ↓
Minimum Dataset
       ↓
AI Processing
```

Rather than:

```text id="dm15"
Business Objective
       ↓
Collect Everything
       ↓
Determine Later
```

---

### 32. Data Field Minimization

Minimization can occur at the field level.

Example:

```text id="dm16"
Original Dataset:
Name
Address
Email
Phone
Date of Birth
Passport Number
Customer ID
Transaction History

AI Requirement:
Customer ID
Transaction Category
Transaction Amount
```

Unnecessary fields should not be included merely because they are available.

---

### 33. Record-Level Minimization

Minimization can also occur at the record level.

For example, an AI system may need:

```text id="dm17"
Recent Transactions
```

rather than:

```text id="dm18"
Entire Historical Customer Record
```

The required scope should be determined by the AI use case.

---

### 34. Temporal Minimization

Data minimization can include limiting the time period.

Example:

```text id="dm19"
Required:
Last 90 Days

Available:
10 Years
```

If historical information is unnecessary, only the required period should be processed.

---

### 35. Geographic Minimization

AI systems may not require information from every geographic region.

For example:

```text id="dm20"
Business Scope:
Spain

Dataset:
Global Customer Population
```

If global data is unnecessary, geographic scope can be reduced.

---

### 36. User-Scope Minimization

Access should also be minimized.

For example:

```text id="dm21"
AI Application
      ↓
Needs:
Customer Support Team Data

Should Not Automatically Receive:
Entire Enterprise Customer Database
```

This combines data minimization with least privilege.

---

### 37. Query-Level Minimization

AI systems should retrieve only the information necessary to answer a request.

Instead of:

```text id="dm22"
Retrieve Entire Customer Record
```

prefer:

```text id="dm23"
Retrieve:
Customer's Open Support Case
```

This reduces unnecessary exposure.

---

### 38. RAG Data Minimization

RAG systems should minimize retrieved context.

Potential controls include:

* Relevant-document filtering.
* Metadata filtering.
* User authorization.
* Query scoping.
* Top-k limits.
* Attribute filtering.

The objective is:

> **Retrieve enough information to perform the task, but no more than necessary.**

---

### 39. Agent Data Minimization

AI agents may call multiple tools.

A secure agent should receive only the data required for its current task.

Example:

```text id="dm24"
Task:
Schedule Customer Appointment

Required:
Customer Name
Appointment Preferences

Not Required:
Full Financial Profile
Complete Medical History
Unrelated Account Data
```

---

### 40. Prompt Data Minimization

Users should avoid placing unnecessary sensitive information into prompts.

For example, if an AI system only needs to classify an issue:

```text id="dm25"
Use:
Issue Category
Relevant Description

Avoid:
Unnecessary Customer Identity Information
```

Prompt minimization can reduce downstream exposure.

---

### 41. Output Data Minimization

Minimization should also apply to AI outputs.

The system should avoid returning information that is not necessary for the user's task.

Example:

```text id="dm26"
Request:
"Show me the customer's open issue."

Appropriate:
Open issue details

Unnecessary:
Full customer profile
```

---

### 42. Log Data Minimization

AI logs can become an overlooked source of excessive data retention.

Logging may capture:

* Prompts.
* Outputs.
* Retrieved documents.
* User identities.
* API requests.
* Error messages.

Organizations should determine what information is genuinely necessary to log.

---

### 43. Sensitive Data in Logs

Sensitive information should not automatically be written to logs.

Potential controls include:

* Redaction.
* Masking.
* Tokenization.
* Field filtering.
* Structured logging.
* Restricted access.

Example:

```text id="dm27"
Original:
Credit Card = 4111 1111 1111 1111

Log:
Credit Card = ************1111
```

---

### 44. Debug Logging

Debug logging can create additional retention risk.

A development configuration might capture detailed prompts and outputs that should never be retained in production.

Production logging should therefore be deliberately configured.

---

### 45. Error Messages

Error messages should avoid exposing unnecessary data.

For example:

```text id="dm28"
Poor:
Database query failed:
SELECT * FROM Customer WHERE SSN='...'

Better:
Customer lookup failed.
```

Error-handling design is part of data minimization.

---

### 46. Telemetry Minimization

AI platforms may collect telemetry for:

* Performance.
* Reliability.
* Usage analytics.
* Security.

Telemetry should be reviewed to determine whether sensitive information is unnecessarily captured.

---

### 47. Analytics Data Minimization

Analytics teams may request large volumes of AI interaction data.

Before approving access, determine:

* Which fields are required.
* Which records are required.
* How long they are needed.
* Whether aggregation is sufficient.
* Whether personal information can be removed.

---

### 48. Aggregation

Aggregation can reduce the need to retain individual-level information.

Instead of retaining:

```text id="dm29"
10 million individual events
```

a business process may only require:

```text id="dm30"
Daily aggregated statistics
```

Aggregation can therefore support minimization where analytical requirements permit it.

---

### 49. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization can reduce direct exposure of identities while preserving analytical relationships.

Example:

```text id="dm31"
Jonathan Morales
      ↓
Customer-84721
```

The mapping information must itself be protected.

Pseudonymization does not necessarily make information anonymous.

---

### 50. Anonymization

Anonymization aims to prevent information from being reasonably linked back to an identifiable individual.

Where effective anonymization is technically and legally appropriate, it can reduce privacy risk.

However, organizations should not assume that simply removing names makes a dataset anonymous.

## Part 2

### 51. Data Minimization by Design

Data minimization should be incorporated into the architecture of an AI system rather than treated as a cleanup activity after deployment.

A useful design sequence is:

```text
Business Purpose
      ↓
Define Required Information
      ↓
Identify Minimum Data Elements
      ↓
Restrict Collection
      ↓
Restrict Access
      ↓
Limit Retention
      ↓
Secure Disposal
```

The objective is to make unnecessary data difficult to collect, process, copy, and retain.

---

### 52. Privacy by Design

Where personal information is processed, data minimization should form part of **privacy by design**.

The AI architecture should consider minimization before:

* Data collection.
* Model development.
* Vendor onboarding.
* System integration.
* Production deployment.

This is more effective than discovering excessive data after the AI system is already operational.

---

### 53. Privacy by Default

Privacy by default means that the system should use the least amount of personal information necessary unless a legitimate requirement justifies additional processing.

For example:

```text
Default:
Minimal User Information

Additional Data:
Only when specifically required
```

This prevents excessive data processing from becoming the normal configuration.

---

### 54. Data Collection Controls

Collection should be governed at the source.

Controls may include:

* Required-field validation.
* Data filtering.
* Collection restrictions.
* API field selection.
* Input validation.
* Consent or authorization mechanisms where applicable.

The best place to minimize unnecessary data is **before it enters the AI environment**.

---

### 55. Source-System Minimization

AI teams should avoid automatically extracting every field available from a source system.

For example:

```text
Source CRM
   ↓
100 Available Fields
   ↓
AI Requirement
   ↓
12 Required Fields
   ↓
AI Dataset
```

The extraction process should deliberately select the required fields.

---

### 56. API Data Minimization

APIs should return only the fields necessary for the requesting application.

Instead of:

```text
GET /customer/full-profile
```

an application may use a more restricted interface such as:

```text
GET /customer/support-details
```

This reduces unnecessary data propagation.

---

### 57. Database Query Minimization

AI applications should avoid unrestricted queries such as:

```text
SELECT *
```

when only specific attributes are required.

A controlled query should request only the necessary fields.

This reduces both processing and exposure.

---

### 58. Data Filtering at Ingestion

Data can be filtered before storage.

Example:

```text
Raw Source
    ↓
Filtering
    ↓
Sensitive Field Removal
    ↓
Required Dataset
```

This prevents unnecessary information from entering downstream AI systems.

---

### 59. Data Transformation for Minimization

Transformations can reduce sensitivity.

Examples include:

* Aggregation.
* Masking.
* Tokenization.
* Generalization.
* Pseudonymization.
* Removal of unnecessary fields.

Transformation should preserve the information required for the approved AI purpose.

---

### 60. Generalization

Generalization reduces precision when exact values are unnecessary.

For example:

```text
Exact Age:
47

Generalized:
40–49
```

or:

```text
Exact Location:
Specific Address

Generalized:
City / Region
```

This can reduce exposure while retaining analytical usefulness.

---

### 61. Precision Minimization

AI systems should use the appropriate level of precision.

If a model only needs:

```text
Month
```

there may be no reason to provide:

```text
Exact Date + Exact Time
```

Similarly, if city-level information is sufficient, an exact address may be unnecessary.

---

### 62. Identifier Minimization

Direct identifiers should be removed or replaced where they are not required.

Examples include:

* Full name.
* Personal email.
* Phone number.
* National identification number.
* Passport number.
* Exact address.

The model should receive an identifier only when the use case requires it.

---

### 63. Tokenization

Tokenization can replace sensitive values with tokens.

Example:

```text
Customer:
Jonathan Morales

Token:
CUST-82941
```

The original value can remain in a separately controlled system.

This can reduce exposure in AI processing environments.

---

### 64. Separation of Identifiers

A stronger architecture can separate identifying information from operational AI data.

```text
Identity Store
      │
      │ Controlled Mapping
      ↓
Tokenized AI Dataset
      ↓
AI System
```

The AI system may therefore operate without directly receiving the individual's identity.

---

### 65. Data Minimization in Training

Training datasets should contain only information necessary to achieve the approved model objective.

For example, a sentiment model may require:

```text
Customer Message
Sentiment Label
```

It may not require:

```text
Full Name
Home Address
Passport Number
Bank Account
```

---

### 66. Training Data Subsetting

Large datasets can sometimes be reduced through controlled sampling or selection.

Potential approaches include:

* Relevant records only.
* Representative samples.
* Time-bounded data.
* Geographic subsets.
* Business-unit subsets.

The reduction should not compromise the model's intended performance or representativeness.

---

### 67. Training Data Retention

Training datasets should have a defined lifecycle.

A typical process is:

```text
Candidate Dataset
      ↓
Approved Dataset
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Model Released
      ↓
Dataset Retained as Required
      ↓
Review
      ↓
Archive / Delete
```

The dataset should not automatically be retained forever simply because a model was trained using it.

---

### 68. Training Dataset Reuse

Reuse should be governed.

A dataset approved for one AI purpose should not automatically become approved for unrelated purposes.

Example:

```text
Approved Purpose:
Fraud Detection

New Purpose:
Employee Performance Prediction
```

The second use should require a separate governance assessment where appropriate.

---

### 69. Purpose Expansion

When the intended use of data changes, reassess:

* Business purpose.
* Data necessity.
* Privacy implications.
* Security requirements.
* Legal basis or contractual restrictions.
* Retention requirements.

This prevents function creep.

---

### 70. Function Creep

Function creep occurs when information collected for one purpose gradually becomes used for unrelated purposes.

Example:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Support AI
       ↓
Marketing AI
       ↓
Employee Analytics
```

Each additional use should be justified rather than assumed to be permissible.

---

### 71. Validation Data Minimization

Validation datasets should contain only the information required to evaluate the model.

Where identity is irrelevant:

```text
Remove:
Customer Name

Retain:
Relevant Features + Expected Outcome
```

This can reduce unnecessary exposure during model evaluation.

---

### 72. Test Data Minimization

Development and testing environments should avoid unnecessary production data.

Preferred hierarchy:

```text
Synthetic Data
     ↓
Anonymized / Appropriately De-identified Data
     ↓
Masked Production Data
     ↓
Restricted Production Data
```

The least sensitive suitable option should generally be preferred.

---

### 73. Production Data in Development

Copying production information into development creates additional copies.

Each copy creates another:

* Access point.
* Storage location.
* Backup.
* Logging environment.
* Potential attack surface.

Therefore:

> **Do not copy production data merely because it is convenient.**

---

### 74. Inference Data Minimization

Inference processing should use only the information required for the individual transaction.

Example:

```text
Task:
Determine Customer Eligibility

Required:
Eligibility Criteria

Not Required:
Complete Customer Profile
```

The AI system should not retrieve unrelated information simply because it is available.

---

### 75. Real-Time Inference

Real-time AI systems should avoid retaining transaction data indefinitely.

A controlled flow may be:

```text
Request
  ↓
Process
  ↓
Generate Result
  ↓
Retain Required Audit Information
  ↓
Delete Temporary Data
```

The exact retention requirements depend on business, legal, security, and regulatory needs.

---

### 76. Prompt Retention

Organizations should explicitly decide whether prompts need to be retained.

Possible approaches include:

**No retention**

Used where operational requirements permit.

**Short retention**

Used for troubleshooting or limited analytics.

**Controlled retention**

Used where audit or regulatory requirements apply.

The default should not automatically be indefinite storage.

---

### 77. Conversation History

AI assistants may retain conversation histories.

Governance should determine:

* Whether history is required.
* Which conversations are retained.
* Who can access them.
* How long they remain available.
* Whether sensitive information should be filtered.
* How deletion works.

---

### 78. Persistent AI Memory

AI agents may maintain long-term memory about users.

This creates additional retention considerations.

Questions include:

* What information is remembered?
* Why is it remembered?
* How long is it retained?
* Can users or administrators remove it?
* Who can access it?
* Is the memory reused for other purposes?

---

### 79. Memory Minimization

AI agents should store only information necessary to perform approved functions.

Example:

```text
Useful:
Preferred Meeting Time

Potentially Unnecessary:
Complete Conversation History
```

Memory should have defined retention and deletion rules.

---

### 80. RAG Retention

RAG systems may maintain document repositories for long periods.

Organizations should periodically identify:

* Obsolete documents.
* Superseded policies.
* Expired records.
* Duplicate documents.
* Unapproved content.

Old documents can continue influencing AI responses if they remain retrievable.

---

### 81. RAG Document Expiration

Documents should have lifecycle metadata where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Document
   ↓
Effective Date
   ↓
Review Date
   ↓
Expiration / Supersession
   ↓
Archive / Remove from Retrieval
```

This combines retention governance with AI accuracy.

---

### 82. Vector Store Retention

Deleting a source document does not necessarily remove every representation of that document.

Potential retained artifacts include:

* Embeddings.
* Metadata.
* Chunks.
* Indexes.
* Caches.
* Backups.

Deletion workflows should therefore account for the entire retrieval architecture.

---

### 83. Derived Data Retention

AI systems create derived information.

Examples include:

* Embeddings.
* Features.
* Labels.
* Aggregates.
* Predictions.
* Profiles.
* Risk scores.

These should be considered in retention decisions.

---

### 84. Derived Data Dependency

A derived dataset may depend on an original dataset.

```text
Source Data
     ↓
Transformation
     ↓
Feature Dataset
     ↓
Model
```

If the source information must be deleted, the organization should determine whether derived information also requires deletion, transformation, or continued retention under applicable requirements.

---

### 85. Model Artifacts and Retention

Models can potentially encode information derived from training data.

Therefore, deleting the original dataset does not necessarily answer every governance question about the trained model.

Organizations should define policies for:

* Model retention.
* Model retirement.
* Model retraining.
* Model deletion.
* Data-deletion impact assessment.

---

### 86. Retraining After Data Removal

If information is later determined to be unauthorized or inappropriate for model training, the organization may need to assess whether the affected model should be:

* Retrained.
* Replaced.
* Retired.
* Revalidated.

This depends on the nature and significance of the data involved.

---

### 87. Data Retention and Model Lifecycle

Model lifecycle and dataset lifecycle should be coordinated.

```text
Dataset Created
      ↓
Model Training
      ↓
Model Deployment
      ↓
Model Maintenance
      ↓
Model Retirement
      ↓
Dataset Review
```

Retiring a model should trigger a review of associated datasets and artifacts.

---

### 88. AI Logs and Retention

AI logging should have separate retention rules from general dataset retention.

For example:

```text
Security Logs
→ Security Retention Requirement

Application Logs
→ Operational Retention

Prompt Logs
→ AI-Specific Retention

Audit Logs
→ Audit Requirement
```

Different purposes may justify different retention periods.

---

### 89. Logging Tiering

Not every log requires the same retention period.

A practical approach is:

```text
Critical Security Logs
→ Longer Retention

Operational Logs
→ Moderate Retention

Debug Logs
→ Short Retention
```

This reduces unnecessary storage of detailed information.

---

### 90. Backup Retention

Backups complicate deletion.

An organization may delete a dataset from production while older backup copies still contain it.

Therefore, retention policies should explicitly address:

* Backup frequency.
* Backup retention.
* Backup expiration.
* Restoration requirements.
* Deletion implications.

---

### 91. Backup Minimization

Organizations should avoid retaining excessive backup generations without a business or compliance reason.

Example:

```text
100 Backup Copies
        ↓
Retention Review
        ↓
Required Backup Sets Only
```

Backup minimization reduces storage and exposure.

---

### 92. Replication and Retention

Cloud and distributed systems may replicate datasets across:

* Regions.
* Availability zones.
* Accounts.
* Clusters.
* Databases.

Retention controls should account for these replicas.

---

### 93. Cache Retention

Caching can unintentionally extend the life of sensitive information.

Controls may include:

* Time-to-live settings.
* Automatic invalidation.
* Encryption.
* Access controls.
* Cache deletion.

Cache lifetime should align with the purpose of the cached information.

---

### 94. Temporary Processing Data

Temporary files should have automatic expiration where practical.

Example:

```text
Processing File
      ↓
Job Completed
      ↓
Automatic Cleanup
```

This is preferable to relying entirely on manual deletion.

---

### 95. Automated Retention Enforcement

Retention policies can be implemented through automation.

Examples:

```text
IF retention_expired
THEN archive_or_delete

IF access_expired
THEN revoke_access

IF dataset_status = revoked
THEN block_processing
```

Automation reduces dependence on manual processes.

---

### 96. Retention Tags and Metadata

Datasets can carry metadata such as:

* Classification.
* Owner.
* Purpose.
* Creation date.
* Retention category.
* Expiration date.
* Legal hold status.
* Disposal status.

Example:

```text

Classification:
Confidential

Owner:
Customer Analytics

Purpose:
Approved Model Training

Retention:
Defined Policy

Status:
Active
```

---

### 97. Automated Expiration

Where technically feasible, datasets should have lifecycle policies that automatically trigger review or disposal.

Example:

```text
Dataset Created
      ↓
Retention Clock
      ↓
Expiration
      ↓
Owner Review
      ↓
Delete / Extend with Justification
```

Automatic expiration prevents indefinite retention by default.

---

### 98. Retention Review

Before extending retention, the owner should determine:

* Is the original purpose still valid?
* Is the data still necessary?
* Has the risk changed?
* Are there new legal or regulatory requirements?
* Can the dataset be minimized further?

Retention extensions should be justified.

---

### 99. Indefinite Retention

Indefinite retention should not be the default.

If indefinite retention is required, there should be a documented reason.

Examples may include:

* Legal requirements.
* Historical records.
* Long-term scientific research.
* Critical institutional knowledge.

Even then, access and security should continue to be reviewed.

---

### 100. Retention and Minimization Governance Principle

A mature AI data-governance model can be summarized as:

```text
COLLECT ONLY WHAT IS NEEDED
             ↓
USE ONLY FOR APPROVED PURPOSES
             ↓
STORE ONLY WHERE REQUIRED
             ↓
RETAIN ONLY AS LONG AS JUSTIFIED
             ↓
MINIMIZE COPIES AND DERIVED DATA
             ↓
DELETE OR ARCHIVE WHEN REQUIRED
             ↓
VERIFY DISPOSAL
```

This turns data retention and minimization from a documentation exercise into an operational security and governance control.

# 08.15 — Data Retention and Data Minimization

## Part 3

### 101. Data Retention Risk Management

Data retention should be treated as a risk-management decision rather than simply a storage configuration.

The organization should consider:

* Sensitivity of the data.
* Business value.
* Legal and regulatory requirements.
* Security exposure.
* AI model dependency.
* Cost of retention.
* Consequences of deletion.
* Consequences of continued retention.

A useful principle is:

> **Retain data because there is a justified reason to retain it—not simply because deletion is inconvenient.**

---

### 102. Retention Risk Assessment

A retention risk assessment can evaluate:

```text id="rsk01"
Data Sensitivity
      +
Business Necessity
      +
Legal Requirement
      +
Security Exposure
      +
Retention Duration
      ↓
Retention Risk
```

Longer retention should generally require stronger justification where risk increases.

---

### 103. Retention Risk vs Business Value

Data may have business value while simultaneously creating security risk.

Example:

| Factor                  | Assessment      |
| ----------------------- | --------------- |
| Historical value        | High            |
| Current business use    | Low             |
| Sensitivity             | High            |
| Security exposure       | High            |
| Retention justification | Requires review |

The correct decision may be to archive, minimize, transform, or delete the information.

---

### 104. Data Retention Decision Matrix

Organizations can use a decision matrix:

| Business Need | Legal Need | Security Risk | Possible Decision              |
| ------------- | ---------- | ------------- | ------------------------------ |
| High          | High       | Low           | Retain                         |
| High          | Low        | High          | Minimize / restrict            |
| Low           | High       | Medium        | Retain under controlled access |
| Low           | Low        | High          | Delete                         |
| Low           | Low        | Low           | Review for disposal            |

This provides a repeatable governance approach.

---

### 105. Retention Policy Hierarchy

Retention decisions may be influenced by several policy layers:

```text id="rsk02"
Law / Regulation
      ↓
Contract
      ↓
Enterprise Records Policy
      ↓
Data Governance Policy
      ↓
AI Data Retention Standard
      ↓
Dataset-Specific Rule
```

The most restrictive applicable requirement should be identified through appropriate legal and governance review.

---

### 106. Retention Period Conflicts

Different requirements may establish different retention periods.

For example:

```text id="rsk03"
Business Requirement → 2 Years
Security Requirement → 1 Year
Contractual Requirement → 3 Years
Legal Requirement → 5 Years
```

The organization should resolve the conflict through its legal, records-management, privacy, and governance processes rather than allowing the application team to choose arbitrarily.

---

### 107. Minimum Necessary Retention

Retention should be proportionate to the purpose.

If the purpose requires:

```text id="rsk04"
30 Days
```

retaining the same information for:

```text id="rsk05"
10 Years
```

requires additional justification.

The trigger should be documented because different triggers can produce significantly different disposal dates.

```text id="rsk08"
       ↓
```

### 113. Retention and AI Project Closure

When an AI project ends, the project team should review:

* Training data.

* Test data.
* Prompts.
* Outputs.
* Logs.
* Embeddings.
* Temporary files.

### 114. AI Sandbox Retention

A sandbox may contain:

* Downloaded datasets.
* Experimental prompts.
* Model outputs.
* Intermediate files.
* Notebooks.
* Embeddings.

Sandbox data should have shorter retention unless there is a documented reason to preserve it.

### 115. Shadow AI Data Retention

```text id="rsk09"

   ↓

   ↓
```

### 116. Third-Party AI Retention

Before sending enterprise information to an external AI service, governance should establish:

* Whether the provider retains the data.
* How long it is retained.
* Whether it is used for provider model training.
* Where it is stored.
* How deletion requests work.
* Whether backups retain copies.
* Which subprocessors may receive it.

These requirements should be addressed through appropriate security, privacy, procurement, and contractual controls.

### 117. Provider Data-Use Restrictions

Organizations should distinguish between:

**Processing**

The provider uses data to perform the requested service.

**Retention**

The provider stores the data after processing.

**Training**

The provider uses customer data to improve or train models.

These are different governance questions and should be evaluated separately.

### 118. AI Training Opt-Out Governance

Where an external provider offers controls regarding use of customer information for model training, the organization should verify that the appropriate configuration is enabled.

Evidence may include:

* Contract terms.
* Service configuration.
* Provider documentation.
* Administrative settings.
* Audit records.

### 119. Third-Party Deletion Assurance

* Deletion confirmation.
* Provider certification.
* System record.
* Contractual attestation.

The required assurance depends on the risk and applicable requirements.

* Provider locations.
* Subprocessors.
* Backup locations.

Retention and minimization should therefore be evaluated across the entire processing chain.

### 122. Data Replication Governance

### 127. Data Minimization and Data Quality

Excessive data can sometimes reduce AI data quality.

Examples include:

* Duplicate records.
* Outdated information.
* Irrelevant fields.
* Stale documents.
* Contradictory records.

Minimization can therefore support both **security and model quality**.

* Governance.

### 139. Data Minimization and Bias

More data is not automatically better.

Including unnecessary variables may introduce:

* Proxy variables.
* Historical bias.
* Irrelevant correlations.
* Unintended discrimination.

Data minimization can therefore contribute to responsible AI design, although it does not by itself eliminate bias.

### 140. Data Minimization and Attack Resistance

For example:

```text id="rsk22"

     ↓

     ↓
Smaller Attack Surface
     ↓

```

### 141. Data Minimization and Insider Risk

Employees should not automatically have access to complete datasets.

Instead:

```text id="rsk23"

      ↓

      ↓

      ↓

```

This reduces the amount of information available to a compromised or malicious account.

Governance should distinguish:

from:

> **Needs access.**

### 143. Just-in-Time Data Access

```text id="rsk24"
 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓
Access Revoked
```

This minimizes the period during which information is exposed.

AI APIs should expose only the minimum required data.

API responses should avoid unnecessarily returning:

* Full records.
* Sensitive attributes.
* Internal identifiers.
* Unrelated historical information.

API design therefore becomes part of data-minimization architecture.

### 145. Field-Level Security

Field-level controls can restrict access to particular attributes.

```text id="rsk25"
Name + Case
```

Different roles receive different subsets of the same underlying dataset.

Row-level security restricts which records a user or application can access.

Example:

```text id="rsk26"

      ↓
Only European Customer Records
```

This can significantly reduce unnecessary data exposure in enterprise AI systems.

---

### 147. Purpose-Based Access

Access policies can include purpose.

Example:

```text id="rsk27"
IF
User = Fraud Analyst
AND
Purpose = Fraud Investigation

THEN
Allow Access to Fraud Dataset
```

The same user may not automatically receive access for unrelated purposes.

---

### 148. Retention and Access Relationship

Long retention does not justify unrestricted access.

A dataset may need to be retained for five years while being accessible only to a small group.

```text id="rsk28"
Long Retention
      +
Restricted Access
      +
Strong Encryption
      +
Monitoring
```

This reduces exposure while satisfying legitimate retention requirements.

---

### 149. Retention Review Automation

Automated workflows can identify datasets approaching expiration.

Example:

```text id="rsk29"

       ↓

30 Days Before Expiry
       ↓
Review Required

Expiry Date
       ↓
Archive / Delete / Approved Extension
```

Automation makes retention enforcement more consistent.

---

### 150. Part 3 Governance Principle

The central governance principle for retention and minimization is:

> **AI data should have a defined purpose, a defined owner, a defined retention rule, a controlled lifecycle, and the minimum data footprint necessary to achieve the approved objective.**

This ensures that data governance addresses not only **how data is protected**, but also **whether the organization should continue to possess and process it at all**.

### 194. Governance Decision Example

**Question:**

Should an AI team retain ten years of historical customer data?

**Assessment:**

1. What is the AI purpose?
2. Is ten years necessary?
3. What data elements are required?
4. Are older records still relevant?
5. Are there legal or contractual requirements?
6. Can the dataset be reduced?
7. Can older information be aggregated?
8. What security risk does retention create?

The answer should be based on evidence rather than the assumption that **more historical data is always better**.

### 200. Final Governance Principle

The complete concept of **Data Retention and Data Minimization** can be summarized as:

> **An organization should collect and process only the data necessary for a defined AI purpose, protect it throughout its lifecycle, retain it only for as long as there is a justified requirement, minimize unnecessary copies and derived information, and securely dispose of it when the retention requirement ends.**

The objective is not simply to **store less data**.

The objective is to establish a controlled relationship between:

```text id="r4g29"
PURPOSE
   ↓
NECESSITY
   ↓
DATA
   ↓
SECURITY
   ↓
RETENTION
   ↓
DISPOSAL
   ↓
ASSURANCE
```

This makes data retention and minimization a core component of **AI governance, privacy, cybersecurity, risk management, and GRC assurance**.
