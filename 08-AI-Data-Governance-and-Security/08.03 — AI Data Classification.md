# 08.03 — AI Data Classification

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to AI Data Classification

AI data classification is the process of categorizing data according to its **sensitivity, criticality, regulatory requirements, business value, and security needs** so that appropriate governance and protection controls can be applied.

AI systems can process enormous volumes of information from many sources. Without classification, an organization may not know:

* Which data can safely be used by AI.
* Which data requires additional protection.
* Which data requires approval before AI processing.
* Which data must not be sent to external AI services.
* Which data requires special retention or deletion controls.

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI data should be classified according to the risk created by its use, not merely according to where the data is stored.**

---

### 2. Why AI Data Classification Matters

Traditional enterprise classification often focuses on documents, databases, and business records.

AI expands the classification problem because information may appear in:

* Training datasets.
* Fine-tuning datasets.
* Prompts.
* Conversation history.
* RAG repositories.
* Vector databases.
* Embeddings.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* Agent memory.
* AI-generated content.

Therefore, classification must follow the data throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

### 3. AI Data Classification Objectives

A classification program should help the organization determine:

1. What data is being processed?
2. How sensitive is it?
3. How critical is it?
4. Who may access it?
5. Where may it be processed?
6. Which AI systems may use it?
7. What security controls are required?
8. How long may it be retained?
9. Can it be shared externally?
10. What happens when it is no longer required?

---

### 4. Classification Is a Governance Control

Classification is not merely an administrative label.

It drives downstream controls.

```text id="k4m8q2"
Data
 ↓
Classification
 ↓
Risk
 ↓
Protection Requirements
 ↓
Security Controls
 ↓
Monitoring
```

For example, a dataset classified as restricted may require stronger controls than an internal dataset.

---

### 5. Classification vs Categorization

These concepts are related but different.

**Categorization** describes what the data is.

Examples:

* Customer data.
* Financial data.
* HR data.
* Security data.

**Classification** determines the level of protection required.

For example:

```text id="p7q3m9"
Customer Data
      ↓
Confidential
```

---

### 6. Classification vs Criticality

Sensitivity and criticality should not be confused.

**Sensitivity** asks:

> What would happen if this information were disclosed?

**Criticality** asks:

> What would happen if this information became unavailable or unreliable?

A public dataset could therefore be low sensitivity but highly critical to an AI service.

---

### 7. Common Classification Model

An organization may use four levels:

```text id="m8q4v7"
PUBLIC
  ↓
INTERNAL
  ↓
CONFIDENTIAL
  ↓
RESTRICTED
```

The exact names and definitions should be established by enterprise policy.

---

### 8. Public Data

Public data is information intentionally made available to the public.

Examples:

* Public product information.
* Published company information.
* Public documentation.
* Public government information.
* Public marketing content.

However, public availability does not automatically mean unrestricted AI use.

---

### 9. Public Data and AI

A dataset can be publicly accessible but still have restrictions.

Possible considerations include:

* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* Terms of use.
* Data integrity.
* Source reliability.
* Prohibited automated collection.

Therefore:

> **Public does not automatically mean unrestricted.**

---

### 10. Internal Data

Internal data is intended for use within the organization.

Examples:

* Internal procedures.
* Operational documentation.
* Internal project information.
* Employee communications.
* Internal technical documentation.

It may be appropriate for enterprise AI but should still be protected from unauthorized access.

---

### 11. Confidential Data

Confidential information could cause meaningful harm if disclosed.

Examples may include:

* Customer information.
* Business strategy.
* Contracts.
* Financial information.
* Internal security information.
* Intellectual property.

AI processing of confidential information generally requires stronger controls.

---

### 12. Restricted Data

Restricted data requires the highest level of protection under the organization's classification model.

Examples may include:

* Highly sensitive personal information.
* Credentials.
* Cryptographic secrets.
* Highly sensitive financial information.
* Certain regulated information.
* Critical security information.

The exact definition depends on organizational policy and applicable requirements.

---

### 13. Classification and AI Use

Classification should influence whether AI processing is permitted.

Example:

| Classification | Example AI Treatment              |
| -------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Public         | General AI use may be permitted   |
| Internal       | Approved enterprise AI            |
| Confidential   | Controlled enterprise AI          |
| Restricted     | Highly restricted AI environments |

This is only a conceptual model; actual permissions must be defined by policy.

---

### 14. AI Data Handling Rules

Each classification level should have handling requirements.

For example:

```text id="q6m3r8"
PUBLIC
→ General processing may be allowed

INTERNAL
→ Authenticated enterprise access

CONFIDENTIAL
→ Restricted access + stronger protection

RESTRICTED
→ Explicit authorization + enhanced controls
```

---

### 15. Classification and AI Providers

Classification should influence whether data can be sent to an external AI provider.

Example:

```text id="v8m4q7"
Public
  ↓
Potentially External AI

Internal
  ↓
Approved Enterprise AI

Confidential
  ↓
Controlled Provider

Restricted
  ↓
Potentially Prohibited
```

The actual decision depends on contractual, legal, privacy, security, and architectural requirements.

---

### 16. Classification and Data Residency

Sensitive AI data may have geographic processing restrictions.

Classification should therefore help determine:

* Where data may be stored.
* Where it may be processed.
* Which cloud regions are permitted.
* Which providers may process it.
* Whether cross-border transfer is allowed.

---

### 17. Classification and Access Control

Classification should directly influence access control.

A simple relationship is:

```text id="r7q3m9"
Classification
      ↓
Access Policy
      ↓
Identity
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Data
```

Higher-sensitivity data generally requires stronger access restrictions.

---

### 18. Classification and Least Privilege

Least privilege means users and systems should receive only the access necessary for their responsibilities.

For example:

```text id="m9q4v7"
AI Analyst
   ↓
Internal Dataset

Security Administrator
   ↓
Restricted Security Dataset
```

Access should be based on legitimate need rather than job title alone.

---

### 19. Machine Classification

AI systems and applications may require machine-readable classifications.

Example metadata:

```text id="q8m3r6"
classification = CONFIDENTIAL
owner = Finance
purpose = Forecasting
retention = 7 years
external_ai = PROHIBITED
```

Machine-readable metadata enables automated policy enforcement.

---

### 20. Classification Metadata

Useful metadata may include:

* Classification.
* Owner.
* Business purpose.
* Data category.
* Criticality.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Retention.
* Approved AI use.
* Approved locations.
* Approved consumers.

---

### 21. Automated Classification

Organizations can use automated tools to identify sensitive information.

Potential detection methods include:

* Pattern matching.
* Regular expressions.
* Data discovery.
* Machine learning.
* DLP engines.
* Metadata analysis.

Examples of detectable information include:

* Email addresses.
* Telephone numbers.
* Payment-card information.
* National identifiers.
* Credentials.

Automation should be validated to reduce false positives and false negatives.

---

### 22. Manual Classification

Some data cannot be reliably classified automatically.

For example:

* Strategic plans.
* Legal documents.
* Proprietary algorithms.
* Sensitive business decisions.

Human review may therefore be necessary.

---

### 23. Hybrid Classification

A mature approach combines automation and human judgment.

```text id="p4m8q7"
Data
 ↓
Automated Discovery
 ↓
Proposed Classification
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Approved Classification
```

This provides scalability while maintaining accountability.

---

### 24. Classification Confidence

Automated classification may produce a confidence score.

Example:

```text id="v6q3m9"
Dataset A
→ Confidential
→ Confidence: 98%

Dataset B
→ Possible Restricted
→ Confidence: 64%
→ Human Review Required
```

Low-confidence classifications should receive additional review.

---

### 25. Classification Errors

Classification systems can make two important types of errors.

**Under-classification**

Sensitive information receives a classification that is too low.

**Over-classification**

Low-risk information receives a classification that is unnecessarily high.

Both create problems.

---

### 26. Under-Classification Risk

Under-classification can result in:

* Excessive access.
* Unauthorized AI processing.
* Data leakage.
* Inappropriate external sharing.
* Insufficient retention controls.

This is generally a security and compliance concern.

---

### 27. Over-Classification Risk

Over-classification can cause:

* Excessive restrictions.
* Higher operational costs.
* Reduced data availability.
* Slower AI development.
* Unnecessary approval processes.

Therefore, classification should be **accurate and risk-based**, not simply restrictive.

---

### 28. Classification and Data Minimization

Classification helps identify whether all information in a dataset is actually necessary.

Example:

```text id="m7q4r9"
Customer Dataset
 ↓
Name
Email
Phone
Address
National ID
Payment Data
 ↓
AI Use Requires:
Name + Email
```

Unnecessary sensitive fields should be removed where practical.

---

### 29. Field-Level Classification

Entire datasets do not always have one classification.

Different fields may have different sensitivity.

Example:

| Field        | Classification |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| Product Name | Public         |
| Customer ID  | Internal       |
| Email        | Confidential   |
| National ID  | Restricted     |
| Password     | Restricted     |

This creates a need for granular classification.

---

### 30. Record-Level Classification

Individual records may also have different sensitivity.

For example, a customer dataset could contain:

```text id="q9m4r7"
Record 001 → Internal
Record 002 → Confidential
Record 003 → Restricted
```

The AI platform must be capable of applying appropriate controls where required.

---

### 31. Classification Inheritance

Derived data may inherit classification from its source.

Example:

```text id="v8m3q6"
Restricted Source
      ↓
Transformation
      ↓
Derived Dataset
      ↓
Potentially Restricted
```

Classification should not automatically disappear merely because the format changed.

---

### 32. Classification During Transformation

Transformation can sometimes reduce sensitivity.

Example:

```text id="m4q8v7"
Customer Records
      ↓
Aggregation
      ↓
Statistical Dataset
```

The resulting dataset may have lower sensitivity, but that determination should be assessed rather than assumed.

---

### 33. Classification After Aggregation

Aggregating information can sometimes reduce individual-level exposure.

However, aggregation may also create sensitive insights.

For example:

```text id="q7m3r9"
Individual Records
      ↓
Aggregation
      ↓
Employee Trend
```

The aggregated output may still reveal sensitive information.

---

### 34. Classification of AI Outputs

Classification should not stop at input data.

AI-generated outputs may contain:

* Confidential information.
* Personal information.
* Proprietary information.
* Security information.

Therefore:

> **AI outputs should be evaluated for appropriate handling based on their actual content.**

---

### 35. Output Classification

Example:

```text id="p8m4q7"
Internal Documents
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Generated Report
      ↓
Confidential
```

The output should not automatically be considered public merely because it was generated by AI.

---

### 36. Classification of RAG Content

RAG systems require careful classification because a knowledge base may contain multiple sensitivity levels.

Example:

```text id="m6q3r8"
RAG Repository
 ├── Public Documents
 ├── Internal Documents
 ├── Confidential Documents
 └── Restricted Documents
```

Retrieval controls must respect those classifications.

---

### 37. Classification-Aware Retrieval

A RAG system should ideally evaluate both:

```text id="q4m8v7"
User Authorization
        +
Document Classification
        ↓
Retrieval Decision
```

This reduces the risk of unauthorized information retrieval.

---

### 38. Classification of Embeddings

Embeddings should be governed according to the sensitivity of the underlying information.

A vector representation should not automatically be considered harmless simply because it does not display the original text directly.

---

### 39. Classification of AI Logs

AI logs may contain:

* User prompts.
* AI responses.
* Retrieved documents.
* User identifiers.
* System information.

Therefore, logs may inherit or acquire sensitive classifications.

---

### 40. Classification of Agent Memory

Agent memory can contain information accumulated over multiple interactions.

Example:

```text id="r7m3q9"
Conversation
   ↓
Agent Memory
   ↓
Long-Term Context
```

The memory store should be classified according to the information it contains.

---

### 41. Classification of Synthetic Data

Synthetic data is generated rather than directly collected from real individuals or systems.

It should not automatically be considered low risk.

Questions should include:

* Is it derived from sensitive data?
* Can individuals be reconstructed?
* Does it reproduce confidential information?
* Does it contain secrets?
* What was used to generate it?

---

### 42. Classification of Test Data

Test data can contain production-derived information.

Example:

```text id="m8q4r6"
Production Data
      ↓
Test Dataset
      ↓
Potentially Confidential
```

The test environment should not be treated as low risk merely because it is non-production.

---

### 43. Classification of Backup Data

Backups must retain appropriate protection.

If a production dataset is confidential:

```text id="q6m3v8"
Confidential Data
      ↓
Backup
      ↓
Confidential Protection
```

Changing the storage location does not automatically change the sensitivity.

---

### 44. Classification and Data Retention

Classification should influence retention requirements.

Higher-risk information may require:

* Shorter retention.
* Stronger deletion controls.
* More frequent reviews.

However, retention must also consider legal and business requirements.

---

### 45. Classification and Data Disposal

When data is no longer required, classification should influence disposal methods.

For example:

```text id="v9m4q7"
Public
→ Standard Disposal

Confidential
→ Controlled Deletion

Restricted
→ Enhanced Secure Disposal
```

---

### 46. Classification and DLP

Data Loss Prevention systems can use classification metadata to determine what information may leave the organization.

Example:

```text id="p7m3q9"
Restricted Data
      ↓
DLP Policy
      ↓
External AI Provider
      ↓
BLOCK
```

---

### 47. Classification and Encryption

Classification can determine encryption requirements.

For example:

```text id="q8m4r6"
Public
→ Standard Protection

Confidential
→ Strong Encryption

Restricted
→ Strong Encryption + Enhanced Key Controls
```

The actual requirements should be defined by enterprise standards.

---

### 48. Classification and Monitoring

Sensitive data should generally receive greater monitoring attention.

Monitoring may include:

* Access.
* Transfers.
* Downloads.
* API calls.
* Retrieval activity.
* Administrative changes.

---

### 49. Classification Review

Classification should be periodically reviewed.

Reasons include:

* Business changes.
* Regulatory changes.
* New AI use cases.
* Data aggregation.
* Changes in sensitivity.
* New threats.

---

### 50. AI Data Classification Foundation Model

The first stage of AI data classification can be summarized as:

```text id="x5q9m3"
                 AI DATA
                    │
                    ▼
              DATA DISCOVERY
                    │
                    ▼
             DATA CATEGORIZATION
                    │
                    ▼
               CLASSIFICATION
                    │
          ┌─────────┼─────────┐
          ↓         ↓         ↓
      Sensitivity Criticality Regulatory
          │         │         │
          └─────────┼─────────┘
                    ↓
             HANDLING RULES
                    ↓
            SECURITY CONTROLS
                    ↓
              AI USAGE POLICY
                    ↓
              MONITORING
                    ↓
              PERIODIC REVIEW
```

The central GRC principle is:

> **AI data classification establishes the decision point between knowing what data an AI system handles and knowing how that data must actually be governed and protected.**

A mature classification program therefore goes beyond applying labels. It connects **data sensitivity and criticality to authorization, AI usage, security controls, privacy requirements, retention, monitoring, and disposal**, ensuring that the protection applied to AI data is proportionate to the risk it creates.

### 51. Classification Dimensions

AI data classification should not rely on a single dimension.

A mature model may evaluate:

```text
                    AI DATA
                       │
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
   Sensitivity     Criticality    Regulation
        │              │              │
        └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                       ↓
                 Risk Profile
                       ↓
                Handling Rules
```

The resulting classification should reflect the organization's overall risk requirements.

---

### 52. Confidentiality

Confidentiality addresses the consequences of unauthorized disclosure.

Questions include:

* Could disclosure harm an individual?
* Could disclosure harm the organization?
* Is the information proprietary?
* Is the information regulated?
* Would disclosure create competitive or security risk?

---

### 53. Integrity

AI data classification should also consider the consequences of unauthorized modification.

For example, incorrect data could cause an AI system to:

* Produce incorrect recommendations.
* Misclassify transactions.
* Generate inaccurate reports.
* Make unsafe decisions.

Therefore, some datasets may require high-integrity protection even when they are not highly confidential.

---

### 54. Availability

Availability determines how important continuous access to the data is.

For example:

```text
Public Product Catalog
→ Low Confidentiality
→ High Availability

Security Configuration Data
→ High Confidentiality
→ High Integrity
→ High Availability
```

Classification can therefore support a broader **CIA-based** protection model.

---

### 55. CIA and AI Data Classification

The three classic security objectives can be applied to AI data:

| Dimension       | Question                                |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Confidentiality | Who should see the data?                |
| Integrity       | Who can modify the data?                |
| Availability    | How quickly must the data be available? |

The appropriate controls depend on the risk associated with each dimension.

---

### 56. Business Criticality

Business criticality determines the importance of the dataset to business operations.

Examples:

* Revenue forecasting dataset.
* Customer service knowledge base.
* Security intelligence dataset.
* Regulatory reporting dataset.

A dataset can therefore be low sensitivity but high business criticality.

---

### 57. Regulatory Classification

Some information may have specific regulatory or contractual requirements.

Examples include:

* Personal information.
* Financial information.
* Health-related information.
* Payment information.
* Government information.

Regulatory classification may impose additional controls beyond the organization's general classification scheme.

---

### 58. Legal Classification

Legal restrictions may apply independently of confidentiality.

Examples:

* Copyrighted content.
* Licensed datasets.
* Contract-restricted information.
* Trade secrets.
* Data subject to legal holds.

Therefore:

> **Security classification does not replace legal or regulatory analysis.**

---

### 59. Privacy Classification

Personal data should receive appropriate privacy treatment.

Possible categories include:

```text
Non-Personal
      ↓
Personal
      ↓
Sensitive Personal
```

The exact categories should be aligned with applicable law and organizational policy.

---

### 60. Security Classification vs Privacy Classification

These are related but not identical.

For example:

```text
Public Personal Data
```

may have:

* Low confidentiality under an enterprise model.
* Significant privacy obligations.

Therefore, AI governance should maintain both perspectives where appropriate.

---

### 61. Data Category

AI governance should identify what type of information a dataset contains.

Common categories include:

* Customer.
* Employee.
* Financial.
* Operational.
* Security.
* Legal.
* Intellectual property.
* Public information.
* Technical information.

---

### 62. Purpose Classification

The same dataset may be permitted for one purpose but prohibited for another.

Example:

```text
Customer Support Data
       ↓
Approved:
Customer Service

       ↓
Not Automatically Approved:
Model Training
```

Therefore, classification should sometimes include **purpose restrictions**.

---

### 63. Purpose-Based Access

Access decisions can incorporate intended use.

```text
User
 +
Dataset Classification
 +
Business Purpose
 ↓
Authorization Decision
```

This is more precise than relying only on the user's identity.

---

### 64. AI Use Classification

Organizations can classify data according to permitted AI activities.

For example:

| AI Activity                  | Possible Requirement |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Prompting                    | Approved             |
| RAG retrieval                | Approved             |
| Fine-tuning                  | Restricted           |
| Foundation-model training    | Highly restricted    |
| External provider processing | Restricted           |
| Public AI tool               | Prohibited           |

The exact matrix must be defined by organizational policy.

---

### 65. Classification and Processing Environment

Data classification should influence where AI processing may occur.

Example:

```text
PUBLIC
→ Public / Enterprise AI

INTERNAL
→ Enterprise AI

CONFIDENTIAL
→ Approved Controlled Environment

RESTRICTED
→ Dedicated / Highly Controlled Environment
```

---

### 66. Trusted AI Environments

Organizations may establish approved AI environments based on data classification.

Example:

```text
Zone 1 — Public AI
       ↓
Public Data

Zone 2 — Enterprise AI
       ↓
Internal Data

Zone 3 — Restricted AI
       ↓
Confidential / Restricted Data
```

This provides a practical enforcement mechanism.

---

### 67. AI Data Classification Matrix

An enterprise can create a matrix connecting classification with controls.

| Classification | Access            | External AI                 | Encryption                     | Monitoring |
| -------------- | ----------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------- |
| Public         | Broad             | Possible                    | Standard                       | Standard   |
| Internal       | Employees         | Approved only               | Required                       | Standard   |
| Confidential   | Restricted        | Controlled                  | Strong                         | Enhanced   |
| Restricted     | Explicit approval | Typically highly restricted | Strong + enhanced key controls | High       |

This is an example framework, not a universal standard.

---

### 68. Classification and Identity

Classification should work with identity and access management.

```text
Identity
   +
Role
   +
Context
   +
Data Classification
   ↓
Access Decision
```

This supports risk-based access.

---

### 69. Classification and Authentication

Highly sensitive AI data may require stronger authentication.

Possible controls include:

* Multi-factor authentication.
* Privileged access management.
* Strong service authentication.
* Conditional access.

---

### 70. Classification and Authorization

Authentication establishes **who** the requester is.

Authorization determines **what that requester may do**.

Example:

```text
Authenticated User
       ↓
Authorization Check
       ↓
Confidential Dataset?
       ↓
Permission Required
```

---

### 71. Classification and Service Accounts

AI applications often access data through machine identities.

These service identities should also be governed according to data classification.

A service account accessing restricted data should not receive unrestricted permissions.

---

### 72. Classification and API Access

APIs can become another path to classified data.

Controls should include:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Scope restrictions.
* Rate limiting.
* Logging.
* Data filtering.

---

### 73. Classification and Network Segmentation

Network architecture can reinforce classification.

```text
Public AI Zone
       │
       X
Restricted Data Zone
```

Sensitive data repositories should not automatically be reachable from every AI application.

---

### 74. Classification and Storage

Storage systems should enforce appropriate controls based on classification.

Examples:

* Access restrictions.
* Encryption.
* Key management.
* Backup protection.
* Integrity controls.

---

### 75. Classification and Vector Databases

Vector databases require special attention because they may contain embeddings derived from sensitive content.

Example:

```text
Confidential Document
       ↓
Chunking
       ↓
Embedding
       ↓
Vector Database
```

The vector database should therefore be governed as part of the data lifecycle.

---

### 76. Classification and Embedding Access

Users should not be able to query embeddings merely because they cannot directly access the source documents.

The retrieval layer should enforce appropriate authorization.

---

### 77. Classification and RAG Authorization

A secure RAG process can be represented as:

```text
User
 ↓
Identity
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Query
 ↓
Classified Knowledge Base
 ↓
Authorized Retrieval
 ↓
AI Model
```

The classification of the retrieved content must be respected.

---

### 78. Classification and AI Agents

AI agents may access multiple systems.

Therefore, classification should influence which tools and data sources an agent can use.

```text
Agent
 ├── CRM → Allowed
 ├── Knowledge Base → Allowed
 ├── HR Database → Restricted
 └── Finance System → Not Authorized
```

---

### 79. Classification and Agent Tool Access

Tool permissions should follow the principle of least privilege.

An agent should receive only the capabilities necessary for its approved task.

---

### 80. Classification and Agent Memory

Agent memory should be classified based on the information it stores.

If memory contains confidential customer information, the memory system should inherit appropriate protection requirements.

---

### 81. Classification and Prompt Data

Prompts can contain sensitive information even when the AI application itself is classified as low risk.

Example:

```text
Normal AI Application
       ↓
User enters:
Customer financial information
       ↓
Sensitive Prompt Data
```

Classification must therefore consider **actual content**, not just application classification.

---

### 82. Dynamic Classification

AI environments may require classification to change dynamically.

Example:

```text
Public Prompt
      ↓
No Sensitive Data

Public Prompt
      +
Customer Record
      ↓
Confidential Context
```

The processing session may therefore require stronger controls.

---

### 83. Context Aggregation

AI systems often combine multiple data elements.

Example:

```text
Public Data
+
Internal Data
+
Confidential Data
+
User Context
↓
AI Context
```

The combined context should be evaluated for its resulting sensitivity.

---

### 84. Aggregation Risk

Multiple low-sensitivity pieces of information can collectively reveal sensitive information.

For example:

```text
Data A + Data B + Data C
        ↓
Sensitive Business Insight
```

Therefore, classification should consider the risk of aggregation.

---

### 85. Inference Risk

AI systems can derive sensitive information from seemingly harmless inputs.

For example:

```text
Non-Sensitive Data
       ↓
AI Analysis
       ↓
Sensitive Inference
```

This creates a classification challenge because the resulting information may be more sensitive than the source data.

---

### 86. Derived Sensitive Information

Generated information can reveal:

* Individual characteristics.
* Business strategies.
* Security weaknesses.
* Customer behavior.
* Confidential relationships.

Therefore, derived data should be evaluated rather than automatically inheriting the source classification.

---

### 87. Classification of Model Outputs

AI outputs should be assessed based on their content.

Example:

```text
User Question
      ↓
AI
      ↓
Generated Output
      ↓
Content Assessment
      ↓
Appropriate Classification
```

---

### 88. Output Filtering

Organizations may implement controls to prevent classified information from being disclosed through AI outputs.

Potential controls include:

* DLP.
* Content inspection.
* Policy enforcement.
* Access-aware retrieval.
* Human review.

---

### 89. Classification and Data Loss Prevention

Classification provides DLP with context.

Example:

```text
Restricted Data
      ↓
DLP Detection
      ↓
External AI Request
      ↓
BLOCK
```

Without reliable classification, DLP may have difficulty determining the appropriate action.

---

### 90. Classification and Data Discovery

Before classification can occur, the organization needs visibility into where AI data exists.

Discovery may identify:

* Databases.
* File shares.
* Cloud storage.
* RAG repositories.
* Vector databases.
* AI platforms.
* Logs.
* Backups.

---

### 91. Shadow AI Data

Employees may create AI-related datasets outside approved platforms.

Examples:

* Downloaded datasets.
* Local AI experiments.
* Personal cloud storage.
* Unapproved AI tools.
* Local vector stores.

These create **shadow AI data**.

---

### 92. Shadow AI Classification

Organizations should identify unauthorized AI data stores where possible.

The governance objective is not simply to block experimentation but to ensure that sensitive information does not enter unmanaged AI environments.

---

### 93. Classification Discovery Process

A practical discovery process is:

```text
Identify Data Stores
        ↓

        ↓
Discover Data
        ↓
Detect Sensitive Content
        ↓
Assign Classification
        ↓
Apply Controls
```

A data inventory should provide visibility into important datasets.

| Dataset       | Owner     | Classification | AI Use    | Location      |

Useful fields include:

Classification metadata can be incorporated into automated policy decisions.

```text
IF classification = "restricted"

```

A mature environment may use classification to automatically:

* Block transfers.

Classification should be continuously maintained.

```text
   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
Improve
```

It should not be treated as a one-time labeling exercise.

### 100. AI Data Classification Operating Model

The foundation of a mature classification program can be summarized as:

```text
                       AI DATA
                          │
                          ▼
                     DISCOVERY
                          │
                          ▼
                  DATA CATEGORIZATION
                          │
                          ▼
                    CLASSIFICATION
                          │
        ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
        ↓                 ↓                 ↓
 Confidentiality     Integrity        Availability
        │                 │                 │
        └─────────────────┼─────────────────┘
                          ↓
                   Criticality
                          ↓
                  Regulatory Needs
                          ↓
                     AI Purpose
                          ↓
                  Handling Rules
                          ↓
                Technical Controls
                          ↓
                  MONITORING
                          ↓
                    REVIEW
```

The central GRC principle is:

> **AI data classification must translate the characteristics and risk of data into enforceable handling requirements across the AI ecosystem.**

Classification therefore becomes much more powerful when it is connected to **identity, authorization, AI usage, RAG retrieval, agent permissions, DLP, encryption, storage, monitoring, retention, and disposal**.

A mature organization should ultimately be able to move from:

**“This data is Confidential.”**

to:

**“Because this data is Confidential, these are the AI systems that may process it, these are the users and services that may access it, these are the locations where it may be stored, these are the transfers that are prohibited, these controls must protect it, and these events must be monitored.”**

### 101. Classification Policy Hierarchy

AI data classification should operate within a broader governance hierarchy.

```text

       ↓
AI Governance Policy
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓
Monitoring & Assurance
```

### 102. Classification Policy Requirements

A classification policy should define:

* Classification levels.
* Classification criteria.
* Data-owner responsibilities.
* Handling requirements.
* Access requirements.
* AI-processing restrictions.
* Review requirements.
* Exception procedures.
* Retention requirements.
* Disposal requirements.

---

### 103. Classification Standard

The standard should provide objective criteria for assigning classifications.

For example:

| Classification | General Definition                                                             |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Public         | Approved for public disclosure                                                 |
| Internal       | Intended for authorized organizational use                                     |
| Confidential   | Unauthorized disclosure could cause significant harm                           |
| Restricted     | Unauthorized disclosure could cause severe harm or violate strict requirements |

Definitions must be tailored to the organization.

---

### 104. Classification Decision Tree

A practical decision process can be:

```text id="q8m3r7"
              DATA
                ↓
       Is it publicly approved?
          ↙           ↘
        YES            NO
         ↓              ↓
      PUBLIC      Does disclosure create
                  meaningful harm?
                    ↙       ↘
                  YES        NO
                   ↓          ↓
             CONFIDENTIAL  INTERNAL
```

Additional regulatory and business-criticality checks should then be applied.

---

### 105. Restricted Data Decision

A separate question should identify highly sensitive information.

```text id="m7q4v9"
Does the data contain:
      ↓
Highly sensitive personal data?
Credentials?
Cryptographic secrets?
Critical security information?
Strictly regulated information?
      ↓
YES
      ↓
RESTRICTED
```

The exact criteria depend on organizational policy.

---

### 106. Classification by Business Impact

Another approach is to assess the consequences of compromise.

Consider:

* Financial impact.
* Operational impact.
* Legal impact.
* Regulatory impact.
* Reputational impact.
* Security impact.
* Privacy impact.

The classification should reflect the organization's defined thresholds.

---

### 107. Business Impact Analysis

For important AI datasets, the organization can ask:

> What would happen if this data were disclosed, modified, or unavailable?

Example:

```text id="v8m3q6"
Unauthorized Disclosure
       ↓
Business Impact
       ↓
Low / Moderate / High / Critical
       ↓
Classification
```

---

### 108. Classification and Risk Appetite

Classification should align with organizational risk appetite.

If the organization has very low tolerance for exposure of certain data, the corresponding classification and controls should reflect that.

---

### 109. Classification and AI Risk Appetite

AI introduces additional questions:

* Can this data be processed by generative AI?
* Can it be sent to a third-party model?
* Can it be used for training?
* Can it be retained by an AI provider?
* Can it be used in prompts?
* Can it be indexed into a vector database?

These questions should be connected to classification.

---

### 110. AI Data Handling Matrix

A mature organization can establish a handling matrix.

| Data Level   | Prompt            | RAG               | Fine-Tuning       | External AI          |
| ------------ | ----------------- | ----------------- | ----------------- | -------------------- |
| Public       | Allowed           | Allowed           | Possible          | Possible             |
| Internal     | Controlled        | Allowed           | Controlled        | Approved only        |
| Confidential | Restricted        | Controlled        | Highly controlled | Restricted           |
| Restricted   | Explicit approval | Highly controlled | Exceptional       | Generally prohibited |

This is an illustrative model; actual enterprise rules may differ.

---

### 111. Classification and Model Training

Training creates a particularly important classification decision.

Before training, determine:

```text id="p6m4r8"
Dataset Classification
       +
Training Purpose
       +
Model Environment
       +
Provider Terms
       ↓
Training Authorization
```

A dataset approved for operational use should not automatically be approved for training.

---

### 112. Classification and Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning may embed patterns from training data into model behavior.

Therefore, organizations should consider:

* Dataset sensitivity.
* Model ownership.
* Model distribution.
* Provider access.
* Model retention.
* Reuse restrictions.

---

### 113. Classification and Model Outputs

A model trained on sensitive information may generate outputs that expose sensitive patterns or information.

Classification governance should therefore address both:

```text id="q7m3v9"
Input Data
    ↓
Model
    ↓
Output Data
```

---

### 114. Classification and RAG

RAG generally keeps source information outside the model's parameters, but the retrieved content still enters the AI processing context.

Therefore:

> **RAG does not eliminate data-classification requirements.**

---

### 115. Classification and Fine-Tuned Models

A fine-tuned model may itself become a controlled enterprise asset.

The organization should determine:

* What data influenced it.
* Who can access it.
* Where it can be deployed.
* Whether it can be shared.
* What restrictions apply.

---

### 116. Classification and Model Artifacts

AI development produces many artifacts:

* Datasets.
* Checkpoints.
* Model weights.
* Tokenizers.
* Configuration files.
* Evaluation results.
* Logs.

Some artifacts may require the same or stronger protection as the original data.

---

### 117. Model Weights and Sensitive Data

Model weights should not automatically be assumed to contain no sensitive information.

Depending on the system and training process, models may retain information derived from training data.

Therefore, model artifacts should receive appropriate security assessment.

---

### 118. Classification and Evaluation Data

Evaluation datasets may contain sensitive information.

They should therefore be classified and protected independently from the model being tested.

---

### 119. Classification and Benchmark Data

Public benchmarks may be low sensitivity but still subject to:

* Licensing.
* Copyright.
* Integrity requirements.
* Usage restrictions.

Classification should therefore include more than confidentiality.

---

### 120. Classification and Data Licensing

A dataset may be publicly accessible while still being restricted by license.

Example:

```text id="m4q8v7"
Public Dataset
       ↓
License Restriction
       ↓
AI Training
       ↓
Requires Review
```

This is why **classification and legal-use analysis should work together**.

---

### 121. Classification and Copyright

Copyright status is not the same thing as security classification.

A public document may be:

```text id="q8m3r6"
Security Classification:
PUBLIC

Legal Status:
COPYRIGHTED
```

Both dimensions must be respected.

---

### 122. Classification and Third-Party Data

Third-party datasets should be assessed before AI processing.

Consider:

* Contractual restrictions.
* Permitted purposes.
* Data ownership.
* Security requirements.
* Geographic restrictions.
* Retention requirements.

---

### 123. Classification and Data Contracts

Where appropriate, data contracts can define:

* Permitted use.
* Classification.
* Quality requirements.
* Access requirements.
* Retention.
* Security obligations.

This creates clearer expectations between data producers and AI consumers.

---

### 124. Classification and Data Sharing

Classification should determine whether data can be shared.

Potential sharing categories include:

```text id="v7m4q9"
Internal
   ↓
Approved Partners
   ↓
Third Parties
   ↓
Public
```

Each transition may require additional authorization.

---

### 125. Classification and External AI Providers

Before sending classified data to an external provider, organizations should assess:

* Provider security.
* Data processing terms.
* Data retention.
* Training usage.
* Data residency.
* Subprocessors.
* Administrative access.
* Contractual restrictions.

---

### 126. Provider Data Retention

One important question is:

> **Does the AI provider retain submitted data?**

Classification requirements should influence whether provider retention is acceptable.

---

### 127. Provider Training Use

Organizations should determine whether submitted data may be used by the provider for model improvement or training.

This can materially affect whether sensitive data may be submitted.

---

### 128. Classification and Contractual Controls

Technical controls should be supported by contracts where external providers process sensitive AI data.

Potential contractual requirements include:

* Purpose limitation.
* Confidentiality.
* Security controls.
* Data deletion.
* Incident notification.
* Subprocessor restrictions.

---

### 129. Classification and Third-Party Risk

An external AI provider becomes part of the organization's data-processing chain.

```text id="m9q4v7"
Enterprise Data
       ↓
AI Application
       ↓
Third-Party Provider
       ↓
Model Infrastructure
```

Third-party risk management should therefore consider the classification of the data involved.

---

### 130. Classification and Data Residency

For sensitive AI data, classification may determine approved geographic locations.

Example:

```text id="q6m3r8"
Restricted Data
      ↓
Approved Region
      ↓
Approved Cloud
      ↓
Approved AI Service
```

---

### 131. Classification and Data Sovereignty

Some organizations must maintain control over where data is processed.

Classification can be used as one input into sovereignty requirements, but legal and regulatory analysis remains necessary.

---

### 132. Classification and Cross-Border Processing

When AI data crosses borders, the organization should determine whether:

* Transfer is permitted.
* Additional safeguards are required.
* Provider location is acceptable.
* Subprocessor locations are acceptable.

---

### 133. Classification and Data Minimization

Classification should encourage removal of unnecessary sensitive information.

Example:

```text id="v8m4q7"
Original Dataset
100 fields
      ↓
AI Requirement
15 fields
      ↓
Minimized Dataset
15 fields
```

This reduces the AI system's exposure.

---

### 134. Classification and Tokenization

Sensitive information may be replaced with tokens before AI processing.

Example:

```text id="p7m3q9"
Customer Name
      ↓
Tokenization
      ↓
CUSTOMER_001
      ↓
AI Processing
```

Tokenization does not automatically eliminate all governance requirements, but it can reduce direct exposure.

---

### 135. Classification and Masking

Masking can hide sensitive values.

Example:

```text id="q4m8r6"
Original:
john.smith@example.com

Masked:
j***@example.com
```

Masking may be appropriate for certain AI use cases.

---

### 136. Classification and Anonymization

Where genuinely anonymized data is produced, privacy risk may be reduced.

However, organizations should verify that the anonymization is effective and appropriate for the intended use.

---

### 137. Classification and Pseudonymization

Pseudonymized data may still be linked back to individuals using additional information.

Therefore:

> **Pseudonymization does not necessarily make data non-sensitive.**

Classification and privacy controls should continue to apply where appropriate.

---

### 138. Classification and Data Transformation

Transformations should be evaluated for their effect on classification.

```text id="m8q3r7"
Sensitive Data
      ↓
Transformation
      ↓
New Dataset
      ↓
Reassess Classification
```

Do not automatically assume the classification has changed.

---

### 139. Classification and Derived Insights

AI can produce insights that were not explicitly present in the source dataset.

Example:

```text id="v6m4q9"
Multiple Data Sources
        ↓
AI Analysis
        ↓
Sensitive Business Insight
```

The resulting insight may require a higher classification.

---

### 140. Classification Escalation

Classification may need to increase when the data becomes more sensitive.

```text id="q7m3r8"
Internal
   ↓
Combined with Sensitive Data
   ↓
Confidential
   ↓
Sensitive Inference
   ↓
Restricted
```

Classification should therefore support **escalation as risk changes**.

---

### 141. Classification Downgrading

Classification may also be reduced after appropriate transformation.

Example:

```text id="m4q8v7"
Restricted Dataset
       ↓
Validated Anonymization
       ↓
Lower-Risk Dataset
```

Downgrading should require appropriate validation and authorization.

---

### 142. Classification Review Triggers

Reclassification should occur when:

* Data content changes.
* New data is added.
* New AI purposes are introduced.
* Regulatory requirements change.
* Data is aggregated.
* A security incident occurs.
* Business impact changes.

---

### 143. Classification Reassessment

A reassessment process can be:

```text id="r8m3q7"
  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓

  ↓
```

### 144. Classification Expiration

For some datasets, classification decisions may need periodic renewal.

Example:

```text id="q6m4v8"

        ↓

        ↓

   ↙        ↘
 YES        NO
  ↓          ↓
```

### 145. Classification Exceptions

Organizations may create exceptions to standard classification rules.

Exceptions should be:

* Justified.
* Risk assessed.
* Approved.
* Documented.
* Time-limited.

### 146. Classification Disputes

Different stakeholders may disagree about classification.

For example:

```text id="v7m3q9"
Business → Internal

```

The organization should have an escalation mechanism.

### 147. Classification Authority

The organization should define who has final authority.

Typically:

```text id="m8q4r6"
     ↓
Classification Decision
     ↓

     ↓
GRC Oversight
```

The precise model depends on governance structure.

### 148. Classification Auditability

Classification decisions should be traceable.

Evidence may include:

* Classification record.
* Approver.
* Date.
* Reason.
* Supporting assessment.
* Applicable requirements.
* Review date.

---

### 149. Classification Evidence

A classification record might look like:

### 150. AI Data Classification Governance Cycle

The complete classification governance cycle can be represented as:

```text id="x8m3q6"
                  DISCOVER
                     ↓
                 CATEGORIZE
                     ↓
                 CLASSIFY
                     ↓
             DOCUMENT DECISION
                     ↓
               APPLY RULES
                     ↓
              ENFORCE CONTROLS
                     ↓
                  MONITOR
                     ↓
                   REVIEW
                     ↓
                RECLASSIFY
                     ↺
```

The central principle is:

> **AI data classification is a continuous governance process, not a one-time label applied when data is created.**

A mature organization continuously evaluates **what the data contains, how sensitive it is, how critical it is, what legal and regulatory obligations apply, what AI purposes are permitted, where it can be processed, who can access it, and whether its classification remains appropriate as the data and AI environment evolve.**

### 151. Classification Across the AI Data Lifecycle

Classification must remain attached to data as it moves through the AI lifecycle.

```text
Acquire
   ↓
Store
   ↓
Prepare
   ↓
Train
   ↓
Validate
   ↓
Deploy
   ↓
Infer
   ↓
Log
   ↓
Retain
   ↓
Dispose
```

At every stage, the organization should know:

* What classification applies.
* Who can access the data.
* What processing is permitted.
* What controls are required.

---

### 152. Classification During Data Acquisition

Classification should begin as early as practical.

When acquiring a dataset, identify:

* Source.
* Owner.
* Purpose.
* Sensitivity.
* Legal restrictions.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Intended AI use.

A dataset should not enter an AI pipeline without an appropriate governance decision.

---

### 153. Classification During Ingestion

During ingestion, data may move between systems.

Example:

```text
Source System
     ↓
Data Pipeline
     ↓
AI Data Platform
```

The classification metadata should travel with the data wherever technically possible.

---

### 154. Classification Metadata Propagation

Classification should be propagated through transformations.

```text
Dataset
  ↓
Pipeline
  ↓
Processed Dataset
  ↓
Feature Store
  ↓
Model
```

Each downstream artifact should have appropriate metadata and handling rules.

---

### 155. Classification During Data Preparation

Data preparation can include:

* Cleaning.
* Filtering.
* Deduplication.
* Normalization.
* Transformation.
* Sampling.

These activities may change the characteristics of the dataset.

Therefore, classification should be reassessed when material transformations occur.

---

### 156. Classification During Training

Training data should be explicitly approved.

A training decision should consider:

```text
Dataset Classification
        +
Training Purpose
        +
Model Sensitivity
        +
Processing Environment
        +
Provider Risk
        ↓
Training Authorization
```

---

### 157. Classification During Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning requires similar governance.

Questions include:

* What information is being introduced?
* Is the model privately controlled?
* Can the model be exported?
* Can the model be shared?
* Can the provider access the fine-tuning data?

---

### 158. Classification During Validation

Validation datasets should be separately classified.

This prevents organizations from assuming:

> "It is only test data, so it is safe."

Test and validation environments can still expose sensitive information.

---

### 159. Classification During Deployment

Before deployment, organizations should confirm that the model and supporting data can operate within the approved classification boundaries.

Example:

```text
Approved Model
      +
Approved Data
      +
Approved Environment
      ↓
Production Deployment
```

---

### 160. Classification During Inference

Inference creates a new classification challenge.

A user may submit:

* Personal information.
* Customer records.
* Confidential documents.
* Business strategy.
* Security information.

Therefore, runtime classification controls may be necessary.

---

### 161. Prompt Classification

Prompt content should be evaluated independently from the application.

For example:

```text
Application:
Internal

Prompt:
Customer financial information
```

The prompt may require **Confidential** or **Restricted** handling even if the application itself is classified as Internal.

---

### 162. Conversation Classification

Conversation history may contain sensitive information accumulated over time.

Example:

```text
Prompt 1 → Internal
Prompt 2 → Confidential
Prompt 3 → Restricted
       ↓
Conversation History
       ↓
Highest Applicable Handling Requirement
```

Organizations should define how mixed-classification conversations are handled.

---

### 163. Classification of Conversation History

Conversation storage should consider:

* User identity.
* Prompt content.
* AI responses.
* Retrieved documents.
* Uploaded files.
* Agent actions.

Conversation history should not automatically be treated as ordinary application logs.

---

### 164. Classification of Uploaded Files

Users may upload sensitive documents to AI applications.

The platform should ideally:

1. Detect the content.
2. Classify it.
3. Apply access restrictions.
4. Enforce permitted AI processing.
5. Apply retention rules.

---

### 165. Classification of Retrieved Data

In RAG systems, retrieved information should maintain its classification.

```text
Knowledge Base
      ↓
Document
      ↓
Classification
      ↓
Retrieval Authorization
      ↓
AI Context
```

---

### 166. Classification-Aware RAG

A mature RAG architecture should prevent a user from retrieving information above their authorization level.

Conceptually:

```text
User Clearance
       +
Document Classification
       ↓
Authorization Decision
       ↓
Retrieve / Deny
```

---

### 167. Classification of Vector Stores

Vector stores should be classified according to the sensitivity of their contents.

Controls should address:

* Access.
* Tenant isolation.
* Encryption.
* Query authorization.
* Administrative access.
* Backup protection.

---

### 168. Multi-Tenant Classification

Multi-tenant AI environments create additional classification requirements.

```text
Tenant A
   ↓
Tenant A Data

Tenant B
   ↓
Tenant B Data
```

The system must prevent classification boundaries from being bypassed through shared infrastructure.

---

### 169. Classification and Tenant Isolation

Tenant isolation should be treated as both an architecture and governance requirement.

A user authorized for Tenant A should not gain access to Tenant B's classified information through:

* RAG.
* Vector search.
* AI memory.
* Logs.
* APIs.
* Administrative interfaces.

---

### 170. Classification of Agent Memory

Agent memory can accumulate sensitive information over time.

Therefore, organizations should establish:

* Memory classification.
* Access restrictions.
* Retention.
* Deletion.
* Monitoring.
* User controls where applicable.

---

### 171. Classification of Tool Outputs

Agents can retrieve information from external systems.

Example:

```text
Agent
 ↓
CRM
 ↓
Customer Data
 ↓
Tool Response
 ↓
AI Context
```

The tool response should retain appropriate classification while being processed by the agent.

---

### 172. Classification and Agent Actions

Classification should also influence what actions an AI agent may perform.

For example:

```text
Internal Data
→ Read

Confidential Data
→ Read with authorization

Restricted Data
→ Read + Explicit Approval

Financial Transaction
→ Human Approval
```

Classification can therefore support both **data access** and **action authorization**.

---

### 173. Classification of AI Outputs

AI outputs should be evaluated based on their actual content.

Possible outputs include:

* Public summaries.
* Internal reports.
* Confidential analysis.
* Restricted security information.

Output classification should therefore be dynamic where necessary.

---

### 174. Classification and Human Review

Certain outputs may require human review before distribution.

For example:

```text
AI Output
   ↓
Classification Assessment
   ↓
Sensitive?
 ↙       ↘
No        Yes
↓          ↓
Release   Human Review
```

---

### 175. Classification and External Sharing

Before an AI-generated output is shared externally, the organization should evaluate:

* Classification.
* Recipient.
* Business purpose.
* Contractual restrictions.
* Privacy considerations.
* Regulatory requirements.

---

### 176. Classification and Email

AI-generated content may be copied into email.

DLP and information-protection controls should continue to apply after the content leaves the AI application.

---

### 177. Classification and Collaboration Platforms

AI-generated content may also enter:

* Collaboration platforms.
* Messaging systems.
* Shared drives.
* Ticketing systems.
* Project-management systems.

Classification should remain meaningful across these environments.

---

### 178. Classification and Logging

AI logs may contain sensitive information.

Logs can include:

```text
User
+
Prompt
+
Retrieved Data
+
AI Response
+
System Metadata
```

Therefore, logging systems require their own classification assessment.

---

### 179. Classification and Security Monitoring

Security monitoring systems may receive AI data.

For example:

```text
AI Application
      ↓
Security Logs
      ↓
SIEM
```

Sensitive information should not unnecessarily spread into monitoring infrastructure.

---

### 180. Classification and DLP Monitoring

DLP can monitor movement of classified AI data.

Potential monitored channels include:

* Email.
* Web uploads.
* APIs.
* Cloud storage.
* External AI services.
* USB devices.
* Collaboration tools.

---

### 181. Classification and Encryption

Encryption requirements should correspond to the sensitivity of the data.

Controls may include:

* Encryption in transit.
* Encryption at rest.
* Key management.
* Key rotation.
* Access-controlled decryption.

---

### 182. Classification and Key Management

Highly sensitive AI data may require stronger key-management practices.

For example:

```text
Restricted Data
      ↓
Dedicated Encryption Key
      ↓
Restricted Key Access
      ↓
Enhanced Monitoring
```

---

### 183. Classification and Backup

Backups should preserve appropriate classification and protection.

A backup containing Restricted AI data should not become an unprotected copy simply because it resides in a backup environment.

---

### 184. Classification and Disaster Recovery

Disaster recovery environments should preserve:

* Classification.
* Access restrictions.
* Encryption.
* Geographic requirements.
* Retention rules.

Security controls should not be weakened simply because a system is operating in recovery mode.

---

### 185. Classification and Data Retention

Retention should be linked to classification and business requirements.

Example:

```text
Data Classification
       +
Legal Requirement
       +
Business Requirement
       +
Risk
       ↓
Retention Period
```

Classification alone should not determine retention.

---

### 186. Classification and Data Disposal

When data reaches the end of its approved lifecycle, disposal should be performed appropriately.

This may involve:

* Secure deletion.
* Cryptographic erasure.
* Destruction of physical media.
* Removal from backups according to applicable processes.
* Revocation of access.

---

### 187. Classification and Data Replication

AI platforms may replicate data across:

* Regions.

### Key GRC Principle

> **AI data classification is the foundation for determining how data may be accessed, processed, transformed, transferred, retained, and ultimately disposed of throughout the AI lifecycle.**

The objective is not simply to place a label such as **Public, Internal, Confidential, or Restricted** on a dataset.

The real objective is to create an enforceable relationship:

```text
DATA
  ↓
CLASSIFICATION
  ↓
RISK
  ↓
PERMITTED AI USE
  ↓
ACCESS REQUIREMENTS
  ↓
SECURITY CONTROLS
  ↓
MONITORING
  ↓
RETENTION
  ↓
DISPOSAL
```

A mature AI governance program should therefore be able to answer five questions for every important dataset:

**What is it?**
**How sensitive and critical is it?**
**What is AI allowed to do with it?**
**Who and what may access it?**
**How is its protection maintained throughout its lifecycle?**

That is the difference between **data labeling** and **AI data governance**.
