# 08.03 — AI Data Classification

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to AI Data Classification

AI data classification is the process of categorizing data according to its **sensitivity, criticality, regulatory requirements, business value, and security needs** so that appropriate governance and protection controls can be applied.

AI systems can process enormous volumes of information from many sources. Without classification, an organization may not know:

* Which data can safely be used by AI.
* Which data requires additional protection.
* Which data requires approval before AI processing.
* Which data must not be sent to external AI services.
* Which data requires special retention or deletion controls.

The fundamental principle is:

> **AI data should be classified according to the risk created by its use, not merely according to where the data is stored.**

---

### 2. Why AI Data Classification Matters

Traditional enterprise classification often focuses on documents, databases, and business records.

AI expands the classification problem because information may appear in:

* Training datasets.
* Fine-tuning datasets.
* Prompts.
* Conversation history.
* RAG repositories.
* Vector databases.
* Embeddings.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* Agent memory.
* AI-generated content.

Therefore, classification must follow the data throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

### 3. AI Data Classification Objectives

A classification program should help the organization determine:

1. What data is being processed?
2. How sensitive is it?
3. How critical is it?
4. Who may access it?
5. Where may it be processed?
6. Which AI systems may use it?
7. What security controls are required?
8. How long may it be retained?
9. Can it be shared externally?
10. What happens when it is no longer required?

---

### 4. Classification Is a Governance Control

Classification is not merely an administrative label.

It drives downstream controls.

```text id="k4m8q2"
Data
 ↓
Classification
 ↓
Risk
 ↓
Protection Requirements
 ↓
Security Controls
 ↓
Monitoring
```

For example, a dataset classified as restricted may require stronger controls than an internal dataset.

---

### 5. Classification vs Categorization

These concepts are related but different.

**Categorization** describes what the data is.

Examples:

* Customer data.
* Financial data.
* HR data.
* Security data.

**Classification** determines the level of protection required.

For example:

```text id="p7q3m9"
Customer Data
      ↓
Confidential
```

---

### 6. Classification vs Criticality

Sensitivity and criticality should not be confused.

**Sensitivity** asks:

> What would happen if this information were disclosed?

**Criticality** asks:

> What would happen if this information became unavailable or unreliable?

A public dataset could therefore be low sensitivity but highly critical to an AI service.

---

### 7. Common Classification Model

An organization may use four levels:

```text id="m8q4v7"
PUBLIC
  ↓
INTERNAL
  ↓
CONFIDENTIAL
  ↓
RESTRICTED
```

The exact names and definitions should be established by enterprise policy.

---

### 8. Public Data

Public data is information intentionally made available to the public.

Examples:

* Public product information.
* Published company information.
* Public documentation.
* Public government information.
* Public marketing content.

However, public availability does not automatically mean unrestricted AI use.

---

### 9. Public Data and AI

A dataset can be publicly accessible but still have restrictions.

Possible considerations include:

* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* Terms of use.
* Data integrity.
* Source reliability.
* Prohibited automated collection.

Therefore:

> **Public does not automatically mean unrestricted.**

---

### 10. Internal Data

Internal data is intended for use within the organization.

Examples:

* Internal procedures.
* Operational documentation.
* Internal project information.
* Employee communications.
* Internal technical documentation.

It may be appropriate for enterprise AI but should still be protected from unauthorized access.

---

### 11. Confidential Data

Confidential information could cause meaningful harm if disclosed.

Examples may include:

* Customer information.
* Business strategy.
* Contracts.
* Financial information.
* Internal security information.
* Intellectual property.

AI processing of confidential information generally requires stronger controls.

---

### 12. Restricted Data

Restricted data requires the highest level of protection under the organization's classification model.

Examples may include:

* Highly sensitive personal information.
* Credentials.
* Cryptographic secrets.
* Highly sensitive financial information.
* Certain regulated information.
* Critical security information.

The exact definition depends on organizational policy and applicable requirements.

---

### 13. Classification and AI Use

Classification should influence whether AI processing is permitted.

Example:

| Classification | Example AI Treatment              |
| -------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Public         | General AI use may be permitted   |
| Internal       | Approved enterprise AI            |
| Confidential   | Controlled enterprise AI          |
| Restricted     | Highly restricted AI environments |

This is only a conceptual model; actual permissions must be defined by policy.

---

### 14. AI Data Handling Rules

Each classification level should have handling requirements.

For example:

```text id="q6m3r8"
PUBLIC
→ General processing may be allowed

INTERNAL
→ Authenticated enterprise access

CONFIDENTIAL
→ Restricted access + stronger protection

RESTRICTED
→ Explicit authorization + enhanced controls
```

---

### 15. Classification and AI Providers

Classification should influence whether data can be sent to an external AI provider.

Example:

```text id="v8m4q7"
Public
  ↓
Potentially External AI

Internal
  ↓
Approved Enterprise AI

Confidential
  ↓
Controlled Provider

Restricted
  ↓
Potentially Prohibited
```

The actual decision depends on contractual, legal, privacy, security, and architectural requirements.

---

### 16. Classification and Data Residency

Sensitive AI data may have geographic processing restrictions.

Classification should therefore help determine:

* Where data may be stored.
* Where it may be processed.
* Which cloud regions are permitted.
* Which providers may process it.
* Whether cross-border transfer is allowed.

---

### 17. Classification and Access Control

Classification should directly influence access control.

A simple relationship is:

```text id="r7q3m9"
Classification
      ↓
Access Policy
      ↓
Identity
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Data
```

Higher-sensitivity data generally requires stronger access restrictions.

---

### 18. Classification and Least Privilege

Least privilege means users and systems should receive only the access necessary for their responsibilities.

For example:

```text id="m9q4v7"
AI Analyst
   ↓
Internal Dataset

Security Administrator
   ↓
Restricted Security Dataset
```

Access should be based on legitimate need rather than job title alone.

---

### 19. Machine Classification

AI systems and applications may require machine-readable classifications.

Example metadata:

```text id="q8m3r6"
classification = CONFIDENTIAL
owner = Finance
purpose = Forecasting
retention = 7 years
external_ai = PROHIBITED
```

Machine-readable metadata enables automated policy enforcement.

---

### 20. Classification Metadata

Useful metadata may include:

* Classification.
* Owner.
* Business purpose.
* Data category.
* Criticality.
* Regulatory restrictions.
* Retention.
* Approved AI use.
* Approved locations.
* Approved consumers.

---

### 21. Automated Classification

Organizations can use automated tools to identify sensitive information.

Potential detection methods include:

* Pattern matching.
* Regular expressions.
* Data discovery.
* Machine learning.
* DLP engines.
* Metadata analysis.

Examples of detectable information include:

* Email addresses.
* Telephone numbers.
* Payment-card information.
* National identifiers.
* Credentials.

Automation should be validated to reduce false positives and false negatives.

---

### 22. Manual Classification

Some data cannot be reliably classified automatically.

For example:

* Strategic plans.
* Legal documents.
* Proprietary algorithms.
* Sensitive business decisions.

Human review may therefore be necessary.

---

### 23. Hybrid Classification

A mature approach combines automation and human judgment.

```text id="p4m8q7"
Data
 ↓
Automated Discovery
 ↓
Proposed Classification
 ↓
Human Review
 ↓
Approved Classification
```

This provides scalability while maintaining accountability.

---

### 24. Classification Confidence

Automated classification may produce a confidence score.

Example:

```text id="v6q3m9"
Dataset A
→ Confidential
→ Confidence: 98%

Dataset B
→ Possible Restricted
→ Confidence: 64%
→ Human Review Required
```

Low-confidence classifications should receive additional review.

---

### 25. Classification Errors

Classification systems can make two important types of errors.

**Under-classification**

Sensitive information receives a classification that is too low.

**Over-classification**

Low-risk information receives a classification that is unnecessarily high.

Both create problems.

---

### 26. Under-Classification Risk

Under-classification can result in:

* Excessive access.
* Unauthorized AI processing.
* Data leakage.
* Inappropriate external sharing.
* Insufficient retention controls.

This is generally a security and compliance concern.

---

### 27. Over-Classification Risk

Over-classification can cause:

* Excessive restrictions.
* Higher operational costs.
* Reduced data availability.
* Slower AI development.
* Unnecessary approval processes.

Therefore, classification should be **accurate and risk-based**, not simply restrictive.

---

### 28. Classification and Data Minimization

Classification helps identify whether all information in a dataset is actually necessary.

Example:

```text id="m7q4r9"
Customer Dataset
 ↓
Name
Email
Phone
Address
National ID
Payment Data
 ↓
AI Use Requires:
Name + Email
```

Unnecessary sensitive fields should be removed where practical.

---

### 29. Field-Level Classification

Entire datasets do not always have one classification.

Different fields may have different sensitivity.

Example:

| Field        | Classification |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| Product Name | Public         |
| Customer ID  | Internal       |
| Email        | Confidential   |
| National ID  | Restricted     |
| Password     | Restricted     |

This creates a need for granular classification.

---

### 30. Record-Level Classification

Individual records may also have different sensitivity.

For example, a customer dataset could contain:

```text id="q9m4r7"
Record 001 → Internal
Record 002 → Confidential
Record 003 → Restricted
```

The AI platform must be capable of applying appropriate controls where required.

---

### 31. Classification Inheritance

Derived data may inherit classification from its source.

Example:

```text id="v8m3q6"
Restricted Source
      ↓
Transformation
      ↓
Derived Dataset
      ↓
Potentially Restricted
```

Classification should not automatically disappear merely because the format changed.

---

### 32. Classification During Transformation

Transformation can sometimes reduce sensitivity.

Example:

```text id="m4q8v7"
Customer Records
      ↓
Aggregation
      ↓
Statistical Dataset
```

The resulting dataset may have lower sensitivity, but that determination should be assessed rather than assumed.

---

### 33. Classification After Aggregation

Aggregating information can sometimes reduce individual-level exposure.

However, aggregation may also create sensitive insights.

For example:

```text id="q7m3r9"
Individual Records
      ↓
Aggregation
      ↓
Employee Trend
```

The aggregated output may still reveal sensitive information.

---

### 34. Classification of AI Outputs

Classification should not stop at input data.

AI-generated outputs may contain:

* Confidential information.
* Personal information.
* Proprietary information.
* Security information.

Therefore:

> **AI outputs should be evaluated for appropriate handling based on their actual content.**

---

### 35. Output Classification

Example:

```text id="p8m4q7"
Internal Documents
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Generated Report
      ↓
Confidential
```

The output should not automatically be considered public merely because it was generated by AI.

---

### 36. Classification of RAG Content

RAG systems require careful classification because a knowledge base may contain multiple sensitivity levels.

Example:

```text id="m6q3r8"
RAG Repository
 ├── Public Documents
 ├── Internal Documents
 ├── Confidential Documents
 └── Restricted Documents
```

Retrieval controls must respect those classifications.

---

### 37. Classification-Aware Retrieval

A RAG system should ideally evaluate both:

```text id="q4m8v7"
User Authorization
        +
Document Classification
        ↓
Retrieval Decision
```

This reduces the risk of unauthorized information retrieval.

---

### 38. Classification of Embeddings

Embeddings should be governed according to the sensitivity of the underlying information.

A vector representation should not automatically be considered harmless simply because it does not display the original text directly.

---

### 39. Classification of AI Logs

AI logs may contain:

* User prompts.
* AI responses.
* Retrieved documents.
* User identifiers.
* System information.

Therefore, logs may inherit or acquire sensitive classifications.

---

### 40. Classification of Agent Memory

Agent memory can contain information accumulated over multiple interactions.

Example:

```text id="r7m3q9"
Conversation
   ↓
Agent Memory
   ↓
Long-Term Context
```

The memory store should be classified according to the information it contains.

---

### 41. Classification of Synthetic Data

Synthetic data is generated rather than directly collected from real individuals or systems.

It should not automatically be considered low risk.

Questions should include:

* Is it derived from sensitive data?
* Can individuals be reconstructed?
* Does it reproduce confidential information?
* Does it contain secrets?
* What was used to generate it?

---

### 42. Classification of Test Data

Test data can contain production-derived information.

Example:

```text id="m8q4r6"
Production Data
      ↓
Test Dataset
      ↓
Potentially Confidential
```

The test environment should not be treated as low risk merely because it is non-production.

---

### 43. Classification of Backup Data

Backups must retain appropriate protection.

If a production dataset is confidential:

```text id="q6m3v8"
Confidential Data
      ↓
Backup
      ↓
Confidential Protection
```

Changing the storage location does not automatically change the sensitivity.

---

### 44. Classification and Data Retention

Classification should influence retention requirements.

Higher-risk information may require:

* Shorter retention.
* Stronger deletion controls.
* More frequent reviews.

However, retention must also consider legal and business requirements.

---

### 45. Classification and Data Disposal

When data is no longer required, classification should influence disposal methods.

For example:

```text id="v9m4q7"
Public
→ Standard Disposal

Confidential
→ Controlled Deletion

Restricted
→ Enhanced Secure Disposal
```

---

### 46. Classification and DLP

Data Loss Prevention systems can use classification metadata to determine what information may leave the organization.

Example:

```text id="p7m3q9"
Restricted Data
      ↓
DLP Policy
      ↓
External AI Provider
      ↓
BLOCK
```

---

### 47. Classification and Encryption

Classification can determine encryption requirements.

For example:

```text id="q8m4r6"
Public
→ Standard Protection

Confidential
→ Strong Encryption

Restricted
→ Strong Encryption + Enhanced Key Controls
```

The actual requirements should be defined by enterprise standards.

---

### 48. Classification and Monitoring

Sensitive data should generally receive greater monitoring attention.

Monitoring may include:

* Access.
* Transfers.
* Downloads.
* API calls.
* Retrieval activity.
* Administrative changes.

---

### 49. Classification Review

Classification should be periodically reviewed.

Reasons include:

* Business changes.
* Regulatory changes.
* New AI use cases.
* Data aggregation.
* Changes in sensitivity.
* New threats.

---

### 50. AI Data Classification Foundation Model

The first stage of AI data classification can be summarized as:

```text id="x5q9m3"
                 AI DATA
                    │
                    ▼
              DATA DISCOVERY
                    │
                    ▼
             DATA CATEGORIZATION
                    │
                    ▼
               CLASSIFICATION
                    │
          ┌─────────┼─────────┐
          ↓         ↓         ↓
      Sensitivity Criticality Regulatory
          │         │         │
          └─────────┼─────────┘
                    ↓
             HANDLING RULES
                    ↓
            SECURITY CONTROLS
                    ↓
              AI USAGE POLICY
                    ↓
              MONITORING
                    ↓
              PERIODIC REVIEW
```

The central GRC principle is:

> **AI data classification establishes the decision point between knowing what data an AI system handles and knowing how that data must actually be governed and protected.**

A mature classification program therefore goes beyond applying labels. It connects **data sensitivity and criticality to authorization, AI usage, security controls, privacy requirements, retention, monitoring, and disposal**, ensuring that the protection applied to AI data is proportionate to the risk it creates.
