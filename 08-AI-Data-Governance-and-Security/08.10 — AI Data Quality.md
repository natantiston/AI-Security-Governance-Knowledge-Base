# 08.10 — AI Data Quality

## Part 1

### 1. AI Data Quality

**AI data quality** is the degree to which data used by an AI system is fit for its intended purpose.

AI systems depend heavily on the quality of the data they consume.

Poor-quality data can lead to:

* Incorrect predictions.
* Unreliable classifications.
* Biased outcomes.
* Model instability.
* Poor recommendations.
* Security weaknesses.
* Compliance failures.

A useful principle is:

> **An AI system cannot reliably produce high-quality results from systematically poor-quality data.**

---

### 2. Why AI Data Quality Matters

Traditional applications may fail visibly when incorrect data is introduced.

AI systems can behave differently.

They may continue operating while producing plausible but incorrect results.

```text
Poor Data
   ↓
AI Model
   ↓
Plausible Output
   ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

This makes AI data-quality problems particularly dangerous.

---

### 3. Data Quality as an AI Governance Control

Data quality should not be treated only as a data-engineering concern.

From a GRC perspective, it is a control area.

```text
Data Quality
     ↓
Model Reliability
     ↓
AI Risk
     ↓
Business Risk
     ↓
Governance
```

Therefore, AI governance should establish measurable data-quality requirements.

---

### 4. Data Quality Dimensions

Common data-quality dimensions include:

1. Accuracy.
2. Completeness.
3. Consistency.
4. Timeliness.
5. Validity.
6. Uniqueness.
7. Relevance.
8. Integrity.
9. Representativeness.
10. Traceability.

Not every AI use case requires the same weighting across these dimensions.

---

### 5. Accuracy

**Accuracy** measures whether the data correctly represents the real-world value it is intended to represent.

Example:

```text
Actual Customer Age: 42
Dataset Age:         42
```

Accurate.

But:

```text
Actual Customer Age: 42
Dataset Age:         72
```

The data is inaccurate.

---

### 6. AI Accuracy vs Data Accuracy

These concepts should not be confused.

**Data accuracy:**

> Is the input data correct?

**Model accuracy:**

> Does the model produce correct predictions?

Example:

```text
Accurate Data
     ↓
Poor Model
     ↓
Poor Prediction
```

Therefore, high-quality data does not automatically guarantee a high-performing model.

---

### 7. Completeness

**Completeness** measures whether required information is present.

Example:

```text
Customer ID       ✓
Customer Name     ✓
Email             ✓
Date of Birth     ✗
```

If Date of Birth is required for the AI use case, the record is incomplete.

---

### 8. Missing Data

Missing information can affect AI performance.

Example:

```text
Training Dataset

Income:
100%
Age:
98%
Employment:
72%
Location:
95%
```

The 28% missing employment information may materially affect a model if employment status is an important feature.

---

### 9. Missingness Is Not Always Random

Missing data may itself contain information.

For example:

```text
High-income customers
→ frequently provide income

Low-income customers
→ frequently omit income
```

The missingness pattern can therefore introduce bias.

---

### 10. Types of Missing Data

Common categories include:

* Missing completely at random.
* Missing at random.
* Missing not at random.

The distinction matters because different missing-data patterns require different treatment.

---

### 11. Consistency

**Consistency** means that data does not conflict across systems or records.

Example:

```text
CRM:
Customer Status = Active

Data Warehouse:
Customer Status = Inactive
```

This inconsistency can create problems for downstream AI systems.

---

### 12. Cross-System Consistency

Enterprise AI frequently combines information from multiple systems.

```text
CRM
   +
ERP
   +
Billing
   +
Support
      ↓
Unified AI Dataset
```

Data quality controls should identify conflicting values before they reach the AI system.

---

### 13. Timeliness

**Timeliness** measures whether data is sufficiently current for its intended purpose.

Example:

```text
Fraud Detection
   ↓
Data from 5 minutes ago
```

may be appropriate.

But:

```text
Fraud Detection
   ↓
Data from 6 months ago
```

may be unacceptable.

---

### 14. Data Freshness

Freshness measures how recently the data was updated.

Example:

```text
Dataset:
Customer Transactions

Last Updated:
08:00

Current Time:
08:05

Freshness:
5 minutes
```

Freshness requirements should be defined according to the AI use case.

---

### 15. Validity

**Validity** measures whether data conforms to defined rules.

Example:

```text
Email:
john@example.com
```

Valid according to a basic email-format rule.

But:

```text
Email:
john@unknown
```

may fail validation depending on the organization's rules.

---

### 16. Domain Validity

Some values must belong to an approved domain.

Example:

```text
Customer Risk:
LOW
MEDIUM
HIGH
```

If the dataset contains:

```text
Customer Risk:
EXTREME
```

the value may be invalid.

---

### 17. Format Validity

AI pipelines often expect specific formats.

Example:

```text
Date:
2026-08-31
```

A source providing:

```text
31/08/26
```

may require transformation.

Incorrect parsing could result in incorrect AI features.

---

### 18. Uniqueness

**Uniqueness** ensures that records or identifiers are not unintentionally duplicated.

Example:

```text
Customer ID 1001
Customer ID 1001
Customer ID 1001
```

If these represent the same customer, duplicate records may distort model training.

---

### 19. Duplicate Data

Duplicate records can cause:

* Overrepresentation.
* Training bias.
* Incorrect frequency calculations.
* Distorted statistics.
* Inflated dataset size.

Example:

```text
Original:
Customer A → 1 record

After accidental duplication:
Customer A → 10 records
```

The model may effectively give Customer A's characteristics excessive weight.

---

### 20. Relevance

Data may be technically correct but irrelevant to the AI purpose.

Example:

```text
AI Use Case:
Predict equipment failure

Dataset:
Employee favorite color
```

The data may be accurate but have little or no legitimate relevance to the intended use case.

---

### 21. Data Quality Is Purpose-Dependent

The same dataset can be suitable for one purpose and unsuitable for another.

Example:

```text
Historical Sales Data
```

may be:

* Appropriate for historical analysis.
* Inadequate for real-time fraud detection.
* Potentially outdated for current customer behavior modeling.

Therefore:

> **Data quality must always be evaluated against intended use.**

---

### 22. Fitness for Purpose

A practical AI data-quality question is:

> **Is this data fit for the specific AI purpose?**

This includes assessing:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Timeliness.
* Relevance.
* Representativeness.
* Validity.
* Integrity.

---

### 23. Data Quality Requirements

Before an AI system uses a dataset, requirements should be established.

Example:

```text
Training Dataset Requirements

Accuracy       ≥ 98%
Completeness   ≥ 95%
Duplicates     < 0.5%
Freshness      < 30 days
Invalid Values < 1%
```

The actual thresholds should be determined by the business and risk requirements.

---

### 24. Data Quality Rules

Rules convert expectations into measurable controls.

Example:

```text
Rule:
Customer_ID must not be NULL.

Rule:
Country must belong to approved country list.

Rule:
Transaction_Date cannot be in the future.
```

These rules can be automated.

---

### 25. Automated Data Quality Validation

A pipeline can perform validation before data reaches an AI environment.

```text
Source Data
     ↓
Quality Validation
     ↓
PASS ─────→ AI Dataset
     │
     └─ FAIL → Quarantine / Remediation
```

This creates a preventive control.

---

### 26. Data Quality Gates

A **data quality gate** prevents unacceptable data from progressing.

Example:

```text
                    DATA QUALITY GATE
                           │
             ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
             ▼                           ▼
           PASS                         FAIL
             │                           │
             ▼                           ▼
       AI Pipeline                 Quarantine
                                         ↓
                                    Investigation
```

Quality gates are particularly important before model training and production inference.

---

### 27. Training Data Quality Gate

Before training:

```text
Training Dataset
       ↓
Quality Checks
       ↓
Completeness
Accuracy
Duplicates
Bias Indicators
Validity
       ↓
Approval
       ↓
Training
```

Training should not automatically proceed simply because a dataset exists.

---

### 28. Validation Data Quality

Validation datasets should also undergo quality controls.

A poor validation dataset can produce misleading conclusions about model performance.

```text
Poor Validation Data
       ↓
Incorrect Evaluation
       ↓
False Confidence
       ↓
Production Risk
```

---

### 29. Test Data Quality

Test data must be sufficiently reliable to support independent evaluation.

Important considerations include:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Representativeness.
* Independence.
* Integrity.

A flawed test dataset can make a weak model appear reliable.

---

### 30. Inference Data Quality

Production AI systems also require data-quality controls.

Example:

```text
User Request
     ↓
Inference Data
     ↓
Validation
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Output
```

Bad production inputs can result in unreliable outputs even when the model itself has not changed.

---

### 31. Input Validation

AI applications should validate inputs where appropriate.

Examples:

* Data type.
* Length.
* Range.
* Format.
* Allowed values.
* Required fields.

This reduces the probability of invalid data reaching the model.

---

### 32. Outlier Detection

Outliers may indicate:

* Genuine unusual behavior.
* Data-entry errors.
* Sensor failures.
* Fraud.
* Data corruption.
* Distribution changes.

Example:

```text
Normal Transaction:
€10 – €5,000

Observed:
€8,500,000
```

The value should be investigated rather than automatically assumed to be incorrect.

---

### 33. Outliers Are Not Automatically Errors

An important governance principle is:

> **An unusual value is not necessarily an incorrect value.**

For example:

```text
Normal Purchase:
€50

Luxury Purchase:
€500,000
```

The €500,000 transaction may be legitimate.

Therefore, quality controls should identify anomalies for assessment rather than blindly delete them.

---

### 34. Data Distribution

AI data quality should include distribution analysis.

Example:

```text
Training Data:
Age 20–60

Production Data:
Age 60–90
```

The production distribution differs significantly from training.

This may affect model performance.

---

### 35. Distribution Shift

**Distribution shift** occurs when the characteristics of incoming data change over time.

```text
Training Distribution
        ↓
Model
        ↓
Production Distribution
        ↓
Significant Difference
```

This can become an AI operational risk.

---

### 36. Data Drift

Data drift refers to changes in input data characteristics.

Examples:

* Customer demographics change.
* Transaction behavior changes.
* Sensor patterns change.
* Market conditions change.

Monitoring data drift helps identify when a model may no longer be operating under expected conditions.

---

### 37. Concept Drift

**Concept drift** occurs when the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

Example:

```text
Historical:
Feature A → High Risk

Current:
Feature A → Low Risk
```

The data may still be accurate, but the underlying relationship has changed.

---

### 38. Data Quality vs Data Drift

These should not be confused.

**Data quality problem:**

```text
Age = "ABC"
```

**Data drift:**

```text
Average customer age:
35 → 52
```

The second may represent valid data but a changing population.

---

### 39. Representativeness

AI training data should adequately represent the population or environment relevant to the intended use.

Example:

```text
Target Population:
Global Customers

Training Data:
95% One Country
5% Other Countries
```

The dataset may be large but poorly representative.

---

### 40. Representation Gaps

A representation gap occurs when important groups or conditions are insufficiently represented.

Examples:

* Geographic groups.
* Customer segments.
* Languages.
* Device types.
* Operating conditions.
* Transaction types.

Such gaps may affect model performance and fairness.

---

### 41. Sampling Quality

How data is collected matters.

A dataset created from only one convenient source may not represent the full population.

Example:

```text
Entire Customer Population
          ↓
Random / Controlled Sampling
          ↓
Training Dataset
```

versus:

```text
Entire Customer Population
          ↓
Only Most Active Customers
          ↓
Training Dataset
```

The second approach can introduce sampling bias.

---

### 42. Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when the method of selecting data systematically favors certain observations.

Example:

```text
Customer Survey
↓
Only Customers Who Responded
```

Customers who do not respond may have materially different characteristics.

---

### 43. Label Quality

Many supervised AI systems depend on labeled data.

Example:

```text
Email
 ↓
Label:
Spam / Not Spam
```

If labels are incorrect, the model learns incorrect relationships.

Therefore:

> **Label quality is a critical component of training-data quality.**

---

### 44. Label Accuracy

Suppose:

```text
Actual:
Fraud

Label:
Legitimate
```

The training system receives an incorrect signal.

A high number of label errors can materially reduce model performance.

---

### 45. Label Consistency

Different annotators may classify the same information differently.

Example:

```text
Annotator A:
Fraud

Annotator B:
Not Fraud
```

Organizations should establish clear labeling standards.

---

### 46. Annotation Guidelines

Annotation processes should define:

* Classification rules.
* Examples.
* Edge cases.
* Escalation procedures.
* Quality thresholds.
* Reviewer responsibilities.

This improves consistency.

---

### 47. Inter-Annotator Agreement

Where human labeling is involved, organizations can measure agreement between annotators.

Example:

```text
Annotator A vs Annotator B

Agreement:
94%
```

Low agreement may indicate:

* Ambiguous definitions.
* Poor training.
* Complex cases.
* Weak labeling standards.

---

### 48. Data Quality Sampling

Organizations do not always need to manually inspect every record.

They can use statistical sampling.

```text
1,000,000 Records
        ↓
Representative Sample
        ↓
Manual Quality Review
        ↓
Estimated Quality
```

Sampling should be designed appropriately for the risk and purpose.

---

### 49. Data Profiling

**Data profiling** examines the structure and characteristics of a dataset.

Typical profiling includes:

* Null percentage.
* Unique values.
* Min/max.
* Mean/median.
* Distribution.
* Duplicate rate.
* Invalid values.
* Outliers.

Example:

```text
Field: Customer_Age

Nulls:       1.2%
Duplicates:  N/A
Min:         18
Max:         96
Invalid:     0.3%
```

---

### 50. AI Data Quality Baseline

Before deploying an AI system, organizations should establish a baseline.

Example:

```text
Dataset:
Customer Risk Training Data

Accuracy:        98.7%
Completeness:    97.2%
Validity:        99.1%
Duplicates:       0.4%
Freshness:       14 days
Representation:  Approved
```

This baseline provides a reference point for ongoing monitoring.

The fundamental GRC principle is:

> **AI data quality should be defined as measurable fitness for purpose, governed through explicit requirements, validated through controls, and continuously monitored throughout the AI data lifecycle.**

## Part 2

### 51. Data Quality Thresholds

Data-quality requirements should be translated into measurable thresholds.

For example:

```text
Completeness     ≥ 95%
Accuracy         ≥ 98%
Validity         ≥ 99%
Duplicate Rate   ≤ 0.5%
Freshness        ≤ 24 hours
```

The thresholds should reflect the AI use case and its risk.

A low-risk analytics model may tolerate more variation than an AI system supporting high-impact decisions.

---

### 52. Risk-Based Data Quality

Data quality should be proportional to AI risk.

```text
Low-Risk AI
    ↓
Basic Quality Controls

Medium-Risk AI
    ↓
Enhanced Validation

High-Risk AI
    ↓
Strict Quality Gates
+ Continuous Monitoring
+ Evidence
```

This avoids applying expensive controls uniformly to every dataset.

---

### 53. Data Quality Service-Level Objectives

Organizations can establish **Data Quality SLOs**.

Example:

> Critical inference datasets must maintain at least 98% completeness and 99% validity.

The SLO can then become a measurable governance requirement.

```text
Requirement
    ↓
Metric
    ↓
Threshold
    ↓
Monitoring
    ↓
Exception
```

---

### 54. Data Quality KPIs

Useful KPIs include:

* Percentage of datasets meeting quality thresholds.
* Percentage of records passing validation.
* Duplicate rate.
* Missing-value rate.
* Invalid-value rate.
* Average remediation time.
* Number of recurring quality incidents.
* Percentage of critical datasets monitored.

---

### 55. Data Quality KRIs

**Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** can identify deteriorating conditions.

Examples:

* Critical dataset completeness below threshold.
* Increasing invalid-record percentage.
* Significant distribution shift.
* Increasing data-quality incidents.
* Unresolved quality exceptions.
* Production data significantly different from training data.

---

### 56. Quality Score

An organization may calculate a composite quality score.

For example:

```text
Quality Score =
Accuracy
+ Completeness
+ Validity
+ Timeliness
+ Consistency
```

The exact calculation should be documented.

A score should not hide serious weaknesses in an individual dimension.

---

### 57. Dimension-Level Thresholds

A single overall score may be misleading.

Consider:

```text
Accuracy:       99%
Completeness:   99%
Validity:       99%
Sensitive PII:  40% incorrectly classified
```

The overall score might appear acceptable while the classification problem creates significant security or privacy risk.

Therefore, critical dimensions should have independent thresholds.

---

### 58. Data Quality Exceptions

Not every quality failure requires immediate rejection.

An exception process should define:

* Reason.
* Business justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Compensating controls.
* Owner.
* Expiration date.
* Approval authority.

Example:

```text
Quality Failure
      ↓
Exception Assessment
      ↓
Approved?
 ┌────┴────┐
Yes       No
 ↓         ↓
Controlled Reject
Use
```

---

### 59. Quality Exception Register

A GRC team can maintain:

| Exception | Dataset     | Issue               | Risk   | Owner      | Expiry  |
| --------- | ----------- | ------------------- | ------ | ---------- | ------- |
| DQ-001    | Customer AI | 7% missing values   | Medium | Data Owner | 30 days |
| DQ-002    | Fraud ML    | Label inconsistency | High   | ML Lead    | 14 days |

Expired exceptions should not remain indefinitely.

---

### 60. Data Quality Remediation

When data fails quality controls, remediation may include:

* Correction.
* Standardization.
* Deduplication.
* Enrichment.
* Reprocessing.
* Removal.
* Quarantine.
* Source-system correction.

The preferred solution is often to correct the problem at its source rather than repeatedly fixing downstream copies.

---

### 61. Root Cause Analysis

Repeated data-quality problems should trigger root-cause analysis.

Example:

```text
Incorrect Data
     ↓
Investigation
     ↓
Source System
     ↓
Incorrect Validation Rule
     ↓
Process Defect
     ↓
Permanent Correction
```

Simply cleaning the dataset may not address the underlying problem.

---

### 62. Data Quality Incident Management

Significant data-quality failures should be handled through an incident process.

Example:

```text
Detection
   ↓
Classification
   ↓
Containment
   ↓
Impact Assessment
   ↓
Remediation
   ↓
Validation
   ↓
Closure
```

The process should integrate with cybersecurity and AI incident-management procedures where appropriate.

---

### 63. AI Data Quality Incident

Example scenario:

A production AI system begins receiving malformed customer information.

```text
Source System
     ↓
Malformed Data
     ↓
AI Pipeline
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Incorrect Output
```

The organization should determine:

* When the issue began.
* Which data was affected.
* Which models were affected.
* Whether decisions were impacted.
* Whether remediation is required.

---

### 64. Quality Incident Severity

A practical classification could be:

| Severity | Example                                                                      |
| -------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low      | Minor non-critical formatting issue                                          |
| Medium   | Quality degradation in non-critical AI                                       |
| High     | Material impact on model performance                                         |
| Critical | Incorrect AI decisions affecting significant business or regulatory outcomes |

Severity should be defined by organizational risk criteria.

---

### 65. Data Quarantine

A dataset or subset of records may be quarantined when quality cannot be trusted.

```text
Incoming Data
      ↓
Validation
      ↓
FAIL
      ↓
QUARANTINE
      ↓
Investigation
```

Quarantine prevents potentially defective data from contaminating downstream systems.

---

### 66. Fail-Open vs Fail-Closed

AI pipelines should define what happens when quality checks fail.

**Fail closed:**

```text
Quality Failure
      ↓
Stop Processing
```

**Fail open:**

```text
Quality Failure
      ↓
Continue Processing
```

High-risk AI systems may require stronger fail-closed behavior for critical quality conditions.

---

### 67. Quality Gate Design

Not every quality rule should necessarily stop the pipeline.

For example:

```text
Critical Failure
→ Block

Warning
→ Continue + Alert

Informational
→ Log
```

This creates a risk-based quality-gate architecture.

---

### 68. Data Quality Monitoring

Monitoring should continue after initial validation.

```text
Data
 ↓
Quality Check
 ↓
AI System
 ↓
Continuous Monitoring
```

This is important because data quality can degrade over time.

---

### 69. Continuous Data Quality

A production AI pipeline should monitor important dimensions continuously or at an appropriate frequency.

Example:

```text
08:00 → 99.1%
09:00 → 98.8%
10:00 → 97.4%
11:00 → 92.1%
```

A sudden decline may trigger an alert.

---

### 70. Quality Trend Analysis

A single quality measurement can be misleading.

Consider:

```text
January:   99%
February:  98%
March:     96%
April:     92%
```

The trend indicates deteriorating quality even if April has not yet reached the formal failure threshold.

Trend monitoring provides earlier warning.

---

### 71. Data Quality Alerts

Alerts can be triggered by:

* Threshold violations.
* Sudden changes.
* Missing pipelines.
* Unexpected volumes.
* Schema changes.
* Invalid values.
* Distribution changes.

Example:

```text
Completeness < 95%
        ↓
Alert
        ↓
Data Owner
        ↓
Investigation
```

---

### 72. Data Volume Monitoring

Unexpected changes in data volume can indicate quality problems.

Example:

```text
Expected:
1,000,000 records/day

Actual:
45,000 records/day
```

Possible causes include:

* Pipeline failure.
* Source outage.
* Filtering error.
* Schema change.
* Data loss.

---

### 73. Volume Anomaly Detection

Conversely:

```text
Expected:
1,000,000 records/day

Actual:
18,000,000 records/day
```

may indicate:

* Duplicate ingestion.
* Replay.
* Incorrect filtering.
* Source-system error.
* Data injection.

Volume should therefore be treated as a quality signal.

---

### 74. Distribution Monitoring

Quality monitoring should consider distributions.

Example:

```text
Training:
Age 20–80

Production:
Age 20–25 only
```

The production distribution has materially changed.

Even if every record is syntactically valid, the data may no longer be suitable for the model.

---

### 75. Statistical Data Quality

Statistical methods can identify:

* Distribution shifts.
* Outliers.
* Unexpected correlations.
* Missingness patterns.
* Sampling anomalies.

This is particularly valuable for large AI datasets where manual review is impractical.

---

### 76. Data Quality and Machine Learning

Machine-learning systems can amplify data problems.

Example:

```text
Small Data Bias
       ↓
Training
       ↓
Learned Pattern
       ↓
Large-Scale Predictions
```

A relatively small training-data defect can therefore affect many downstream predictions.

---

### 77. Data Quality and Model Performance

Organizations should correlate data-quality metrics with model performance.

Example:

```text
Data Completeness ↓
        ↓
Model Accuracy ↓
```

This helps demonstrate whether a quality problem is materially affecting the AI system.

---

### 78. Quality-Performance Correlation

A governance dashboard might show:

```text
Data Quality:       97%
Model Accuracy:     94%

Data Quality:       91%
Model Accuracy:     86%
```

This can help identify relationships between data conditions and AI performance.

Correlation does not automatically establish causation, but it provides an important investigation signal.

---

### 79. Data Quality and Model Retraining

If data quality changes materially, retraining may be necessary.

Possible process:

```text
Quality Degradation
       ↓
Impact Assessment
       ↓
Model Review
       ↓
Retraining Decision
       ↓
Validation
       ↓
Deployment
```

Retraining should not be an automatic response to every data-quality issue.

---

### 80. Training Data Quality Review

Before training, organizations should assess:

```text
Source
 ↓
Collection Method
 ↓
Quality
 ↓
Representativeness
 ↓
Labels
 ↓
Transformation
 ↓
Training Dataset
```

This provides assurance that the training material is fit for purpose.

---

### 81. Data Quality and Training Bias

Poor-quality data can introduce or amplify bias.

Examples:

* Missing demographic groups.
* Incorrect labels.
* Unequal sampling.
* Historical inaccuracies.
* Measurement differences.

Therefore, data quality and AI fairness are closely related.

---

### 82. Measurement Error

A dataset can contain systematic measurement errors.

Example:

```text
Actual Income:
€50,000

Recorded Income:
€35,000
```

If the error disproportionately affects a particular group, it can create both quality and fairness problems.

---

### 83. Historical Data Quality

Historical datasets may contain:

* Outdated classifications.
* Legacy business rules.
* Incomplete records.
* Historical system errors.
* Changed definitions.

Older data should not automatically be considered reliable simply because it has been used historically.

---

### 84. Data Versioning

AI datasets should be versioned where appropriate.

Example:

```text
Customer Dataset v1.0
Customer Dataset v1.1
Customer Dataset v2.0
```

Versioning allows organizations to identify which exact dataset was used for a training run.

---

### 85. Dataset Reproducibility

A model should ideally be reproducible from its recorded inputs.

```text
Model v3.1
   ↓
Training Run #452
   ↓
Dataset v7.4
   ↓
Data Snapshot
```

This is important for:

* Audits.
* Investigations.
* Model validation.
* Incident response.

---

### 86. Data Snapshot

A snapshot captures the dataset state at a specific point in time.

Example:

```text
Dataset:
Customer Risk

Snapshot:
2026-08-31 00:00 UTC

Version:
v8.3
```

This provides a stable reference for model development and testing.

---

### 87. Data Quality and Lineage

Data quality becomes significantly more useful when combined with lineage.

```text
Quality Issue
     ↓
Lineage
     ↓
Source
     ↓
Transformation
     ↓
Affected Dataset
     ↓
Affected Model
```

This allows teams to move from detection to impact analysis.

---

### 88. Quality Root Cause Through Lineage

Example:

```text
AI Feature Quality ↓
       ↑

       ↑
```

### 102. Quality at Data Collection

Quality begins at the point where data is generated or collected.

Controls may include:

* Input validation.
* Required-field enforcement.
* Source authentication.
* Standardized formats.
* Controlled collection methods.
* Timestamp validation.
* Source-system quality checks.

The earlier an error is detected, the cheaper it is generally to remediate.

---

### 103. Quality at Data Ingestion

During ingestion, organizations should verify:

* Schema compatibility.
* Data types.
* Record counts.
* Encoding.
* Required fields.
* Source identity.
* Transfer completeness.

Example:

```text id="k2v8mx"
Source
  ↓
Ingestion
  ↓
Schema Validation
  ↓
Record Count Validation
  ↓
Quality Gate
```

---

### 104. Schema Validation

A schema defines the expected structure of data.

Example:

```text id="9f4qtm"
Customer_ID     → String
Age             → Integer
Country         → String
Transaction     → Decimal
Timestamp       → DateTime
```

If the source suddenly sends:

```text id="j5w8qn"
Age → "Forty-Two"
```

the ingestion control should identify the mismatch.

---

### 105. Schema Drift

Schema drift occurs when the structure of incoming data changes.

Example:

```text id="n3p7vx"
Previous:
Customer_ID
Name
Age

New:
Customer_ID
Name
Age
Risk_Score
```

The new field may be harmless, but uncontrolled schema changes can break downstream AI pipelines.

---

### 106. Schema Drift Detection

A mature pipeline should detect:

* Added fields.
* Removed fields.
* Renamed fields.
* Changed data types.
* Changed constraints.
* Changed field meaning.

This is particularly important when AI systems depend on automated pipelines.

---

### 107. Semantic Drift

A field can retain the same name while its meaning changes.

Example:

```text id="x7m4qz"
Field:
Customer_Status
```

Historically:

```text Active = Customer with transaction in last 12 months
```

Later:

```text Active = Customer with transaction in last 90 days
```

The schema did not change, but the meaning did.

This is **semantic drift**.

---

### 108. Data Transformation Quality

AI data frequently passes through multiple transformations.

```text id="m8q2vw"
Raw Data
   ↓
Cleaning
   ↓
Normalization
   ↓
Feature Engineering
   ↓
Training Dataset
```

Each transformation can introduce errors.

Therefore, quality must be checked both before and after important transformations.

---

### 109. Transformation Validation

A transformation should preserve intended information.

Example:

```text id="r4v8kp"

Transformation:

Expected:
125050
```

If the output becomes:

```text id="q7m3xz"
12505
```

the transformation introduced an error.

---

### 110. Data Conversion Errors

Conversion problems can occur when moving between:

* Integer and decimal.
* Local and UTC timestamps.
* Different currencies.
* Different character encodings.
* Different measurement units.

Example:

```text id="z5m8qr"
Kilograms
      ↓
Pounds
      ↓
Incorrect Conversion
      ↓
Incorrect Feature
```

Such errors can propagate into AI predictions.

---

### 111. Unit Consistency

AI datasets should use controlled units.

Example:

```text id="p3x7vn"

Celsius

Weight:
Kilograms

Distance:
Kilometers
```

Mixing units can create significant model errors.

---

### 112. Time-Zone Quality

Time-sensitive AI applications must account for time zones.

Example:

```text id="c8q2mw"
Source A:
2026-08-31 09:00 UTC

Source B:
2026-08-31 09:00 CET
```

These timestamps represent different actual moments.

Incorrect time-zone handling can distort event sequencing and temporal models.

---

### 113. Temporal Consistency

Data should maintain logical time relationships.

Example:

```text id="y4v8qk"
Account Created:
2026-08-20

Transaction:
2026-08-15
```

This may indicate:

* Incorrect timestamps.
* Incorrect customer matching.
* Data migration issues.

Temporal validation can identify such anomalies.

---

### 114. Referential Integrity

Data relationships should remain valid.

Example:

```text id="h7m3xq"
Transaction
   ↓
Customer_ID = 10452
```

If Customer 10452 does not exist in the customer master dataset, referential integrity has failed.

---


Data cleansing can include:

* Correcting known errors.

### 120. Quality and Reproducibility

```text id="x3v7mq"
     +

     ↓
```

### 121. Training Data Leakage

Data quality must consider unintended leakage between datasets.

Example:

```text id="q8m4vx"

     ↘
      Customer Record
     ↗
Test Data
```

If information appears in both training and test datasets when it should not, evaluation may be overly optimistic.

---

### 122. Target Leakage

**Target leakage** occurs when training data contains information that would not legitimately be available at prediction time.

Example:

```text id="k7m3xz"
Goal:
Predict Loan Default

Feature:
Final Default Status
```

The model has effectively been given the answer.

This is a data-quality and model-governance problem.

---

### 123. Temporal Leakage

Temporal leakage occurs when future information enters a dataset intended to represent past information.

Example:

```text id="p5v8mq"
Prediction Date:
January 1

Feature:
Information created:
January 15
```

The model should not have access to information created after the prediction point.

---

### 124. Data Leakage Testing

Quality assurance should examine:

* Temporal relationships.
* Feature availability.
* Dataset overlap.
* Target-related fields.
* Future information.
* Derived variables.

This should occur before model training and validation.

### 237. Final AI Data Quality Model

The complete concept can be reduced to:


The essential principle is:

> **AI data quality is the continuous governance, measurement, validation, and assurance of data to ensure that it remains accurate, complete, valid, consistent, timely, relevant, representative, and fit for the AI system's intended purpose.**

For an AI GRC professional, the objective is not to guarantee that every record is perfect.

The objective is to ensure that:

**1. Quality expectations are defined.**
**2. Quality is measurable.**

**4. Quality failures are detected and managed.**
**5. Business and AI risks are understood.**
**6. Accountability is clearly assigned.**
**7. Evidence exists to demonstrate control effectiveness.**

That is what turns **AI data quality** from a technical data-management activity into an **enterprise AI governance and risk-management capability**.
