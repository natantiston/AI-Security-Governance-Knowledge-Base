# 08.10 — AI Data Quality

## Part 1

### 1. AI Data Quality

**AI data quality** is the degree to which data used by an AI system is fit for its intended purpose.

AI systems depend heavily on the quality of the data they consume.

Poor-quality data can lead to:

* Incorrect predictions.
* Unreliable classifications.
* Biased outcomes.
* Model instability.
* Poor recommendations.
* Security weaknesses.
* Compliance failures.

A useful principle is:

> **An AI system cannot reliably produce high-quality results from systematically poor-quality data.**

---

### 2. Why AI Data Quality Matters

Traditional applications may fail visibly when incorrect data is introduced.

AI systems can behave differently.

They may continue operating while producing plausible but incorrect results.

```text
Poor Data
   ↓
AI Model
   ↓
Plausible Output
   ↓
Incorrect Decision
```

This makes AI data-quality problems particularly dangerous.

---

### 3. Data Quality as an AI Governance Control

Data quality should not be treated only as a data-engineering concern.

From a GRC perspective, it is a control area.

```text
Data Quality
     ↓
Model Reliability
     ↓
AI Risk
     ↓
Business Risk
     ↓
Governance
```

Therefore, AI governance should establish measurable data-quality requirements.

---

### 4. Data Quality Dimensions

Common data-quality dimensions include:

1. Accuracy.
2. Completeness.
3. Consistency.
4. Timeliness.
5. Validity.
6. Uniqueness.
7. Relevance.
8. Integrity.
9. Representativeness.
10. Traceability.

Not every AI use case requires the same weighting across these dimensions.

---

### 5. Accuracy

**Accuracy** measures whether the data correctly represents the real-world value it is intended to represent.

Example:

```text
Actual Customer Age: 42
Dataset Age:         42
```

Accurate.

But:

```text
Actual Customer Age: 42
Dataset Age:         72
```

The data is inaccurate.

---

### 6. AI Accuracy vs Data Accuracy

These concepts should not be confused.

**Data accuracy:**

> Is the input data correct?

**Model accuracy:**

> Does the model produce correct predictions?

Example:

```text
Accurate Data
     ↓
Poor Model
     ↓
Poor Prediction
```

Therefore, high-quality data does not automatically guarantee a high-performing model.

---

### 7. Completeness

**Completeness** measures whether required information is present.

Example:

```text
Customer ID       ✓
Customer Name     ✓
Email             ✓
Date of Birth     ✗
```

If Date of Birth is required for the AI use case, the record is incomplete.

---

### 8. Missing Data

Missing information can affect AI performance.

Example:

```text
Training Dataset

Income:
100%
Age:
98%
Employment:
72%
Location:
95%
```

The 28% missing employment information may materially affect a model if employment status is an important feature.

---

### 9. Missingness Is Not Always Random

Missing data may itself contain information.

For example:

```text
High-income customers
→ frequently provide income

Low-income customers
→ frequently omit income
```

The missingness pattern can therefore introduce bias.

---

### 10. Types of Missing Data

Common categories include:

* Missing completely at random.
* Missing at random.
* Missing not at random.

The distinction matters because different missing-data patterns require different treatment.

---

### 11. Consistency

**Consistency** means that data does not conflict across systems or records.

Example:

```text
CRM:
Customer Status = Active

Data Warehouse:
Customer Status = Inactive
```

This inconsistency can create problems for downstream AI systems.

---

### 12. Cross-System Consistency

Enterprise AI frequently combines information from multiple systems.

```text
CRM
   +
ERP
   +
Billing
   +
Support
      ↓
Unified AI Dataset
```

Data quality controls should identify conflicting values before they reach the AI system.

---

### 13. Timeliness

**Timeliness** measures whether data is sufficiently current for its intended purpose.

Example:

```text
Fraud Detection
   ↓
Data from 5 minutes ago
```

may be appropriate.

But:

```text
Fraud Detection
   ↓
Data from 6 months ago
```

may be unacceptable.

---

### 14. Data Freshness

Freshness measures how recently the data was updated.

Example:

```text
Dataset:
Customer Transactions

Last Updated:
08:00

Current Time:
08:05

Freshness:
5 minutes
```

Freshness requirements should be defined according to the AI use case.

---

### 15. Validity

**Validity** measures whether data conforms to defined rules.

Example:

```text
Email:
john@example.com
```

Valid according to a basic email-format rule.

But:

```text
Email:
john@unknown
```

may fail validation depending on the organization's rules.

---

### 16. Domain Validity

Some values must belong to an approved domain.

Example:

```text
Customer Risk:
LOW
MEDIUM
HIGH
```

If the dataset contains:

```text
Customer Risk:
EXTREME
```

the value may be invalid.

---

### 17. Format Validity

AI pipelines often expect specific formats.

Example:

```text
Date:
2026-08-31
```

A source providing:

```text
31/08/26
```

may require transformation.

Incorrect parsing could result in incorrect AI features.

---

### 18. Uniqueness

**Uniqueness** ensures that records or identifiers are not unintentionally duplicated.

Example:

```text
Customer ID 1001
Customer ID 1001
Customer ID 1001
```

If these represent the same customer, duplicate records may distort model training.

---

### 19. Duplicate Data

Duplicate records can cause:

* Overrepresentation.
* Training bias.
* Incorrect frequency calculations.
* Distorted statistics.
* Inflated dataset size.

Example:

```text
Original:
Customer A → 1 record

After accidental duplication:
Customer A → 10 records
```

The model may effectively give Customer A's characteristics excessive weight.

---

### 20. Relevance

Data may be technically correct but irrelevant to the AI purpose.

Example:

```text
AI Use Case:
Predict equipment failure

Dataset:
Employee favorite color
```

The data may be accurate but have little or no legitimate relevance to the intended use case.

---

### 21. Data Quality Is Purpose-Dependent

The same dataset can be suitable for one purpose and unsuitable for another.

Example:

```text
Historical Sales Data
```

may be:

* Appropriate for historical analysis.
* Inadequate for real-time fraud detection.
* Potentially outdated for current customer behavior modeling.

Therefore:

> **Data quality must always be evaluated against intended use.**

---

### 22. Fitness for Purpose

A practical AI data-quality question is:

> **Is this data fit for the specific AI purpose?**

This includes assessing:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Timeliness.
* Relevance.
* Representativeness.
* Validity.
* Integrity.

---

### 23. Data Quality Requirements

Before an AI system uses a dataset, requirements should be established.

Example:

```text
Training Dataset Requirements

Accuracy       ≥ 98%
Completeness   ≥ 95%
Duplicates     < 0.5%
Freshness      < 30 days
Invalid Values < 1%
```

The actual thresholds should be determined by the business and risk requirements.

---

### 24. Data Quality Rules

Rules convert expectations into measurable controls.

Example:

```text
Rule:
Customer_ID must not be NULL.

Rule:
Country must belong to approved country list.

Rule:
Transaction_Date cannot be in the future.
```

These rules can be automated.

---

### 25. Automated Data Quality Validation

A pipeline can perform validation before data reaches an AI environment.

```text
Source Data
     ↓
Quality Validation
     ↓
PASS ─────→ AI Dataset
     │
     └─ FAIL → Quarantine / Remediation
```

This creates a preventive control.

---

### 26. Data Quality Gates

A **data quality gate** prevents unacceptable data from progressing.

Example:

```text
                    DATA QUALITY GATE
                           │
             ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
             ▼                           ▼
           PASS                         FAIL
             │                           │
             ▼                           ▼
       AI Pipeline                 Quarantine
                                         ↓
                                    Investigation
```

Quality gates are particularly important before model training and production inference.

---

### 27. Training Data Quality Gate

Before training:

```text
Training Dataset
       ↓
Quality Checks
       ↓
Completeness
Accuracy
Duplicates
Bias Indicators
Validity
       ↓
Approval
       ↓
Training
```

Training should not automatically proceed simply because a dataset exists.

---

### 28. Validation Data Quality

Validation datasets should also undergo quality controls.

A poor validation dataset can produce misleading conclusions about model performance.

```text
Poor Validation Data
       ↓
Incorrect Evaluation
       ↓
False Confidence
       ↓
Production Risk
```

---

### 29. Test Data Quality

Test data must be sufficiently reliable to support independent evaluation.

Important considerations include:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Representativeness.
* Independence.
* Integrity.

A flawed test dataset can make a weak model appear reliable.

---

### 30. Inference Data Quality

Production AI systems also require data-quality controls.

Example:

```text
User Request
     ↓
Inference Data
     ↓
Validation
     ↓
Model
     ↓
Output
```

Bad production inputs can result in unreliable outputs even when the model itself has not changed.

---

### 31. Input Validation

AI applications should validate inputs where appropriate.

Examples:

* Data type.
* Length.
* Range.
* Format.
* Allowed values.
* Required fields.

This reduces the probability of invalid data reaching the model.

---

### 32. Outlier Detection

Outliers may indicate:

* Genuine unusual behavior.
* Data-entry errors.
* Sensor failures.
* Fraud.
* Data corruption.
* Distribution changes.

Example:

```text
Normal Transaction:
€10 – €5,000

Observed:
€8,500,000
```

The value should be investigated rather than automatically assumed to be incorrect.

---

### 33. Outliers Are Not Automatically Errors

An important governance principle is:

> **An unusual value is not necessarily an incorrect value.**

For example:

```text
Normal Purchase:
€50

Luxury Purchase:
€500,000
```

The €500,000 transaction may be legitimate.

Therefore, quality controls should identify anomalies for assessment rather than blindly delete them.

---

### 34. Data Distribution

AI data quality should include distribution analysis.

Example:

```text
Training Data:
Age 20–60

Production Data:
Age 60–90
```

The production distribution differs significantly from training.

This may affect model performance.

---

### 35. Distribution Shift

**Distribution shift** occurs when the characteristics of incoming data change over time.

```text
Training Distribution
        ↓
Model
        ↓
Production Distribution
        ↓
Significant Difference
```

This can become an AI operational risk.

---

### 36. Data Drift

Data drift refers to changes in input data characteristics.

Examples:

* Customer demographics change.
* Transaction behavior changes.
* Sensor patterns change.
* Market conditions change.

Monitoring data drift helps identify when a model may no longer be operating under expected conditions.

---

### 37. Concept Drift

**Concept drift** occurs when the relationship between inputs and outcomes changes.

Example:

```text
Historical:
Feature A → High Risk

Current:
Feature A → Low Risk
```

The data may still be accurate, but the underlying relationship has changed.

---

### 38. Data Quality vs Data Drift

These should not be confused.

**Data quality problem:**

```text
Age = "ABC"
```

**Data drift:**

```text
Average customer age:
35 → 52
```

The second may represent valid data but a changing population.

---

### 39. Representativeness

AI training data should adequately represent the population or environment relevant to the intended use.

Example:

```text
Target Population:
Global Customers

Training Data:
95% One Country
5% Other Countries
```

The dataset may be large but poorly representative.

---

### 40. Representation Gaps

A representation gap occurs when important groups or conditions are insufficiently represented.

Examples:

* Geographic groups.
* Customer segments.
* Languages.
* Device types.
* Operating conditions.
* Transaction types.

Such gaps may affect model performance and fairness.

---

### 41. Sampling Quality

How data is collected matters.

A dataset created from only one convenient source may not represent the full population.

Example:

```text
Entire Customer Population
          ↓
Random / Controlled Sampling
          ↓
Training Dataset
```

versus:

```text
Entire Customer Population
          ↓
Only Most Active Customers
          ↓
Training Dataset
```

The second approach can introduce sampling bias.

---

### 42. Sampling Bias

Sampling bias occurs when the method of selecting data systematically favors certain observations.

Example:

```text
Customer Survey
↓
Only Customers Who Responded
```

Customers who do not respond may have materially different characteristics.

---

### 43. Label Quality

Many supervised AI systems depend on labeled data.

Example:

```text
Email
 ↓
Label:
Spam / Not Spam
```

If labels are incorrect, the model learns incorrect relationships.

Therefore:

> **Label quality is a critical component of training-data quality.**

---

### 44. Label Accuracy

Suppose:

```text
Actual:
Fraud

Label:
Legitimate
```

The training system receives an incorrect signal.

A high number of label errors can materially reduce model performance.

---

### 45. Label Consistency

Different annotators may classify the same information differently.

Example:

```text
Annotator A:
Fraud

Annotator B:
Not Fraud
```

Organizations should establish clear labeling standards.

---

### 46. Annotation Guidelines

Annotation processes should define:

* Classification rules.
* Examples.
* Edge cases.
* Escalation procedures.
* Quality thresholds.
* Reviewer responsibilities.

This improves consistency.

---

### 47. Inter-Annotator Agreement

Where human labeling is involved, organizations can measure agreement between annotators.

Example:

```text
Annotator A vs Annotator B

Agreement:
94%
```

Low agreement may indicate:

* Ambiguous definitions.
* Poor training.
* Complex cases.
* Weak labeling standards.

---

### 48. Data Quality Sampling

Organizations do not always need to manually inspect every record.

They can use statistical sampling.

```text
1,000,000 Records
        ↓
Representative Sample
        ↓
Manual Quality Review
        ↓
Estimated Quality
```

Sampling should be designed appropriately for the risk and purpose.

---

### 49. Data Profiling

**Data profiling** examines the structure and characteristics of a dataset.

Typical profiling includes:

* Null percentage.
* Unique values.
* Min/max.
* Mean/median.
* Distribution.
* Duplicate rate.
* Invalid values.
* Outliers.

Example:

```text
Field: Customer_Age

Nulls:       1.2%
Duplicates:  N/A
Min:         18
Max:         96
Invalid:     0.3%
```

---

### 50. AI Data Quality Baseline

Before deploying an AI system, organizations should establish a baseline.

Example:

```text
Dataset:
Customer Risk Training Data

Accuracy:        98.7%
Completeness:    97.2%
Validity:        99.1%
Duplicates:       0.4%
Freshness:       14 days
Representation:  Approved
```

This baseline provides a reference point for ongoing monitoring.

The fundamental GRC principle is:

> **AI data quality should be defined as measurable fitness for purpose, governed through explicit requirements, validated through controls, and continuously monitored throughout the AI data lifecycle.**
