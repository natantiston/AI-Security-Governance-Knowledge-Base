# 08.04 — Training Data Governance

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to Training Data Governance

Training Data Governance is the framework of **policies, processes, roles, controls, and assurance activities used to ensure that data used to train AI models is appropriate, lawful, secure, accurate, traceable, and fit for its intended purpose**.

Training data can directly influence how an AI model behaves.

Therefore:

> **The security and governance of an AI model are strongly influenced by the governance of the data used to train it.**

Poorly governed training data can introduce:

* Security vulnerabilities.
* Privacy violations.
* Bias.
* Data poisoning.
* Copyright concerns.
* Intellectual-property exposure.
* Model-performance problems.
* Regulatory risk.

---

### 2. Why Training Data Governance Matters

A model may be technically secure while still being built on inappropriate data.

For example:

```text id="v8m4q7"
Poor Training Data
       ↓
Poor Model Behavior
       ↓
Business / Security Impact
```

Training-data governance therefore forms an important part of AI governance.

---

### 3. Training Data Governance Objectives

A mature program should ensure that training data is:

1. **Authorized** — approved for the intended use.
2. **Relevant** — appropriate for the model's purpose.
3. **Accurate** — sufficiently reliable.
4. **Representative** — appropriate for the intended population or environment.
5. **Secure** — protected from unauthorized access or modification.
6. **Traceable** — provenance and lineage are available.
7. **Compliant** — legal, privacy, and regulatory requirements are addressed.
8. **Controlled** — access and processing are governed.
9. **Maintained** — changes are managed.
10. **Auditable** — decisions and controls can be evidenced.

---

### 6. Training Data Sources

Training data can originate from many sources:

* Internal databases.
* Enterprise documents.
* Customer information.
* Public datasets.
* Licensed datasets.
* Third-party providers.
* Open-source repositories.
* Web content.
* Sensor data.
* Application logs.
* User-generated content.
* Synthetic data.

Each source introduces different governance considerations.

---

### 7. Internal Training Data

Internal data may be valuable for enterprise AI.

Examples:

* Internal policies.
* Product documentation.
* Historical transactions.
* Operational records.
* Technical documentation.

However, internal availability does not automatically authorize model training.

---

### 8. Customer Data as Training Data

Customer information requires careful assessment before being used for training.

Questions include:

* Was the data collected for this purpose?
* Is AI training an approved use?
* Is additional authorization required?
* Is sensitive information present?
* Can the dataset be minimized?
* Is the data shared with a third-party model provider?

---

### 9. Employee Data as Training Data

Employee information may also require restrictions.

Examples:

* HR records.
* Performance information.
* Internal communications.
* Employee identifiers.

Organizations should avoid assuming that employee data can automatically be used for AI training simply because the organization owns or controls the systems containing it.

---

### 10. Public Data

Publicly accessible data may sometimes be used for AI training, but public availability does not eliminate governance requirements.

Consider:

* Copyright.
* Licensing.
* Terms of use.
* Data quality.
* Source reliability.
* Privacy.
* Collection methods.

---

### 11. Third-Party Training Data

Third-party datasets should undergo appropriate due diligence.

The organization should understand:

* Who supplied the data.
* What rights were obtained.
* Permitted uses.
* Restrictions.
* Data quality.
* Security controls.
* Retention.
* Geographic limitations.

---

### 12. Licensed Training Data

A license may permit some uses but prohibit others.

For example:

```text id="q6m3r9"
Dataset License
      ↓
Permitted:
Research

Prohibited:
Commercial Model Training
```

Therefore, licensing terms should be evaluated before training.

---

### 13. Training Data Ownership

Ownership should be clearly assigned.

A dataset may have:

* Business owner.
* Technical custodian.
* Data steward.
* Security owner.
* Privacy stakeholder.

Ownership provides accountability for governance decisions.

---

### 14. Training Data Approval

Before a dataset enters a production training pipeline, approval should be established.

Example:

```text id="v7m4q8"

Security Review
 ↓
Privacy Review
 ↓
Legal Review

Data Owner Approval
 ↓
Training Approved
```

Not every dataset requires every review; the review depth should be risk-based.

Training datasets should be classified according to applicable enterprise requirements.

Possible levels:

For reproducibility, a training run should ideally reference a specific dataset snapshot.

Example:

### 20. Training Data Provenance

Provenance answers:

> **Where did this training data come from?**

Possible provenance information includes:

* Original source.
* Provider.
* Collection method.
* Acquisition date.
* Transformation history.
* Licensing information.

---

### 21. Training Data Lineage

Lineage answers:

> **How did the data change before becoming training data?**

Example:

```text id="m8q3r7"
Source Database
      ↓
Extraction
      ↓
Filtering
      ↓
Cleaning
      ↓
Transformation
      ↓
Training Dataset
```

---

### 22. Provenance vs Lineage

The distinction is important.

**Provenance** focuses primarily on origin.

**Lineage** focuses on movement and transformation.

Together:

```text id="v6m4q9"
PROVENANCE
Where did it come from?

LINEAGE
What happened to it?
```

---

### 23. Training Data Quality

Training data quality can significantly affect model performance.

Important dimensions include:

* Accuracy.
* Completeness.
* Consistency.
* Timeliness.
* Validity.
* Uniqueness.

---

### 24. Training Data Accuracy

Incorrect training information can teach a model incorrect patterns.

Example:

```text id="q7m3r8"
Incorrect Data
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Incorrect Learned Pattern
      ↓
Incorrect Output
```

Quality controls should therefore exist before training.

---

### 25. Training Data Completeness

Missing information may create incomplete learning.

Questions include:

* Are important categories missing?
* Are certain populations underrepresented?
* Are critical fields incomplete?
* Are historical periods missing?

---

### 26. Training Data Consistency

Training datasets may combine information from multiple systems.

Conflicting definitions can create problems.

Example:

```text id="m4q8v7"
System A:
Customer = Active

System B:
Customer = Closed

       ↓

Training Dataset Conflict
```

Data governance should establish authoritative definitions where appropriate.

---

### 27. Training Data Timeliness

Old data may no longer represent current reality.

For example:

* Business processes change.
* Threats evolve.
* Products change.
* Customer behavior changes.
* Regulations change.

Training data should therefore be assessed for temporal relevance.

---

### 28. Training Data Relevance

A dataset should support the model's intended purpose.

Example:

```text id="v8m3q6"
AI Use Case:
Fraud Detection

Training Data:
Unrelated Marketing Data

        ↓

Poor Relevance
```

More data is not necessarily better data.

---

### 29. Training Data Representativeness

Training data should reasonably represent the environment in which the model will operate.

Potential concerns include:

* Population imbalance.
* Geographic imbalance.
* Historical bias.
* Missing scenarios.
* Underrepresented groups.

---

### 30. Training Data Bias

Bias in training data can influence model behavior.

Potential sources include:

* Historical decisions.
* Sampling methods.
* Human labeling.
* Missing data.
* Measurement bias.
* Structural bias.

Governance should identify and manage material risks.

---

### 31. Training Data Labeling

Supervised learning often requires labeled data.

Labels should be governed for:

* Accuracy.
* Consistency.
* Quality.
* Reviewer competence.
* Labeling instructions.

---

### 32. Labeling Standards

Organizations should establish clear labeling rules.

Example:

```text id="q6m3r8"
Label Definition
      ↓
Annotation Guidelines
      ↓
Annotator Training
      ↓
Quality Review
      ↓
Approved Labels
```

---

### 33. Labeling Quality Assurance

Quality assurance may include:

* Sample reviews.
* Double labeling.
* Inter-annotator agreement.
* Expert validation.
* Automated consistency checks.

---

### 34. Human Annotators

Where humans label training data, governance should consider:

* Training.
* Access.
* Confidentiality.
* Data exposure.
* Quality.
* Worker oversight.
* Contractual requirements.

---

### 35. Third-Party Annotation

External annotation providers create additional third-party risk.

Consider:

* Access to sensitive data.
* Geographic processing.
* Confidentiality.
* Security controls.
* Subcontractors.
* Data deletion.

---

### 36. Training Data Security

Training data should be protected against:

* Unauthorized access.
* Unauthorized modification.
* Data theft.
* Data destruction.
* Data poisoning.
* Unauthorized copying.

Security controls should reflect data classification and risk.

---

### 37. Access Control

Training datasets should follow least privilege.

Example:

```text id="m7q4r9"
Researcher
   ↓
Read Approved Dataset

ML Engineer
   ↓
Read + Process

Data Administrator
   ↓
Controlled Administrative Access
```

Not every participant needs the same privileges.

---

### 38. Training Data Encryption

Sensitive training data should receive appropriate encryption controls.

Consider:

* Encryption at rest.
* Encryption in transit.
* Key management.
* Key rotation.
* Access to decryption keys.

---

### 39. Training Data Integrity

Training datasets should be protected against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

* Hashing.
* Digital signatures.
* Immutable storage.
* Version control.
* Access controls.
* Change management.

---

### 40. Training Data Integrity Verification

A simplified integrity process is:

```text id="q8m3r6"
Approved Dataset
      ↓
Generate Integrity Reference
      ↓
Training Begins
      ↓
Verify Dataset
      ↓
Train
```

Unexpected changes should trigger investigation.

---

### 41. Training Data Poisoning

Data poisoning occurs when an attacker or malicious actor manipulates training data to influence model behavior.

Conceptually:

```text id="v7m4q8"
Legitimate Data
      +
Malicious Data
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Compromised Model Behavior
```

Training-data governance should include controls against this threat.

---

### 42. Training Data Contamination

Contamination can occur when inappropriate, low-quality, duplicated, or unintended data enters the training dataset.

Examples:

* Test data accidentally included in training.
* Evaluation data included in training.
* Duplicate records.
* Incorrect labels.
* Unapproved sources.

---

### 43. Training vs Test Separation

Training and evaluation datasets should generally be appropriately separated.

```text id="m9q3v7"
Training Data
      ↓
Model Training

Test Data
      ↓
Independent Evaluation
```

If test information leaks into training, evaluation results may become misleading.

---

### 44. Data Leakage During Training

Training pipelines can accidentally expose sensitive information.

Potential leakage points include:

* Logs.
* Temporary files.
* Debug output.
* Data exports.
* Developer environments.
* Cloud storage.

---

### 45. Training Environment Security

The training environment should be treated as a security-sensitive environment.

Controls may include:

* Network segmentation.
* IAM.
* Endpoint protection.
* Encryption.
* Logging.
* Secrets management.
* DLP.

---

### 46. Training Data in Development

Developers may want to use training datasets during experimentation.

Production or restricted data should not automatically be copied into development environments.

Instead, organizations may use:

* Synthetic data.
* Masked data.
* Tokenized data.
* Minimized datasets.

---

### 47. Training Data Replication

Training data may be copied into:

* Data warehouses.
* Data lakes.
* Feature stores.
* Training clusters.
* Object storage.
* Backup systems.

Each copy creates additional governance requirements.

---

### 48. Training Data Retention

Organizations should determine how long training datasets must be retained.

Consider:

* Reproducibility.
* Legal requirements.
* Business requirements.
* Model lifecycle.
* Security risk.
* Cost.

---

### 49. Training Data Disposal

When a dataset is no longer required, disposal should be controlled.

The organization should consider:

* Primary copies.
* Backups.
* Replicas.
* Temporary files.
* Derived datasets.

---

### 50. Training Data Governance Foundation

The foundation of training data governance can be summarized as:

```text id="x8m4q7"
                 TRAINING DATA
                       │
                       ▼
                    SOURCE
                       │
                       ▼
                  OWNERSHIP
                       │
                       ▼
                 CLASSIFICATION
                       │
                       ▼
                PROVENANCE
                       │
                       ▼
                   QUALITY
                       │
                       ▼
                  SECURITY
                       │
                       ▼
                  VALIDATION
                       │
                       ▼
                   APPROVAL
                       │
                       ▼
                    VERSION
                       │
                       ▼
                    TRAINING
                       │
                       ▼
                  EVALUATION
```

The central GRC principle is:

> **Training data must be governed as a controlled enterprise asset because its characteristics can directly influence the security, reliability, fairness, privacy, compliance, and behavior of the resulting AI model.**

A mature organization should therefore be able to demonstrate **where its training data came from, why it is permitted for AI training, who owns it, how it was classified, how it was transformed, how its quality was validated, how it was protected, which version was used, and which models depend on it.**

### 51. Training Data Eligibility

Before a dataset is approved for AI training, the organization should determine whether it is **eligible** for the intended training purpose.

Eligibility should consider:

* Data ownership.
* Intended purpose.
* Classification.
* Privacy requirements.
* Legal restrictions.
* Licensing.
* Security risks.
* Data quality.
* Business relevance.

A dataset being available does not automatically make it eligible.

---

### 52. Training Data Approval Criteria

A practical approval model can be:

```text id="q8m4r7"
Dataset Identified
      ↓
Business Purpose
      ↓
Classification
      ↓
Legal / Privacy Review
      ↓
Security Review
      ↓
Quality Assessment
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Approval Decision
```

The depth of review should be proportionate to risk.

---

### 53. Training Data Authorization

Authorization should answer:

> **Is this specific dataset authorized for this specific AI training activity?**

This distinction is important.

```text id="m7q3v9"
Authorized for Business Use
        ≠
Automatically Authorized for AI Training
```

A dataset may be approved for operational processing but not for model training.

---

### 54. Purpose Limitation

Training data should be used consistently with its approved purpose.

Example:

```text id="v6m4q8"
Collected for:
Customer Support

Requested Use:
AI Model Training

        ↓

Purpose Compatibility Assessment
```

Organizations should document the decision rather than assuming compatibility.

---

### 55. Secondary Use

AI training can represent a secondary use of data.

Examples:

* Customer data collected for service delivery.
* Employee data collected for HR administration.
* Security logs collected for monitoring.

Using these datasets for model development may require additional governance.

---

### 56. Training Data Consent and Authorization

Where applicable, organizations should determine whether the original collection and applicable notices, permissions, contracts, or other legal bases support the intended AI training use.

The specific requirement depends on the jurisdiction, data type, and processing context.

---

### 57. Data Minimization Before Training

Only data necessary for the intended model should be included.

Example:

```text id="q4m8v7"
Source Dataset
      ↓
100 Fields
      ↓
AI Requirements Analysis
      ↓
18 Required Fields
      ↓
Training Dataset
```

This reduces unnecessary exposure.

---

### 58. Attribute Minimization

Minimization should operate at the field level where practical.

For example:

| Field              | Training Need | Treatment |
| ------------------ | ------------- | --------- |
| Customer ID        | No            | Remove    |
| Name               | No            | Remove    |
| Product Type       | Yes           | Retain    |
| Transaction Amount | Yes           | Retain    |
| Address            | No            | Remove    |
| Country            | Maybe         | Assess    |

---

### 59. Sampling

Sampling can reduce the volume of data exposed to the training environment.

However, sampling should preserve sufficient representation.

```text id="m8q3r6"
Large Dataset
      ↓
Risk + Relevance Analysis
      ↓
Representative Sample
      ↓
Training
```

---

### 60. Data Filtering

Filtering removes data that should not enter the training dataset.

Possible filters include:

* Restricted records.
* Invalid records.
* Duplicate records.
* Irrelevant content.
* Known malicious content.
* Test records.

---

### 61. Data Cleaning

Cleaning improves dataset quality.

Activities may include:

* Removing duplicates.
* Correcting malformed records.
* Normalizing formats.
* Resolving inconsistencies.
* Removing corrupted files.

Cleaning procedures should be documented.

---

### 62. Data Deduplication

Duplicate data can distort training.

Example:

```text id="v7m4q9"
Record A
Record A
Record A
Record B
Record C
```

The repeated record may unintentionally receive excessive influence.

---

### 63. Duplicate Detection

Organizations can use:

* Exact matching.
* Hashing.
* Similarity detection.
* Record linkage.
* Content analysis.

The appropriate method depends on the data type.

---

### 64. Data Normalization

Normalization can make datasets more consistent.

Examples:

```text id="q6m3r8"
United States
USA
U.S.
US
```

may represent the same value.

Standardization can improve model learning and data quality.

---

### 65. Data Validation

Before training, validation should confirm that the dataset meets defined requirements.

Validation can cover:

* Schema.
* Completeness.
* Accuracy.
* Labels.
* Duplicates.
* Missing values.
* Outliers.
* Classification.

---

### 66. Training Data Quality Gates

A quality gate can prevent unsuitable datasets from entering training.

```text id="m9q4v7"
Dataset
 ↓
Quality Checks
 ↓
Pass?
 ↙       ↘
YES       NO
 ↓         ↓
Train    Remediate
```

This turns data quality into an enforceable control.

---

### 67. Automated Quality Gates

Quality checks can be automated.

Examples:

* Missing-value thresholds.
* Duplicate thresholds.
* Schema validation.
* File integrity.
* Label consistency.
* Classification checks.

Automation improves repeatability.

---

### 68. Human Quality Review

Automation should not replace human judgment for high-risk datasets.

Human reviewers may assess:

* Business relevance.
* Semantic quality.
* Bias.
* Context.
* Sensitive information.
* Legal restrictions.

---

### 69. Training Data Acceptance Criteria

Organizations should define acceptance criteria before training.

Example:

```text id="q8m3r6"
Accuracy ≥ defined threshold
Completeness ≥ defined threshold
Duplicate Rate ≤ defined threshold
Restricted Data = Approved
Provenance = Established
Integrity = Verified
```

The thresholds should be appropriate for the use case.

---

### 70. Data Quality Exceptions

If a dataset fails a quality requirement, an exception may sometimes be considered.

The exception should document:

* Deficiency.
* Business justification.
* Risk.
* Compensating controls.
* Approval.
* Expiration.

---

### 74. Dataset Change Records

A change record should capture:

* What changed.
* Why it changed.
* Who changed it.
* When it changed.
* Which controls were rerun.
* Whether approval was required.

### 75. Immutable Training Snapshots

For critical models, organizations may preserve an immutable snapshot of the dataset used for training.

This enables:

> **“Show me exactly what data was used to produce this model.”**

### 76. Reproducibility

Training governance should support reproducibility.

A training run may need to identify:

```text id="v8m3q6"
Dataset Version
+
Code Version
+
Model Configuration
+
Training Parameters
+
Environment
↓
Training Run
```

This is particularly important for investigations and audits.

---

### 77. Dataset-to-Model Traceability

The organization should ideally be able to trace:

```text id="m4q8v7"
Dataset
   ↓
Training Run
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Application
   ↓
Business Process
```

This allows data risks to be connected to downstream AI systems.

---

### 78. Model-to-Dataset Traceability

The reverse relationship is equally important.

Given a model:

> Which datasets influenced this model?

Example:

```text id="q7m3r9"
Model M-004
 ├── Dataset A v3
 ├── Dataset B v7
 └── Dataset C v2
```

This becomes important when a dataset is later found to be problematic.

---

### 79. Dataset Withdrawal

If a dataset is later determined to be unsuitable, the organization should be able to identify affected models.

Example:

```text id="m8q4v7"

      ↓
Affected Models
      ↓
Risk Assessment
      ↓
Remediation
```

### 80. Training Data Recall

A mature governance program should support controlled withdrawal of training data.

Possible triggers include:

* Legal dispute.
* Privacy concern.
* Security compromise.
* Data poisoning.
* Licensing problem.
* Quality failure.

---

### 81. Model Retraining After Data Withdrawal

If withdrawn data materially affects a model, retraining may be required.

```text id="v6m3q9"
Problematic Dataset
       ↓
Identify Affected Model
       ↓
Remove / Replace Data
       ↓
Retrain
       ↓
Validate
       ↓
Redeploy
```

The need for retraining should be determined based on risk and technical assessment.

### 82. Training Data Security Boundaries

Training data should be processed inside clearly defined security boundaries.

Example:

```text id="q8m4r7"
Enterprise Network
      ↓
Controlled Data Platform
      ↓
Training Environment
      ↓
Model Artifact
```

Access between zones should be controlled.

---

### 83. Training Environment Segmentation

Sensitive training environments may require segmentation from:

* General user networks.
* Development environments.
* Internet-facing systems.
* Untrusted workloads.

Segmentation can reduce attack paths.

---

### 84. Training Data Access Reviews

Access to sensitive training datasets should be periodically reviewed.

Review questions include:

* Who currently has access?
* Is access still required?
* Are service accounts still valid?
* Are privileged permissions necessary?

---

### 85. Training Data Privileged Access

Administrative access should receive stronger controls.

Examples:

* Privileged Access Management.
* MFA.
* Just-in-time access.
* Session monitoring.
* Approval workflows.

---

### 86. Training Data Service Accounts

AI pipelines may use service accounts to retrieve training data.

These accounts should have:

* Unique identities.
* Limited permissions.
* Credential protection.
* Monitoring.
* Periodic review.

---

### 87. Secrets in Training Data

Training datasets can accidentally contain secrets.

Examples:

* API keys.
* Passwords.
* Access tokens.
* Private keys.
* Connection strings.

Secret scanning should therefore be considered before training.

---

### 88. Secret Scanning

A simplified process:

```text id="m7q4r8"

      ↓

   ↙        ↘
 YES        NO
  ↓          ↓
```

### 89. Credentials and Model Training

Credentials should generally not be intentionally included in training datasets.

If discovered:

* Remove them.
* Rotate affected credentials where necessary.
* Investigate exposure.
* Determine whether models or artifacts were affected.

---

### 90. Sensitive Data Scanning

Before training, datasets may be scanned for:

* Personal information.
* Payment information.
* Credentials.
* Confidential content.
* Security-sensitive information.

The scanning depth should be risk-based.

---

### 91. Data Loss Prevention Before Training

DLP can help prevent unauthorized sensitive data from entering training environments.

Example:


### 92. Training Data Transfer Controls

Large datasets may be transferred between systems.

Controls should address:

```text id="m4q7v9"
                              │

                              │

                              │

                              │

                              │
                              ▼
```

The central GRC principle is:

> **Training data governance establishes a controlled chain of accountability from dataset acquisition through model training, ensuring that the organization can demonstrate why the data was used, whether its use was authorized, whether it was appropriate and secure, and which AI systems were influenced by it.**

The most important distinction is:

**Data availability ≠ training authorization.**

A mature organization therefore asks:

> **“Is this data merely available, or have we established that it is appropriate, authorized, secure, traceable, and fit for this specific AI training purpose?”**

* Credential theft.

---

### 183. Training Data Supply-Chain Assurance

Where datasets come from external suppliers, assurance can include:

* Supplier assessment.
* Contract review.
* Provenance validation.
* Integrity verification.
* Change notifications.

---

### 184. Dataset Change Notification

Third-party suppliers should ideally notify the organization about material changes.

For example:

```text
Dataset v3.0
      ↓
Supplier Changes Source
      ↓
Dataset v3.1
      ↓
Reassessment
```

The organization should not blindly accept updates.

---

### 185. Training Data Dependency Management

Organizations should maintain dependencies between datasets and models.

Example:

```text
Dataset A
 ├── Model 1
 ├── Model 2
 └── Model 3

Dataset B
 └── Model 2
```

If Dataset A is withdrawn, Models 1–3 may require assessment.

---

### 186. Impact Analysis

When training data changes, assess:

* Which models are affected?
* Which applications are affected?
* Which business processes are affected?
* Is retraining required?
* Is regulatory reporting necessary?

---

### 187. Dataset Revocation

A dataset should have a mechanism for being revoked.

Triggers may include:

* Security incident.
* Legal challenge.
* Privacy issue.
* Poisoning.
* Licensing problem.
* Quality failure.

---

### 188. Revocation Workflow

```text
Problem Identified
       ↓
Dataset Revoked
       ↓
Training Blocked
       ↓
Affected Models Identified
       ↓
Risk Assessment
       ↓
Remediation
```

### 189. Model Retraining Governance

Retraining should be controlled similarly to initial training.

The organization should document:

* Why retraining occurred.
* What changed.
* Which dataset version was used.
* What validation occurred.
* Who approved deployment.

A training-data incident should be integrated with enterprise incident response.

Potential incident categories include:

* Unauthorized modification.
* Unauthorized access.
* Licensing violation.
* Sensitive data exposure.

```text
+
Regulatory Impact
```

### 192. Training Data Breach Investigation

Investigators should establish:

1. What data was affected?
2. What classification applied?
3. Who accessed it?
4. Was it copied?
5. Which models used it?
6. Was sensitive information learned or exposed?
7. What remediation is required?

### 193. Training Data Forensics

Preserving dataset versions and training metadata can support investigations.

Without historical versions, it may be difficult to determine:

> **Exactly what data influenced the model.**

### Final GRC Perspective

The most important question is not:

> **“Do we have good training data?”**

It is:

> **“Can we demonstrate that every significant dataset used to train our AI systems is appropriately sourced, authorized, classified, secure, fit for purpose, traceable, validated, controlled, and governed throughout its lifecycle?”**

That is the foundation of an enterprise-grade **Training Data Governance** capability.
