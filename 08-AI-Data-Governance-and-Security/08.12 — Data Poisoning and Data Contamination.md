# 08.12 — Data Poisoning and Data Contamination

## Part 1

### 01. Introduction to Data Poisoning and Data Contamination

AI systems learn from data. If that data is intentionally manipulated or unintentionally contaminated, the resulting AI system may become unreliable, biased, insecure, or maliciously influenced.

This creates two closely related but distinct concepts:

**Data poisoning**

> Deliberate manipulation or insertion of data intended to influence the behavior, performance, or security of an AI system.

**Data contamination**

> The introduction of unintended, inappropriate, corrupted, irrelevant, or otherwise unsuitable data into an AI dataset or processing pipeline.

The distinction is important:

```text id="f7m3qa"
                AI Data Problem
                      │
          ┌───────────┴───────────┐
          ▼                       ▼
   Data Poisoning          Data Contamination
          │                       │
      Intentional            May be accidental
      manipulation           or malicious
          │                       │
          └───────────┬───────────┘
                      ▼
               AI Risk / Impact
```

---

### 02. Why This Topic Matters

Traditional cybersecurity often focuses on protecting systems from unauthorized access.

AI introduces another dimension:

> **An attacker may not need to compromise the AI system itself if they can influence the data from which the system learns or makes decisions.**

For example:

```text id="x84n2v"
Attacker
   ↓
Manipulates Data
   ↓
Training Pipeline
   ↓
Model Learns Manipulation
   ↓
Production Model
   ↓
Incorrect Behavior
```

This makes data governance a critical component of AI security.

---

### 03. Data Poisoning as an AI Security Threat

Data poisoning can be viewed as an attack against the **learning process**.

Traditional attack:

```text id="p63m8q"
Attacker
   ↓
Compromises System
   ↓
Changes Configuration
```

Data poisoning:

```text id="m75v3x"
Attacker
   ↓
Influences Data
   ↓
Training Process
   ↓
Model Behavior
```

The attack may therefore occur **before the model is deployed**.

---

### 04. Data Contamination Is Broader

Contamination is broader than poisoning.

Examples include:

* Incorrect records.
* Duplicate records.
* Irrelevant information.
* Test data entering production.
* Training data entering validation data.
* Future information entering historical datasets.
* Corrupted files.
* Incorrect labels.
* Untrusted external content.
* Maliciously inserted records.

Not all contamination is an attack.

---

### 05. Intent Matters

One useful distinction is:

| Characteristic   | Data Poisoning             | Data Contamination             |
| ---------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Intent           | Usually malicious          | May be accidental or malicious |
| Objective        | Influence AI behavior      | Introduce unsuitable data      |
| Security concern | High                       | Variable                       |
| GRC concern      | High                       | High                           |
| Detection        | Often difficult            | Often easier                   |
| Example          | Malicious training records | Duplicate records              |

However, intent may not always be immediately known.

Therefore, incident investigation should avoid assuming the cause before evidence is available.

---

### 06. Poisoning Across the AI Lifecycle

Poisoning can occur at different stages.

```text id="q85m3v"
Collection
    ↓
Ingestion
    ↓
Storage
    ↓
Preparation
    ↓
Training
    ↓
Fine-Tuning
    ↓
Evaluation
    ↓
Deployment
```

The attack surface therefore extends beyond the training dataset itself.

---

### 07. Training Data Poisoning

The most commonly discussed form is **training data poisoning**.

An attacker attempts to manipulate the data used to train a model.

Example:

```text id="v74m8q"
Legitimate Training Data
        +
Malicious Records
        ↓
Training
        ↓
Compromised Model
```

The objective may be to reduce overall model accuracy or produce specific incorrect behavior.

---

### 08. Targeted Poisoning

A targeted poisoning attack attempts to influence a particular type of prediction or behavior.

Conceptually:

```text id="m83q4x"
Poisoned Data
      ↓
Specific Learned Association
      ↓
Targeted Model Behavior
```

The attacker may attempt to affect:

* A particular class.
* A specific type of transaction.
* A particular user category.
* A specific feature combination.

---

### 09. Availability-Oriented Poisoning

Some poisoning attacks aim to degrade the overall usefulness of a model.

The objective may be:

> **Make the model perform significantly worse.**

Example:

```text id="q64m8v"
Clean Dataset
     ↓
Poisoned Dataset
     ↓
Training
     ↓
Reduced Model Performance
```

This can resemble an availability or reliability attack against an AI capability.

---

### 10. Integrity-Oriented Poisoning

Other attacks attempt to manipulate what the model learns.

The objective is not necessarily to make the entire model fail.

Instead:

```text id="x75m3q"
Normal Behavior
      +
Hidden Manipulation
      ↓
Selective Incorrect Behavior
```

This can be particularly difficult to detect.

---

### 11. Backdoor Poisoning

A **backdoor** involves manipulating training data so that a model behaves normally under ordinary conditions but behaves differently when a particular trigger or condition is encountered.

Conceptually:

```text id="p84m7x"
Normal Input
    ↓
Normal Model Behavior

Trigger Condition
    ↓
Altered Model Behavior
```

The model may therefore appear normal during ordinary validation.

---

### 12. Why Backdoors Are Dangerous

A backdoored model can potentially:

* Pass ordinary testing.
* Produce expected results for most inputs.
* Activate only under specific conditions.
* Remain difficult to identify through routine monitoring.

This creates a significant challenge for AI assurance.

---

### 13. Label Poisoning

Labels are particularly important in supervised learning.

Example:

```text id="m73q8v"
Transaction
      ↓
Correct Label: Fraud

Poisoned Label:
      ↓
Legitimate
```

If enough labels are manipulated, the model may learn an incorrect relationship.

---

### 14. Feature Poisoning

Features can also be manipulated.

Example:

```text id="q85m4x"
Customer Risk Score
Expected: 850
Observed: 120
```

If malicious or incorrect feature values enter training data, the model may learn incorrect relationships.

---

### 15. Metadata Poisoning

AI systems depend on metadata as well as raw data.

Examples include:

* Dataset labels.
* Source identifiers.
* Timestamps.
* Classification tags.
* Data lineage.
* Quality scores.
* Access permissions.

If metadata is manipulated, governance controls themselves may become unreliable.

---

### 16. Provenance Manipulation

Data provenance answers:

> **Where did this data come from?**

If provenance records are manipulated:

```text id="v63m8q"
Untrusted Data
      ↓
False Provenance
      ↓
"Trusted Source"
      ↓
AI Pipeline
```

This can undermine source-validation controls.

---

### 17. Lineage Manipulation

Data lineage describes how data moves and transforms through systems.

An attacker who manipulates lineage information could make unauthorized data appear legitimate.

Therefore:

> **Lineage itself must be protected as governance evidence.**

---

### 18. Supply-Chain Data Poisoning

AI organizations frequently depend on external datasets.

Potential sources include:

* Public datasets.
* Commercial data providers.
* Open-source repositories.
* APIs.
* Data brokers.
* Research datasets.
* Vendor feeds.

If an upstream source becomes compromised, downstream AI systems may inherit poisoned data.

---

### 19. AI Data Supply Chain

A simplified supply chain:

```text id="q74m3x"
External Provider
       ↓
Data Transfer
       ↓
Enterprise Repository
       ↓
Data Pipeline
       ↓
Training Dataset
       ↓
Model
```

A compromise at the beginning can propagate through the entire chain.

---

### 20. Third-Party Dataset Risk

Third-party datasets should not automatically be treated as trustworthy simply because they came from a recognized vendor.

GRC should consider:

* Source reputation.
* Security controls.
* Data provenance.
* Change management.
* Integrity mechanisms.
* Update procedures.
* Contractual requirements.
* Incident notification.

---

### 21. Open Data Risk

Publicly available data can be useful but introduces additional governance risks.

Potential problems include:

* Unknown contributors.
* Inconsistent quality.
* Malicious submissions.
* Unclear provenance.
* Licensing uncertainty.
* Dataset modification.
* Hidden contamination.

Public availability does not equal trustworthiness.

---

### 22. Web-Sourced AI Data

AI systems that collect data from websites may encounter:

* Malicious content.
* Fake information.
* Spam.
* Manipulated content.
* Duplicate content.
* Coordinated campaigns.
* Adversarial examples.

Therefore, web-scale collection requires appropriate validation and provenance controls.

---

### 23. Data Contamination Through Web Crawling

Consider:

```text id="m83q5v"
Web Content
    ↓
Crawler
    ↓
Collection
    ↓
Dataset
    ↓
Training
```

If unsuitable content enters the dataset without detection, the model may learn from it.

---

### 24. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Contamination

RAG systems introduce another contamination pathway.

```text id="x64m8q"
Document Repository
       ↓
Ingestion
       ↓
Chunking
       ↓
Embedding
       ↓
Vector Database
       ↓
Retrieval
       ↓
LLM
```

If an untrusted document enters the knowledge base, the model may retrieve and use that content.

---

### 25. RAG Poisoning vs Model Poisoning

These should be distinguished.

**Model poisoning:**

> Manipulates data used to alter the trained model.

**RAG poisoning:**

> Manipulates or inserts content into the knowledge source retrieved at inference time.

Conceptually:

```text id="q75m3v"
Model Poisoning
Data → Training → Model

RAG Poisoning
Document → Retrieval → Response
```

The second may not require retraining the model.

---

### 26. Fine-Tuning Data Poisoning

Fine-tuning datasets can also be manipulated.

```text id="v84m7x"
Base Model
    +
Fine-Tuning Dataset
    ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
```

If the fine-tuning data contains malicious examples, model behavior may change.

---

### 27. Human Feedback Poisoning

Some AI systems incorporate human feedback.

Potential sources include:

* Human ratings.
* Preference data.
* Reviewer annotations.
* Safety labels.
* Feedback submissions.

If feedback is manipulated:

```text id="m63q8v"
Manipulated Feedback
       ↓
Training / Alignment
       ↓
Changed AI Behavior
```

This creates another poisoning pathway.

---

### 28. Annotation Poisoning

Human annotation systems may be targeted.

For example:

```text id="q84m3x"
Raw Data
   ↓
Human Annotation
   ↓
Training Dataset
```

If annotators intentionally submit incorrect labels, model behavior can be affected.

Controls should therefore consider:

* Annotator identity.
* Quality monitoring.
* Sampling.
* Review.
* Agreement rates.

---

### 29. Insider Data Poisoning

An insider with legitimate access may have greater ability to manipulate datasets.

Potential risks include:

* Privileged administrator.
* Data engineer.
* ML engineer.
* Contractor.
* Vendor personnel.

This demonstrates why:

> **Authorized access does not equal authorized intent.**

---

### 30. Insider Threat Controls

Relevant controls include:

* Least privilege.
* Separation of duties.
* Privileged access management.
* Audit logging.
* Change approval.
* Behavioral monitoring.
* Data integrity verification.
* Periodic access reviews.

---

### 31. Accidental Contamination

A common contamination scenario is human error.

Example:

```text id="v73m8q"
Production Dataset
      +
Test Records
      ↓
Training Dataset
```

No attacker is involved, but the resulting model may still be unreliable.

---

### 32. Duplicate Data Contamination

Duplicate records can distort model learning.

Example:

```text id="m85q3v"
Record A
Record A
Record A
Record B
Record C
```

The repeated record may receive disproportionate influence.

---

### 33. Train-Test Contamination

One of the most important forms of contamination is overlap between training and evaluation datasets.

```text id="q64m8x"
Training
  ├── Record A
  ├── Record B
  └── Record C

Testing
  ├── Record C  ← contamination
  ├── Record D
  └── Record E
```

This can produce artificially high evaluation results.

---

### 34. Data Leakage Through Contamination

Contamination can introduce information that should not be available.

For example:

```text id="x74m3q"
Historical Prediction
       ↓
Future Outcome Included
       ↓
Training Dataset
```

The model may appear highly accurate because it has indirectly seen future information.

---

### 35. Temporal Contamination

Historical datasets should preserve appropriate time boundaries.

Example:

```text id="p85m7x"
2024 Data
   ↓
2025 Information
   ↓
2024 Prediction Model
```

If later information enters the earlier training period, the evaluation may no longer represent real-world conditions.

---

### 36. Cross-Environment Contamination

Data can accidentally cross environments:

```text id="m73q8v"
Production
   ↓
Development
   ↓
Training Dataset
```

This can create:

* Privacy risk.
* Security risk.
* Evaluation contamination.
* Compliance issues.

---

### 37. Synthetic Data Contamination

Synthetic data can introduce unexpected artifacts.

For example:

```text id="q84m3x"
Synthetic Generator
       ↓
Synthetic Dataset
       ↓
Training
```

If synthetic records contain unrealistic patterns, models may learn those patterns.

Synthetic-data governance is therefore important.

---

### 38. Contamination Through Data Transformation

Data transformations can unintentionally introduce errors.

Examples:

* Incorrect normalization.
* Incorrect joins.
* Incorrect filtering.
* Wrong encoding.
* Incorrect aggregation.
* Timestamp conversion errors.

The original data may be trustworthy while the transformed dataset becomes contaminated.

---

### 39. Pipeline Contamination

A compromised or defective pipeline may introduce unwanted data.

```text id="v63m8q"
Trusted Source
      ↓
Compromised / Defective Pipeline
      ↓
Contaminated Dataset
```

Therefore, data integrity controls must extend into pipeline governance.

---

### 40. The Core GRC Principle

The fundamental principle of this topic is:

> **AI organizations must govern not only whether data is available, but whether the data entering AI systems is trustworthy, appropriate, traceable, and protected against deliberate poisoning and accidental contamination.**

The key distinction is:

```text id="x75m3q"
DATA POISONING
= Intentional manipulation

DATA CONTAMINATION
= Unwanted or unsuitable data entering the AI lifecycle
```

And the GRC objective is:

```text id="m84q7x"
Identify Sources
      ↓
Establish Trust
      ↓
Validate Data
      ↓
Protect Integrity
      ↓
Detect Anomalies
      ↓
Investigate Suspicious Data
      ↓
Contain / Remediate
      ↓
Verify Before AI Use
```

# 08.12 — Data Poisoning and Data Contamination

## Part 2

### 41. Data Poisoning Attack Surface

The AI data supply chain contains multiple potential poisoning points.

```text
External Sources
      ↓
Collection
      ↓
Ingestion
      ↓
Storage
      ↓
Cleaning
      ↓
Labeling
      ↓
Feature Engineering
      ↓
Training
      ↓
Fine-Tuning
      ↓
Evaluation
```

Each stage can introduce malicious or unintended changes.

A GRC assessment should therefore ask:

> **At which points can data be inserted, modified, deleted, or misclassified?**

---

### 42. Poisoning Through Data Collection

Data collection is the first major attack surface.

Potential sources include:

* Web crawlers.
* APIs.
* Sensors.
* Customer submissions.
* Application logs.
* External databases.
* Public datasets.
* Vendor feeds.

If collection controls are weak, attackers may influence the data before it reaches the enterprise environment.

---

### 43. Fake Data Injection

An attacker may attempt to introduce fabricated records.

Example:

```text id="m84q7x"
Legitimate Records
       +
Fake Records
       ↓
Training Dataset
       ↓
Model
```

The objective may be to alter statistical distributions or teach the model incorrect relationships.

---

### 44. Volume-Based Poisoning

An attacker may attempt to overwhelm legitimate data with malicious records.

Conceptually:

```text id="q73m8v"
90% Legitimate
10% Malicious
```

If the malicious portion becomes sufficiently influential, model behavior may change.

The risk depends on:

* Dataset size.
* Attack volume.
* Model architecture.
* Training process.
* Data weighting.

---

### 45. Repetition-Based Poisoning

Repeated records may receive disproportionate influence.

Example:

```text id="v65m3q"
Record A × 1
Record B × 1
Malicious Record × 1,000
```

Even though the malicious record appears to be a small conceptual addition, repetition can distort the training distribution.

---

### 46. Distribution Manipulation

Instead of adding a specific record repeatedly, an attacker may try to alter the overall distribution.

Example:

```text id="m84q3v"
Expected Distribution
       ↓
Manipulated Distribution
       ↓
Model Learns Different Pattern
```

This may be harder to detect than obvious malicious records.

---

### 47. Class Imbalance Manipulation

An attacker may attempt to influence the proportion of classes.

Example:

```text id="q75m8x"
Normal = 95%
Fraud  = 5%

After Manipulation:

Normal = 99%
Fraud  = 1%
```

The model may become less sensitive to the minority class.

---

### 48. Label Flipping

Label-flipping attacks alter the classification assigned to training examples.

Example:

```text id="v63m7q"
Fraud Transaction
      ↓
Label changed to
      ↓
Legitimate
```

This can be particularly harmful where labels are central to model decisions.

---

### 49. Selective Label Poisoning

Instead of changing random labels, an attacker may selectively manipulate labels associated with particular characteristics.

Conceptually:

```text id="m84q5x"
Target Group
     ↓
Labels Manipulated
     ↓
Model Learns Incorrect Association
```

This can create targeted behavior.

---

### 50. Feature Manipulation

An attacker may modify feature values rather than labels.

Example:

```text id="q74m8v"
Risk Score = 900
      ↓
Changed to
      ↓
Risk Score = 100
```

Repeated manipulation may influence the learned model.

---

### 51. Metadata Manipulation

Attackers may target metadata because it often controls downstream processing.

Examples:

* Source identifier.
* Data classification.
* Quality status.
* Timestamp.
* Dataset version.
* Approval status.

A malicious metadata change can cause otherwise untrusted data to be treated as trusted.

---

### 52. Timestamp Manipulation

Timestamps can influence:

* Training windows.
* Data freshness.
* Historical analysis.
* Event sequencing.
* Point-in-time correctness.

Manipulating timestamps may therefore create subtle model errors.

---

### 53. Source Attribution Manipulation

Consider:

```text id="x83m7q"
Untrusted Source
      ↓
Metadata Changed
      ↓
Trusted Source ID
      ↓
Enterprise Pipeline
```

If source validation relies entirely on metadata, this can bypass governance controls.

---

### 54. Provenance Forgery

An attacker may attempt to create a false history for a dataset.

The dataset appears to have:

* A legitimate source.
* An approved owner.
* A valid version.
* A normal processing history.

But the underlying information may not be trustworthy.

This is why provenance should be supported by independent evidence where risk warrants it.

---

### 55. Data Pipeline Poisoning

The pipeline itself can become an attack vector.

```text id="m75q3x"
Trusted Dataset
      ↓
Compromised Pipeline
      ↓
Modified Dataset
      ↓
Training
```

The attacker does not need direct access to the original source.

---

### 56. ETL Manipulation

**ETL — Extract, Transform, Load** processes can alter data.

Potential risks include:

* Unauthorized transformation.
* Incorrect filtering.
* Malicious record insertion.
* Record deletion.
* Feature manipulation.

ETL pipelines should therefore be treated as security-sensitive assets.

---

### 57. Feature Engineering Poisoning

Feature engineering converts raw data into model inputs.

```text id="q84m7x"
Raw Data
   ↓
Feature Engineering
   ↓
Model Features
```

If feature logic is manipulated, the model may receive systematically incorrect information.

---

### 58. Feature Store Integrity

Organizations using feature stores should protect:

* Feature definitions.
* Feature values.
* Feature versions.
* Access permissions.
* Update processes.
* Historical values.

A compromised feature store can affect both training and inference.

---

### 59. Feature Definition Manipulation

Example:

```text id="v73m8q"
Expected:
customer_risk = approved formula

Modified:
customer_risk = altered formula
```

If the change is not detected, both training and production behavior may be affected.

---

### 60. Pipeline Dependency Poisoning

AI pipelines may depend on:

* Libraries.
* Data connectors.
* Transformation tools.
* APIs.
* External services.

If a dependency modifies data unexpectedly, the resulting dataset may be contaminated.

This connects AI data poisoning with **software supply-chain risk**.

---

### 61. Vendor Feed Poisoning

An enterprise may receive continuous data from a vendor.

Example:

```text id="m85q4x"
Vendor
  ↓
API Feed
  ↓
Enterprise Data Platform
  ↓
AI
```

If the vendor feed is compromised, malicious or corrupted data can enter the AI environment at scale.

---

### 62. API-Based Poisoning

APIs can be used to inject or manipulate data.

Controls may include:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Input validation.
* Rate limiting.
* Schema validation.
* Monitoring.
* Request logging.

---

### 63. User-Generated Data Poisoning

Some AI systems learn from user-generated content.

Examples include:

* Reviews.
* Comments.
* Feedback.
* Uploaded documents.
* Community contributions.

An attacker may intentionally submit large volumes of manipulated content.

---

### 64. Coordinated Data Manipulation

A sophisticated attack may use multiple accounts or sources.

Conceptually:

```text id="q64m8v"
Account A ─┐
Account B ─┤
Account C ─┼→ Manipulated Data
Account D ─┘
```

The objective may be to make malicious data appear independently generated.

---

### 65. Sybil-Like Data Attacks

In systems where multiple identities contribute data, attackers may create many identities.

The risk is:

> **One actor appears to be many independent data contributors.**

This can distort datasets or feedback systems.

---

### 66. Feedback Poisoning

AI systems that learn from user feedback may be vulnerable to manipulated feedback.

Example:

```text id="m75q3x"
Users
 ↓
Feedback
 ↓
Preference Dataset
 ↓
Training
 ↓
AI Behavior
```

If feedback is systematically manipulated, the model may learn undesirable preferences.

---

### 67. Reinforcement Learning Data Risk

Systems using reinforcement learning or feedback-driven optimization may be exposed to manipulation of:

* Rewards.
* Feedback.
* Evaluation signals.
* Interaction data.

This creates a risk that the AI optimizes toward an attacker-influenced objective.

---

### 68. Human Annotation Risk

Annotation is another critical control point.

```text id="q83m7x"
Raw Data
    ↓
Annotators
    ↓
Labels
    ↓
Training
```

Poor-quality or malicious annotation can contaminate the training dataset.

---

### 69. Annotation Quality Controls

Controls may include:

* Annotator qualification.
* Training.
* Sampling.
* Peer review.
* Agreement analysis.
* Outlier detection.
* Escalation.
* Access restrictions.

---

### 70. Annotator Collusion

Multiple annotators could potentially coordinate to produce manipulated labels.

This is why high-impact datasets may require:

* Independent sampling.
* Randomized review.
* Statistical anomaly detection.
* Segregation of duties.

---

### 71. Poisoning Through Model Feedback Loops

Some AI systems generate outputs that later become training data.

```text id="v74m8q"
AI Output
   ↓
User Interaction
   ↓
Collected Data
   ↓
Future Training
   ↓
New Model
```

This creates a feedback loop.

If malicious content enters the loop, it may eventually influence future models.

---

### 72. Recursive Contamination

A particularly important risk is:

```text id="m85q4x"
Model Output
      ↓
Stored as Data
      ↓
Used for Training
      ↓
New Model
      ↓
More Model-Generated Data
```

Repeated cycles may amplify undesirable patterns.

---

### 73. Model-Generated Data Contamination

Synthetic or model-generated data can accidentally enter datasets intended to contain human-generated or authoritative information.

This may lead to:

* Reduced diversity.
* Repeated errors.
* Amplified biases.
* Loss of source authenticity.

---

### 74. Model Collapse Consideration

Repeated training on model-generated content can contribute to degradation of data diversity and model quality.

This is often discussed as **model collapse** or related distribution degradation.

The GRC concern is:

> **Is the organization able to distinguish authoritative source data from AI-generated content?**

---

### 75. Contamination Through Test Data

Test data may accidentally enter training.

```text id="q64m8v"
Training
   +
Test Data
   ↓
Model
```

The resulting evaluation may become unreliable.

---

### 76. Contamination Through Validation Data

Validation data can also leak into training.

This can cause:

* Overestimated performance.
* Poor generalization.
* Misleading governance metrics.

Dataset boundaries should therefore be formally governed.

---

### 77. Cross-Dataset Contamination

Two datasets may unintentionally share records.

For example:

```text id="v75m3q"
Training Dataset
      ↕
Customer Dataset
      ↕
Test Dataset
```

The same individuals or transactions may appear across datasets when they should be isolated.

---

### 78. Contamination Through Joins

Data joins can create unexpected combinations.

Example:

```text id="m83q7x"
Customer Table
      +
Transaction Table
      ↓
Joined Dataset
```

An incorrect join key can associate the wrong transaction with the wrong customer.

---

### 79. Referential Contamination

Incorrect relationships can produce misleading model features.

Example:

```text id="q74m8x"
Customer A
      ↓
Transactions of Customer B
```

The raw records may individually be valid, but the resulting dataset is semantically corrupted.

---

### 80. Contamination Through Missing Data

Missing data is not automatically poisoning.

However, unexpected missingness can alter model behavior.

Example:

```text id="x85m3q"
Expected:
Customer Income = Present

Observed:
Customer Income = NULL
```

If missingness becomes systematic, it can affect training and predictions.

---

### 81. Malicious Missingness

An attacker may intentionally remove or suppress values.

Example:

```text id="m63q8v"
Risk Indicator
     ↓
Deleted
     ↓
Model
```

The effect may be subtle and difficult to distinguish from normal data-quality issues.

---

### 82. Data Deletion Attacks

Poisoning does not always mean adding bad data.

Deleting important records can also influence model behavior.

Examples:

* Removing fraud cases.
* Removing failure events.
* Removing negative outcomes.
* Removing minority examples.

---

### 83. Minority-Class Suppression

An attacker may specifically remove examples from an underrepresented class.

```text id="q84m7x"
Fraud Examples
   ↓
Selective Removal
   ↓
Reduced Representation
   ↓
Lower Detection Capability
```

This can resemble ordinary class imbalance unless investigated carefully.

---

### 84. Poisoning Through Filtering Rules

An attacker may alter filtering logic.

Example:

```text id="v73m8q"
Original:
Include all transactions

Modified:
Exclude high-risk transactions
```

The resulting dataset may appear clean because the malicious records were never included.

---

### 85. Poisoning Through Sampling

Sampling logic can also be manipulated.

For example, a pipeline may select a biased subset of records.

The dataset may technically contain only valid records while its **selection process** has been compromised.

---

### 86. Poisoning Through Weighting

Some AI pipelines assign different weights to records.

If weighting is manipulated:

```text id="m84q3x"
Normal Weight = 1.0

Manipulated Weight = 100.0
```

certain records may disproportionately influence training.

---

### 87. Poisoning Through Data Augmentation

AI training may use augmentation to generate additional examples.

If augmentation logic is compromised, generated samples can introduce systematic errors.

This means both:

* Original data.
* Data-generation processes.

may require governance.

---

### 88. Poisoning Through Preprocessing

Preprocessing may include:

* Normalization.
* Tokenization.
* Cleaning.
* Encoding.
* Filtering.
* Deduplication.

A malicious preprocessing change can influence what information reaches the model.

---

### 89. Tokenization and Text Data

For language models, preprocessing may affect:

* Token boundaries.
* Character handling.
* Encoding.
* Filtering.
* Text normalization.

Unexpected transformations can create contamination or remove important information.

---

### 90. Document Ingestion Poisoning

Enterprise AI systems may ingest documents such as:

* Policies.
* Contracts.
* Procedures.
* Reports.
* Knowledge articles.

A malicious or incorrect document can become part of the AI knowledge base.

---

### 91. Document Replacement

Consider:

```text id="q73m8v"
Approved Policy v3
       ↓
Unauthorized Replacement
       ↓
Policy v4
       ↓
RAG Retrieval
       ↓
Incorrect Guidance
```

This demonstrates that **document integrity is AI integrity** when the document becomes an AI knowledge source.

---

### 92. Embedding-Level Contamination

A document may be legitimate at source but incorrectly processed into embeddings.

Potential causes include:

* Wrong embedding model.
* Incorrect chunking.
* Corrupted content.
* Incorrect metadata.
* Wrong document association.

---

### 93. Vector Index Contamination

A vector store can become contaminated if unauthorized content is inserted.

Possible effects include:

* Incorrect retrieval.
* Irrelevant responses.
* Conflicting information.
* Malicious instructions retrieved into context.

---

### 94. Retrieval Ranking Manipulation

If retrieval systems prioritize certain documents incorrectly, malicious content may become disproportionately likely to reach the model.

This can happen through manipulation of:

* Metadata.
* Ranking parameters.
* Relevance scores.
* Index configuration.

---

### 95. Data Poisoning vs Prompt Injection

These concepts should not be confused.

**Prompt injection:**

> Attempts to influence model behavior through input or retrieved instructions at inference time.

**Data poisoning:**

> Attempts to influence the data used by the AI system, potentially affecting future model behavior or retrieved knowledge.

They can interact, particularly in RAG systems.

---

### 96. Persistent vs Transient Manipulation

A useful distinction is:

**Transient manipulation**

```text id="m85q3v"
Malicious Input
   ↓
One Response
```

**Persistent manipulation**

```text id="q74m8x"
Malicious Data
   ↓
Stored
   ↓
Future Training / Retrieval
   ↓
Repeated Influence
```

Persistent contamination can be considerably more difficult to eliminate.

---

### 97. Poisoning Persistence

A poisoning event becomes more dangerous when the malicious data is:

* Stored long-term.
* Replicated.
* Backed up.
* Included in multiple datasets.
* Used for training.
* Used for fine-tuning.
* Distributed to downstream systems.

---

### 98. Propagation Risk

One contaminated source can propagate:

```text id="v63m7q"
Source
 ↓
Data Lake
 ↓
Feature Store
 ↓
Training Dataset
 ↓
Model
 ↓
Production
```

GRC therefore needs **dependency visibility**.

---

### 99. Poisoning Blast Radius

The blast radius depends on:

* Number of affected records.
* Dataset criticality.
* Number of models.
* Number of applications.
* Duration of exposure.
* Number of decisions influenced.

A small initial poisoning event can create a large downstream impact.

---

### 100. The Core Security Principle

The central principle of Part 2 is:

> **Data poisoning and contamination can occur anywhere data is collected, transformed, labeled, stored, selected, enriched, retrieved, or reused. Therefore, AI security must protect the entire data supply chain rather than focusing exclusively on the final training dataset.**

The next stage of analysis is to understand **who can perform these actions, why they would do it, how poisoning can be detected, and how an enterprise should build preventive and detective controls around the AI data lifecycle.**
