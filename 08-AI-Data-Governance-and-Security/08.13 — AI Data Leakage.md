# 08.13 — AI Data Leakage

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to AI Data Leakage

AI data leakage is the **unauthorized exposure, disclosure, transfer, or retrieval of information through an AI system, its data, its supporting infrastructure, or its operational processes**.

The key concern is:

> **Sensitive or protected information reaches a person, system, model, application, or environment that is not authorized to receive it.**

AI introduces additional leakage pathways because data may move through:

* Training datasets.
* Fine-tuning datasets.
* Prompts.
* Conversations.
* Context windows.
* Retrieval systems.
* Vector databases.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* Monitoring systems.
* APIs.
* Third-party AI platforms.

---

### 2. Why AI Data Leakage Is Different

Traditional data leakage generally involves information leaving a controlled environment.

AI creates additional pathways because information can be transformed and exposed through **model behavior**.

For example:

```text id="a73k9m"
Confidential Data
       ↓
AI System
       ↓
Model Processing
       ↓
Generated Response
       ↓
Unauthorized User
```

The user may never directly access the original database.

The AI system becomes the intermediary through which the information is disclosed.

---

### 3. AI Data Leakage vs Traditional Data Leakage

Traditional leakage:

```text id="m84q2x"
Database
   ↓
Unauthorized Access
   ↓
Data Exfiltration
```

AI leakage:

```text id="q75m8v"
Database / Dataset
       ↓
AI System
       ↓
Prompt / Retrieval / Model
       ↓
Output
       ↓
Information Disclosure
```

The second scenario introduces additional governance questions around:

* Model access.
* Prompt handling.
* Retrieval authorization.
* Output controls.
* AI logging.
* Model memorization.

---

### 4. Confidentiality as the Core Objective

AI data leakage primarily represents a **confidentiality risk**.

The CIA triad provides a useful foundation:

```text id="v63m7q"
             CIA TRIAD
                 │
       ┌─────────┼─────────┐
       ↓         ↓         ↓
Confidentiality Integrity Availability
       │
       ↓
 AI Data Leakage
```

However, leakage can also create secondary:

* Integrity risks.
* Privacy risks.
* Regulatory risks.
* Financial risks.
* Reputational risks.
* Safety risks.

---

### 5. What Counts as AI Data?

For GRC purposes, AI data should be interpreted broadly.

It can include:

* Training data.
* Fine-tuning data.
* Validation data.
* Test data.
* Prompts.
* User conversations.
* Retrieved documents.
* Embeddings.
* Vector-store contents.
* Metadata.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* Feedback data.
* Evaluation datasets.

Therefore:

> **AI data leakage is not limited to the training dataset.**

---

### 6. AI Data Leakage Lifecycle

A useful lifecycle is:

```text id="m74q8x"
COLLECT
   ↓
STORE
   ↓
PROCESS
   ↓
TRAIN
   ↓
RETRIEVE
   ↓
INFER
   ↓
GENERATE
   ↓
LOG
   ↓
SHARE
   ↓
RETAIN / DELETE
```

Every stage can create a leakage opportunity.

---

### 7. AI Data Leakage Attack Surface

The attack surface can be visualized as:

```text id="q84m3v"
                AI DATA
                   │
     ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
     ↓             ↓             ↓
 Training       Inference      Storage
     │             │             │
     ↓             ↓             ↓
Fine-tuning      Prompts       Logs
     │             │             │
     └─────────────┼─────────────┘
                   ↓
              AI OUTPUT
```

Each component needs appropriate confidentiality controls.

---

### 8. Primary AI Leakage Categories

AI data leakage can be categorized into several major areas:

1. **Training data leakage**
2. **Prompt leakage**
3. **Output leakage**
4. **Retrieval leakage**
5. **Model memorization**
6. **Embedding leakage**
7. **Logging leakage**
8. **API leakage**
9. **Third-party leakage**
10. **Cross-tenant leakage**
11. **Access-control leakage**
12. **Human-induced leakage**

These categories will be developed throughout this topic.

---

### 9. Training Data Leakage

Training data may contain:

* Personal information.
* Customer records.
* Intellectual property.
* Credentials.
* Internal documents.
* Source code.
* Confidential business information.

If improperly governed, information from the training dataset may become exposed through the AI system.

---

### 10. Training Data Should Be Minimized

A fundamental principle is:

> **Do not place sensitive information into an AI training environment unless there is a legitimate, authorized, and governed requirement to do so.**

The organization should first ask:

```text id="v73m8q"
Do we need the data?
       ↓
Can we minimize it?
       ↓
Can we anonymize it?
       ↓
Can we use synthetic data?
       ↓
Can we avoid training altogether?
```

---

### 11. Data Minimization and Leakage Prevention

Data minimization reduces the potential impact of leakage.

For example:

```text id="m85q4x"
Full Customer Record
        ↓
Required Fields Only
        ↓
AI Dataset
```

If the AI system does not require a field, removing it reduces unnecessary exposure.

---

### 12. Sensitive Data in Training

Sensitive information requires enhanced governance.

Examples include:

* Identity information.
* Financial information.
* Authentication information.
* Confidential corporate information.
* Health-related information.
* Legal information.
* Proprietary intellectual property.

The exact classification depends on applicable law and organizational policy.

---

### 13. Accidental Training Leakage

Not all leakage is malicious.

An employee might upload:

> A confidential customer spreadsheet into a public AI service to summarize it.

The employee may not intend to disclose the information.

Nevertheless, the organization may have experienced a data-handling violation.

This is why:

> **Human behavior is part of the AI data leakage threat model.**

---

### 14. Shadow AI

**Shadow AI** occurs when employees use AI tools without appropriate organizational approval or oversight.

Example:

```text id="q74m8x"
Employee
   ↓
Copies Confidential Data
   ↓
Unapproved AI Tool
   ↓
External AI Provider
```

This creates a potentially uncontrolled data flow.

---

### 15. Unsanctioned AI Tools

Employees may use:

* Public chatbots.
* Browser AI extensions.
* AI writing assistants.
* AI coding assistants.
* AI transcription tools.
* AI document-analysis services.

GRC should therefore maintain visibility over approved and prohibited AI services.

---

### 16. Prompt-Based Leakage

Prompts can contain sensitive information.

Example:

```text id="v63m7q"
Employee Prompt:
"Analyze this confidential customer complaint..."
```

If the prompt is transmitted to an external service, the confidential information has crossed a trust boundary.

---

### 17. Prompt Data Classification

Organizations should establish rules for what can be entered into AI prompts.

Example:

| Data Type        | Prompt Policy                         |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Public           | Generally permitted                   |
| Internal         | Controlled                            |
| Confidential     | Restricted                            |
| Highly sensitive | Prohibited unless explicitly approved |

The exact policy should reflect the organization's environment.

---

### 18. Prompt Leakage

Prompt leakage can also mean unauthorized exposure of information **from** prompts.

For example:

```text id="m84q7x"
User A
   ↓
Sensitive Prompt
   ↓
AI System
   ↓
User B
```

If the system incorrectly exposes conversation context, User B may receive User A's information.

---

### 19. Conversation History Leakage

AI applications may store conversation histories.

Potential risks include:

* Incorrect access permissions.
* Shared accounts.
* Misconfigured APIs.
* Excessive retention.
* Unauthorized administrator access.

Conversation data should therefore be treated as potentially sensitive.

---

### 20. Cross-User Leakage

A serious AI security failure occurs when information belonging to one user appears in another user's interaction.

Conceptually:

```text id="q75m3x"
User A Data
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
User B Request
     ↓
User A Information
```

This may indicate failures in:

* Authorization.
* Session isolation.
* Retrieval filtering.
* Tenant isolation.
* Application logic.

---

### 21. Cross-Tenant Leakage

In multi-tenant AI environments:

```text id="v74m8q"
Tenant A
   ↓
Shared AI Platform
   ↓
Tenant B
```

The system must ensure that Tenant B cannot retrieve or receive Tenant A's data.

This is especially important for:

* SaaS AI platforms.
* Managed AI services.
* Shared vector databases.
* Multi-tenant RAG systems.

---

### 22. Retrieval-Augmented Generation Leakage

RAG systems introduce a major leakage pathway.

Basic architecture:

```text id="m85q3x"
User
 ↓
Question
 ↓
Retriever
 ↓
Knowledge Base
 ↓
Relevant Documents
 ↓
LLM
 ↓
Answer
```

If retrieval authorization is incorrectly implemented, the AI may retrieve information the user is not entitled to access.

---

### 23. Retrieval Authorization

A critical principle is:

> **The AI system must enforce the user's existing data-access permissions before retrieving information.**

It should not assume that because a document is indexed, every AI user may access it.

---

### 24. RAG Permission Failure

Example:

```text id="q73m8v"
HR Document
   ↓
Vector Database
   ↓
Employee asks AI
   ↓
Retriever ignores HR permissions
   ↓
HR information disclosed
```

The vector database may be functioning correctly technically while the overall AI system still has a confidentiality failure.

---

### 25. Metadata-Based Access Control

RAG systems can use metadata such as:

* User ID.
* Department.
* Role.
* Tenant.
* Classification.
* Document owner.

Example:

```text id="v84m7q"
Document
  ├─ Classification: Confidential
  ├─ Department: Finance
  └─ Access Group: Finance-Managers
```

The retrieval system should use these attributes when determining whether the document can be returned.

---

### 26. Vector Database Leakage

Vector databases may contain:

* Embeddings.
* Document chunks.
* Metadata.
* Source references.
* Access information.

A compromise could expose information even if the original document repository remains protected.

---

### 27. Embedding Security

Embeddings should not automatically be considered harmless because they are numerical representations.

They may still encode information derived from sensitive content.

Therefore:

> **Embedding stores require appropriate access control and security classification based on the information they contain and the risks of reconstruction or inference.**

---

### 28. Model Output Leakage

An AI model may disclose sensitive information through its output.

Example:

```text id="m75q4x"
Sensitive Source
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Generated Response
      ↓
Unauthorized Disclosure
```

The output is the point at which the confidentiality failure becomes visible to the recipient.

---

### 29. Direct Disclosure

The simplest case is direct disclosure.

Example:

> "Here is the customer's private account number."

The model has directly reproduced sensitive information.

---

### 30. Indirect Disclosure

Leakage does not always reproduce the original data exactly.

The AI may reveal:

* Summaries.
* Attributes.
* Statistics.
* Relationships.
* Sensitive conclusions.

Example:

> "The customer has a history of financial difficulties."

Even without revealing the complete record, the output may disclose protected information.

---

### 31. Inference-Based Leakage

AI systems may infer sensitive information from seemingly non-sensitive data.

```text id="q84m7x"
Multiple Non-Sensitive Inputs
          ↓
          AI
          ↓
Sensitive Inference
```

This creates an important governance issue:

> **Data that appears harmless individually may become sensitive when combined or inferred.**

---

### 32. Membership Inference

A model may potentially reveal whether a particular record was included in a training dataset.

Conceptually:

```text id="v73m8q"
Question:
"Was Person X in the training data?"

        ↓

Model Behavior
        ↓
Potential Membership Signal
```

This can create privacy risk when dataset membership itself is sensitive.

---

### 33. Model Memorization

Large models may sometimes retain specific training information.

The concern is greater when training data contains:

* Rare information.
* Repeated information.
* Structured secrets.
* Personal information.
* Credentials.
* Proprietary content.

The governance objective is to minimize unnecessary memorization and exposure.

---

### 34. Data Extraction Attacks

Attackers may intentionally interact with a model to attempt to extract information.

Conceptually:

```text id="m84q3x"
Attacker
   ↓
Repeated Queries
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Sensitive Information
```

This differs from ordinary user interaction because the attacker is deliberately attempting to recover protected information.

---

### 35. Repeated Query Attacks

One query may reveal nothing.

Hundreds or thousands of carefully designed queries may reveal patterns.

Therefore monitoring should consider:

* Query frequency.
* Repeated probing.
* Unusual query patterns.
* Account behavior.
* Output anomalies.

---

### 36. Prompt Manipulation for Leakage

Attackers may attempt to manipulate model instructions to make it disclose information.

This can overlap with:

* Prompt injection.
* Jailbreaking.
* Instruction manipulation.

From a GRC perspective, the important question is:

> **Can an unauthorized user cause the AI system to reveal information outside their authorization boundary?**

---

### 37. System Prompt Leakage

System instructions may contain:

* Internal configuration.
* Business logic.
* Security controls.
* Proprietary instructions.
* Hidden context.

Exposure of system prompts can reveal sensitive implementation information.

However, system prompt disclosure and business-data leakage are distinct risks and should be tracked separately.

---

### 38. Context Window Leakage

AI applications may pass large amounts of contextual information to the model.

Potential sources include:

* Previous messages.
* Retrieved documents.
* Tool results.
* User profiles.
* Application metadata.

If context isolation fails, information may cross user or session boundaries.

---

### 39. Tool-Calling Leakage

AI agents can interact with:

* Databases.
* File systems.
* APIs.
* CRM systems.
* Email.
* Enterprise applications.

An AI agent with excessive permissions can potentially retrieve information beyond what the user should receive.

---

### 40. Excessive Agent Privilege

Consider:

```text id="q75m8x"
User
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Enterprise Systems
 ↓
Too Much Access
```

The AI agent becomes an indirect data-access mechanism.

The principle should therefore be:

> **An AI agent should have no more data access than necessary to perform its approved task.**

---

### 41. API Response Leakage

An API may return more information than the AI application actually needs.

Example:

```text id="v84m3q"
API Response
 ├─ Customer Name
 ├─ Address
 ├─ Phone
 ├─ Account Number
 ├─ Internal Risk Score
 └─ Full Transaction History
```

The AI may require only the customer name and transaction status.

Excessive API responses increase leakage exposure.

---

### 42. Data Overexposure

This leads to a core principle:

> **Only the minimum information required to perform the AI task should be exposed to the AI component.**

This combines:

* Least privilege.
* Data minimization.
* Need-to-know.
* Purpose limitation.

---

### 43. Logging Leakage

AI systems frequently generate logs containing:

* Prompts.
* Outputs.
* User IDs.
* Retrieved documents.
* Tool calls.
* Error messages.

Logs can therefore become a secondary sensitive-data repository.

---

### 44. Observability Leakage

Security and monitoring tools may capture AI interactions.

For example:

```text id="m73q8x"
User Prompt
   ↓
AI Application
   ↓
Observability Platform
   ↓
Log Storage
```

If the observability platform has weaker security than the AI application, it may become the weakest link.

---

### 45. Debug Logging Risk

Developers may temporarily enable verbose logging.

This can unintentionally capture:

* Full prompts.
* Full responses.
* Sensitive API responses.
* Retrieved documents.

Temporary debugging controls should therefore have:

* Defined duration.
* Approval.
* Restricted access.
* Automatic expiration where possible.

---

### 46. Backup Leakage

AI data may be copied into:

* Backups.
* Snapshots.
* Disaster-recovery systems.
* Development environments.

A production dataset that is properly protected may become exposed through a poorly secured backup.

---

### 47. Development Environment Leakage

Production data may be copied into development for troubleshooting.

Example:

```text id="q84m7v"
Production
   ↓
Data Copy
   ↓
Development
   ↓
AI Testing
```

Development environments generally have a larger population of users and should not automatically receive production-sensitive data.

---

### 48. Test Environment Leakage

Test datasets may contain real customer or employee information.

Organizations should prefer:

* Synthetic data.
* Masked data.
* Tokenized data.
* Anonymized data where appropriate.

The objective is to avoid unnecessary exposure.

---

### 49. Third-Party AI Provider Leakage

When enterprise data is sent to an external AI provider, the organization must understand:

* Where the data is processed.
* Who can access it.
* How long it is retained.
* Whether it is used for training.
* Where it is stored.
* What subprocessors are involved.
* How deletion works.

These questions are central to third-party AI governance.

---

### 50. Core GRC Principle

The central lesson of Part 1 is:

> **AI data leakage is not simply the unauthorized downloading of an AI dataset. It is any unauthorized exposure of information through the AI data, model, application, retrieval, agent, logging, infrastructure, or third-party ecosystem.**

A mature AI GRC program therefore needs to answer:

```text id="m75q3x"
WHAT data can the AI access?
        ↓
WHO can access it?
        ↓
WHERE does it go?
        ↓
HOW is it processed?
        ↓
WHAT can the model retrieve?
        ↓
WHAT can the AI disclose?
        ↓
WHAT is logged?
        ↓
WHO receives the output?
        ↓
HOW long is information retained?
```

# 08.13 — AI Data Leakage

## Part 2

### 51. AI Data Leakage Threat Model

AI data leakage should be analyzed as a chain of possible exposure points rather than as a single event.

```text
 id="a81x4m"
DATA SOURCE
    ↓
DATA INGESTION
    ↓
STORAGE
    ↓
TRAINING / FINE-TUNING
    ↓
MODEL / KNOWLEDGE BASE
    ↓
RETRIEVAL
    ↓
PROMPT / CONTEXT
    ↓
INFERENCE
    ↓
TOOLS / AGENTS
    ↓
OUTPUT
    ↓
LOGGING / MONITORING
    ↓
EXTERNAL SYSTEMS
```

A GRC assessment should ask at each stage:

> **What information can be exposed here, to whom, under what conditions, and with what controls?**

---

### 52. Training Data Extraction

One of the most important AI leakage scenarios is attempting to recover information that was present in training data.

Conceptually:

```text
Training Dataset
      ↓
      AI Model
      ↓
Attacker Queries
      ↓
Potential Data Disclosure
```

The attacker does not necessarily need direct access to the training dataset.

---

### 53. Why Training Data Extraction Matters

If training data contains:

* Personal information.
* Credentials.
* Customer records.
* Confidential documents.
* Proprietary source code.
* Internal communications.

then successful extraction could create significant confidentiality and privacy consequences.

---

### 54. Memorization Risk

A model may memorize portions of its training information.

The likelihood and significance depend on factors such as:

* Data repetition.
* Dataset size.
* Model architecture.
* Training methodology.
* Fine-tuning approach.
* Uniqueness of the information.

GRC should therefore ask whether sensitive information was necessary in the training process.

---

### 55. Rare Data Is Particularly Important

Rare or unique information may present greater extraction concerns.

For example:

```text
Common Information
→ Many Examples

Unique Secret
→ One Example
```

A unique secret can be easier to distinguish from general learned patterns.

This reinforces the importance of **not training models unnecessarily on secrets or highly sensitive information**.

---

### 56. Credential Leakage

Credentials should generally never be intentionally included in AI training datasets.

Examples include:

* Passwords.
* API keys.
* Private keys.
* Access tokens.
* Database credentials.
* Service-account secrets.

If discovered in AI data, they should be treated as a security incident or security finding according to organizational policy.

---

### 57. Source-Code Leakage

AI coding systems may process:

* Proprietary source code.
* Internal repositories.
* Architecture documentation.
* Configuration files.

Potential leakage can occur through:

* Prompts.
* Model outputs.
* Logs.
* External AI services.
* Developer tools.

---

### 58. Intellectual Property Leakage

AI systems may process proprietary:

* Designs.
* Algorithms.
* Product specifications.
* Research.
* Business strategies.
* Contracts.

The information may not be classified as personal data, but unauthorized disclosure can still cause substantial business harm.

---

### 59. Personal Data Leakage

Personal information may enter AI systems through:

* Training data.
* Prompts.
* Uploaded documents.
* CRM integrations.
* RAG systems.
* Logs.

The organization must consider applicable privacy requirements in addition to cybersecurity controls.

---

### 60. Sensitive Attribute Inference

An AI system may infer sensitive characteristics from other information.

Example:

```text
Non-sensitive attributes
       ↓
AI inference
       ↓
Potentially sensitive conclusion
```

The inferred information may create a privacy concern even if the original fields appeared relatively harmless.

---

### 61. Membership Inference Attacks

Membership inference attempts to determine whether a particular individual, record, or document was part of training data.

Conceptually:

```text
Target Record
      ↓
Attacker Queries Model
      ↓
Analyze Model Responses
      ↓
Estimate:
"Was this record used?"
```

The privacy impact depends on what dataset membership reveals.

---

### 62. Model Inversion

Model inversion attempts to infer information about the underlying training data from model behavior.

Conceptually:

```text
Model Behavior
      ↓
Repeated Analysis
      ↓
Infer Characteristics
      ↓
Potential Sensitive Information
```

This is another reason model interfaces should be treated as potential information-disclosure channels.

---

### 63. RAG Data Leakage

RAG systems have a distinct leakage risk because the model can retrieve enterprise documents at inference time.

```text
User
 ↓
Query
 ↓
Retriever
 ↓
Enterprise Knowledge Base
 ↓
Retrieved Context
 ↓
LLM
 ↓
Response
```

If authorization is incorrect, sensitive information may be retrieved for an unauthorized user.

---

### 64. Retrieval Before Authorization

A critical architectural mistake is:

```text
User Query
   ↓
Retrieve Everything Relevant
   ↓
Check Permission Later
```

This can expose information during processing or logging even if the final answer is filtered.

A safer principle is:

> **Authorization should constrain retrieval itself, not merely the final response.**

---

### 65. Document-Level Authorization

RAG systems should ideally preserve document-level access controls.

For example:

```text
Document A
→ Finance only

Document B
→ HR only

Document C
→ All employees
```

The retriever should enforce these boundaries.

---

### 66. Chunk-Level Leakage

Documents are often split into chunks.

A single sensitive document might become:

```text
Document
 ├── Chunk 1
 ├── Chunk 2
 ├── Chunk 3
 └── Chunk 4
```

Access controls must account for the resulting retrieval objects, not just the original document repository.

---

### 67. Metadata Leakage

Even when document content is protected, metadata may reveal:

* Customer names.
* Project names.
* Department information.
* Confidential classifications.
* File locations.
* Document titles.

Metadata should therefore be governed as information, not merely as technical attributes.

---

### 68. Citation Leakage

RAG applications may display citations or document references.

Potential leakage can occur if the AI reveals:

* Confidential document titles.
* Internal URLs.
* Restricted file paths.
* Names of confidential projects.

Even a citation that contains no document content may reveal sensitive information.

---

### 69. Search Result Leakage

An AI application may accidentally reveal the existence of restricted information.

Example:

```text
User asks:
"Do we have an investigation about Employee X?"

AI:
"Yes, there are three restricted documents."
```

Even without revealing the documents, the response may disclose confidential information.

---

### 70. Negative-Answer Leakage

Security controls should consider both positive and negative answers.

For example:

> "I cannot show you that document."

This may itself confirm that a restricted document exists.

The application's disclosure policy should determine whether such information can be revealed.

---

### 71. Cross-User Conversation Leakage

A multi-user AI system must maintain strict separation between conversations.

```text
User A Session
      ↓
Conversation A

User B Session
      ↓
Conversation B
```

Context from A should never accidentally become available to B.

---

### 72. Session Isolation

Potential controls include:

* Unique session identifiers.
* Authorization checks.
* Secure session management.
* Context isolation.
* Tenant isolation.
* Secure storage.

Session identifiers should never be treated as authorization by themselves.

---

### 73. Conversation History Leakage

If an application stores previous conversations, the system should define:

* Who can access them.
* How long they are retained.
* Whether administrators can access them.
* Whether they are used for future model improvement.
* How users can delete them.

---

### 74. Shared Account Risk

Shared accounts make attribution and confidentiality more difficult.

Example:

```text
One Account
 ↓
Multiple Employees
 ↓
Shared AI History
```

This can result in one employee seeing another employee's sensitive prompts or outputs.

---

### 75. Browser and Client Leakage

Sensitive AI interactions may also be exposed through:

* Browser history.
* Local caches.
* Clipboard contents.
* Screenshots.
* Downloads.
* Browser extensions.
* Endpoint malware.

AI data governance therefore extends beyond the AI server.

---

### 76. AI Agent Data Leakage

AI agents can access enterprise systems on behalf of users.

```text
User
 ↓
AI Agent
 ├── CRM
 ├── Email
 ├── File Storage
 ├── Database
 └── Ticketing
```

Each integration creates another potential leakage path.

---

### 77. Excessive Agent Permissions

Suppose an agent only needs to read a user's sales opportunities.

If it can instead:

```text
Read All CRM Records
+
Read HR Data
+
Read Finance Data
```

the potential leakage impact becomes much greater.

The correct design is:

> **Give the agent only the minimum permissions required for its approved function.**

---

### 78. Agent-to-Agent Leakage

Organizations may deploy multiple AI agents.

```text
Agent A
   ↓
Agent B
   ↓
Agent C
```

Data passed between agents should have defined:

* Purpose.
* Authorization.
* Classification.
* Retention.
* Logging.

An agent should not automatically trust another agent.

---

### 79. Tool Output Leakage

An AI agent may call an API and receive more information than necessary.

Example:

```text
Tool Request
     ↓
API
     ↓
Full Customer Record
     ↓
AI Agent
```

The tool should ideally return only the information required for the task.

---

### 80. Tool Result Persistence

Tool results may be stored in:

* Conversation history.
* Logs.
* Caches.
* Agent memory.
* Databases.

Therefore, even if the original API is secure, downstream storage can introduce a new leakage path.

---

### 81. Agent Memory Leakage

Some AI systems maintain memory across interactions.

Potential concerns include:

* Excessive retention.
* Incorrect user association.
* Cross-user access.
* Sensitive information stored unnecessarily.

Memory should have explicit governance.

---

### 82. Long-Term Memory

An AI assistant might remember:

```text
User preferences
+
Business information
+
Past conversations
+
Sensitive documents
```

The organization should determine:

* What can be remembered.
* For how long.
* For what purpose.
* Who can access it.
* How it can be deleted.

---

### 83. API Data Leakage

APIs can expose information through:

* Excessive response fields.
* Weak authorization.
* Misconfigured endpoints.
* Debug responses.
* Error messages.
* Unencrypted transport.

AI applications should inherit enterprise API security standards.

---

### 84. Error Message Leakage

An AI application may accidentally return technical details.

Example:

```text

customer_records_prod

Internal path:
/finance/restricted/customer_data
```

Even if the user does not receive the underlying record, such information can assist reconnaissance.

---

### 85. Exception and Debug Information

Production systems should avoid exposing unnecessary:

* Stack traces.
* Database queries.
* Internal identifiers.
* Infrastructure names.
* API credentials.
* Configuration details.

This is particularly important for AI applications because users may deliberately probe error behavior.

---

### 86. Third-Party AI Leakage

When data is sent to a third-party AI provider, the enterprise must understand the complete data flow.

```text
    ↓

    ↓
Subprocessors
    ↓

```

Each boundary introduces governance requirements.

---

### 87. Third-Party Data Processing

Important questions include:

1. Is customer data processed by the provider?
2. Is it retained?
3. Is it used for model training?
4. Where is it processed?
5. Which subprocessors receive it?
6. How is deletion handled?
7. What security controls exist?

These should be addressed before sensitive data is permitted into the service.

---

### 88. Data Residency Leakage

Data may cross geographical or regulatory boundaries.

Example:

```text
     ↓

     ↓

```

This can create:

A provider may rely on additional companies for:

* Cloud hosting.

The enterprise should understand whether these subprocessors can access customer information.

### 90. AI Supply-Chain Leakage

AI applications may depend on:

* External models.
* APIs.
* Datasets.
* Plugins.
* Extensions.
* Libraries.
* Hosting providers.

A compromise in one dependency may create a data-exposure pathway.

An AI application may connect to third-party plugins.

```text
      ↓

      ↓
External Service
```

The plugin may receive user prompts or tool results.

Plugin permissions should therefore be explicitly governed.

### 92. Browser Extension Risk

AI browser extensions may have access to webpage content.

Potential exposure could include:

* Internal applications.
* Customer portals.
* Email.
* Documents.
* Corporate dashboards.

Organizations should establish rules for approved extensions and browser-based AI tools.

---

### 93. Email Integration Leakage

An AI assistant connected to email may process:

* Customer communications.
* Contracts.
* Financial information.
* Internal discussions.

Access should be limited according to user authorization and business purpose.

---

### 94. File-System Integration Leakage

An AI assistant connected to enterprise file storage can potentially access:

```text

HR
Legal
Engineering
Operations
```

The AI should not receive unrestricted file-system access simply because the employee can use the assistant.

---

### 95. Database Integration Leakage

Direct database access creates additional risk.

A poorly designed AI-to-database integration could allow:

```text

     ↓
AI-generated Query
     ↓
Database
     ↓

```

Database access should be:

* Scoped.
* Read-only where possible.
* Parameterized.
* Audited.
* Subject to authorization.

### 96. Natural-Language-to-SQL Risk

An AI system that converts natural language into SQL can unintentionally expose excessive records.

For example:

> "Show me all customer information."

If the underlying database permissions are too broad, the AI could retrieve information far beyond the user's legitimate requirement.

### 97. Output Leakage Through Summarization

Summarization does not automatically make sensitive information safe.

```text
        ↓
```

# 08.13 — AI Data Leakage

## Part 3

### 101. AI Data Leakage Prevention Strategy

Preventing AI data leakage requires a **defense-in-depth approach**.

No single control is sufficient because AI data can move through many components.

```text
Data Classification
        ↓
Data Minimization
        ↓
Access Control
        ↓
Encryption
        ↓
DLP
        ↓
Retrieval Authorization
        ↓
Output Controls
        ↓
Monitoring
        ↓
Incident Response
```

The GRC objective is to ensure that a failure in one control does not automatically result in a material data breach.

---

### 102. Data Classification as the First Control

Before protecting AI data, the organization needs to understand what it is protecting.

A classification model might be:

| Classification | Example                             | AI Handling         |
| -------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Public         | Published information               | Generally permitted |
| Internal       | Internal procedures                 | Controlled          |
| Confidential   | Customer records                    | Restricted          |
| Restricted     | Credentials / highly sensitive data | Strong controls     |

The organization's official classification policy should determine the final categories.

---

### 103. AI-Specific Data Classification

Traditional classification may not be sufficient for AI.

AI governance should consider:

* Training data.
* Prompt data.
* Retrieved data.
* Model outputs.
* Embeddings.
* Conversation history.
* Agent memory.
* Logs.

The classification should follow the **information**, not simply the system containing it.

---

### 104. Data Classification Inheritance

A useful principle is:

> **Derived AI outputs should inherit an appropriate sensitivity level from the information they contain.**

For example:

```text
Confidential Document
       ↓
AI Summary
       ↓
Confidential Summary
```

The summary should not automatically become "Internal" merely because it is shorter.

---

### 105. Data Minimization

Data minimization reduces the amount of information available to the AI system.

The principle is:

> **Provide only the information necessary for the approved AI purpose.**

Example:

```text
Full Customer Record
        ↓
Required Attributes
        ↓
AI System
```

This reduces both probability and impact of leakage.

---

### 106. Purpose Limitation

Data should be used only for its authorized purpose.

For example:

> Customer-support data approved for customer-service assistance should not automatically be reused for AI model training.

A separate assessment and authorization may be required.

---

### 107. Need-to-Know

AI access should be based on business necessity.

```text
Can the AI access it?
        ↓
Does the AI need it?
        ↓
Does the user have permission?
        ↓
Is the use authorized?
```

Access should not be granted simply because the technical integration makes it possible.

---

### 108. Least Privilege

AI systems, agents, services, and integrations should receive the minimum permissions required.

```text
Required Access = Minimum Necessary Access
```

This applies to:

* Models.
* Agents.
* APIs.
* Databases.
* File repositories.
* Vector databases.
* Service accounts.

---

### 109. Identity and Access Management

AI systems should integrate with enterprise IAM where practical.

Controls can include:

* SSO.
* MFA.
* RBAC.
* ABAC.
* Privileged access management.
* Service identities.
* Conditional access.

The goal is to establish:

> **Who is requesting the data and what are they authorized to access?**

---

### 110. Role-Based Access Control

Example:

```text
Employee
 → Internal AI

Finance Manager
 → Finance AI Data

HR Manager
 → HR AI Data

Administrator
 → Administrative Functions
```

Roles should not automatically grant access to all AI data.

---

### 111. Attribute-Based Access Control

ABAC can use attributes such as:

* User.
* Department.
* Location.
* Data classification.
* Tenant.
* Business purpose.
* Device status.

Example:

```text
IF
User.Department = Finance
AND
Document.Department = Finance
AND
Document.Classification <= Confidential

THEN
Permit Retrieval
```

---

### 112. Retrieval Authorization

For RAG systems, authorization should occur **before or during retrieval**.

Preferred model:

```text
User
 ↓
Authenticate
 ↓
Authorize
 ↓
Filter Search Scope
 ↓
Retrieve Authorized Data
 ↓
Generate Response
```

This is stronger than retrieving broadly and attempting to remove unauthorized information later.

---

### 113. Security Trimming

Security trimming ensures that search and retrieval results respect the user's permissions.

For example:

```text
Knowledge Base
 ├── Public
 ├── Finance
 ├── HR
 └── Legal

User = Finance Employee
        ↓
Retrieval Scope
        ↓
Public + Authorized Finance
```

HR and Legal content should not be retrieved.

---

### 114. Document-Level Security

Documents should retain access-control metadata.

Useful metadata can include:

* Owner.
* Department.
* Security classification.
* Access group.
* Tenant.
* Retention category.

The AI retrieval layer should respect those controls.

---

### 115. Chunk-Level Security

If documents are divided into chunks, access controls should remain associated with the chunks.

```text
Document
 ↓
Chunking
 ↓
Chunk + Security Metadata
 ↓
Embedding
 ↓
Vector Store
```

Security metadata must not be lost during transformation.

---

### 116. Vector Database Access Control

Vector stores should have:

* Authentication.
* Authorization.
* Encryption.
* Network controls.
* Audit logging.

They should not be treated merely as technical indexes.

They can contain information derived from protected enterprise content.

---

### 117. Tenant Isolation

Multi-tenant AI environments require strong isolation.

```text
Tenant A
   ↓
Data A
   ↓
AI Environment A

Tenant B
   ↓
Data B
   ↓
AI Environment B
```

Controls should prevent:

* Cross-tenant retrieval.
* Cross-tenant caching.
* Cross-tenant conversation access.
* Cross-tenant model context.

---

### 118. Encryption in Transit

AI data should be protected when transmitted between components.

Examples:

```text
User → AI Application
AI Application → Model
AI → Vector Database
AI → External API
```

Appropriate transport encryption should be used according to enterprise security standards.

---

### 119. Encryption at Rest

Protected AI data may exist in:

* Databases.
* Object storage.
* Vector stores.
* Logs.
* Backups.
* Model repositories.

Encryption at rest helps reduce exposure if storage is compromised.

---

### 120. Key Management

Encryption effectiveness depends on proper key management.

Controls may include:

* Centralized key management.
* Key rotation.
* Access restrictions.
* Separation of duties.
* Audit logging.

Keys should not be stored alongside the sensitive information they protect.

---

### 121. Tokenization

Tokenization replaces sensitive values with tokens.

Example:

```text
Customer Account:
123456789

       ↓

Token:
CUST-8X71K
```

The AI system can operate on the token where the actual value is unnecessary.

---

### 122. Masking

Masking hides portions of sensitive information.

Example:

```text
Original:
1234 5678 9012 3456

Masked:
**** **** **** 3456
```

This can be useful where the AI needs partial information.

---

### 123. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces direct identifiers with pseudonyms.

Example:

```text
Jonathan Morales
       ↓
Customer-10492
```

The mapping remains separately protected.

Pseudonymization reduces exposure but does not necessarily make the data anonymous.

---

### 124. Anonymization

Anonymization aims to prevent identification of individuals.

Where effective, it can significantly reduce privacy risk.

However, organizations should carefully evaluate whether re-identification remains possible.

---

### 125. Synthetic Data

Synthetic data can replace real sensitive information in:

* Development.
* Testing.
* Model evaluation.
* Training experiments.

This reduces unnecessary exposure to real records.

---

### 126. Data Loss Prevention

DLP controls can inspect information entering or leaving AI systems.

Potential channels include:

```text
Email
File Upload
Prompt
API
Chat
Browser
Clipboard
Download
```

DLP can identify prohibited information before transmission.

---

### 127. AI-Aware DLP

Traditional DLP rules may be extended to recognize AI-related workflows.

For example:

```text
IF
Sensitive Data Detected
AND
Destination = Unapproved AI Service

THEN
Block / Warn / Escalate
```

This creates a policy enforcement point around AI usage.

---

### 128. Prompt DLP

Prompt inspection can identify:

* Credit-card information.
* Personal identifiers.
* Credentials.
* Source code.
* Confidential keywords.

Possible outcomes:

```text
Allow
Warn
Mask
Block
Escalate
```

The correct response depends on the organization's policy.

---

### 129. Output DLP

DLP can also inspect AI-generated outputs.

```text
AI Response
      ↓
DLP Inspection
      ↓
Sensitive Data?
   ↙       ↘
 YES       NO
 ↓          ↓
Block      Deliver
```

This provides a second protection layer.

---

### 130. Output Filtering

Output controls can identify:

* Credentials.
* Personal information.
* Confidential information.
* Restricted documents.
* Internal system information.

Potential responses include:

* Redaction.
* Blocking.
* Warning.
* Human review.

---

### 131. Pattern-Based Detection

Simple patterns can identify common sensitive information.

Examples:

* Credit-card formats.
* API-key patterns.
* Email addresses.
* Phone numbers.
* Account identifiers.

However:

> **Pattern matching alone is insufficient for AI data leakage prevention.**

Semantic detection may also be required.

---

### 132. Semantic Data Detection

Sensitive information may not have a predictable format.

For example:

> "Our acquisition strategy is to purchase Company X in Q4."

A simple regex may not identify this as confidential.

Semantic classification can help identify context-sensitive information.

---

### 133. Secret Detection

AI environments should scan for:

* Passwords.
* API keys.
* Access tokens.
* Private keys.
* Connection strings.

If secrets are detected:

```text
Detect
 ↓
Block / Quarantine
 ↓
Alert
 ↓
Rotate Secret
 ↓
Investigate
```

---

### 134. Prompt Inspection vs Privacy

Prompt inspection creates an important governance tradeoff.

Security teams need visibility to detect leakage.

But monitoring itself can create another sensitive-data repository.

Therefore:

> **Security monitoring must not become a new uncontrolled source of data exposure.**

---

### 135. Secure Logging

AI logging should follow:

* Data minimization.
* Access control.
* Retention limits.
* Encryption.
* Monitoring.

Organizations should carefully determine whether full prompts and outputs actually need to be retained.

---

### 136. Log Redaction

Sensitive information can be removed before storage.

Example:

```text
Prompt:
"Check account 123456789"

Logged:
"Check account [REDACTED]"
```

This reduces exposure in monitoring systems.

---

### 137. Log Access Control

Access to AI logs should be restricted.

Potential roles:

* SOC analysts.
* Security administrators.
* Authorized investigators.
* System owners.

Not every administrator needs unrestricted access to every AI conversation.

---

### 138. Retention Controls

AI logs should have defined retention periods.

Questions include:

* Why are we retaining this?
* How long is it needed?
* Who can access it?
* Can sensitive content be removed earlier?

Retention should be aligned with legal, regulatory, security, and business requirements.

---

### 139. Human-in-the-Loop Controls

High-risk outputs can require human review.

```text
AI Generates Output
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
High Risk?
   ↙         ↘
 YES         NO
 ↓            ↓
Human       Automatic
Review       Delivery
```

Human review can reduce the probability of inappropriate disclosure.

---

### 140. High-Risk Output Categories

Examples may include outputs involving:

* Highly sensitive personal information.
* Financial information.
* Legal information.
* Security credentials.
* Confidential strategic information.

Organizations should define their own high-risk categories.

---

### 141. Download Controls

AI applications may allow users to download:

* Documents.
* Reports.
* Chat histories.
* Generated files.

Download controls can include:

* Authorization.
* Watermarking.
* DLP inspection.
* File classification.
* Endpoint controls.

---

### 142. Copy and Clipboard Controls

For highly sensitive environments, organizations may consider controls over:

* Copying.
* Clipboard access.
* Screen capture.
* Printing.

These are particularly relevant where AI output contains sensitive information.

---

### 143. Endpoint Controls

AI leakage can occur at the endpoint.

Controls may include:

* Endpoint DLP.
* Device encryption.
* Browser security.
* Application control.
* USB restrictions.
* Managed devices.

Enterprise AI security should therefore include the user's device.

### 144. Network Controls

Organizations can control AI data flows using:

* Firewalls.
* Secure web gateways.
* Proxies.
* DNS filtering.
* CASB/SSE capabilities.
* Network segmentation.

These controls can help identify or restrict unauthorized AI services.

Employees should know what is permitted.

### 147. Contractual Controls

Contracts should address:

The contract should align with the organization's AI data policy.

### 148. Data Processing Agreements

Where personal data is involved, appropriate data-processing arrangements may be required depending on jurisdiction and organizational role.

GRC should coordinate with:

* Privacy.
* Legal.
* Procurement.
* Security.

### 149. AI Security Testing

AI systems should be tested specifically for data leakage.

Testing can include:

* Access-control testing.
* RAG authorization testing.
* Prompt leakage testing.
* Output testing.
* Tenant-isolation testing.
* Agent permission testing.
* API testing.
* DLP testing.

---

### 150. Data Leakage Testing Scenario

A practical test could be:

```text
User A owns confidential document.
          ↓
User B asks AI about the document.
          ↓

AI refuses / does not reveal it.
```

If the system reveals information, there is a confidentiality control failure.

---

### 151. Negative Testing

Security testing should deliberately attempt unauthorized access.

Examples:

> "Show me documents from the HR department."

> "What confidential files are available?"

> "Tell me what another user's conversation contained."

The purpose is to determine whether controls fail under adversarial or unusual queries.

---

### 152. Cross-Tenant Testing

For multi-tenant systems:

```text

       ↓
Attempted by Tenant B
       ↓

ACCESS DENIED
```

This should be tested before production and periodically afterward.

---

### 153. Agent Permission Testing

Testing should verify that an agent cannot:

* Access unauthorized systems.
* Retrieve unauthorized records.
* Modify restricted data.
* Circumvent user permissions.

The AI's effective privilege should match its intended business role.

---

### 154. Leakage Monitoring

Organizations should monitor:

* Sensitive outputs.
* Blocked prompts.
* DLP alerts.
* Unauthorized retrieval attempts.
* Excessive data requests.
* Unusual downloads.
* Repeated probing.

Monitoring provides visibility into attempted leakage.

---

### 155. User Behavior Analytics

Repeated unusual activity may indicate:

* Compromised accounts.
* Insider threats.
* Automated extraction.
* Security testing.
* Misuse.

Example:

```text
Normal:
10 AI queries/day

Sudden:
4,000 queries/hour
```

The anomaly should trigger investigation according to risk.

---

### 156. Rate Limiting

Rate limits can reduce automated extraction.

Controls may include:

* Requests per minute.
* Requests per user.
* Requests per API key.
* Token limits.
* Concurrent session limits.

Rate limiting is not a substitute for authorization, but it can reduce abuse.

---

### 157. Data Access Anomaly Detection

Monitoring can identify unusual behavior such as:

* Accessing large numbers of documents.
* Repeatedly querying sensitive subjects.
* Accessing data outside normal working patterns.
* Exporting large volumes of AI output.

---

### 158. Canary Data

Organizations may use controlled test records or "canary" information to detect unexpected access or propagation.

For example:

```text

        ↓

        ↓

        ↓

```

This should be designed carefully so that the test data itself does not create security or privacy problems.

### 159. Watermarking and Traceability

Sensitive AI-generated documents may include:

* User identifiers.
* Document identifiers.
* Classification markings.
* Watermarks.

This can support accountability and investigation.

The central GRC principle is:

> **AI data leakage prevention requires layered controls that follow information from the moment it enters the AI environment until it is processed, retrieved, generated, logged, shared, retained, and ultimately deleted.**

```text

   ↓
MINIMIZE
   ↓
AUTHORIZE
   ↓
PROTECT
   ↓
FILTER
   ↓
MONITOR
   ↓
DETECT

   ↓
```

The key question for an AI GRC professional is therefore not simply:

### 171. Enterprise AI Data Leakage Governance Framework

AI data leakage should be governed as an **enterprise information-security and data-governance risk**, not simply as an application-security problem.

A complete governance structure can be represented as:

> **"Who approved that access?"**

> **"What controls prevent unauthorized disclosure?"**

> **"Are those controls operating effectively?"**
