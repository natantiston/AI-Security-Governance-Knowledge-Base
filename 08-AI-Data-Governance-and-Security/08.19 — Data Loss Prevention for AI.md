# 08.19 — AI Data Retention and Deletion

## Part 1 — Retention Governance Fundamentals

### 1. Introduction

AI systems create, copy, transform, and retain data throughout their lifecycle.

Unlike a traditional application, an AI solution may generate multiple representations of the same information:

```text
Original Data
     ↓
Processed Data
     ↓
Embedding
     ↓
Vector Store
     ↓
Prompt / Context
     ↓
Output
     ↓
Logs
     ↓
Backup
```

This creates an important governance question:

> **When the organization no longer needs the data, can it identify and appropriately remove all relevant copies?**

AI Data Retention and Deletion Governance addresses this problem.

---

### 2. What Is Data Retention?

**Data retention** is the controlled practice of determining:

* What information should be kept.
* Why it should be kept.
* Where it should be kept.
* Who may access it.
* How long it should be retained.
* When it should be deleted.

The fundamental lifecycle is:

```text
COLLECT
   ↓
USE
   ↓
STORE
   ↓
RETAIN
   ↓
REVIEW
   ↓
DELETE
   ↓
VERIFY
```

Retention should never simply mean:

> “Keep everything forever.”

---

### 3. Why AI Makes Retention More Difficult

Traditional systems often have relatively obvious data stores.

AI systems may have many hidden or indirect stores:

```text
Application Database
Vector Database
Object Storage
Prompt Logs
Conversation History
Cache
Training Dataset
Model Artifacts
Backups
Vendor Platform
```

Therefore, deleting one record from one system may not constitute complete deletion.

---

### 4. The Data Copy Problem

Consider an employee uploading a confidential document to an AI assistant.

The document could potentially exist as:

```text
Original File
      ↓
AI Upload Storage
      ↓
Processing Copy
      ↓
Embedding
      ↓
Vector Database
      ↓
Conversation Context
      ↓
Application Log
      ↓
Backup
```

A deletion request must consider which of these copies are within scope.

---

### 5. Retention Is a Governance Decision

Technology teams should not independently decide how long sensitive AI data is retained.

Retention requirements may involve:

* Business requirements.
* Legal requirements.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Contractual requirements.
* Security requirements.
* Privacy requirements.
* Records-management requirements.

Therefore:

```text
Business
   +
Legal
   +
Privacy
   +
Security
   +
GRC
      ↓
Retention Requirement
```

---

### 6. Purpose Limitation

A fundamental governance principle is:

> **Data should be retained for a defined and legitimate purpose.**

For example:

```text
Purpose:
Security Investigation

Retention:
12 Months

After Requirement Ends:
Deletion
```

The organization should be able to explain why the information continues to exist.

---

### 7. Data Minimization

The strongest retention control can occur before retention begins.

If information is unnecessary:

```text
Don't Collect
     ↓
Don't Process
     ↓
Don't Store
     ↓
Don't Delete Later
```

This reduces:

* Privacy exposure.
* Security exposure.
* Storage cost.
* Compliance burden.
* Discovery complexity.

---

### 8. Retention Period

A retention period defines how long a particular data category should normally be retained.

For example:

| AI Data                   |      Illustrative Retention |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------: |
| Temporary processing data |                  Hours/days |
| Conversation history      |                  30–90 days |
| Application logs          |                 90–180 days |
| Security logs             |       Risk/policy dependent |
| Training datasets         | Project/lifecycle dependent |
| Model artifacts           |         Lifecycle dependent |

These values are **examples, not universal requirements**.

The actual retention period should be determined by the organization's applicable requirements.

---

### 9. Retention Schedule

A **retention schedule** translates policy into operational rules.

For each data category, it should identify:

```text
DATA
 ↓
OWNER
 ↓
PURPOSE
 ↓
RETENTION PERIOD
 ↓
STORAGE LOCATION
 ↓
DELETION METHOD
 ↓
EVIDENCE
```

This turns a high-level policy into an enforceable control.

---

### 10. Data Classification

Retention should be connected to data classification.

For example:

```text
PUBLIC
 ↓
Standard Retention

INTERNAL
 ↓
Defined Business Retention

CONFIDENTIAL
 ↓
Restricted Retention

RESTRICTED
 ↓
Strict Retention + Enhanced Controls
```

More sensitive data generally requires stronger governance.

---

### 11. Retention Owner

Each important AI data category should have an accountable owner.

For example:

```text
Customer Data
     ↓
Customer Data Owner

HR Data
     ↓
HR Data Owner

Security Logs
     ↓
Security / SOC Owner

AI Training Data
     ↓
AI / Data Owner
```

Ownership prevents the common problem of:

> **“Nobody knows who is responsible for deleting it.”**

---

### 12. Data Stewardship

The **Data Steward** may be responsible for operational governance such as:

* Classification.
* Metadata.
* Access rules.
* Retention implementation.
* Data quality.
* Deletion coordination.

The Data Owner remains accountable for the business decision.

---

### 13. AI Data Inventory

An organization should maintain an inventory of significant AI data stores.

Example:

| Data Store     | Data            | Owner          | Retention         |
| -------------- | --------------- | -------------- | ----------------- |
| AI Chat DB     | Conversations   | AI Product     | 90 days           |
| Vector DB      | Embeddings      | Data Team      | Project lifecycle |
| Object Store   | Uploaded files  | Business Owner | 30 days           |
| SIEM           | Security events | SOC            | Policy-defined    |
| Training Store | Datasets        | AI Team        | Lifecycle-defined |

This inventory becomes important during audits and deletion requests.

---

### 14. Data Mapping

Data mapping identifies where information flows.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
AI Application
 ↓
API Gateway
 ↓
RAG Service
 ↓
Vector Database
 ↓
LLM Provider
 ↓
Logging Platform
```

Each step can introduce another retention location.

---

### 15. System of Record

The organization should identify the authoritative source for important data.

For example:

```text
Customer Record
      ↓
CRM = System of Record
```

The AI may only contain a temporary representation.

This distinction helps determine which system owns the authoritative retention requirement.

---

### 16. Source Data vs Derived Data

AI systems generate **derived data**.

Examples:

* Embeddings.
* Features.
* Classifications.
* Summaries.
* Predictions.
* AI-generated metadata.

Derived data may still require retention or deletion controls.

---

### 17. Embeddings Are Data

An embedding should not automatically be considered harmless simply because it is numerical.

Conceptually:

```text
Sensitive Document
      ↓
Embedding
      ↓
Vector Representation
```

The vector may still represent information derived from sensitive source material.

Therefore, vector stores should be included in data governance.

---

### 18. Metadata Is Data

Metadata can also contain sensitive information.

For example:

```text
Document ID
Employee ID
Department
Classification
Owner
Timestamp
Tenant
```

Even if the document itself is deleted, retained metadata may still reveal information.

---

### 19. Prompt Data

Prompts should be treated as potentially sensitive data.

A user might enter:

> “Summarize this confidential acquisition proposal.”

The prompt, attached document, and resulting conversation may all become records.

Therefore, organizations should define whether prompts are:

* Stored.
* Logged.
* Retained.
* Redacted.
* Deleted.

---

### 20. Conversation History

AI assistants frequently maintain conversation history.

Governance questions include:

* Is history enabled?
* What is retained?
* For how long?
* Who can access it?
* Can users delete it?
* Can administrators access it?
* Is it used for model improvement?

---

### 21. Persistent Memory

Some AI systems maintain user or organizational memory.

This creates another retention category.

```text
User Interaction
      ↓
AI Memory
      ↓
Future Responses
```

The organization should understand:

* What is remembered.
* Why it is remembered.
* How long it remains.
* Who can access it.
* How it can be corrected or deleted.

---

### 22. Cache Retention

AI systems may temporarily cache:

* Prompts.
* Documents.
* Search results.
* Embeddings.
* API responses.

Caching should have defined expiration rules.

```text
CACHE CREATED
      ↓
TTL
      ↓
EXPIRATION
      ↓
DELETION
```

---

### 23. Temporary Data

Temporary processing data should not accidentally become permanent data.

For example:

```text
Uploaded File
      ↓
Temporary Processing
      ↓
AI Task Complete
      ↓
Automatic Expiration
```

This is a useful application of **storage limitation**.

---

### 24. Training Data Retention

Training datasets may contain significant quantities of sensitive information.

Organizations should document:

* Dataset source.
* Purpose.
* Owner.
* Classification.
* Retention.
* Approved use.
* Deletion requirements.

---

### 25. Training Dataset Versions

Training data may exist in multiple versions.

```text
Dataset v1
Dataset v2
Dataset v3
Dataset v4
```

Old versions should not automatically remain forever.

The organization should determine which versions are necessary for:

* Reproducibility.
* Audit.
* Model rollback.
* Investigation.
* Regulatory evidence.

---

### 26. Fine-Tuning Data

Fine-tuning introduces another copy of data.

```text
Source Dataset
      ↓
Fine-Tuning Dataset
      ↓
Fine-Tuned Model
```

Governance must determine the lifecycle of both the dataset and resulting artifacts.

---

### 27. Model Artifacts

Model-related artifacts may include:

* Model weights.
* Checkpoints.
* Adapters.
* Configuration.
* Evaluation results.
* Training metadata.

These should have defined retention and ownership.

---

### 28. Model Decommissioning

When an AI model is retired:

```text
RETIRE
  ↓
DISABLE
  ↓
IDENTIFY DEPENDENCIES
  ↓
ARCHIVE / DELETE
  ↓
REVOKE ACCESS
  ↓
VERIFY
```

The model should not remain accessible indefinitely simply because it is no longer actively deployed.

---

### 29. Logs

AI environments can generate extensive logs.

Examples:

* Authentication logs.
* Authorization logs.
* API logs.
* Prompt logs.
* Output logs.
* Retrieval logs.
* Agent-action logs.
* Security logs.

Each category should have an appropriate retention requirement.

---

### 30. Logging vs Privacy

More logging does not automatically mean better governance.

Consider:

```text
More Logging
     ↓
More Evidence
     +
More Sensitive Data
     ↓
More Retention Risk
```

The objective should be **appropriate logging**, not unlimited logging.

---

### 31. Security Logs

Security logs may require longer retention because they support:

* Threat detection.
* Incident response.
* Forensics.
* Compliance.
* Investigations.

However, sensitive payloads should not be unnecessarily copied into security logs.

---

### 32. Audit Evidence

GRC may need to preserve evidence showing that controls operated effectively.

Examples:

* Access reviews.
* Approval records.
* Risk assessments.
* Control tests.
* Exception approvals.
* Deletion records.

Audit evidence should have its own retention requirements.

---

### 33. Backup Copies

Backups create one of the biggest deletion challenges.

```text
Production
    ↓
Backup 1
    ↓
Backup 2
    ↓
Offsite Backup
```

Deleting data from production does not necessarily immediately remove it from every backup.

---

### 34. Backup Retention Policy

Backup governance should define:

* Backup frequency.
* Backup retention.
* Encryption.
* Access.
* Restoration.
* Expiration.
* Destruction.

Backup policies should be aligned with the organization's broader retention requirements.

---

### 35. Third-Party AI Retention

If an organization uses an external AI provider, retention may occur outside its direct infrastructure.

The organization should determine:

```text
What is stored?
      ↓
Where?
      ↓
For how long?
      ↓
Who can access it?
      ↓
Is it used for training?
      ↓
How is it deleted?
```

These questions should form part of vendor and AI risk assessment.

---

### 36. Contractual Retention

Contracts with AI providers should address, where applicable:

* Data ownership.
* Processing.
* Retention.
* Deletion.
* Backups.
* Subprocessors.
* Data return.
* Certification of deletion.

Technical security controls alone are not sufficient when a third party controls the storage environment.

---

### 37. Data Residency

Retention also has a geographic dimension.

An organization should understand:

* Where data is processed.
* Where it is stored.
* Where backups are located.
* Where support personnel may access it.

This can be particularly important for regulated or sensitive information.

---

### 38. Cross-Border Data

An AI service may process data across multiple jurisdictions.

Therefore:

```text
Enterprise Data
      ↓
AI Provider
      ↓
Country A
      ↓
Country B
      ↓
Backup Country C
```

The organization should understand the applicable legal and contractual requirements for those transfers.

---

### 39. Retention Exceptions

Not every record can simply be deleted when the standard retention period expires.

Examples of exceptions include:

* Legal hold.
* Regulatory investigation.
* Active litigation.
* Security investigation.
* Internal investigation.

Exceptions should be documented and controlled.

---

### 40. Retention Governance Lifecycle

A mature retention process is:

```text
IDENTIFY
   ↓
CLASSIFY
   ↓
DEFINE PURPOSE
   ↓
ASSIGN OWNER
   ↓
SET RETENTION
   ↓
IMPLEMENT
   ↓
MONITOR
   ↓
REVIEW
   ↓
DELETE
   ↓
VERIFY
```

---

## Part 1 Summary

The foundation of **AI Data Retention and Deletion** is understanding that AI data rarely exists in only one place.

A single piece of information can become:

```text
SOURCE
 ↓
COPY
 ↓
PROMPT
 ↓
CONTEXT
 ↓
EMBEDDING
 ↓
LOG
 ↓
BACKUP
```

Therefore, effective retention governance requires:

1. **Data inventory** — know what AI data exists.
2. **Data mapping** — know where it flows and where copies are created.
3. **Classification** — understand sensitivity.
4. **Purpose** — establish why it is retained.
5. **Ownership** — assign accountability.
6. **Retention period** — define how long it should remain.
7. **Exceptions** — control legal and investigative holds.
8. **Deletion planning** — define how data will eventually be removed.

### Interview Anchor

Remember:

> **INVENTORY → CLASSIFY → PURPOSE → OWNER → RETAIN → REVIEW → DELETE → VERIFY**

The strongest GRC statement from Part 1 is:
