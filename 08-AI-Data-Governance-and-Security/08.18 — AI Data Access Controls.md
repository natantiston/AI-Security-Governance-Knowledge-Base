# 08.18 — AI Data Access Controls

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction

AI systems increasingly depend on large volumes of organizational, customer, employee, operational, and third-party data.

As AI adoption grows, the question is no longer simply:

> **“Where is the data stored?”**

The more important governance question is:

> **“Who or what is allowed to access the data, for what purpose, under what conditions, and with what level of authority?”**

This is the purpose of **AI Data Access Controls**.

AI data access controls establish the rules, processes, and technical mechanisms that determine how humans, applications, AI models, agents, services, and automated processes can access data.

---

### 2. What Are AI Data Access Controls?

AI Data Access Controls are the administrative, technical, and procedural controls used to regulate access to data used by or exposed to AI systems.

They govern:

* Who can access data.
* What data can be accessed.
* Why the data is accessed.
* When access is permitted.
* How access occurs.
* What actions can be performed.
* What information can leave the controlled environment.

A simple model is:

```text
WHO
 ↓
WHAT DATA
 ↓
WHAT ACTION
 ↓
FOR WHAT PURPOSE
 ↓
UNDER WHAT CONDITIONS
 ↓
WITH WHAT EVIDENCE
```

---

### 3. Why AI Changes Access Control

Traditional applications generally have relatively predictable access patterns.

An AI system can be considerably more dynamic.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
AI Assistant
 ↓
Retrieval System
 ↓
Multiple Data Sources
 ↓
LLM
 ↓
Generated Response
```

The AI may retrieve information from multiple systems based on a user's request.

This creates a critical governance requirement:

> **The AI should not become a mechanism for bypassing existing data-access restrictions.**

---

### 4. The Core Principle

The fundamental principle is:

> **An AI system should not provide a user with access to information that the user would not otherwise be authorized to access.**

For example:

```text
User
   ↓
Authorized for HR Records?
   ↓
NO
   ↓
AI must not retrieve HR Records
```

Even if the AI technically has access to the HR database, that does not mean every user should receive HR information through the AI.

---

### 5. AI as a New Access Layer

AI can become an additional access layer over existing enterprise systems.

Traditional:

```text
Employee
 ↓
Application
 ↓
Database
```

AI-enabled:

```text
Employee
 ↓
AI Assistant
 ↓
Retriever / API
 ↓
Database
```

The AI layer therefore needs its own access-control design.

---

### 6. Direct vs Indirect Access

AI systems can access data in two ways.

**Direct access**

The AI application directly queries a database, API, file system, or other repository.

**Indirect access**

The AI retrieves information through another service or application.

Example:

```text
User
 ↓
AI
 ↓
Enterprise Search
 ↓
Document Repository
```

Both models require appropriate authorization controls.

---

### 7. Human Access

Traditional user access remains important.

Examples include:

* AI developers.
* Data scientists.
* Security analysts.
* GRC personnel.
* Administrators.
* Business users.
* AI operators.

Access should be based on business need and authorization.

---

### 8. Machine Access

AI systems also require machine identities.

Examples:

* Service accounts.
* API identities.
* Managed identities.
* Workload identities.
* Application credentials.

These identities should be governed just as carefully as human accounts.

---

### 9. AI Agent Access

AI agents create additional access-control challenges because they may perform actions on behalf of users.

For example:

```text
User
 ↓
AI Agent
 ↓
Email System
 ↓
CRM
 ↓
Finance System
```

The agent may have the technical capability to access multiple systems.

Governance must determine:

> **Which actions is the agent actually authorized to perform?**

---

### 10. Identity Is the Foundation

Strong AI data access control begins with reliable identity.

The organization should be able to establish:

* Who the user is.
* Which application is making the request.
* Which AI agent is acting.
* Which service is accessing the data.
* On whose behalf the request is being performed.

Without reliable identity, authorization becomes difficult to enforce.

---

### 11. Authentication vs Authorization

These concepts must be distinguished.

**Authentication**

> “Who are you?”

**Authorization**

> “What are you allowed to do?”

Example:

```text
Authentication
      ↓
User = Jonathan
      ↓
Authorization
      ↓
Can access Dataset A?
      ↓
YES / NO
```

Strong authentication does not automatically provide appropriate authorization.

---

### 12. Least Privilege

The principle of **least privilege** is fundamental to AI data access.

An identity should receive only the access necessary to perform its authorized function.

For example:

```text
AI Application
      ↓
Needs Customer Support Data
      ↓
Grant
Read Support Records

Do NOT automatically grant:
Write
Delete
Admin
Payroll
HR
```

---

### 13. Need-to-Know

Least privilege should be complemented by **need-to-know**.

A user may belong to an organization but still have no legitimate business need to access particular information.

For example:

```text
Employee
 ↓
Company Employee?
YES
 ↓
Need HR Investigation Records?
NO
 ↓
ACCESS DENIED
```

---

### 14. Role-Based Access Control

**RBAC** assigns permissions based on organizational roles.

Example:

| Role             | Customer Data | HR Data | Finance Data |
| ---------------- | ------------: | ------: | -----------: |
| Support Agent    |          Read |      No |           No |
| HR Officer       |            No |    Read |           No |
| Finance Analyst  |       Limited |      No |         Read |
| Security Analyst |   Investigate | Limited |      Limited |

AI applications should respect the underlying role model.

---

### 15. AI-Aware RBAC

AI systems may require roles specifically related to AI operations.

Examples:

* AI Administrator.
* AI Developer.
* AI Data Steward.
* AI Model Operator.
* AI Auditor.
* AI Security Analyst.

Each role should have clearly defined permissions.

---

### 16. Attribute-Based Access Control

**ABAC** evaluates attributes rather than relying solely on roles.

Attributes can include:

* User.
* Department.
* Location.
* Device.
* Data classification.
* Time.
* Business purpose.
* Application.
* Risk level.

Example:

```text
IF
User.Department = Finance
AND
Data.Classification = Finance
AND
Purpose = Financial Reporting

THEN
Allow
```

---

### 17. Context-Aware Access

AI systems can benefit from contextual authorization.

For example:

```text
User
+
Device
+
Location
+
Time
+
Data Sensitivity
+
Purpose
+
Risk
      ↓
Access Decision
```

This can provide stronger control than static permissions alone.

---

### 18. Data Classification

Access controls should be connected to data classification.

A simple classification model might be:

```text
PUBLIC
 ↓
INTERNAL
 ↓
CONFIDENTIAL
 ↓
RESTRICTED
```

The more sensitive the data, the stronger the access requirements should generally be.

---

### 19. Classification-Aware AI Access

Example:

```text
User asks AI:
"Show me the customer contract."

AI
 ↓
Identify document
 ↓
Check classification
 ↓
Check user authorization
 ↓
Allow / Deny
```

The AI should not make authorization decisions based solely on semantic relevance.

---

### 20. Semantic Relevance Is Not Authorization

This is a critical AI governance principle.

An AI system may determine:

> “This document is relevant to the user's question.”

But relevance does not mean permission.

```text
Relevant?
YES

Authorized?
UNKNOWN

Therefore:
DO NOT RELEASE
```

Authorization must be independently enforced.

---

### 21. Retrieval Authorization

In a RAG system, authorization should ideally be evaluated before restricted documents are returned to the model.

Conceptually:

```text
User Query
   ↓
Identity
   ↓
Authorization
   ↓
Permitted Documents
   ↓
Retriever
   ↓
LLM
   ↓
Response
```

This reduces the risk of unauthorized information entering the AI context.

---

### 22. Authorization Before Retrieval

A dangerous architecture can look like:

```text
User
 ↓
Retriever
 ↓
ALL Documents
 ↓
Authorization Later
```

By that point, restricted information may already have entered the AI processing path.

A stronger architecture is:

```text
User
 ↓
Identity
 ↓
Authorization Filter
 ↓
Permitted Data
 ↓
Retriever
```

---

### 23. Document-Level Security

Enterprise documents may have individual access permissions.

For example:

```text
Document A → Everyone
Document B → Finance
Document C → HR
Document D → Executive Team
```

The AI retrieval layer should respect those permissions.

---

### 24. Row-Level Security

Databases may use **row-level security**.

Example:

```text
Customer Database

Customer A → Sales Team 1
Customer B → Sales Team 2
Customer C → Sales Team 3
```

The AI should not retrieve all customer rows simply because its service account technically has database access.

---

### 25. Column-Level Security

Some data may require protection at the field level.

Example:

| Customer | Email   | Salary     | Payment Card |
| -------- | ------- | ---------- | ------------ |
| A        | Allowed | Restricted | Restricted   |

An AI application may need access to customer names and email addresses but not salary or payment-card information.

---

### 26. Field-Level Filtering

A controlled AI architecture may apply filtering before data reaches the model:

```text
Database
 ↓
Authorization
 ↓
Field Filtering
 ↓
Approved Data
 ↓
AI
```

This reduces unnecessary exposure.

---

### 27. Token-Level and Content Filtering

In some systems, sensitive content can be detected and filtered before it reaches the model.

Potential categories include:

* Personal information.
* Credentials.
* Financial information.
* Secrets.
* Confidential business information.

Filtering should complement authorization rather than replace it.

---

### 28. Privileged Access

Administrative access to AI data environments should be tightly controlled.

Privileged users may have capabilities such as:

* Changing permissions.
* Accessing datasets.
* Modifying pipelines.
* Managing service identities.
* Viewing logs.

These activities should receive enhanced monitoring.

---

### 29. Privileged Access Management

**PAM** can provide controls such as:

* Just-in-time access.
* Approval workflows.
* Session monitoring.
* Credential protection.
* Privileged activity logging.

For high-risk AI environments, privileged access should be minimized.

---

### 30. Just-in-Time Access

Instead of granting permanent access:

```text
Normal State
 ↓
No Privileged Access

Approved Request
 ↓
Temporary Access
 ↓
Work Completed
 ↓
Access Removed
```

This reduces persistent privilege.

---

### 31. Service Account Governance

AI services often require service identities.

A service account should have:

* Named owner.
* Business purpose.
* Defined permissions.
* Credential-management controls.
* Rotation requirements.
* Monitoring.
* Review date.

Avoid generic, unmanaged accounts.

---

### 32. Non-Human Identity Governance

AI introduces a growing number of non-human identities.

Examples:

```text
AI Agent
Model Service
RAG Service
Vector Database
API Gateway
Data Pipeline
Automation Bot
```

Each identity should have an appropriate authorization model.

---

### 33. API Access Controls

AI systems frequently consume data through APIs.

Controls may include:

* API authentication.
* Authorization.
* OAuth scopes.
* API keys.
* Rate limits.
* Network restrictions.
* Logging.

An API should expose only the data and functions necessary for the AI application.

---

### 34. OAuth Scopes

OAuth scopes can restrict what an AI application can do.

For example:

```text
Scope:
customer.read

NOT:
customer.write
customer.delete
admin.full
```

This implements least privilege at the API layer.

---

### 35. Database Credentials

AI applications should not normally receive unrestricted database credentials.

Instead:

```text
AI
 ↓
Controlled API / Service
 ↓
Authorized Query
 ↓
Database
```

This creates an additional enforcement point.

---

### 36. Network-Level Controls

Access control should not rely solely on application permissions.

Additional controls may include:

* Network segmentation.
* Private endpoints.
* Firewall rules.
* Security groups.
* Zero Trust policies.
* Service-to-service authentication.

A defense-in-depth approach is preferable.

---

### 37. Zero Trust

Zero Trust can be applied to AI data access.

The principle is:

> **Do not implicitly trust a user, device, application, or AI service merely because it is inside the corporate network.**

Instead:

```text
Identity
+
Device
+
Application
+
Context
+
Authorization
      ↓
Continuous Access Decision
```

---

### 38. AI Data Access and Zero Trust

For AI environments:

```text
User
 ↓
Authenticate
 ↓
Evaluate Context
 ↓
Authorize
 ↓
Retrieve Approved Data
 ↓
Monitor
```

This aligns AI access control with broader enterprise security architecture.

---

### 39. Temporary Access

Temporary access can be useful for:

* Investigations.
* Model debugging.
* Data-quality analysis.
* Incident response.
* Short-term projects.

Access should automatically expire where practical.

---

### 40. Part 1 Summary

The central principle of **AI Data Access Controls** is:

> **AI must enforce authorization—not merely retrieve information that appears relevant to a user's request.**

The core governance model is:

```text
IDENTITY
   ↓
AUTHENTICATION
   ↓
AUTHORIZATION
   ↓
LEAST PRIVILEGE
   ↓
DATA CLASSIFICATION
   ↓
CONTEXT
   ↓
CONTROLLED RETRIEVAL
   ↓
MONITORING
```

The most important distinction to remember is:

> **“The AI's ability to access data does not mean the AI's user is authorized to receive that data.”**

And the key GRC anchor for this topic is:

> **WHO → WHAT → WHY → WHEN → HOW → EVIDENCE**
