# 08.07 — Sensitive and Personal Data in AI

## Part 1

### 1. Introduction to Sensitive and Personal Data in AI

AI systems increasingly process information that can identify individuals, describe their behavior, reveal confidential circumstances, or expose sensitive organizational information.

Examples include:

* Names and contact details.
* Identification numbers.
* Financial information.
* Employment records.
* Customer information.
* Authentication information.
* Location information.
* Communications.
* Confidential business information.

The central governance principle is:

> **AI does not change the sensitivity of data. Instead, AI can increase the scale, speed, and potential impact of how sensitive data is processed.**

---

### 2. What Is Personal Data?

Personal data is information relating to an identified or identifiable individual.

Examples include:

```text
Name
Email Address
Telephone Number
Customer ID
Employee ID
IP Address
Location Information
Online Identifier
```

Whether a specific data element qualifies as personal data depends on the applicable legal and regulatory framework.

---

### 3. Direct Identifiers

A direct identifier can identify an individual by itself or with little additional information.

Examples include:

* Full name.
* Passport number.
* National identification number.
* Email address.
* Telephone number.
* Customer account number.

Example:

```text
John Morales
Passport: X1234567
Email: john@example.com
```

These values require appropriate protection when processed by AI systems.

---

### 4. Indirect Identifiers

Some information may not identify an individual by itself but may contribute to identification when combined with other information.

Examples:

* Age.
* Location.
* Job role.
* Department.
* Date of birth.
* Device information.
* Unique behavioral patterns.

```text
Age
+
Location
+
Employer
+
Job Role
        ↓
Potential Identification
```

---

### 5. Sensitive Data

Some categories of information require stronger safeguards because unauthorized disclosure or misuse could create significant harm.

Depending on the applicable framework, this can include information relating to:

* Health.
* Biometrics.
* Financial circumstances.
* Precise location.
* Authentication.
* Children's information.
* Other specially protected categories.

Organizations should define sensitive-data categories in their own data-classification framework.

---

### 6. Personal Data vs Sensitive Data

These concepts should not automatically be treated as identical.

```text
Personal Data
       │
       ├── Ordinary Personal Data
       │
       └── Sensitive / Specially Protected Data
```

For example:

```text
Name
→ Personal Data

Medical Information
→ Personal + Potentially Specially Protected Data
```

The applicable legal definition determines the precise classification.

---

### 7. Confidential Business Data

Not all sensitive information is personal data.

An AI system may process confidential corporate information such as:

* Business strategies.
* Source code.
* Intellectual property.
* Security architecture.
* Pricing information.
* Contracts.
* Internal investigations.

```text
Sensitive Data
      │
      ├── Personal
      ├── Security
      └── Business Confidential
```

AI governance should address all relevant categories.

---

### 8. Why AI Creates Additional Risk

AI can process very large quantities of information rapidly.

Traditional processing might involve:

```text
100 Records
   ↓
Manual Review
```

AI may process:

```text
1,000,000 Records
       ↓
Automated Analysis
```

The scale of processing can therefore amplify existing data risks.

---

### 9. AI Data Aggregation

AI systems can combine information from multiple sources.

For example:

```text
CRM
+
HR
+
Customer Support
+
Location
+
Transaction Data
       ↓
AI Context
```

The combined dataset may reveal information that was not obvious from each individual source.

---

### 10. Profiling

AI can analyze personal information to create profiles.

Examples include:

* Customer preferences.
* Employee behavior.
* Fraud risk.
* Purchasing patterns.
* Service usage.
* User interests.

Profiling may create additional governance obligations depending on the use case and applicable law.

---

### 11. Automated Decision-Making

AI may be used to support or make decisions affecting individuals.

Examples:

* Credit decisions.
* Recruitment.
* Fraud detection.
* Insurance assessment.
* Customer eligibility.
* Access decisions.

Higher-impact decisions require stronger governance.

---

### 12. Human Oversight

Where AI contributes to significant decisions about individuals, organizations should determine whether meaningful human oversight is required.

A simplified model is:

```text
Personal Data
      ↓
AI Analysis
      ↓
Recommendation
      ↓
Human Review
      ↓
Decision
```

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely a formal approval step.

---

### 13. Purpose Limitation

Personal data should be processed for defined and legitimate purposes.

Example:

```text
Purpose:
Customer Support

Required:
Customer support history

Not Automatically Required:
Entire customer profile
```

The fact that information is technically accessible does not mean it should be submitted to an AI system.

---

### 14. Data Minimization

AI systems should receive only the information necessary for the intended task.

Example:

```text
Task:
Summarize customer complaint

Poor:
Entire customer database

Better:
Specific complaint + necessary context
```

Data minimization reduces exposure and limits potential impact.

---

### 15. Necessity Assessment

Before processing personal data through AI, ask:

> **Is this data actually necessary for the AI task?**

A useful decision model is:

```text
Data Element
     ↓
Required for Purpose?
   ↙          ↘
 Yes           No
 ↓              ↓
Process       Remove
```

---

### 16. Data Classification Before AI Processing

Personal and sensitive data should be identified before entering an AI workflow.

```text
Data
 ↓
Classification
 ↓
Personal?
 ↓
Sensitive?
 ↓
AI Policy
 ↓
Allowed / Restricted / Blocked
```

Classification allows technical controls to make policy decisions.

---

### 17. Sensitive Data Discovery

Organizations can use automated tools to identify sensitive information.

Potential technologies include:

* DLP.
* Data discovery.
* Data classification.
* Sensitive-data scanners.
* Database discovery.
* Content inspection.

---

### 18. Personally Identifiable Information Detection

AI gateways can inspect prompts and documents for potential personal information.

Example:

```text
Prompt
  ↓
PII Detection
  ↓
Email / Phone / ID Found
  ↓
Policy Evaluation
```

The system can then:

* Allow.
* Warn.
* Mask.
* Redact.
* Block.
* Escalate.

---

### 19. Data Redaction

Redaction removes unnecessary sensitive information before AI processing.

Example:

```text
Original:
Customer Maria Santos, account 938271,
requested a refund.

AI Input:
Customer [REDACTED], account [REDACTED],
requested a refund.
```

Redaction can reduce unnecessary exposure while preserving task context.

---

### 20. Data Masking

Masking replaces sensitive information with a controlled representation.

Example:

```text
Original:
maria.santos@example.com

Masked:
customer001@example.test
```

Masking is useful when the AI needs the structure or role of the information but does not need the actual identifier.

---

### 21. Pseudonymization

Pseudonymization replaces identifying information with pseudonyms while maintaining the ability to associate records through separately protected information.

```text
Real Identity
      ↓
Pseudonymous Identifier
      ↓
AI Processing
```

The mapping information must itself be appropriately protected.

---

### 22. Anonymization

Anonymization attempts to prevent individuals from being identified from the resulting information.

However:

> **Removing obvious identifiers does not automatically make a dataset anonymous.**

Re-identification risks should be considered when multiple attributes remain.

---

### 23. Re-Identification Risk

AI systems can sometimes combine seemingly harmless information.

Example:

```text
Age
+
Location
+
Employer
+
Rare Job Role
       ↓
Potential Re-Identification
```

Therefore, organizations should evaluate the combined information rather than relying only on removal of names.

---

### 24. Data Subject Rights

Where applicable, organizations processing personal data through AI should consider relevant data-subject rights.

Depending on the applicable framework, these may include:

* Right of access.
* Right to rectification.
* Right to erasure.
* Right to restriction.
* Right to object.
* Other applicable rights.

AI architecture should be capable of supporting relevant obligations.

---

### 25. Data Subject Access

If an individual requests access to their personal information, the organization should understand where AI-related copies may exist.

Potential locations include:

```text
Source System
     ↓
AI Application
     ↓
Inference Logs
     ↓
Temporary Storage
     ↓
Provider
     ↓
Backups
```

Data mapping is therefore essential.

---

### 26. Data Correction

If inaccurate personal information enters an AI system, the organization should understand how corrections propagate.

Example:

```text
Source Record Corrected
        ↓
AI Data Store Updated
        ↓
Cached Data Updated
        ↓
Future Inference Uses Correct Data
```

Historical AI outputs may require separate consideration depending on the use case.

---

### 27. Data Deletion

Deletion requirements can become more complicated when personal information exists in multiple AI-related locations.

Potential locations include:

* Original datasets.
* Prompt histories.
* Application databases.
* Vector databases.
* Caches.
* Logs.
* Backups.
* Provider systems.

A deletion process should identify the relevant copies.

---

### 28. Personal Data in Prompts

Users may unintentionally place personal data directly into prompts.

Example:

```text
"Review this employee's performance:
Name: John Smith
Employee ID: 12345
Salary: €72,000
Performance history: ..."
```

The organization should determine whether this processing is authorized and necessary.

---

### 29. Personal Data in Documents

AI applications may process entire documents containing personal information.

Examples include:

* Resumes.
* Contracts.
* Customer complaints.
* Employee records.
* Invoices.
* Support tickets.

Before processing, the application should determine whether the complete document is actually necessary.

---

### 30. Core GRC Principle

Sensitive and personal data in AI requires governance across the entire processing lifecycle:

```text
              PERSONAL / SENSITIVE DATA
                         │
                         ▼
                    IDENTIFICATION
                         │
                         ▼
                    CLASSIFICATION
                         │
                         ▼
                      PURPOSE
                         │
                         ▼
                     NECESSITY
                         │
                         ▼
                    MINIMIZATION
                         │
                         ▼
              REDACTION / MASKING
                         │
                         ▼
                    AUTHORIZATION
                         │
                         ▼
                    AI PROCESSING
                         │
                         ▼
                 MONITORING / LOGGING
                         │
                         ▼
                  RETENTION / DELETION
                         │
                         ▼
                      ASSURANCE
```

The fundamental principle is:

> **Personal and sensitive data should enter an AI system only when the processing is justified, authorized, necessary, appropriately protected, and governed throughout its lifecycle.**

AI governance must therefore extend beyond simply asking **"Can the model process this data?"**

The more important GRC questions are:

**Should the organization process this data?**

**Does the AI system need this data?**

**Is the user authorized to provide it?**

**Is the AI provider authorized to process it?**

**Can the data be minimized or de-identified?**

**Can the organization control retention and deletion?**

**Can the organization demonstrate compliance and accountability?**

# 08.07 — Sensitive and Personal Data in AI

## Part 2

### 31. Special Categories of Personal Data

Certain types of personal information may receive enhanced protection under applicable privacy laws and regulations.

Depending on the jurisdiction, examples can include information relating to:

* Health.
* Biometric characteristics used for identification.
* Genetic information.
* Political opinions.
* Religious or philosophical beliefs.
* Trade-union membership.
* Sex life or sexual orientation.
* Racial or ethnic origin.

The exact legal definition and permitted processing conditions depend on the applicable jurisdiction.

---

### 32. Higher-Risk Personal Data

Organizations should identify personal-data categories that create elevated risk if processed by AI.

A practical classification could be:

```text id="p7m4v8"
Personal Data
     │
     ├── Standard
     │
     ├── Confidential
     │
     ├── Sensitive
     │
     └── Highly Restricted
```

The classification should align with the organization's legal, privacy, security, and risk requirements.

---

### 33. Children's Data

Children's information may require additional safeguards.

AI systems may process:

* Student records.
* Educational information.
* Communications.
* Images.
* Behavioral information.
* Account information.

Organizations should apply heightened governance where applicable.

---

### 34. Employee Personal Data

AI systems may process employee information such as:

* Employee records.
* Performance information.
* Attendance.
* Compensation.
* Recruitment information.
* Communications.

Employee data should not automatically be made available to AI systems merely because the organization possesses it.

---

### 35. Customer Personal Data

Customer-facing AI systems may process:

* Names.
* Contact details.
* Account information.
* Support history.
* Transaction information.
* Customer communications.

Controls should ensure that AI processing follows the organization's approved customer-data governance requirements.

---

### 36. Financial Data

Financial information can be highly sensitive.

Examples include:

* Bank account information.
* Payment information.
* Transaction records.
* Credit information.
* Financial statements.

AI applications should receive only the financial information necessary for the approved purpose.

---

### 37. Health Information

Health-related information generally requires particularly strong governance.

Examples:

* Medical records.
* Diagnoses.
* Treatment information.
* Medication information.
* Health-insurance information.

AI processing should be subject to applicable legal, contractual, privacy, and security requirements.

---

### 38. Biometric Data

Biometric information can include characteristics used to identify individuals.

Examples:

* Facial recognition templates.
* Fingerprint templates.
* Voice characteristics.
* Iris information.

Biometric data can create significant risks because unlike a password, a person's biometric characteristic cannot simply be replaced.

---

### 39. Authentication Data

Authentication information should be treated as highly sensitive.

Examples:

* Passwords.
* API keys.
* Access tokens.
* Private keys.
* Recovery codes.

These should generally never be submitted to AI systems unless there is a specifically authorized and controlled security use case.

---

### 40. Credentials in AI Prompts

A common operational error is copying configuration information into an AI prompt.

Example:

```text id="k7m3v9"
API_KEY=sk-xxxxxxxx
PASSWORD=xxxxxxxx
DATABASE_PASSWORD=xxxxxxxx
```

This can expose credentials to the AI processing environment.

Organizations should implement controls to detect and prevent such submissions.

---

### 41. Security Information

Security-related data may also require restricted handling.

Examples:

* Network diagrams.
* Firewall configurations.
* Vulnerability reports.
* Incident reports.
* Security credentials.
* Detection rules.

AI security assistants may legitimately need this information, but access should be governed.

---

### 42. Intellectual Property

AI systems may process proprietary information such as:

* Source code.
* Product designs.
* Algorithms.
* Technical documentation.
* Research.
* Engineering specifications.

The organization should determine whether the AI service is authorized to process such information.

---

### 43. Confidential Contracts

Contracts may contain:

* Customer information.
* Pricing.
* Legal obligations.
* Intellectual property.
* Confidentiality clauses.

Before uploading contracts to an AI system, the organization should evaluate both the data and the contractual restrictions.

---

### 44. Data Classification and AI Policy

Enterprise data classification should directly influence AI-processing permissions.

Example:

```text id="q8m4v7"
Data Classification
       ↓
AI Processing Rule
       ↓
Provider / Model Selection
       ↓
Security Controls
       ↓
Retention
```

Classification becomes the bridge between data governance and AI governance.

---

### 45. Classification Labels

Documents may contain labels such as:

```text id="m4q8v3"
PUBLIC
INTERNAL
CONFIDENTIAL
RESTRICTED
```

AI applications should ideally be able to interpret these classifications before processing the content.

---

### 46. Classification Inheritance

AI-generated information may inherit classification from the information used to generate it.

Example:

```text id="x7m3q8"
CONFIDENTIAL Documents
        ↓
AI Analysis
        ↓
CONFIDENTIAL Report
```

However, classification should be determined according to the organization's information-classification policy rather than assumed automatically in every situation.

---

### 47. Data Aggregation and Classification

When multiple data sources are combined, the resulting information should be assessed.

```text id="r8m4q7"
Internal Data
+
Confidential Data
+
Personal Data
       ↓
Combined AI Context
       ↓
Higher Sensitivity
```

This is particularly important in RAG and agentic AI systems.

---

### 48. Data Access vs AI Access

Having access to a database does not necessarily mean having permission to send that database to an AI model.

```text id="p5m8v3"
Database Access
       ≠
AI Processing Authorization
```

AI introduces an additional processing context that may require separate controls.

---

### 49. Purpose-Based AI Access

Access should be linked to the intended AI use case.

Example:

```text id="q4m8v7"
Customer Support Agent
        ↓
Support Records
        ↓
AI Summarization
        ↓
Allowed

Same User
        ↓
HR Records
        ↓
AI Analysis
        ↓
Not Automatically Allowed
```

---

### 50. Need-to-Know Principle

Sensitive information should be available only to individuals and systems that require it for a legitimate purpose.

AI should not weaken this principle.

---

### 51. Least Privilege

AI applications should receive the minimum privileges necessary.

```text id="v8m4q3"
AI Application
      ↓
Minimum Required Access
      ↓
Specific Dataset
      ↓
Specific Operation
```

Avoid giving an AI agent unrestricted access to enterprise databases.

---

### 52. Attribute-Based Access Control

ABAC can make AI data access more context-aware.

A decision may consider:

* User.
* Role.
* Department.
* Data classification.
* Location.
* Device.
* Purpose.
* Time.

Example:

```text id="m7q4v8"
IF
User = SupportAgent
AND
Purpose = CustomerSupport
AND
Data = CustomerSupportData

THEN
ALLOW
```

---

### 53. Role-Based Access Control

RBAC can provide simpler AI access management.

Example:

| Role             | AI Data Access        |
| ---------------- | --------------------- |
| Customer Support | Customer support data |
| HR               | Employee data         |
| Security Analyst | Security data         |
| Finance          | Financial data        |

Roles should still follow least privilege.

---

### 54. Segregation of Duties

Sensitive AI operations may require separation between:

* Data owner.
* AI application owner.
* Security reviewer.
* Privacy reviewer.
* Business approver.

This reduces the risk of one individual controlling the entire processing lifecycle.

---

### 55. Data Owner Responsibility

The data owner should understand:

* What data is being processed.
* Why it is being processed.
* Who can access it.
* Which AI systems may process it.
* Retention requirements.

The data owner does not necessarily operate the AI platform.

---

### 56. AI Application Owner Responsibility

The AI application owner should ensure that:

* The application has an approved purpose.
* Data access is controlled.
* Security controls operate.
* AI providers are approved.
* Logs and monitoring exist.
* Risks are managed.

---

### 57. Privacy Responsibility

The privacy function may evaluate:

* Lawful processing.
* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Data subject rights.
* Transparency.
* Cross-border processing.
* Privacy risks.

---

### 58. Security Responsibility

Security teams may focus on:

* Access control.
* Encryption.
* DLP.
* Monitoring.
* Threat detection.
* Incident response.
* Data exfiltration.

---

### 59. GRC Responsibility

GRC can connect the different functions.

Organizations should establish whether an AI provider may:

### 65. Data Processing Agreements

Where applicable, contractual arrangements should clearly establish the provider's role and responsibilities regarding personal data processing.

Relevant provisions may include:

* Processing instructions.
* Confidentiality.
* Security.
* Subprocessors.

### 66. Data Residency Requirements

Some organizations may have restrictions concerning where personal or sensitive information can be processed.

Governance should therefore document:

```text id="p7m4q8"
     ↓
```

---

### 67. Cross-Border Data Transfers

If AI processing involves international transfers, the organization should determine what legal and contractual mechanisms apply.

This should be assessed before production use rather than after deployment.

---

### 68. Encryption of Personal Data

Sensitive information should be protected through appropriate encryption controls.

Consider:

* Encryption in transit.
* Encryption at rest.

---

### 69. Tokenization for AI

Tokenization can keep sensitive values outside the AI environment.

```text id="q8m3v7"
Actual Customer ID
       ↓
Token Service
       ↓

       ↓
AI
```

The AI can process the token without necessarily seeing the underlying identifier.

---

### 70. Format-Preserving Protection

In some applications, sensitive values can be replaced while maintaining useful structure.

Example:

```text id="m4v8q3"
Actual:
4111-5555-1234-9876

Protected:

```

The exact technique should be selected according to the use case and security requirements.

---

### 71. Privacy by Design

Privacy should be incorporated into AI architecture from the beginning.

Instead of:

```text id="r7m3q8"

Add Privacy
```

Use:

```text id="v8q4m3"

   ↓
Privacy Requirements
   ↓
Security Requirements
   ↓

   ↓

```

---

### 72. Privacy by Default

Default configurations should minimize unnecessary personal-data processing.

Examples:

* Minimum logging.
* Restricted access.
* Shorter retention.
* DLP enabled.
* Sensitive-data detection enabled.

---

### 73. Default Data Retention

AI systems should not retain personal data indefinitely simply because storage is technically available.

Retention should have a defined business, legal, or operational purpose.

---

### 74. Privacy Risk in AI Logs

AI observability can create secondary copies of personal information.

Example:

```text id="q5m8v3"
Original Prompt
      ↓
AI Application
      ↓
Monitoring Log
      ↓
SIEM
      ↓
Backup
```

Each copy creates additional governance considerations.

---

### 75. Log Minimization

Where possible, logs should capture metadata instead of full sensitive content.

For example:

```text id="m8q4v7"

Timestamp
Request ID
Policy Result
Data Classification
```

rather than storing an entire confidential prompt.

---

### 76. Human Review Risk

Some AI services may involve human review or support processes.

Organizations should understand:

* Whether human reviewers can access data.
* Under what circumstances.
* Where reviewers are located.
* What confidentiality controls exist.

---

### 77. Human-in-the-Loop Governance

If humans review AI inputs or outputs, governance should define:

```text id="v7m3q8"
Who can review?
       ↓
What data can they see?
       ↓
Why?
       ↓
How long?
       ↓
What is recorded?
```

Access should follow least privilege.

---

### 78. AI Training from Customer Data

Organizations should distinguish between:

**Using customer information to provide an AI service**

and

**Using customer information to train or improve another model.**

These may involve different governance considerations.

---

### 79. Secondary Purpose Controls

Before using personal data for a new AI purpose, organizations should reassess:

* Purpose.
* Necessity.
* Authorization.
* Privacy impact.
* Security.
* Legal requirements.

---

### 80. Core Governance Principle

Sensitive and personal data requires governance at multiple levels:

```text id="q7m4v8"
DATA
 ↓
IDENTIFY
 ↓
CLASSIFY
 ↓
JUSTIFY
 ↓
MINIMIZE
 ↓
AUTHORIZE
 ↓
PROTECT
 ↓
PROCESS
 ↓
MONITOR
 ↓
RETAIN
 ↓
DELETE
 ↓
ASSURE
```

The key principle is:

> **AI systems should not receive personal or sensitive information simply because they can technically access it. Access and processing must be justified, authorized, minimized, protected, and auditable.**

A mature GRC program therefore treats sensitive data as a **controlled enterprise asset throughout the AI lifecycle**, rather than merely as input provided to a model.

# 08.07 — Sensitive and Personal Data in AI

## Part 3

### 81. Privacy Risk in AI Data Processing

AI can introduce privacy risks beyond traditional data-processing systems because it can combine, infer, summarize, classify, and generate information at significant scale.

A simplified risk chain is:

```text
Personal Data
      ↓
AI Processing
      ↓
Aggregation / Inference
      ↓
New Information
      ↓
Potential Privacy Impact
```

The governance question is therefore not only **what personal data enters the AI system**, but also **what the AI system can derive from it**.

---

### 82. Inferred Personal Information

AI may infer characteristics that were not explicitly provided.

For example:

```text
Purchase History
+
Browsing Behavior
+
Location
       ↓
AI Analysis
       ↓
Potential Customer Preference
```

The resulting inference may itself become information requiring governance.

---

### 83. Inference Risk

Organizations should consider whether AI-generated conclusions could reveal sensitive information about individuals.

Examples include:

* Health-related conclusions.
* Financial-risk assessments.
* Behavioral profiles.
* Employment assessments.
* Customer preferences.

The sensitivity of an AI output may therefore exceed the sensitivity of an individual input.

---

### 84. Profiling Risk

AI can process large datasets to create detailed profiles.

```text id="p7m4v8"
Multiple Data Sources
        ↓
AI Analytics
        ↓
Individual Profile
        ↓
Prediction / Recommendation
```

Organizations should establish appropriate governance around profiling activities.

---

### 85. Behavioral Analytics

AI may analyze:

* User activity.
* Purchase patterns.
* Website behavior.
* Communications.
* System usage.
* Employee activity.

The organization should define the legitimate business purpose and appropriate controls.

---

### 86. Employee Monitoring

AI-based employee monitoring can create elevated privacy and governance risks.

Examples:

* Productivity analysis.
* Communication analysis.
* Attendance prediction.
* Behavioral scoring.

Such systems should undergo appropriate legal, privacy, ethical, and employee-relations review.

---

### 87. Recruitment AI

AI may process applicant information such as:

* Resumes.
* Employment history.
* Skills.

### 130. Core Privacy GRC Principle

Sensitive and personal data governance must extend beyond the initial AI input.


A mature organization therefore treats **inputs, context, embeddings, prompts, outputs, logs, metadata, and AI-derived profiles** as potential governance objects rather than focusing exclusively on the original dataset.

### 131. Sensitive Data Governance Control Objectives

An enterprise AI data-governance program should establish explicit objectives for protecting personal and sensitive information.

### 182. Final GRC Perspective

Sensitive and personal data is one of the most important governance considerations in enterprise AI.

The fundamental mistake is to treat AI as simply another application that consumes data.

AI can:

* Aggregate information.
* Infer new information.
* Create profiles.
* Generate decisions.
* Replicate data.
* Store context.
* Retrieve information.
* Produce sensitive outputs.

Therefore, governance must extend across the complete AI data-processing lifecycle.

```text
                 PERSONAL DATA
                      │
                      ▼
                  IDENTIFY
                      │
                      ▼
                 CLASSIFY
                      │
                      ▼
                  JUSTIFY
                      │
                      ▼
                 MINIMIZE
                      │
                      ▼
                AUTHORIZE
                      │
                      ▼
                  PROTECT
                      │
                      ▼
               AI PROCESSING
                      │
                      ▼
                 MONITOR
                      │
                      ▼
             DERIVED INFORMATION
                      │
                      ▼
              OUTPUT PROTECTION
                      │
                      ▼
             RETENTION / DELETION
                      │
                      ▼
                  ASSURANCE
```

The core GRC principle is:

> **Sensitive and personal data should be processed by AI only when the processing is justified, authorized, necessary, proportionate, appropriately protected, and demonstrably governed.**

A mature AI governance program should therefore be capable of answering:

**What personal or sensitive data is being processed?**

**Why is the AI system processing it?**

**Is the processing authorized?**

**Can less data be used?**

**Who can access the data and the resulting AI outputs?**

**Where is the data processed and retained?**

**Can the organization detect unauthorized disclosure?**

**Can the organization satisfy applicable privacy obligations?**

**Can the organization prove that its controls are operating effectively?**

This transforms privacy from a policy statement into an **operational AI security and GRC control system**.
