# 04.04 — AI Policy and Management-System Requirements

## 1. Introduction to AI Policy and Management-System Requirements

An AI Management System (AIMS) requires a structured set of policies, principles, requirements, and management practices that establish how the organization governs the development, provision, deployment, use, monitoring, and continual improvement of AI systems.

AI policy provides the organizational direction for AI governance. Management-system requirements translate that direction into structured expectations that can be implemented, monitored, evaluated, and improved.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
Organizational Strategy
        ↓
AI Governance Direction
        ↓
AI Policy
        ↓
Management-System Requirements
        ↓
Processes and Controls
        ↓
Operational Implementation
        ↓
Monitoring and Evaluation
        ↓
Continual Improvement
```

A policy should therefore not exist as an isolated document. It should provide the foundation for the broader management system.

---

## 2. Purpose of an AI Policy

An AI policy establishes the organization's high-level position regarding the responsible management and use of AI.

It may communicate the organization's commitment to:

* Responsible AI.
* Appropriate AI governance.
* Risk-based decision-making.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Transparency.
* Accountability.
* Regulatory compliance.
* Human oversight.
* Continual improvement.

The policy provides a common direction for business units, technical teams, governance functions, and other stakeholders.

---

## 3. AI Policy as a Management Direction

An effective policy communicates management's expectations.

It should answer fundamental questions such as:

* Why does the organization govern AI?
* What AI activities are within scope?
* What principles apply?
* Who is accountable?
* What expectations apply to employees and third parties?
* How are significant AI risks managed?
* How are AI governance requirements maintained?

A policy should provide direction without attempting to document every operational procedure.

---

## 4. AI Policy and Organizational Context

The AI policy should reflect the organization's context.

Relevant factors may include:

* Organizational size.
* Industry.
* Geographic presence.
* Regulatory environment.
* AI use cases.
* Business objectives.
* Risk profile.
* Technology environment.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* AI maturity.

For example, an organization developing safety-critical AI systems may require substantially different governance emphasis from an organization using AI primarily for internal productivity.

---

## 5. AI Policy and Organizational Strategy

AI policy should be aligned with organizational strategy.

```text
Business Strategy
      ↓
Digital Strategy
      ↓
AI Strategy
      ↓
AI Governance
      ↓
AI Policy
      ↓
AIMS Implementation
```

This alignment helps ensure that governance supports business objectives rather than becoming disconnected from organizational priorities.

---

## 6. AI Policy and Risk Management

AI policy should establish a risk-based approach.

This means the organization should not necessarily apply identical governance requirements to every AI system.

Risk considerations may include:

* Business criticality.
* Impact on individuals.
* Data sensitivity.
* Security exposure.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Degree of automation.
* Decision-making authority.
* External stakeholder impact.
* Potential harm.

Higher-risk AI systems may require stronger governance and oversight.

---

## 7. AI Policy and Scope

The policy should establish an appropriate scope.

Scope may cover:

* AI systems developed internally.
* AI systems acquired from suppliers.
* AI-enabled SaaS platforms.
* Generative AI tools.
* Machine-learning models.
* AI embedded within products.
* AI used for internal processes.
* AI services provided to customers.

The scope should be sufficiently clear to prevent uncertainty about which activities fall under the AIMS.

---

## 8. Organizational AI Definition

The organization should establish how it defines AI for governance purposes.

A definition may encompass technologies and systems that use techniques such as:

* Machine learning.
* Deep learning.
* Natural language processing.
* Generative AI.
* Computer vision.
* Predictive analytics.
* Automated reasoning.
* Other computational techniques producing AI-related outputs.

The definition should be practical enough to support consistent identification and governance.

---

## 9. AI Policy Applicability

The policy should identify who must comply with it.

Potentially applicable parties include:

* Employees.
* Contractors.
* Consultants.
* Developers.
* Data scientists.
* Business users.
* System owners.
* Managers.
* Third-party providers.

Where appropriate, supplier contracts may establish additional AI governance requirements.

---

## 10. AI Policy Hierarchy

An organization should establish a logical hierarchy of governance documentation.

A common structure is:

```text
AI Governance Charter
        ↓
AI Policy
        ↓
Standards
        ↓
Procedures
        ↓
Guidelines
        ↓
Work Instructions
        ↓
Operational Evidence
```

Each layer should have a distinct purpose.

---

## 11. AI Governance Charter

The governance charter establishes the organizational authority and governance structure.

It may define:

* Governance bodies.
* Mandates.
* Membership.
* Decision rights.
* Escalation.
* Reporting.
* Accountability.

The charter establishes the governance environment within which the AI policy operates.

---

## 12. AI Policy

The policy establishes high-level mandatory organizational requirements.

It may state that:

* AI systems must be appropriately governed.
* Significant AI risks must be assessed.
* AI systems must have accountable owners.
* Applicable requirements must be identified.
* Appropriate controls must be implemented.
* AI performance must be monitored.
* Significant incidents must be escalated.

The policy should establish expectations without becoming an operational procedure manual.

---

## 13. AI Standards

Standards translate policy requirements into more specific technical or organizational expectations.

Examples include:

* AI security standard.
* AI data governance standard.
* AI model management standard.
* AI supplier standard.
* AI testing standard.
* AI logging standard.

Standards provide greater specificity than policy.

---

## 14. AI Procedures

Procedures describe how requirements are executed.

Examples include procedures for:

* AI risk assessment.
* AI impact assessment.
* AI system approval.
* AI incident management.
* AI model change management.
* AI supplier assessment.
* AI retirement.

Procedures should provide sufficiently clear steps for consistent execution.

---

## 15. AI Guidelines

Guidelines provide recommended practices where strict mandatory requirements may not be appropriate.

Examples include:

* Recommended prompt-security practices.
* Recommended documentation practices.
* Recommended human-review practices.
* Recommended model-testing practices.

Guidelines should be clearly distinguished from mandatory requirements.

---

## 16. AI Policy and Mandatory Language

Policy language should distinguish between mandatory and recommended requirements.

Common mandatory terms include:

* Must.
* Shall.
* Required.
* Prohibited.
* Mandatory.

Recommended language may include:

* Should.
* Recommended.
* May.
* Where appropriate.

Ambiguous language can create inconsistent interpretation.

---

## 17. AI Policy and Prohibited Activities

The policy may identify activities that are prohibited or restricted.

Examples could include:

* Using unauthorized AI services for confidential information.
* Circumventing required AI assessments.
* Deploying unapproved high-risk AI systems.
* Using AI systems outside approved purposes.
* Bypassing security controls.
* Misrepresenting AI-generated information.

Prohibited activities should be clearly defined and appropriately governed.

---

## 18. AI Policy Exceptions

Organizations may require a formal exception mechanism.

An exception process may define:

* Who can request an exception.
* Required justification.
* Risk assessment.
* Approval authority.
* Compensating controls.
* Expiration date.
* Review requirements.

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Exception Request
     ↓
Risk Assessment
     ↓
Approval
     ↓
Conditions
     ↓
Monitoring
     ↓
Review / Expiration
```

Exceptions should not become an informal mechanism for avoiding governance requirements.

---

## 19. AI Policy Ownership

Every major policy should have an accountable owner.

The policy owner may be responsible for:

* Maintaining the policy.
* Coordinating reviews.
* Monitoring regulatory changes.
* Managing stakeholder input.
* Addressing interpretation questions.
* Initiating revisions.

Policy ownership should be documented.

---

## 20. AI Policy Approval

Policies should be approved by an authority appropriate to their significance.

Approval may involve:

* AI Governance Committee.
* AI Steering Committee.
* Executive management.
* Security leadership.
* Risk leadership.
* Board-level authority for strategically significant policies.

Approval authority should be defined within the governance model.

---

## 21. AI Policy Review

AI policies should be reviewed periodically and when significant changes occur.

Triggers may include:

* New regulations.
* Major AI incidents.
* Significant changes in AI technology.
* Changes in organizational strategy.
* New AI use cases.
* Audit findings.
* Changes in stakeholder expectations.

Periodic review helps keep policy requirements relevant.

---

## 22. AI Policy Version Control

Policies should have appropriate document-control mechanisms.

Important metadata may include:

| Field          | Example                |
| -------------- | ---------------------- |
| Policy Name    | AI Governance Policy   |
| Owner          | AI Governance Function |
| Version        | 2.0                    |
| Approval Date  | Defined date           |
| Effective Date | Defined date           |
| Review Date    | Defined date           |
| Approver       | Authorized authority   |
| Status         | Approved               |

Version control helps establish which requirements were applicable at a particular point in time.

---

## 23. AI Policy Communication

Approved policies should be communicated to relevant stakeholders.

Communication methods may include:

* Employee portals.
* Training.
* Awareness campaigns.
* Governance briefings.
* Management communications.
* Supplier communications.

Publishing a policy without ensuring appropriate awareness may limit its effectiveness.

---

## 24. AI Policy Awareness

Personnel should understand the AI governance requirements relevant to their responsibilities.

Awareness may include:

* What AI activities are governed.
* What employees are allowed to do.
* What activities require approval.
* What information must not be entered into AI systems.
* How to report AI concerns.
* How to escalate incidents.

Awareness requirements should be proportionate to role and risk.

---

## 25. AI Policy and Competence

Policy compliance depends partly on organizational competence.

For example:

```text
Policy
  ↓
Understanding
  ↓
Competence
  ↓
Correct Application
  ↓
Evidence
```

An employee may have access to a policy but still require training to apply its requirements correctly.

---

## 26. AI Policy and Employee Responsibilities

The policy should establish relevant employee responsibilities.

Employees may be required to:

* Use only approved AI tools.
* Follow AI security requirements.
* Protect confidential information.
* Complete required training.
* Report suspected misuse.
* Follow approved AI processes.
* Escalate significant concerns.

Responsibilities should be clear and realistic.

---

## 27. AI Policy and Management Responsibilities

Managers may have additional responsibilities, including:

* Ensuring appropriate AI use within their teams.
* Supporting required assessments.
* Ensuring employees receive training.
* Escalating AI risks.
* Monitoring compliance.
* Supporting corrective actions.

Management accountability helps translate enterprise policy into operational behavior.

---

## 28. AI Policy and AI System Owners

AI system owners should understand their governance obligations.

These may include:

* Maintaining system documentation.
* Ensuring appropriate risk assessment.
* Monitoring performance.
* Managing changes.
* Coordinating security controls.
* Addressing incidents.
* Supporting audits.
* Managing retirement.

Ownership should remain throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

## 29. AI Policy and Data Responsibilities

AI policy should establish expectations for data use.

Relevant requirements may address:

* Data classification.
* Data quality.
* Data provenance.
* Data access.
* Data protection.
* Data retention.
* Data usage rights.
* Personal information.

Data governance should be coordinated with AI governance.

---

## 30. AI Policy and Information Security

AI policy should establish appropriate security expectations.

These may include:

* Access control.
* Secure development.
* Security testing.
* Logging.
* Monitoring.
* Vulnerability management.
* Incident response.
* Supplier security.

AI security requirements should be integrated with the organization's broader information security framework.

---

## 31. AI Policy and Privacy

Where AI systems process personal information, the policy should establish expectations for privacy and data protection.

Relevant areas may include:

* Lawful processing.
* Data minimization.
* Purpose limitation.
* Transparency.
* Data subject rights.
* Retention.
* Privacy risk assessment.

The specific requirements depend on applicable laws and organizational context.

---

## 32. AI Policy and Human Oversight

Where appropriate, AI policy should establish expectations for human oversight.

Human oversight may be required when:

* AI makes significant decisions.
* AI outputs can materially affect individuals.
* Errors could create serious consequences.
* Regulatory requirements require human involvement.

The degree of human oversight should be proportionate to risk.

---

## 33. AI Policy and Transparency

AI policy may establish requirements concerning transparency.

Depending on the use case, transparency may address:

* Disclosure that AI is being used.
* Explanation of relevant AI decisions.
* Communication of limitations.
* Identification of AI-generated content.
* Documentation of system behavior.

Transparency requirements should reflect the nature and impact of the AI system.

---

## 34. AI Policy and Accountability

The policy should reinforce the principle that AI systems do not eliminate human or organizational accountability.

```text
AI System
    ↓
AI Output
    ↓
Human / Organizational Decision
    ↓
Accountability
```

The existence of automation does not automatically transfer accountability to the technology.

---

## 35. AI Policy and AI Risk Acceptance

The policy should establish that residual AI risk requires appropriate acceptance.

Risk acceptance should occur at an authority level consistent with:

* Risk severity.
* Business impact.
* Regulatory implications.
* Organizational risk appetite.

This connects AI policy with the organization's risk-management framework.

---

## 36. AI Policy and AI Incident Management

The policy should establish expectations for AI incident reporting and response.

Potential incidents include:

* Security compromise.
* Data leakage.
* Harmful AI output.
* Significant model failure.
* Unauthorized AI use.
* Regulatory breach.
* Unexpected system behavior.

Employees should know how to report relevant incidents.

---

## 37. AI Policy and AI Change Management

AI systems can change through:

* Model updates.
* Data changes.
* Prompt changes.
* Configuration changes.
* Integration changes.
* Vendor updates.

The policy should establish that significant changes must be appropriately assessed before implementation where required.

---

## 38. AI Policy and AI Supplier Management

Third-party AI services can introduce significant dependencies.

Policy requirements may address:

* Supplier assessment.
* Contractual requirements.
* Data handling.
* Security.
* Service availability.
* Model transparency.
* Subcontractors.
* Exit arrangements.

Supplier governance should be integrated with enterprise third-party risk management.

---

## 39. AI Policy and AI Lifecycle Governance

The policy should apply across the AI lifecycle.

```text
Concept
  ↓
Design
  ↓
Development / Acquisition
  ↓
Assessment
  ↓
Approval
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Monitoring
  ↓
Change
  ↓
Retirement
```

Governance should not stop once an AI system enters production.

---

## 40. AI Policy and Continual Improvement

The policy framework should support continual improvement.

Improvement inputs may include:

* Audit findings.
* Incidents.
* Risk assessments.
* Regulatory changes.
* Performance results.
* Stakeholder feedback.
* Technology developments.

Policy should evolve when evidence demonstrates that changes are necessary.

---

## 41. Management-System Requirements

An AIMS is more than a collection of policies.

Management-system requirements establish how the organization systematically:

* Establishes governance.
* Plans AI activities.
* Manages risks.
* Allocates resources.
* Operates controls.
* Evaluates performance.
* Conducts audits.
* Performs management review.
* Implements corrective action.
* Continually improves.

This management-system perspective distinguishes governance from simple policy publication.

---

## 42. Policy-to-Process Relationship

Each significant policy requirement should have an identifiable mechanism for implementation.

```text
Policy Requirement
       ↓
Process
       ↓
Procedure
       ↓
Control
       ↓
Responsible Owner
       ↓
Evidence
       ↓
Monitoring
```

If a policy requirement cannot be connected to an operational mechanism, it may not be effectively implemented.

---

## 43. Policy-to-Control Mapping

Organizations should consider mapping policy requirements to controls.

For example:

| Policy Requirement               | Control                 |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| AI systems must be registered    | AI inventory control    |
| High-risk AI must be assessed    | AI risk assessment      |
| Sensitive data must be protected | Data protection control |
| AI changes must be governed      | AI change management    |
| AI incidents must be reported    | AI incident management  |
| AI performance must be monitored | AI monitoring control   |

This improves traceability.

---

## 44. Management-System Integration

The AIMS should integrate with existing management systems where practical.

Relevant systems may include:

* Information Security Management System.
* Privacy Management System.
* Quality Management System.
* Business Continuity Management System.
* Enterprise Risk Management.
* Compliance Management.
* Data Governance.

Integration reduces duplication and strengthens enterprise governance.

---

## 45. Integrated Management-System Model

An integrated model may look like:

```text
              Enterprise Governance
                       ↓
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
      AIMS          Security        Privacy
        ↓              ↓              ↓

                       ↓
              Business Operations
```

AI governance should connect to these existing capabilities rather than operate independently wherever integration is appropriate.

---

Each major requirement should produce appropriate evidence.

Examples include:

* Approved policies.
* Risk assessments.
* Impact assessments.
* Governance meeting records.
* Training records.
* Monitoring results.
* Audit reports.
* Corrective actions.
* Management review records.

Evidence demonstrates that the management system operates in practice.

## 47. Requirements and Documented Information

The organization should determine what documented information is necessary to support effective operation and evidence of conformity.

Documentation may include:

* Policies.
* Standards.
* Procedures.
* Records.
* Assessments.
* Reports.
* Decisions.
* Audit evidence.

Documentation should be proportionate to organizational needs and risk.

---

## 48. Requirements and Document Control

Controlled information should be appropriately:

* Approved.
* Reviewed.
* Updated.
* Protected.
* Distributed.
* Retained.
* Disposed of where appropriate.

Document control supports consistency and traceability.

---

## 49. Requirements and Operational Control

Management-system requirements should ultimately influence operational activities.

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Operational Process
     ↓
Control Activity
     ↓
AI System
     ↓
Evidence
```

This ensures that the AIMS is embedded in actual AI operations.

---

## 50. Requirements and Performance Evaluation

The organization should evaluate whether the AIMS is achieving intended outcomes.

Performance evaluation may consider:

* AI governance metrics.
* Risk trends.
* Control effectiveness.
* Incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Objective achievement.
* Stakeholder feedback.

Performance evaluation provides the information needed for management review and improvement.
