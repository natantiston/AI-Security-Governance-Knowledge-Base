# 04.12 — AI Impact Assessment

## 1. Introduction to AI Impact Assessment

AI Impact Assessment is a structured process for identifying, analyzing, evaluating and addressing the potential impacts of an AI system on individuals, groups, organizations and other relevant stakeholders.

Within an AI Management System, impact assessment provides a systematic mechanism for understanding consequences that may arise from the development, deployment or use of AI.

An impact assessment may consider impacts related to:

* Individuals.
* Groups of individuals.
* Employees.
* Customers.
* Suppliers.
* Society.
* Organizations.
* The environment.
* Fundamental rights.
* Safety.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Fairness.
* Transparency.
* Human oversight.

The depth of assessment should be appropriate to the AI system, its intended use and the potential significance of its impacts.

---

## 2. Purpose of AI Impact Assessment

The primary purpose is to help the organization understand potential consequences before and during AI system operation.

An assessment can help determine:

* Who may be affected.
* How they may be affected.
* What could cause the impact.
* How significant the impact could be.
* What controls are necessary.
* Whether additional safeguards are required.
* Whether the AI system remains appropriate for its intended purpose.

Impact assessment therefore supports informed AI governance decisions.

---

## 3. AI Impact Assessment and Risk Assessment

AI impact assessment and AI risk assessment are closely related but are not necessarily identical.

**Risk assessment** generally focuses on uncertainty, threats, opportunities and potential consequences affecting organizational objectives.

**Impact assessment** focuses more specifically on the potential effects of an AI system on relevant affected parties and other areas of concern.

A simplified relationship can be represented as:

```text
AI System
    ↓
Potential Impacts
    ↓
Impact Assessment
    ↓
Risk Identification
    ↓
Risk Evaluation
    ↓
Controls and Safeguards
    ↓
Monitoring
```

The two processes should be coordinated rather than treated as completely independent activities.

---

## 4. AI Impact Assessment as a Lifecycle Activity

Impact assessment should not necessarily be treated as a one-time exercise.

It may be performed or revisited during relevant stages of the AI lifecycle:

```text
Concept
   ↓
Design
   ↓
Development / Acquisition
   ↓
Testing
   ↓
Deployment
   ↓
Operation
   ↓
Change
   ↓
Retirement
```

The assessment should be updated when material changes could alter the AI system's impacts.

---

## 5. Triggers for an AI Impact Assessment

An organization may establish criteria for determining when an impact assessment is required.

Potential triggers include:

* Introduction of a new AI system.
* Significant modification of an existing AI system.
* New intended use.
* Expansion to new users.
* Processing of new types of data.
* New affected stakeholders.
* Significant increase in scale.
* Material change in AI capability.
* Significant incident.
* New legal or regulatory requirements.

Trigger criteria should be documented and consistently applied.

---

## 6. AI System Scope

An impact assessment should clearly identify the AI system being assessed.

Relevant information may include:

* System name.
* System owner.
* Business owner.
* Intended purpose.
* Business process.
* Users.
* Deployment environment.
* Model or technology.
* Data sources.
* External providers.
* System boundaries.

A clearly defined scope prevents important components from being overlooked.

---

## 7. Intended Purpose

The intended purpose of the AI system should be clearly documented.

It may describe:

* What the system is designed to accomplish.
* Which business process it supports.
* Who uses it.
* What decisions it supports.
* What outputs it produces.
* What actions may follow from those outputs.

Understanding intended purpose is essential for evaluating potential impacts.

---

## 8. Intended Use

The assessment should consider how the AI system is expected to be used in practice.

This may include:

* Authorized users.
* Approved workflows.
* Permitted data.
* Decision contexts.
* Human involvement.
* Operational limitations.

The assessment should distinguish intended use from unauthorized or prohibited use.

---

## 9. Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse

Impact assessment may also consider reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Examples may include:

* Using the system for an unintended decision.
* Applying outputs outside the validated context.
* Using inappropriate data.
* Excessive reliance on AI output.
* Circumventing human oversight.
* Expanding system use without reassessment.

Considering foreseeable misuse can reveal impacts that may not appear when assessing intended use alone.

---

## 10. Stakeholder Identification

Relevant stakeholders should be identified as part of the assessment.

Stakeholders may include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Applicants.
* Patients.
* Students.
* Suppliers.
* Business partners.
* Regulators.
* Members of the public.

The relevant stakeholder groups depend on the AI system's purpose and operating context.

---

## 11. Affected Individuals

The assessment should identify individuals who may be directly or indirectly affected by the AI system.

Directly affected individuals may interact with the AI system or be subject to its outputs.

Indirectly affected individuals may experience consequences through decisions or processes influenced by the AI system.

Both categories may be relevant to impact assessment.

---

## 12. Affected Groups

Impacts may affect groups differently.

Groups may be differentiated according to characteristics such as:

* Role.
* Location.
* Age.
* Access requirements.
* Language.
* Socioeconomic circumstances.
* Relationship with the organization.

Where appropriate, organizations should examine whether impacts are distributed unevenly among groups.

---

## 13. Stakeholder Engagement

Where appropriate, relevant stakeholders may provide useful information for understanding potential impacts.

Engagement may involve:

* Interviews.
* Surveys.
* Workshops.
* User testing.
* Expert consultation.
* Complaints analysis.
* Feedback channels.

Stakeholder input can identify concerns that may not be visible from technical analysis alone.

---

## 14. AI Impact Categories

Impact assessments may consider multiple categories of impact.

Potential categories include:

* Human rights.
* Privacy.
* Safety.
* Security.
* Fairness.
* Accessibility.
* Transparency.
* Explainability.
* Autonomy.
* Economic impact.
* Social impact.
* Environmental impact.

The categories selected should reflect the AI system and its context.

---

## 15. Human Rights Impacts

AI systems may affect rights and freedoms depending on their purpose and use.

Potential concerns may include:

* Discrimination.
* Privacy.
* Freedom of expression.
* Equal treatment.
* Access to services.
* Employment-related rights.
* Due process.

The assessment should identify relevant rights considerations rather than assuming that every AI system presents the same impacts.

---

## 16. Privacy Impacts

AI systems that process personal information may create privacy impacts.

Potential issues include:

* Excessive data collection.
* Inappropriate use.
* Unauthorized access.
* Unintended disclosure.
* Excessive retention.
* Inference of sensitive information.

Privacy considerations should be integrated with applicable privacy and data-protection processes.

---

## 17. Safety Impacts

AI systems operating in safety-relevant contexts may create physical or operational safety impacts.

Examples may include:

* Incorrect recommendations.
* Unsafe automation.
* Failure to detect hazards.
* Incorrect system actions.
* Inadequate human intervention.

Safety-related AI systems may require enhanced assessment and safeguards.

---

## 18. Security Impacts

AI systems may create or amplify cybersecurity risks.

Potential concerns include:

* Unauthorized access.
* Manipulation.
* Data poisoning.
* Prompt injection.
* Model abuse.
* Adversarial attacks.
* Information leakage.

Security impact considerations should connect with the organization's cybersecurity risk-management processes.

---

## 19. Fairness Impacts

AI outputs may affect different individuals or groups differently.

Potential concerns include:

* Unequal treatment.
* Disparate outcomes.
* Systematic errors.
* Biased data.
* Unequal access.
* Inappropriate decision criteria.

Fairness assessment should consider the AI system's context, intended use and affected populations.

---

## 20. Accessibility Impacts

AI systems may create barriers for individuals with different accessibility needs.

Assessment may consider:

* Interface accessibility.
* Language accessibility.
* Communication formats.
* Assistive technologies.
* Alternative interaction methods.

Accessibility considerations should be incorporated where the AI system affects access to products, services or processes.

---

## 21. Transparency Impacts

Individuals may need to understand when and how AI is involved in a process.

Transparency considerations may include:

* Disclosure of AI use.
* Purpose of AI processing.
* Relevant limitations.
* Human involvement.
* Available explanations.
* Communication of significant decisions.

The appropriate level of transparency depends on the context and potential impact.

---

## 22. Human Autonomy Impacts

AI systems may influence or constrain human decision-making.

Potential concerns include:

* Excessive automation.
* Over-reliance on AI recommendations.
* Manipulative recommendations.
* Lack of meaningful human choice.
* Inability to challenge AI-supported decisions.

The assessment should consider whether humans retain appropriate control over consequential activities.

---

## 23. Environmental Impacts

Where relevant, an AI system's environmental impacts may be considered.

Potential factors include:

* Energy consumption.
* Computing requirements.
* Infrastructure use.
* Hardware lifecycle.
* Resource consumption.

Environmental considerations should be proportionate to the system and organizational context.

---

## 24. Economic Impacts

AI systems can produce positive or negative economic effects.

Potential impacts may involve:

* Employment.
* Costs.
* Productivity.
* Access to services.
* Financial decisions.
* Business opportunities.

The assessment should consider material economic consequences where relevant.

---

## 25. Social Impacts

Some AI systems may affect social relationships, communities or public behavior.

Potential impacts may include:

* Social exclusion.
* Misinformation.
* Changes in communication.
* Community effects.
* Trust in institutions.

Social impacts should be considered where they are reasonably foreseeable and relevant to the AI system.

---

## 26. Positive AI Impacts

Impact assessment should not focus exclusively on negative consequences.

Potential positive impacts may include:

* Improved accessibility.
* Faster services.
* Better decision support.
* Increased safety.
* Reduced operational errors.
* Improved resource allocation.
* Greater productivity.

Understanding positive impacts helps management evaluate the overall value and appropriateness of the AI system.

---

## 27. Negative AI Impacts

Potential negative impacts should be identified and evaluated.

Examples include:

* Incorrect decisions.
* Discrimination.
* Privacy violations.
* Safety incidents.
* Security compromise.
* Financial loss.
* Loss of human control.

Negative impacts should be considered together with their likelihood, severity and affected stakeholders.

---

## 28. Direct AI Impacts

Direct impacts arise directly from AI system behavior.

Examples include:

* An incorrect classification.
* An inaccurate recommendation.
* An automated rejection.
* An incorrect prediction.

Direct impacts are often easier to identify because the connection between AI output and consequence is relatively clear.

---

## 29. Indirect AI Impacts

Indirect impacts may occur through downstream decisions, processes or users.

For example:

```text
AI Recommendation
       ↓
Human Decision
       ↓
Business Action
       ↓
Impact on Individual
```

The assessment should consider important downstream effects rather than examining the AI output in isolation.

---

## 30. Cumulative AI Impacts

Multiple AI systems or repeated AI decisions may create cumulative effects.

For example:

* Repeated automated decisions.
* Multiple systems using the same data.
* Several AI tools affecting the same individual.
* Large-scale deployment across an organization.

Cumulative impacts may become significant even when individual interactions appear relatively minor.

## 31. AI Impact Severity

The severity of an AI impact represents the magnitude of the potential consequence if the impact occurs.

Severity may consider:

* Degree of harm.
* Number of affected individuals.
* Duration of impact.
* Reversibility.
* Sensitivity of the affected interest.
* Organizational consequences.
* Societal consequences.

Severity should be evaluated using criteria appropriate to the organization's context.

---

## 32. AI Impact Likelihood

Likelihood represents the possibility that an identified impact may occur.

Factors may include:

* Frequency of AI use.
* Exposure.
* System reliability.
* Data quality.
* Human behavior.
* Environmental conditions.
* Existing controls.

Likelihood should be assessed using a consistent methodology.

---

## 33. AI Impact Significance

Impact significance can be evaluated by considering the combination of potential consequence and likelihood.

A simplified model may be:

```text
Impact Significance
        =
Potential Consequence
        +
Likelihood
        +
Contextual Factors
```

Organizations may use qualitative, quantitative or hybrid methodologies depending on their needs.

---

## 34. AI Impact Assessment Criteria

Assessment criteria should be established before evaluating impacts where practical.

Criteria may define:

* Severity categories.
* Likelihood categories.
* Impact thresholds.
* Acceptability criteria.
* Escalation requirements.
* Required safeguards.

Consistent criteria improve comparability between assessments.

---

## 35. AI Impact Classification

Organizations may classify identified impacts according to significance.

For example:

| Classification | General Interpretation                         |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Low            | Limited potential consequence                  |
| Moderate       | Noticeable consequence requiring controls      |
| High           | Significant potential consequence              |
| Critical       | Potentially severe or unacceptable consequence |

The actual classification methodology should reflect organizational requirements.

---

## 36. AI Impact Acceptability

The organization should determine whether identified impacts are acceptable within its established governance framework.

Acceptability may depend on:

* Organizational values.
* Risk appetite.
* Legal requirements.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* Potential harm.
* Available safeguards.

An impact that is technically manageable may still be unacceptable if it conflicts with mandatory requirements.

---

## 37. Unacceptable AI Impacts

Certain impacts may be considered unacceptable regardless of potential business benefit.

Examples may include:

* Serious foreseeable harm.
* Prohibited uses.
* Significant violations of rights.
* Unlawful processing.
* Uncontrolled safety risks.

Where an impact cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, the organization should reconsider whether the AI system should be deployed or continued.

---

## 38. AI Impact Mitigation

Impact mitigation involves implementing measures to reduce the likelihood or severity of undesirable impacts.

Measures may include:

* Human oversight.
* Data controls.
* Technical safeguards.
* Access restrictions.
* Model testing.
* Output validation.
* User training.
* Monitoring.

Mitigation should address the specific mechanism through which the impact could occur.

---

## 39. AI Impact Prevention

Where feasible, controls should prevent significant negative impacts before they occur.

Preventive measures may include:

* Restricted use cases.
* Pre-deployment assessment.
* Data validation.
* Approval requirements.
* System testing.
* Access restrictions.

Prevention is generally preferable to relying exclusively on detection after an impact has occurred.

---

## 40. AI Impact Detection

Some impacts cannot be completely prevented and therefore require detection mechanisms.

Detection may involve:

* Monitoring.
* Automated alerts.
* User reporting.
* Complaint mechanisms.
* Quality checks.
* Security monitoring.
* Periodic assessments.

Detection mechanisms should be connected to defined response processes.

---

## 41. AI Impact Response

When an actual or suspected impact occurs, the organization should have appropriate response mechanisms.

Response may include:

1. Detecting the issue.
2. Assessing the impact.
3. Containing the problem.
4. Protecting affected parties.
5. Escalating where necessary.
6. Correcting the issue.
7. Documenting the event.
8. Evaluating lessons learned.

Response procedures should reflect the severity of the potential impact.

---

## 42. AI Impact Remediation

Remediation addresses an impact that has already occurred.

Possible measures include:

* Correcting an inaccurate decision.
* Restoring access.
* Removing incorrect information.
* Reversing an inappropriate action.
* Providing appropriate notification.
* Correcting system behavior.

Remediation should consider both immediate and longer-term consequences.

---

## 43. AI Impact Reversibility

The reversibility of an impact is an important assessment consideration.

Some impacts may be:

* Easily reversible.
* Partially reversible.
* Difficult to reverse.
* Irreversible.

For example, an incorrect internal recommendation may be easily corrected, while certain decisions affecting an individual's rights or opportunities may have consequences that cannot be fully reversed.

---

## 44. AI Impact Duration

The duration of an impact should be considered.

An impact may be:

* Temporary.
* Short-term.
* Long-term.
* Persistent.

Long-lasting impacts may require stronger safeguards even when the initial event appears limited.

---

## 45. AI Impact Scope

The scope of an impact considers how broadly the consequence may extend.

Scope may include:

* One individual.
* A defined group.
* A business unit.
* Multiple organizations.
* A large population.
* Society more broadly.

Large-scale AI deployment may increase the importance of scope analysis.

---

## 46. AI Impact Exposure

Exposure represents the extent to which individuals or systems may be subject to the potential impact.

Factors may include:

* Number of users.
* Frequency of decisions.
* Geographic coverage.
* Duration of deployment.
* Degree of automation.

Higher exposure may increase the overall significance of an impact.

---

## 47. AI Impact Concentration

Impacts may be concentrated among particular groups.

For example:

```text
AI System
    ↓
General Population
    ↓
Specific Subgroup
    ↓
Higher Impact Exposure
```

Concentration analysis can help identify groups that require additional safeguards or engagement.

---

## 48. AI Disproportionate Impact

An AI system may produce significantly different outcomes for different groups.

Assessment may therefore consider whether:

* Error rates differ.
* Access differs.
* Outcomes differ.
* Complaints differ.
* Human review differs.

Where disproportionate impacts are identified, the organization should evaluate their significance and potential mitigation.

---

## 49. AI Vulnerable Stakeholders

Some stakeholders may be more susceptible to harm from particular AI applications.

Depending on context, this may include:

* Children.
* Individuals with disabilities.
* People with limited digital access.
* Individuals in vulnerable circumstances.
* People subject to consequential decisions.

The identification of vulnerable stakeholders should be context-specific rather than assumed universally.

---

## 50. AI Impact Dependencies

An impact may depend on external systems, people or organizations.

Dependencies may include:

* Third-party models.
* Data providers.
* Cloud infrastructure.
* Human decision-makers.
* External APIs.
* Business partners.

Impact assessment should consider important dependencies that may influence outcomes.

---

## 51. AI Impact Propagation

An impact can propagate from one system or process into another.

For example:

```text
Data Quality Problem
       ↓
AI Model Output
       ↓
Business Decision
       ↓
Downstream System
       ↓
Stakeholder Impact
```

Understanding propagation helps identify consequences beyond the immediate AI system.

---

## 52. AI Downstream Impact

AI outputs may be reused by other systems or processes.

Downstream uses may include:

* Automated workflows.
* Human decisions.
* Customer communications.
* Financial processes.
* Security processes.
* Operational systems.

The assessment should consider significant downstream dependencies.

---

## 53. AI Cascading Impacts

A single AI failure may trigger multiple related consequences.

For example:

```text
AI Failure
   ↓
Incorrect Decision
   ↓
Operational Disruption
   ↓
Customer Impact
   ↓
Financial / Reputational Consequence
```

Cascading effects are particularly important for interconnected or highly automated environments.

---

## 54. AI Impact Interdependencies

Different impact categories may interact.

For example:

```text
Poor Data Quality
       ↓
Accuracy Problem
       ↓
Fairness Impact
       ↓
Customer Impact
       ↓
Reputational Impact
```

An assessment should consider these relationships rather than evaluating each impact category entirely in isolation.

---

## 55. AI Impact Trade-Offs

AI systems may create situations where improving one outcome affects another.

Examples include:

* Accuracy versus explainability.
* Security versus usability.
* Automation versus human control.
* Efficiency versus human interaction.
* Data availability versus privacy.

Trade-offs should be explicitly considered when they materially affect stakeholders or organizational objectives.

---

## 56. AI Impact Controls

Controls should be selected based on identified impacts.

Possible controls include:

* Technical controls.
* Organizational controls.
* Procedural controls.
* Human controls.
* Contractual controls.
* Monitoring controls.

Controls should address the actual causes and consequences identified during assessment.

---

## 57. AI Impact Control Mapping

Impact controls can be mapped to identified impacts.

```text
Impact
   ↓
Cause
   ↓
Existing Control
   ↓
Control Effectiveness
   ↓
Residual Impact
   ↓
Additional Safeguard
```

This creates traceability between assessment findings and mitigation measures.

---

## 58. AI Residual Impact

Residual impact represents the remaining potential impact after existing safeguards are considered.

The organization should determine whether residual impact remains within acceptable boundaries.

If not, additional controls or changes to the AI system may be necessary.

---

## 59. AI Impact Treatment Options

Treatment options may include:

* Avoiding the activity.
* Reducing the impact.
* Reducing exposure.
* Adding safeguards.
* Increasing human oversight.
* Changing the AI system.
* Restricting use.
* Transferring certain responsibilities.
* Accepting the remaining impact where appropriate.

Treatment decisions should be documented where necessary.

---

## 60. AI Impact Treatment Prioritization

Not all identified impacts require the same level of treatment.

Priority may be influenced by:

* Severity.
* Likelihood.
* Number of affected parties.
* Vulnerability.
* Irreversibility.
* Legal requirements.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* Existing control effectiveness.

Higher-significance impacts should generally receive greater management attention.

* Assessment scope.

* Stakeholder identification.

```text
Define AI System
      ↓
Define Intended Purpose
      ↓
Identify Stakeholders
      ↓
Identify Potential Impacts
      ↓
Analyze Impacts
      ↓
Evaluate Significance
      ↓
Identify Controls
      ↓
Determine Residual Impact
      ↓
Define Treatment
      ↓
Approve / Escalate
      ↓
Monitor and Review
```

The process should be adapted to the organization's context and the characteristics of the AI system.

Known limitations should be documented and considered when interpreting assessment results.

* New use cases.

* Complaints.

Sampling methods should be appropriate to the assessment objective and potential impact.

## 76. AI Impact Assessment Testing

Testing may provide evidence about potential impacts.

Testing may examine:

* System outputs.
* Different input conditions.
* Edge cases.
* Failure scenarios.
* Different stakeholder groups.
* Human interaction.

Testing should reflect realistic operating conditions where practical.

---

## 77. AI Impact Assessment Scenario Analysis

Scenario analysis can explore how impacts might occur under different conditions.

Scenarios may include:

* Normal operation.
* High-volume operation.
* Incorrect input.
* System failure.
* Malicious use.
* Human error.
* Unexpected model behavior.

Scenario analysis can reveal impacts that may not appear during routine testing.

---

## 78. AI Impact Assessment Stress Testing

For appropriate AI systems, stress testing may examine behavior under unusual or demanding conditions.

Examples include:

* High processing volume.
* Unusual inputs.
* Poor-quality data.
* Extreme operational conditions.
* Increased system dependency.

Stress testing should be proportionate to system criticality.

---

## 79. AI Impact Assessment Edge Cases

Edge cases can reveal potential impacts that average performance measurements may hide.

Examples include:

* Rare inputs.
* Unusual user circumstances.
* Boundary conditions.
* Exceptional transactions.
* Unexpected combinations of data.

High-consequence edge cases may require particular attention.

---

## 80. AI Impact Assessment Human Factors

Human behavior can significantly influence AI impacts.

Relevant factors may include:

* Over-reliance on AI.
* Misinterpretation of outputs.
* Automation bias.
* Insufficient training.
* Failure to follow procedures.
* Excessive trust in system recommendations.

Human factors should therefore be considered in both impact identification and mitigation.

---

## 81. AI Automation Bias

Automation bias occurs when people give excessive weight to an automated recommendation because it was produced by a system.

Potential consequences include:

* Failure to challenge incorrect outputs.
* Reduced independent judgment.
* Incorrect decisions.
* Insufficient human oversight.

Controls may include training, review requirements and clear decision authority.

---

## 82. AI Human Reliance

The assessment should consider how much users are expected to rely on AI outputs.

Reliance may range from:

```text
Informational Support
        ↓
Decision Support
        ↓
Decision Recommendation
        ↓
Automated Decision
```

Greater reliance may increase the potential significance of incorrect AI outputs.

---

## 83. AI Human Oversight Effectiveness

The presence of a human reviewer does not automatically eliminate AI-related impacts.

The assessment should consider whether the human reviewer:

* Has sufficient competence.
* Has sufficient time.
* Can challenge the AI.
* Has appropriate authority.
* Understands system limitations.
* Can override the output.

Human oversight should be meaningful rather than merely procedural.

---

## 84. AI Explainability and Impact Assessment

Where relevant, the assessment should consider whether users or affected individuals need an appropriate understanding of AI-supported outcomes.

Considerations may include:

* Purpose.
* Factors influencing outcomes.
* Significant limitations.
* Human involvement.
* Available challenge mechanisms.

The required level of explanation depends on the context and consequences.

---

## 85. AI Contestability

Where AI contributes to consequential outcomes, appropriate mechanisms may allow decisions or outputs to be questioned or reviewed.

Mechanisms may include:

* Human review.
* Appeals.
* Complaints.
* Reconsideration.
* Correction processes.

Contestability can help reduce the consequences of erroneous or inappropriate AI-supported decisions.

---

## 86. AI Impact Assessment and Complaints

Complaints can provide evidence of real-world impacts.

Complaint analysis may identify:

* Recurring problems.
* Affected groups.
* Unexpected consequences.
* Communication weaknesses.
* Potential control failures.

Complaint information can therefore become an important input to reassessment.

---

## 87. AI Impact Assessment and Incidents

AI incidents can reveal impacts that were underestimated during the original assessment.

Incident information may lead to:

* Reclassification.
* Additional safeguards.
* Risk reassessment.
* Impact reassessment.
* Changes to intended use.
* System modification.

Impact assessment should therefore incorporate relevant operational experience.

---

## 88. AI Impact Assessment and Near Misses

Near misses can reveal potential impacts before significant harm occurs.

Examples include:

* Incorrect output caught by human review.
* Potential privacy disclosure prevented.
* Unsafe recommendation rejected.
* Biased output detected during testing.

Near misses should be analyzed where they provide useful evidence about potential impacts.

---

## 89. AI Impact Assessment and Stakeholder Feedback

Stakeholder feedback may reveal impacts that technical testing does not capture.

Feedback may concern:

* Accessibility.
* Fairness.
* Usability.
* Transparency.
* Trust.
* Unintended consequences.

Relevant feedback should be incorporated into impact assessment activities.

---

## 90. AI Impact Assessment and Regulatory Requirements

Applicable regulatory or legal requirements may define specific impact-assessment obligations.

The organization should identify which requirements apply to:

* The AI system.
* The data.
* The business process.
* The affected stakeholders.
* The geographic jurisdiction.

Regulatory requirements should be integrated into the organization's broader assessment process where appropriate.
