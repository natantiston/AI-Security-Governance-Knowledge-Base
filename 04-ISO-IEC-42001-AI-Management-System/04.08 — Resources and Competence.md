# 04.08 — Resources and Competence

## 1. Introduction to AI Resources and Competence

An effective AI Management System (AIMS) depends on the organization having sufficient resources and competent personnel to establish, implement, maintain and continually improve its AI management capabilities.

AI governance cannot operate effectively through policies and procedures alone. The organization must provide the people, technology, information, infrastructure, financial resources and organizational capabilities necessary to perform AI-related activities.

Resources and competence should be considered across the AI lifecycle and should reflect the organization's:

* AI strategy.
* AI portfolio.
* Risk profile.
* Organizational structure.
* Regulatory environment.
* AI system complexity.
* Stakeholder expectations.

---

## 2. Purpose of AI Resource Management

AI resource management ensures that appropriate resources are available when and where they are required.

Effective resource management supports:

* AI governance.
* Risk management.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Data governance.
* AI development.
* AI assessment.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.
* Continual improvement.

Resource planning should consider both current requirements and anticipated future needs.

---

AI resources may include several categories:

| Resource Category  | Examples                                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| People             | AI specialists, engineers, risk professionals    |
| Technology         | AI platforms, monitoring tools, security systems |
| Data               | Training, validation and operational data        |

The required resources will vary according to organizational circumstances.

* AI system owners.
* Data scientists.
* Machine learning engineers.
* Software engineers.
* Cybersecurity specialists.
* Privacy professionals.

## 5. AI Governance Personnel

AI governance personnel support the design, coordination and operation of the AIMS.

Responsibilities may include:

* Maintaining governance processes.
* Coordinating assessments.
* Managing AI policies.
* Supporting committees.
* Monitoring governance metrics.
* Coordinating risk activities.
* Reporting to management.

Governance personnel should have appropriate authority, knowledge and access to stakeholders.

---

## 6. AI Technical Personnel

Technical personnel may be required to design, develop, deploy and maintain AI systems.

Examples include:

* Machine learning engineers.
* Data scientists.
* AI architects.
* Software developers.
* MLOps engineers.
* Cloud engineers.
* Data engineers.

Technical competence should be appropriate to the technologies being used.

## 7. AI Risk and Compliance Personnel

AI risk and compliance functions may provide specialized expertise.

Their responsibilities may include:

* Risk assessment.
* Regulatory analysis.
* Control evaluation.
* Compliance monitoring.
* Risk reporting.
* Exception management.

These roles should work collaboratively with business and technical teams.

## 8. AI Security Personnel

AI security requires appropriate cybersecurity expertise.

Security personnel may address:

* Identity and access management.
* Secure AI architecture.
* Threat modeling.
* Vulnerability management.
* AI-specific attacks.
* Monitoring.
* Incident response.

Security competence should reflect the organization's AI threat landscape.

---

## 9. AI Privacy Personnel

Privacy professionals may be required where AI systems process personal information.

Their responsibilities may include:

* Privacy risk assessment.
* Data protection analysis.
* Privacy impact assessments.
* Data-use requirements.
* Privacy controls.
* Regulatory interpretation.

Privacy competence should be integrated into relevant AI lifecycle activities.

---

## 10. AI Data Governance Personnel

Data governance personnel support the quality and appropriate management of AI-related data.

Responsibilities may include:

* Data ownership.
* Data quality.
* Data classification.
* Data lineage.
* Data access.
* Data retention.
* Data stewardship.

Strong data governance supports reliable and trustworthy AI outcomes.

---

## 11. AI Business Expertise

Technical expertise alone is insufficient for effective AI management.

Business subject-matter experts can provide knowledge regarding:

* Business processes.
* Customer needs.
* Operational requirements.
* Business risks.
* Decision-making.
* Expected outcomes.

Business expertise should be included in relevant AI decisions.

---

## 12. AI Leadership Capability

AI leadership requires sufficient understanding of both opportunity and risk.

Leaders should be capable of understanding:

* AI strategic value.
* AI risks.
* Governance responsibilities.
* Resource requirements.
* Significant AI decisions.
* Stakeholder impact.

Leadership competence supports effective oversight.

---

## 13. AI Competence

Competence refers to the ability to apply appropriate knowledge and skills to achieve intended outcomes.

AI competence may involve:

* Technical knowledge.
* Governance knowledge.
* Risk-management skills.
* Security knowledge.
* Privacy knowledge.
* Data-management capabilities.
* Business understanding.

Competence requirements should be appropriate to each role.

---

## 14. Role-Based AI Competence

Different roles require different levels and types of AI competence.

For example:

```text id="q6g0nq"
Board Member
→ Strategic AI Awareness

Executive
→ AI Risk + Strategic Decision-Making

AI Governance
→ Governance + Risk + Compliance

AI Engineer
→ Technical + Security + Lifecycle

Business Owner
→ Business + AI Risk + Accountability
```

Competence should therefore be role-specific rather than identical for everyone.

---

## 15. AI Competence Requirements

The organization should define competence requirements for roles that can affect AI management outcomes.

Requirements may address:

* Education.
* Training.
* Experience.
* Certifications.
* Technical skills.
* Professional knowledge.
* Business knowledge.

Requirements should reflect actual responsibilities.

---

## 16. AI Competence Assessment

Organizations should evaluate whether personnel possess the required competence.

Assessment methods may include:

* Skills assessments.
* Interviews.
* Training evaluations.
* Certifications.
* Practical demonstrations.
* Performance reviews.
* Manager assessments.

---

## 17. AI Competence Gaps

A competence gap exists when required capabilities exceed available capabilities.

Examples include:

* Lack of AI risk expertise.
* Insufficient security knowledge.
* Limited privacy knowledge.
* Lack of model validation expertise.
* Insufficient governance experience.

Competence gaps should be addressed according to their importance and risk.

---

## 18. AI Skills Gap Analysis

A skills gap analysis compares current capabilities with required capabilities.

A simplified model is:

```text id="0g0tmt"
Required Competence
        ↓
Current Competence
        ↓
Gap Analysis
        ↓
Training / Recruitment / External Expertise
        ↓
Reassessment
```

This supports structured workforce planning.

## 22. AI Awareness versus AI Competence

Awareness and competence are related but different.

**Awareness** means understanding relevant AI concepts, policies and responsibilities.

**Competence** means having the knowledge and ability to perform assigned responsibilities effectively.

For example:

> An employee may be aware that confidential information should not be entered into an unauthorized AI service but may lack the technical competence to identify whether a particular AI service securely processes organizational data.

Both awareness and competence may therefore be required.

## 24. AI Competence Evaluation

Training completion alone does not necessarily demonstrate competence.

Competence evaluation may examine whether personnel can actually apply what they learned.

Methods may include:

* Practical exercises.
* Assessments.
* Simulations.
* Technical testing.
* Performance observation.

Evaluation should focus on capability rather than attendance alone.

---

## 25. AI Competence Maintenance

AI competence should be maintained over time.

This is important because:

* AI technology evolves.
* Threats change.
* Regulations change.
* Governance practices mature.
* Organizational responsibilities change.

Periodic refresher training and professional development may therefore be necessary.

---

## 26. AI Competence and Emerging Technology

Personnel may need additional competence when new AI technologies are introduced.

Examples include:

* Generative AI.
* Large language models.
* AI agents.
* Multimodal systems.
* Retrieval-augmented generation.
* Autonomous AI systems.

Competence requirements should evolve with the organization's technology landscape.

---

## 27. AI Competence and Risk Level

Higher-risk AI systems may require greater levels of expertise.

For example:

```text id="3w7vbi"
Low-Risk AI
→ General AI Awareness

Moderate-Risk AI
→ Role-Specific Competence

High-Risk AI
→ Specialized Technical + Governance Expertise
```

Competence requirements should therefore be proportionate to risk.

---

## 28. AI Competence and Human Oversight

Personnel responsible for human oversight should understand:

* AI system purpose.
* System limitations.
* Expected behavior.
* Warning indicators.
* Escalation procedures.
* Intervention mechanisms.

Human oversight is less effective when personnel lack sufficient understanding of the system they are expected to supervise.

---

## 29. AI Competence and AI System Ownership

AI system owners should possess sufficient understanding to manage their accountability.

They should understand:

* System purpose.
* Business impact.
* Key risks.
* Governance requirements.
* Performance expectations.
* Control responsibilities.

Where technical expertise is required, system owners should have access to appropriate specialists.

---

## 30. AI Competence and Accountability

Accountability should be supported by competence.

Assigning a person responsibility for an AI activity without providing the required knowledge, skills or resources can weaken governance effectiveness.

Competence and accountability should therefore be considered together.
