# 04.17 — Nonconformity and Corrective Action

## 1. Introduction to Nonconformity and Corrective Action

Nonconformity and corrective action provide a structured mechanism for addressing failures within the AI Management System (AIMS).

A nonconformity indicates that a requirement has not been fulfilled. Corrective action addresses the underlying cause of the nonconformity so that the problem can be controlled and recurrence reduced.

The fundamental relationship is:

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Actual Condition
     ↓
Deviation
     ↓
Nonconformity
     ↓
Cause Analysis
     ↓
Corrective Action
     ↓
Effectiveness Verification
```

This process transforms identified weaknesses into opportunities for strengthening the AIMS.

---

## 2. Understanding Nonconformity

A nonconformity occurs when an applicable requirement is not fulfilled.

Within an AIMS, requirements may originate from:

* ISO/IEC 42001.
* Organizational policies.
* Internal procedures.
* AI governance requirements.
* Legal or regulatory obligations.
* Contractual requirements.
* Defined controls.
* Approved organizational criteria.

The existence of a problem does not automatically establish a nonconformity; the condition should be evaluated against a defined requirement.

---

## 3. Sources of Nonconformity

Nonconformities may be identified through multiple sources.

Examples include:

* Internal audits.
* External audits.
* Certification assessments.
* Monitoring.
* Management review.
* AI impact assessments.
* Risk assessments.
* Incidents.
* Complaints.
* Testing.
* Employee reports.
* Supplier assessments.

A mature AIMS should provide multiple mechanisms for identifying deviations.

---

## 4. Nonconformity and AI Governance

AI governance can involve complex interactions between people, processes, technologies and third parties.

Nonconformities may therefore occur in areas such as:

* AI governance.
* Risk management.
* AI impact assessment.
* Data management.
* Human oversight.
* AI security.
* AI supplier management.
* Monitoring.
* Documentation.
* Incident management.

Effective corrective action helps ensure that these weaknesses do not remain isolated operational problems.

---

## 5. Examples of AIMS Nonconformity

Examples may include:

* A required AI risk assessment was not performed.
* An approved procedure was not followed.
* Required evidence was unavailable.
* An assigned control was not implemented.
* A required review was not completed.
* A corrective action was implemented but not verified.
* Required responsibilities were not assigned.

The finding should be supported by objective evidence.

---

## 6. Requirement-Based Evaluation

Nonconformity should be established through comparison between a requirement and the actual condition.

A useful structure is:

```text
Requirement:
What should happen?

Evidence:
What actually happened?

Gap:
What is different?

Conclusion:
Does the gap constitute a nonconformity?
```

This approach helps maintain objectivity and prevents findings from being based solely on personal expectations.

---

## 7. Objective Evidence

Objective evidence is essential when identifying nonconformity.

Evidence may include:

* Records.
* System logs.
* Approved documents.
* Audit observations.
* Assessment results.
* Interviews supported by other evidence.
* Configuration information.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence should be sufficient to support the finding.

---

## 8. Documenting a Nonconformity

A nonconformity should be documented clearly enough that another qualified person can understand:

* What requirement applies.
* What evidence was found.
* What condition was observed.
* Why the condition does not satisfy the requirement.

A strong finding avoids vague statements such as:

> "AI governance is insufficient."

Instead, the finding should describe the specific requirement, evidence and observed deviation.

---

## 9. Immediate Correction

When a nonconformity is identified, the organization may take immediate correction to address the observed condition.

For example:

```text
Missing Approval
      ↓
Approval Obtained
      ↓
Immediate Condition Corrected
```

Immediate correction addresses the current problem.

It does not necessarily address why the problem occurred.

---

## 10. Correction Versus Corrective Action

Correction and corrective action are different concepts.

| Correction                              | Corrective Action           |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Addresses the immediate condition       | Addresses the cause         |
| Fixes the identified problem            | Seeks to prevent recurrence |
| Usually short-term                      | Usually longer-term         |
| Does not necessarily address root cause | Requires analysis of cause  |

For example, replacing a missing AI assessment may be a correction. Changing the governance process that caused assessments to be missed may be corrective action.

---

## 11. Containment

Containment may be necessary when a nonconformity creates an ongoing risk.

Containment may involve:

* Restricting system use.
* Suspending a process.
* Applying temporary controls.
* Increasing monitoring.
* Blocking a deployment.
* Limiting access.

Containment reduces exposure while a permanent solution is developed.

---

## 12. Evaluating the Significance of Nonconformity

The organization should consider the significance of a nonconformity.

Relevant factors may include:

* AI risk.
* Potential stakeholder impact.
* Regulatory significance.
* Security implications.
* Privacy implications.
* Scope of affected processes.
* Recurrence.
* Duration.

The response should be proportionate to the significance of the issue.

---

## 13. Major and Minor Nonconformities

Organizations and external assessment bodies may classify nonconformities according to their significance.

A major nonconformity may involve:

* A significant management-system failure.
* A systemic weakness.
* Failure to fulfill an important requirement.
* A condition that significantly affects the effectiveness of the AIMS.

A minor nonconformity may represent a more limited deviation that does not indicate a systemic failure.

Classification criteria should be defined and applied consistently.

---

## 14. Systemic Nonconformity

A systemic nonconformity extends beyond an isolated incident.

Indicators may include:

* Similar failures across multiple AI systems.
* Repeated findings.
* Common process weaknesses.
* Organization-wide control gaps.
* Repeated failure to implement required processes.

Systemic nonconformities require broader investigation because correcting one instance may not resolve the underlying organizational problem.

---

## 15. Recurring Nonconformity

A recurring nonconformity occurs when a previously identified problem returns.

Recurrence may indicate:

* Ineffective corrective action.
* Incorrect root-cause analysis.
* Insufficient resources.
* Weak accountability.
* Poor control design.
* Inadequate monitoring.

Recurring issues should receive appropriate management attention.

---

## 16. Root Cause

Root cause analysis seeks to determine why the nonconformity occurred.

Possible causes may include:

* Inadequate processes.
* Unclear responsibilities.
* Insufficient competence.
* Poor communication.
* Inadequate resources.
* Weak system design.
* Ineffective controls.
* Inadequate management oversight.

The objective is to identify causes that can be addressed rather than simply describing the immediate symptom.

---

## 17. Root Cause Versus Symptom

A symptom describes what went wrong.

A root cause explains why the condition occurred.

For example:

```text
Symptom:
AI impact assessment was not completed.

Possible Immediate Cause:
Project team did not perform the assessment.

Possible Root Cause:
The project governance workflow did not contain a mandatory
assessment checkpoint before deployment.
```

Corrective action should address the underlying cause rather than only the visible symptom.

---

## 18. Root Cause Analysis Techniques

Organizations may use different techniques to analyze causes.

Common approaches include:

* Five Whys.
* Cause-and-effect analysis.
* Fault-tree analysis.
* Process analysis.
* Barrier analysis.
* Event analysis.

The technique should be proportionate to the complexity and significance of the nonconformity.

---

## 19. Five Whys

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why a condition occurred.

Example:

```text
Why was the AI assessment missing?
        ↓
The project team did not complete it.

Why?
        ↓
The assessment requirement was not triggered.

Why?
        ↓
The project workflow did not contain an AI assessment checkpoint.

Why?
        ↓
AI governance requirements were not integrated into project governance.

Why?
        ↓
The organization's AI governance framework had not been integrated
with the enterprise project-management process.
```

This can reveal a systemic process weakness rather than simply blaming an individual.

---

## 20. Cause-and-Effect Analysis

Cause-and-effect analysis organizes potential causes into categories.

For an AIMS nonconformity, categories might include:

* People.
* Process.
* Technology.
* Data.
* Governance.
* Resources.
* Communication.
* External dependencies.

This approach can be useful when multiple factors contribute to the same problem.

---

## 21. Corrective Action

Corrective action is action taken to eliminate the cause of a nonconformity and prevent recurrence.

Examples include:

* Revising a governance process.
* Introducing mandatory approval checkpoints.
* Improving training.
* Redesigning controls.
* Automating evidence collection.
* Clarifying responsibilities.
* Strengthening monitoring.
* Changing supplier requirements.

The corrective action should correspond to the identified cause.

---

## 22. Risk-Based Corrective Action

Corrective actions should reflect the significance of the associated risk.

Higher-risk issues may require:

* Faster action.
* Stronger containment.
* Greater management involvement.
* Additional resources.
* More extensive verification.

Risk-based prioritization helps ensure that serious AIMS weaknesses receive appropriate attention.

---

## 23. Corrective Action Planning

A corrective-action plan should establish what needs to be done and who is responsible.

A practical action record may include:

| Element           | Description                 |
| ----------------- | --------------------------- |
| Finding           | Identified nonconformity    |
| Cause             | Identified underlying cause |
| Correction        | Immediate response          |
| Corrective action | Action addressing the cause |
| Owner             | Responsible person          |
| Target date       | Planned completion          |
| Evidence          | Proof of implementation     |
| Verification      | Effectiveness assessment    |

This creates accountability throughout the corrective-action lifecycle.

---

## 24. Corrective Action Ownership

Each corrective action should have a clearly identified owner.

The owner should have sufficient:

* Authority.
* Resources.
* Competence.
* Access.
* Accountability.

Assigning responsibility to a person or function without sufficient authority can delay resolution.

---

## 25. Corrective Action Timeframes

Corrective actions should be assigned appropriate target dates.

Timeframes may depend on:

* Severity.
* Risk.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Regulatory significance.
* Complexity.
* Resource requirements.

High-risk issues may require immediate containment followed by a structured permanent corrective action.

---

## 26. Corrective Action Implementation

Implementation should produce evidence that the planned action has actually occurred.

Evidence may include:

* Revised procedures.
* Updated systems.
* New controls.
* Training records.
* Approval records.
* Monitoring results.
* Updated governance documentation.

An action should not be considered implemented solely because someone states that it has been completed.

---

## 27. Corrective Action Effectiveness

After implementation, the organization should determine whether the corrective action was effective.

Effectiveness may be evaluated through:

* Re-testing.
* Monitoring.
* Follow-up audits.
* Trend analysis.
* Incident reduction.
* Control testing.
* Stakeholder feedback.

The key question is:

**Has the underlying problem been sufficiently addressed to reduce the likelihood of recurrence?**
