# 04.17 — Nonconformity and Corrective Action

## 1. Introduction to Nonconformity and Corrective Action

Nonconformity and corrective action provide a structured mechanism for addressing failures within the AI Management System (AIMS).

A nonconformity indicates that a requirement has not been fulfilled. Corrective action addresses the underlying cause of the nonconformity so that the problem can be controlled and recurrence reduced.

The fundamental relationship is:

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Actual Condition
     ↓
Deviation
     ↓
Nonconformity
     ↓
Cause Analysis
     ↓
Corrective Action
     ↓
Effectiveness Verification
```

This process transforms identified weaknesses into opportunities for strengthening the AIMS.

---

## 2. Understanding Nonconformity

A nonconformity occurs when an applicable requirement is not fulfilled.

Within an AIMS, requirements may originate from:

* ISO/IEC 42001.
* Organizational policies.
* Internal procedures.
* AI governance requirements.
* Legal or regulatory obligations.
* Contractual requirements.
* Defined controls.
* Approved organizational criteria.

The existence of a problem does not automatically establish a nonconformity; the condition should be evaluated against a defined requirement.

---

## 3. Sources of Nonconformity

Nonconformities may be identified through multiple sources.

Examples include:

* Internal audits.
* External audits.
* Certification assessments.
* Monitoring.
* Management review.
* AI impact assessments.
* Risk assessments.
* Incidents.
* Complaints.
* Testing.
* Employee reports.
* Supplier assessments.

A mature AIMS should provide multiple mechanisms for identifying deviations.

---

## 4. Nonconformity and AI Governance

AI governance can involve complex interactions between people, processes, technologies and third parties.

Nonconformities may therefore occur in areas such as:

* AI governance.
* Risk management.
* AI impact assessment.
* Data management.
* Human oversight.
* AI security.
* AI supplier management.
* Monitoring.
* Documentation.
* Incident management.

Effective corrective action helps ensure that these weaknesses do not remain isolated operational problems.

---

## 5. Examples of AIMS Nonconformity

Examples may include:

* A required AI risk assessment was not performed.
* An approved procedure was not followed.
* Required evidence was unavailable.
* An assigned control was not implemented.
* A required review was not completed.
* A corrective action was implemented but not verified.
* Required responsibilities were not assigned.

The finding should be supported by objective evidence.

---

## 6. Requirement-Based Evaluation

Nonconformity should be established through comparison between a requirement and the actual condition.

A useful structure is:

```text
Requirement:
What should happen?

Evidence:
What actually happened?

Gap:
What is different?

Conclusion:
Does the gap constitute a nonconformity?
```

This approach helps maintain objectivity and prevents findings from being based solely on personal expectations.

---

## 7. Objective Evidence

Objective evidence is essential when identifying nonconformity.

Evidence may include:

* Records.
* System logs.
* Approved documents.
* Audit observations.
* Assessment results.
* Interviews supported by other evidence.
* Configuration information.
* Monitoring results.

Evidence should be sufficient to support the finding.

---

## 8. Documenting a Nonconformity

A nonconformity should be documented clearly enough that another qualified person can understand:

* What requirement applies.
* What evidence was found.
* What condition was observed.
* Why the condition does not satisfy the requirement.

A strong finding avoids vague statements such as:

> "AI governance is insufficient."

Instead, the finding should describe the specific requirement, evidence and observed deviation.

---

## 9. Immediate Correction

When a nonconformity is identified, the organization may take immediate correction to address the observed condition.

For example:

```text
Missing Approval
      ↓
Approval Obtained
      ↓
Immediate Condition Corrected
```

Immediate correction addresses the current problem.

It does not necessarily address why the problem occurred.

---

## 10. Correction Versus Corrective Action

Correction and corrective action are different concepts.

| Correction                              | Corrective Action           |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Addresses the immediate condition       | Addresses the cause         |
| Fixes the identified problem            | Seeks to prevent recurrence |
| Usually short-term                      | Usually longer-term         |
| Does not necessarily address root cause | Requires analysis of cause  |

For example, replacing a missing AI assessment may be a correction. Changing the governance process that caused assessments to be missed may be corrective action.

---

## 11. Containment

Containment may be necessary when a nonconformity creates an ongoing risk.

Containment may involve:

* Restricting system use.
* Suspending a process.
* Applying temporary controls.
* Increasing monitoring.
* Blocking a deployment.
* Limiting access.

Containment reduces exposure while a permanent solution is developed.

---

## 12. Evaluating the Significance of Nonconformity

The organization should consider the significance of a nonconformity.

Relevant factors may include:

* AI risk.
* Potential stakeholder impact.
* Regulatory significance.
* Security implications.
* Privacy implications.
* Scope of affected processes.
* Recurrence.
* Duration.

The response should be proportionate to the significance of the issue.

---

## 13. Major and Minor Nonconformities

Organizations and external assessment bodies may classify nonconformities according to their significance.

A major nonconformity may involve:

* A significant management-system failure.
* A systemic weakness.
* Failure to fulfill an important requirement.
* A condition that significantly affects the effectiveness of the AIMS.

A minor nonconformity may represent a more limited deviation that does not indicate a systemic failure.

Classification criteria should be defined and applied consistently.

---

## 14. Systemic Nonconformity

A systemic nonconformity extends beyond an isolated incident.

Indicators may include:

* Similar failures across multiple AI systems.
* Repeated findings.
* Common process weaknesses.
* Organization-wide control gaps.
* Repeated failure to implement required processes.

Systemic nonconformities require broader investigation because correcting one instance may not resolve the underlying organizational problem.

---

## 15. Recurring Nonconformity

A recurring nonconformity occurs when a previously identified problem returns.

Recurrence may indicate:

* Ineffective corrective action.
* Incorrect root-cause analysis.
* Insufficient resources.
* Weak accountability.
* Poor control design.
* Inadequate monitoring.

Recurring issues should receive appropriate management attention.

---

## 16. Root Cause

Root cause analysis seeks to determine why the nonconformity occurred.

Possible causes may include:

* Inadequate processes.
* Unclear responsibilities.
* Insufficient competence.
* Poor communication.
* Inadequate resources.
* Weak system design.
* Ineffective controls.
* Inadequate management oversight.

The objective is to identify causes that can be addressed rather than simply describing the immediate symptom.

---

## 17. Root Cause Versus Symptom

A symptom describes what went wrong.

A root cause explains why the condition occurred.

For example:

```text
Symptom:
AI impact assessment was not completed.

Possible Immediate Cause:
Project team did not perform the assessment.

Possible Root Cause:
The project governance workflow did not contain a mandatory
assessment checkpoint before deployment.
```

Corrective action should address the underlying cause rather than only the visible symptom.

---

## 18. Root Cause Analysis Techniques

Organizations may use different techniques to analyze causes.

Common approaches include:

* Five Whys.
* Cause-and-effect analysis.
* Fault-tree analysis.
* Process analysis.
* Barrier analysis.
* Event analysis.

The technique should be proportionate to the complexity and significance of the nonconformity.

---

## 19. Five Whys

The Five Whys technique repeatedly asks why a condition occurred.

Example:

```text
Why was the AI assessment missing?
        ↓
The project team did not complete it.

Why?
        ↓
The assessment requirement was not triggered.

Why?
        ↓
The project workflow did not contain an AI assessment checkpoint.

Why?
        ↓
AI governance requirements were not integrated into project governance.

Why?
        ↓
The organization's AI governance framework had not been integrated
with the enterprise project-management process.
```

This can reveal a systemic process weakness rather than simply blaming an individual.

---

## 20. Cause-and-Effect Analysis

Cause-and-effect analysis organizes potential causes into categories.

For an AIMS nonconformity, categories might include:

* People.
* Process.
* Technology.
* Data.
* Governance.
* Resources.
* Communication.
* External dependencies.

This approach can be useful when multiple factors contribute to the same problem.

---

## 21. Corrective Action

Corrective action is action taken to eliminate the cause of a nonconformity and prevent recurrence.

Examples include:

* Revising a governance process.
* Introducing mandatory approval checkpoints.
* Improving training.
* Redesigning controls.
* Automating evidence collection.
* Clarifying responsibilities.
* Strengthening monitoring.
* Changing supplier requirements.

The corrective action should correspond to the identified cause.

---

## 22. Risk-Based Corrective Action

Corrective actions should reflect the significance of the associated risk.

Higher-risk issues may require:

* Faster action.
* Stronger containment.
* Greater management involvement.
* Additional resources.
* More extensive verification.

Risk-based prioritization helps ensure that serious AIMS weaknesses receive appropriate attention.

---

## 23. Corrective Action Planning

A corrective-action plan should establish what needs to be done and who is responsible.

A practical action record may include:

| Element           | Description                 |
| ----------------- | --------------------------- |
| Finding           | Identified nonconformity    |
| Cause             | Identified underlying cause |
| Correction        | Immediate response          |
| Corrective action | Action addressing the cause |
| Owner             | Responsible person          |
| Target date       | Planned completion          |
| Evidence          | Proof of implementation     |
| Verification      | Effectiveness assessment    |

This creates accountability throughout the corrective-action lifecycle.

---

## 24. Corrective Action Ownership

Each corrective action should have a clearly identified owner.

The owner should have sufficient:

* Authority.
* Resources.
* Competence.
* Access.
* Accountability.

Assigning responsibility to a person or function without sufficient authority can delay resolution.

---

## 25. Corrective Action Timeframes

Corrective actions should be assigned appropriate target dates.

Timeframes may depend on:

* Severity.
* Risk.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Regulatory significance.
* Complexity.
* Resource requirements.

High-risk issues may require immediate containment followed by a structured permanent corrective action.

---

## 26. Corrective Action Implementation

Implementation should produce evidence that the planned action has actually occurred.

Evidence may include:

* Revised procedures.
* Updated systems.
* New controls.
* Training records.
* Approval records.
* Monitoring results.
* Updated governance documentation.

An action should not be considered implemented solely because someone states that it has been completed.

---

## 27. Corrective Action Effectiveness

After implementation, the organization should determine whether the corrective action was effective.

Effectiveness may be evaluated through:

* Re-testing.
* Monitoring.
* Follow-up audits.
* Trend analysis.
* Incident reduction.
* Control testing.
* Stakeholder feedback.

The key question is:

**Has the underlying problem been sufficiently addressed to reduce the likelihood of recurrence?**

## 28. Corrective Action Verification

Corrective action verification determines whether the action was implemented as planned and whether sufficient evidence exists to support closure.

Verification may include:

* Reviewing implementation records.
* Testing revised controls.
* Examining updated procedures.
* Reviewing monitoring results.
* Conducting follow-up interviews.
* Performing a follow-up audit.

Verification should be based on objective evidence.

---

## 29. Effectiveness Versus Completion

Completion of a corrective action does not automatically mean that it was effective.

For example:

```text
Action Completed
      ↓
Implementation Confirmed
      ↓
Effectiveness Tested
      ↓
Problem Resolved?
      ↓
Recurrence Reduced?
```

An organization should distinguish between **"the action was completed"** and **"the action achieved its intended result."**

---

## 30. Corrective Action Closure

A corrective action may be closed when appropriate evidence demonstrates that:

* The planned action was implemented.
* The identified cause was addressed.
* Required evidence exists.
* Effectiveness was appropriately evaluated.
* Remaining risk is acceptable.

Closure criteria should be defined according to organizational requirements.

---

## 31. Residual Risk After Corrective Action

Corrective action may reduce risk without completely eliminating it.

Management may therefore need to consider:

* Remaining risk.
* Existing controls.
* Risk acceptance.
* Additional treatment.
* Monitoring requirements.

Corrective action should not be assumed to eliminate all associated AI risk.

---

## 32. Inadequate Corrective Action

Corrective action may be considered inadequate when it:

* Addresses only the symptom.
* Does not address the root cause.
* Is not implemented.
* Produces insufficient evidence.
* Does not reduce recurrence.
* Creates another significant weakness.

In such circumstances, additional corrective action may be required.

---

## 33. Reopening a Closed Finding

A previously closed finding may need to be reopened if evidence demonstrates that:

* The issue has recurred.
* The corrective action was ineffective.
* The original root cause was incorrectly identified.
* The control remains ineffective.
* New evidence changes the assessment.

Finding closure should therefore not prevent future reassessment.

---

## 34. Corrective Action Escalation

Significant or overdue corrective actions may require escalation.

Escalation may be appropriate where:

* Risk remains high.
* Deadlines are repeatedly missed.
* The issue is systemic.
* Management attention is required.
* The issue affects multiple AI systems.
* Stakeholder impact is significant.

Escalation should follow established governance arrangements.

---

## 35. Corrective Action and Management Review

Significant nonconformities and corrective actions should provide relevant inputs to management review.

Management may evaluate:

* Significant findings.
* Recurring issues.
* Corrective-action effectiveness.
* Resource requirements.
* Systemic weaknesses.
* Improvement priorities.

This connects operational problem resolution with executive oversight.

## 38. Corrective Action and AI Impact

This may include:

* Updating the AI impact assessment.
* Reassessing affected stakeholders.
* Reviewing mitigation measures.
* Evaluating residual impact.
* Updating controls.

Significant changes should be reflected in relevant AIMS documentation.

* Insufficient logging.

Security corrective actions should be integrated with the organization's cybersecurity processes where appropriate.

## 45. Corrective Action and Change Management

Where technical or operational changes are necessary, the organization may need to evaluate:

* Change impact.
* Testing.
* Approval.
* Security implications.
* AI risk.
* AI impact.

## 47. Corrective Action and Training

Where lack of competence contributes to a nonconformity, corrective action may include:

* Training.
* Awareness.
* Role-specific education.
* Competence assessment.
* Updated guidance.

Training alone should not automatically be treated as an adequate corrective action if the underlying problem is actually a process or control weakness.

## 48. Corrective Action and Automation

Automation may be appropriate where recurring human-process failures contribute to nonconformity.

Examples include:

* Automated approval gates.
* Mandatory assessment workflows.
* Automated evidence collection.
* Automated notifications.
* Compliance checks.
* Monitoring alerts.

Automation should address the identified cause rather than simply add technological complexity.

---

## 49. Corrective Action and Control Redesign

Sometimes an existing control is ineffective and requires redesign.

Control redesign may involve:

* Changing control ownership.
* Increasing control frequency.
* Adding approval requirements.
* Introducing automated validation.
* Improving monitoring.
* Separating conflicting responsibilities.

The redesigned control should subsequently be tested for effectiveness.

---

## 50. Corrective Action and Preventive Thinking

Corrective action focuses on identified nonconformities, but the organization can also examine whether similar weaknesses exist elsewhere.

For example:

```text
Finding in AI System A
        ↓
Root Cause Identified
        ↓
Search for Similar Conditions
        ↓
AI Systems B, C and D
        ↓
Preventive Improvements
```

This helps prevent isolated findings from becoming repeated organizational problems.

---

## 51. Horizontal Review

A horizontal review examines whether a finding identified in one area may exist in other areas.

It may examine:

* Other AI systems.
* Other business units.
* Other suppliers.
* Other processes.
* Other geographic locations.

Horizontal review is particularly useful when the root cause involves a common governance process.

---

## 52. Corrective Action Scope

The scope of corrective action should be proportionate to the identified cause and potential impact.

A narrow problem may require a localized correction.

A systemic problem may require:

* Organization-wide process changes.
* Governance changes.
* Technology changes.
* Training.
* Additional oversight.

Corrective action should neither be unnecessarily narrow nor disproportionately broad.

---

## 53. Corrective Action Prioritization

Prioritization may consider:

* Severity.

* Business criticality.
* Recurrence.

# 04.17 — Nonconformity and Corrective Action

## 61. Determining the Appropriate Corrective Action

The corrective action selected should be directly connected to the nature and cause of the nonconformity.

Management should consider:

* The significance of the nonconformity.
* The associated AI risk.
* Potential stakeholder impact.
* Root cause.
* Recurrence potential.
* Available controls.
* Resource requirements.

The objective is to select an action that is effective and proportionate.

---

## 62. Corrective Action Hierarchy

Corrective actions can range from simple process corrections to broader governance changes.

A practical hierarchy is:

```text id="a6z5pf"
Immediate Correction
        ↓
Containment
        ↓
Root Cause Analysis
        ↓
Corrective Action
        ↓
Control Improvement
        ↓
Systemic Improvement
```

Not every nonconformity requires every level, but significant issues may require multiple layers of response.

---

## 63. Addressing the Root Cause

Corrective action should target the underlying cause rather than merely the visible failure.

For example, if an AI risk assessment was repeatedly missed, simply reminding employees to complete the assessment may not be sufficient.

A stronger response could involve:

* Integrating the assessment into project approval.
* Assigning explicit ownership.
* Adding mandatory workflow controls.
* Introducing system validation.
* Monitoring compliance.

The corrective action should make recurrence less likely.

---

## 64. Multiple Root Causes

Some nonconformities have more than one contributing cause.

For example:

```text id="2p9q4b"
Nonconformity
      ↓
Process Weakness
      +
Competence Gap
      +
Unclear Ownership
      +
Technology Limitation
```

Corrective action may therefore require several coordinated measures.

---

## 65. Contributing Factors

Not every contributing factor is necessarily a root cause.

Contributing factors may include:

* Workload.
* Communication failures.
* Time pressure.
* System limitations.
* Organizational changes.
* Supplier dependencies.

These factors should be considered when determining the appropriate response.

---

## 66. Avoiding Individual Blame

Corrective-action processes should focus primarily on improving the management system rather than assigning personal blame.

A failure by an individual may reveal a broader weakness in:

* Training.
* Process design.
* Workload.
* Supervision.
* System controls.
* Responsibility assignment.

The objective is organizational learning and risk reduction.

---

## 67. Corrective Action for Human Error

Human error should be evaluated carefully.

Possible responses include:

* Training.
* Clearer procedures.
* Improved interfaces.
* Automated controls.
* Additional verification.
* Segregation of duties.

Simply instructing employees to "be more careful" is generally a weak systemic response.

---

## 68. Corrective Action for Process Failure

When the process itself causes a nonconformity, corrective action may involve:

* Process redesign.
* Additional approval steps.
* Removal of unnecessary steps.
* Clearer responsibilities.
* Automated workflow.
* Updated procedures.

Process-level corrective action can reduce repeated operational failures.

---

## 69. Corrective Action for Governance Failure

Governance-related nonconformities may require changes to:

* Decision authority.
* Accountability.
* Policies.
* Committees.
* Escalation mechanisms.
* Reporting.
* Oversight.

Governance failures may require senior-management involvement.

---

## 70. Corrective Action for Control Failure

Where a control is ineffective, the organization should determine whether the problem concerns:

* Control design.
* Control implementation.
* Control operation.
* Control ownership.
* Control frequency.
* Control monitoring.

Corrective action should address the relevant failure mode.

---

## 71. Control Design Failure

A control-design failure occurs when the control itself is insufficient to address the relevant risk.

For example:

```text id="h3q6bw"
Risk
 ↓
Control
 ↓
Control Cannot Adequately Address Risk
 ↓
Design Weakness
```

Corrective action may require redesigning the control rather than simply improving execution.

---

## 72. Control Implementation Failure

A control may be appropriately designed but not implemented.

Examples include:

* Required monitoring was never configured.
* Required approval was not established.
* Required access restrictions were not deployed.

Corrective action should address the implementation gap.

---

## 73. Control Operating Failure

A control may exist and be implemented but fail during operation.

Examples include:

* Monitoring not performed consistently.
* Reviews completed incorrectly.
* Alerts ignored.
* Evidence not retained.

Corrective action should address why the control is not operating as intended.

---

## 74. Control Monitoring Failure

An organization may have effective controls but insufficient monitoring of those controls.

This can prevent management from detecting control deterioration.

Corrective action may involve:

* Control testing.
* Automated monitoring.
* Performance indicators.
* Periodic reviews.
* Independent assurance.

---

## 120. Nonconformity and Corrective Action Governance Principle

Effective nonconformity management is not simply about fixing what went wrong.

It is about understanding **why the failure occurred, determining whether the failure could exist elsewhere, implementing proportionate corrective action, verifying effectiveness, and using the resulting knowledge to strengthen the AIMS.**

The core principle is:

**Identify → Correct → Analyze → Act → Verify → Learn → Improve.**
