# 04.19 — Annex A Controls and Statement of Applicability

## 1. Introduction to Annex A

Annex A of ISO/IEC 42001 provides a reference set of controls that organizations can consider when establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

The Annex A controls are designed to help organizations address important areas of AI governance, including:

* AI policies.
* Internal organization.
* Resources for AI systems.
* Assessments of AI impacts.
* AI system lifecycle management.
* AI data management.
* Information for interested parties.
* Use of AI systems.
* Third-party and customer relationships.

Annex A should be understood as an important control framework within ISO/IEC 42001 rather than as a standalone checklist.

---

## 2. Purpose of Annex A

The purpose of Annex A is to provide organizations with a structured set of AI-specific controls that can support the treatment of identified risks and achievement of AIMS objectives.

The controls can help organizations establish governance over areas such as:

* AI system development.
* AI system deployment.
* AI system operation.
* AI system monitoring.
* AI system use.
* AI-related data.
* Third-party AI services.
* Stakeholder communication.

The organization determines which controls are applicable based on its own context, risks, objectives and requirements.

---

## 3. Annex A and the AIMS

Annex A should not be treated as the entire AIMS.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
Organizational Context
        ↓
AI Risks & Opportunities
        ↓
AIMS Objectives
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
Annex A Controls
        ↓
Implementation
        ↓
Monitoring & Assurance
        ↓
Continual Improvement
```

The management system provides the governance structure, while the controls provide mechanisms for addressing relevant AI governance needs.

---

## 4. Annex A Is Risk-Based

Organizations should not automatically implement every Annex A control in exactly the same way.

Control applicability should be evaluated based on factors such as:

* Organizational context.
* AI use cases.
* AI risks.
* Legal requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* AI system lifecycle.
* Organizational objectives.

A control that is highly relevant to one organization may be less relevant to another.

---

## 5. Annex A and Risk Treatment

Annex A controls can support the organization's risk-treatment process.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
AI Risk
   ↓
Risk Assessment
   ↓
Risk Treatment Decision
   ↓
Applicable Control
   ↓
Implementation
   ↓
Residual Risk
```

The selected control should have a clear relationship with the risk or requirement it is intended to address.

---

## 6. Annex A and Organizational Objectives

Control selection should support organizational objectives rather than become an isolated compliance exercise.

For example, an organization deploying customer-facing AI may need controls addressing:

* AI system transparency.
* User information.
* Human oversight.
* AI performance.
* Data governance.
* Incident management.

The controls should help the organization achieve responsible AI outcomes while supporting its business objectives.

---

## 7. Annex A Control Groups

Annex A is organized into control objectives and controls covering different areas of AI governance.

The control areas address subjects including:

1. **Policies related to AI**
2. **Internal organization**
3. **Resources for AI systems**
4. **Assessing impacts of AI systems**
5. **AI system life cycle**
6. **AI data for AI systems**
7. **Information for interested parties**
8. **Use of AI systems**
9. **Third-party and customer relationships**

These areas provide a structured way of considering AI governance across the organization and AI lifecycle.

---

## 8. Policies Related to AI

AI policies establish organizational direction and expectations for the responsible management and use of AI.

Policies may establish principles concerning:

* Acceptable AI use.
* AI governance.
* AI risk management.
* AI system development.
* AI system operation.
* Human oversight.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Accountability.

Policies should be appropriate to the organization's AI activities and risk environment.

---

## 9. Internal Organization

Effective AI governance requires appropriate organizational structures and responsibilities.

This area can include:

* Roles and responsibilities.
* Accountability.
* Segregation of duties.
* Governance structures.
* Coordination between functions.
* AI-related decision authority.

Responsibilities should be sufficiently clear to prevent important AI governance activities from falling between organizational functions.

---

## 10. Resources for AI Systems

AI governance requires appropriate resources.

Resources may include:

* Personnel.
* Competence.
* Data.
* Computing infrastructure.
* Technology.
* Tools.
* Documentation.
* Financial resources.

Management should ensure that the resources required to manage AI risks are appropriate to the organization's circumstances.

---

## 11. Assessing Impacts of AI Systems

AI systems can create impacts beyond conventional cybersecurity or technology risks.

Impact assessment may consider:

* Individuals.
* Groups.
* Organizations.
* Society.
* Safety.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Fairness.
* Other relevant consequences.

Impact assessment helps the organization understand how AI systems may affect interested parties and determine appropriate governance measures.

---

Controls should be applied at appropriate lifecycle stages based on the organization's context and risk.

---

## 13. AI Data

Data can significantly influence AI system performance and risk.

AI data governance may address:

* Data quality.
* Data provenance.
* Data preparation.
* Data suitability.
* Data access.
* Data protection.
* Data management.
* Data lifecycle.

The organization should establish appropriate controls based on how data is acquired, processed and used.

---

## 14. Information for Interested Parties

Relevant interested parties may require appropriate information about AI systems.

Information may concern:

* AI system purpose.
* AI use.
* Responsibilities.
* Relevant risks.
* Limitations.
* Human involvement.
* Appropriate instructions.

The type and extent of information should reflect the system, context and applicable requirements.

---

## 15. Use of AI Systems

AI systems should be used consistently with their intended purpose and established governance requirements.

Controls may address:

* Authorized use.
* User responsibilities.
* Human oversight.
* Monitoring.
* Restrictions.
* Escalation.
* Appropriate use.

Governance should continue after an AI system has been deployed.

## 16. Third-Party and Customer Relationships

Organizations may rely on external parties for AI capabilities.

Examples include:

* AI model providers.
* Cloud providers.
* AI SaaS platforms.
* Data providers.

Annex A controls should be considered across the AI lifecycle rather than only during implementation.

For example:

---

## 18. Annex A and Other ISO/IEC 42001 Requirements

Annex A should be considered together with the broader requirements of ISO/IEC 42001.

The standard establishes requirements for the management system, while Annex A provides a set of controls that can support risk treatment and governance objectives.

Therefore:

**Conformity is not demonstrated simply by claiming that Annex A controls exist.**

The organization needs to demonstrate that its AIMS has been appropriately established, implemented, maintained and continually improved.

---

## 19. Annex A and the Statement of Applicability

The **Statement of Applicability (SoA)** is a key mechanism for documenting the organization's treatment of Annex A controls.

The SoA should provide a structured explanation of:

* Which controls are applicable.
* Which controls are not applicable.
* Why controls are included.
* Why controls are excluded where appropriate.
* How applicable controls are addressed.
* The implementation status of relevant controls.

The SoA therefore provides traceability between the organization's AI risks, requirements and control decisions.

---

## 20. Annex A Is Not a Simple Checklist

A weak approach may look like:

```text
Control 1 → Yes
Control 2 → Yes
Control 3 → Yes
Control 4 → Yes
```

A stronger approach asks:

```text
What risk or requirement does this control address?
        ↓
Why is it applicable?
        ↓
How is it implemented?
        ↓
Who owns it?
        ↓
What evidence demonstrates implementation?
        ↓
How is effectiveness evaluated?
```

This distinction is important because an audit should be able to establish not merely that a control was selected, but that the organization has a defensible reason for its selection and an effective mechanism for implementing it.

---

## 21. Control Applicability

A control may be considered applicable when it is relevant to:

* An identified AI risk.
* An AI impact.
* A legal requirement.
* A contractual requirement.
* A stakeholder requirement.
* An organizational objective.
* An identified governance need.

Applicability should be supported by documented reasoning.

---

## 22. Control Exclusions

Not every Annex A control will necessarily apply to every organization or every AI environment.

Where a control is determined to be not applicable, the organization should have a defensible justification.

For example, an organization that does not perform a particular AI lifecycle activity internally may have a different control applicability rationale from an organization that develops and trains its own models.

Exclusions should be based on actual organizational circumstances rather than convenience.

---

## 23. Control Implementation

Selecting a control does not automatically mean that the control has been implemented.

Implementation may involve:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Technical mechanisms.
* Governance processes.
* Assigned responsibilities.
* Training.
* Monitoring.
* Evidence.

The organization should be able to demonstrate how the control operates in practice.

---

## 24. Control Ownership

Applicable controls should have appropriate ownership.

A control owner may be responsible for:

* Maintaining the control.
* Ensuring implementation.
* Providing evidence.
* Monitoring performance.
* Addressing deficiencies.
* Supporting audits.

Ownership should be aligned with actual authority and responsibility.

---

## 25. Control Evidence

Evidence supports the organization's ability to demonstrate that applicable controls are implemented and operating as intended.

Examples may include:

* Approved policies.
* Procedures.
* Risk assessments.
* AI impact assessments.
* System records.
* Monitoring reports.
* Training records.
* Audit results.
* Meeting decisions.
* Supplier assessments.
* Technical configurations.

Evidence should be reliable, relevant and appropriately maintained.

---

## 26. Control Effectiveness

A control can exist without being effective.

Management should therefore consider whether controls:

* Operate consistently.
* Address the intended risk.
* Produce the intended outcome.
* Are monitored.
* Remain appropriate as circumstances change.

Control effectiveness should be evaluated using appropriate evidence and assurance activities.

---

## 27. Annex A and Governance Accountability

Annex A supports a broader principle:

> **AI governance should be translated from organizational intent into accountable, implemented and demonstrable practices.**

Policies establish direction.

Risks establish priorities.

Controls establish mechanisms.

Owners establish accountability.

Evidence demonstrates implementation.

Monitoring evaluates performance.

Continual improvement strengthens the overall system.

This creates a connection between governance strategy and operational execution.
