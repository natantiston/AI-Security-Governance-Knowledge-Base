# 04.19 — Annex A Controls and Statement of Applicability

## 1. Introduction to Annex A

Annex A of ISO/IEC 42001 provides a reference set of controls that organizations can consider when establishing, implementing, maintaining and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

The Annex A controls are designed to help organizations address important areas of AI governance, including:

* AI policies.
* Internal organization.
* Resources for AI systems.
* Assessments of AI impacts.
* AI system lifecycle management.
* AI data management.
* Information for interested parties.
* Use of AI systems.
* Third-party and customer relationships.

Annex A should be understood as an important control framework within ISO/IEC 42001 rather than as a standalone checklist.

---

## 2. Purpose of Annex A

The purpose of Annex A is to provide organizations with a structured set of AI-specific controls that can support the treatment of identified risks and achievement of AIMS objectives.

The controls can help organizations establish governance over areas such as:

* AI system development.
* AI system deployment.
* AI system operation.
* AI system monitoring.
* AI system use.
* AI-related data.
* Third-party AI services.
* Stakeholder communication.

The organization determines which controls are applicable based on its own context, risks, objectives and requirements.

---

## 3. Annex A and the AIMS

Annex A should not be treated as the entire AIMS.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text
Organizational Context
        ↓
AI Risks & Opportunities
        ↓
AIMS Objectives
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
Annex A Controls
        ↓
Implementation
        ↓
Monitoring & Assurance
        ↓
Continual Improvement
```

The management system provides the governance structure, while the controls provide mechanisms for addressing relevant AI governance needs.

---

## 4. Annex A Is Risk-Based

Organizations should not automatically implement every Annex A control in exactly the same way.

Control applicability should be evaluated based on factors such as:

* Organizational context.
* AI use cases.
* AI risks.
* Legal requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* AI system lifecycle.
* Organizational objectives.

A control that is highly relevant to one organization may be less relevant to another.

---

## 5. Annex A and Risk Treatment

```text
   ↓

   ↓

```

The selected control should have a clear relationship with the risk or requirement it is intended to address.

---

## 6. Annex A and Organizational Objectives

Control selection should support organizational objectives rather than become an isolated compliance exercise.

For example, an organization deploying customer-facing AI may need controls addressing:

* AI system transparency.
* User information.
* Human oversight.
* AI performance.
* Data governance.
* Incident management.

The controls should help the organization achieve responsible AI outcomes while supporting its business objectives.

---

## 7. Annex A Control Groups

Annex A is organized into control objectives and controls covering different areas of AI governance.

The control areas address subjects including:

1. **Policies related to AI**
2. **Internal organization**
3. **Resources for AI systems**
4. **Assessing impacts of AI systems**
5. **AI system life cycle**
6. **AI data for AI systems**
7. **Information for interested parties**
8. **Use of AI systems**
9. **Third-party and customer relationships**

These areas provide a structured way of considering AI governance across the organization and AI lifecycle.

## 18. Annex A and Other ISO/IEC 42001 Requirements

Annex A should be considered together with the broader requirements of ISO/IEC 42001.

The standard establishes requirements for the management system, while Annex A provides a set of controls that can support risk treatment and governance objectives.

Therefore:

**Conformity is not demonstrated simply by claiming that Annex A controls exist.**

The organization needs to demonstrate that its AIMS has been appropriately established, implemented, maintained and continually improved.

---

## 19. Annex A and the Statement of Applicability

The **Statement of Applicability (SoA)** is a key mechanism for documenting the organization's treatment of Annex A controls.

The SoA should provide a structured explanation of:

* Which controls are applicable.
* Which controls are not applicable.
* Why controls are included.
* Why controls are excluded where appropriate.
* How applicable controls are addressed.
* The implementation status of relevant controls.

The SoA therefore provides traceability between the organization's AI risks, requirements and control decisions.

---

## 20. Annex A Is Not a Simple Checklist

A weak approach may look like:

```text
Control 1 → Yes
Control 2 → Yes
Control 3 → Yes
Control 4 → Yes
```

A stronger approach asks:

```text
What risk or requirement does this control address?
        ↓
Why is it applicable?
        ↓
How is it implemented?
        ↓
Who owns it?
        ↓
What evidence demonstrates implementation?
        ↓
How is effectiveness evaluated?
```

This distinction is important because an audit should be able to establish not merely that a control was selected, but that the organization has a defensible reason for its selection and an effective mechanism for implementing it.

---

## 21. Control Applicability

A control may be considered applicable when it is relevant to:

* An identified AI risk.
* An AI impact.
* A legal requirement.
* A contractual requirement.
* A stakeholder requirement.
* An organizational objective.
* An identified governance need.

Applicability should be supported by documented reasoning.

---

## 22. Control Exclusions

Not every Annex A control will necessarily apply to every organization or every AI environment.

Where a control is determined to be not applicable, the organization should have a defensible justification.

For example, an organization that does not perform a particular AI lifecycle activity internally may have a different control applicability rationale from an organization that develops and trains its own models.

Exclusions should be based on actual organizational circumstances rather than convenience.

---

## 23. Control Implementation

Selecting a control does not automatically mean that the control has been implemented.

Implementation may involve:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Technical mechanisms.
* Governance processes.
* Assigned responsibilities.
* Training.
* Monitoring.
* Evidence.

The organization should be able to demonstrate how the control operates in practice.

---

## 24. Control Ownership

Applicable controls should have appropriate ownership.

A control owner may be responsible for:

* Maintaining the control.
* Ensuring implementation.
* Providing evidence.
* Monitoring performance.
* Addressing deficiencies.
* Supporting audits.

Ownership should be aligned with actual authority and responsibility.

---

## 25. Control Evidence

Evidence supports the organization's ability to demonstrate that applicable controls are implemented and operating as intended.

Examples may include:

* Approved policies.
* Procedures.
* Risk assessments.
* AI impact assessments.
* System records.
* Monitoring reports.
* Training records.
* Audit results.
* Meeting decisions.
* Supplier assessments.
* Technical configurations.

Evidence should be reliable, relevant and appropriately maintained.

---

## 26. Control Effectiveness

A control can exist without being effective.

Management should therefore consider whether controls:

* Operate consistently.
* Address the intended risk.
* Produce the intended outcome.
* Are monitored.
* Remain appropriate as circumstances change.

Control effectiveness should be evaluated using appropriate evidence and assurance activities.

---

## 27. Annex A and Governance Accountability

Annex A supports a broader principle:

> **AI governance should be translated from organizational intent into accountable, implemented and demonstrable practices.**

Policies establish direction.

Risks establish priorities.

Controls establish mechanisms.

Owners establish accountability.

Evidence demonstrates implementation.

Monitoring evaluates performance.

Continual improvement strengthens the overall system.

This creates a connection between governance strategy and operational execution.

## 28. Control Selection and Organizational Context

Annex A control selection should be based on the organization's specific circumstances rather than performed mechanically.

Relevant considerations include:

* The organization's size and structure.
* The nature and scale of AI activities.
* AI system types.
* AI system lifecycle responsibilities.
* Applicable legal and regulatory requirements.
* Contractual obligations.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* AI-related risks and opportunities.
* Internal capabilities.
* Use of third-party AI services.

The same control may have different implementation requirements depending on organizational context.

---

## 29. Control Selection and AI System Scope

The scope of the AIMS influences which Annex A controls are relevant.

For example, an organization that develops and trains AI models internally may require extensive controls around:

* Development.
* Data management.
* Testing.
* Validation.
* Model performance.

An organization that primarily consumes externally provided AI services may place greater emphasis on:

* Supplier governance.
* Contractual requirements.
* AI system use.
* Monitoring.
* User responsibilities.
* Third-party assurance.

Control selection should therefore reflect the actual scope of AI activities.

---

---

## 35. Control Selection and Organizational Objectives

Controls should support the organization's AI governance objectives.

For example, if an objective is to ensure responsible deployment of customer-facing AI, relevant controls may address:

* AI impact assessment.
* Human oversight.
* Information provided to users.
* Monitoring.
* Incident management.

Control selection should therefore be connected to organizational outcomes.

---

## 36. Control Selection and Risk Treatment Options

Organizations may use different approaches when treating AI risks.

Possible approaches include:

* Avoiding the activity.
* Reducing the risk.
* Sharing or transferring the risk.
* Accepting the residual risk.

Annex A controls are primarily considered where controls are appropriate to the chosen risk-treatment strategy.

---

## 37. Control Applicability Analysis

A structured applicability analysis can help ensure consistency.

For each control, the organization may ask:

1. Is the control relevant to the AIMS scope?
2. Does it address an identified risk?
3. Does it address a legal or contractual requirement?
4. Does it support an AIMS objective?
5. Does it address an identified AI impact?
6. Is there another reason the control is necessary?

The answers should be appropriately documented.

## 39. Statement of Applicability

The Statement of Applicability is a documented representation of the organization's decisions concerning relevant Annex A controls.

```text id="r8x3ny"
  ↓
```

---

## 40. Purpose of the Statement of Applicability

The SoA helps demonstrate that control decisions were made deliberately.

It can provide visibility into:

* Applicable controls.
* Non-applicable controls.
* Justifications.
* Implementation status.
* Control ownership.
* Related documentation.

It also provides a useful reference for internal governance and assurance activities.

---

## 41. SoA and Control Justification

Each applicability decision should have an appropriate rationale.

For an applicable control, the rationale may explain:

> The control is relevant because the organization develops and operates AI systems that require formal lifecycle governance.

For a non-applicable control, the rationale should explain the actual organizational circumstance supporting the exclusion.

The justification should be factual rather than generic.

---

## 42. Avoiding Generic Justifications

Weak justification:

> "Not applicable to our organization."

This does not provide sufficient context.

A stronger justification explains the organizational circumstance, such as:

> "The activity addressed by this control is outside the defined AIMS scope because the organization does not perform that lifecycle activity and has no responsibility for its execution."

The exact rationale should reflect the organization's actual circumstances.

---

## 43. SoA and Control Implementation Status

The SoA should distinguish between applicability and implementation.

For example:

```text id="h7v4ps"
Applicable
    ↓
Not Yet Implemented
    ↓
Implementation Planned
```

Therefore:

**Applicable does not necessarily mean fully implemented.**

An organization may have selected a control but still be working toward complete implementation.

## 44. Implementation Status Categories

* Implemented.

* Planned.

* Not implemented.

The organization should define the meaning of its selected status categories so that they are used consistently.

## 53. Partial Control Implementation

A control may be partially implemented when some required elements exist but others remain incomplete.

For example:

* Policy exists.
* Responsible owner exists.
* Operational process is incomplete.
* Evidence collection is inconsistent.

Such a control should not automatically be represented as fully implemented.

Accurate status reporting supports management transparency and audit readiness.

---

## 54. SoA and Risk Treatment Plans

Where control implementation is incomplete, the organization may connect the gap to a risk-treatment or improvement plan.

This may identify:

* Required action.
* Responsible owner.
* Target date.
* Resources.
* Dependencies.
* Expected outcome.

This links the SoA to actual AIMS improvement activity.

Management may use it to understand:

* Which controls are applicable.
* Which controls are implemented.
* Which controls remain incomplete.
* Where significant gaps exist.
* Where additional resources may be required.

The SoA therefore has value beyond external certification audits.

Control implementation should be proportionate to:

* AI risk.
* AI impact.
* System criticality.
* Organizational context.
* Applicable requirements.

The objective is not to maximize the number of controls but to establish an effective and defensible control environment.

A strong Annex A approach can be summarized as:

```text id="e2p6mk"

       ↓

       ↓
Evaluate Control Applicability
       ↓

       ↓
Implement Applicable Controls
       ↓
```

The fundamental principle is that **Annex A control selection should be deliberate, risk-informed, documented, implemented and supported by evidence**, with the Statement of Applicability providing a clear record of the organization's control decisions.

## 61. Statement of Applicability Structure

A well-designed Statement of Applicability should provide a clear and auditable view of the organization's Annex A control decisions.

A practical structure may include:

| Field                 | Purpose                                         |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Control Reference     | Identifies the relevant Annex A control         |
| Control Description   | Summarizes the control                          |
| Applicability         | Indicates whether the control applies           |
| Justification         | Explains the applicability decision             |
| Risk / Requirement    | Links the control to the relevant driver        |
| Owner                 | Identifies accountability                       |
| Implementation Status | Shows implementation maturity                   |
| Supporting Documents  | Identifies relevant policies or procedures      |
| Evidence              | Identifies implementation evidence              |
| Review Status         | Indicates whether the control has been reviewed |

The exact format may vary according to organizational needs.

---

## 62. Control Reference

Each Annex A control should be uniquely identifiable in the organization's SoA.

The reference allows the organization to connect the control with:

* Risk registers.
* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Control assessments.
* Audit findings.
* Evidence.
* Improvement plans.

Consistent identification improves traceability.

---

## 63. Control Description

The SoA should make it clear what the control is intended to achieve.

The organization may use the official control wording or an appropriately controlled summary.

The description should be sufficiently clear that management, control owners and auditors understand the subject of the control.

The organization should explicitly record whether a control is applicable.

A simple structure may be:

```text id="q2m8ks"
 └── No
```

The decision should be supported by an appropriate rationale.

* An AI lifecycle activity.

For a non-applicable control, the explanation should describe the specific organizational circumstance that makes the control outside the relevant scope.

## 92. Common SoA Weaknesses

Common weaknesses include:

* Generic applicability justifications.
* Controls marked applicable without evidence.
* Outdated implementation statuses.
* Unclear control ownership.
* No connection to risk assessment.
* No connection to impact assessment.
* Missing evidence.
* Stale documentation.
* Controls considered implemented solely because policies exist.
* SoA not updated after major organizational changes.

These weaknesses reduce the reliability of the SoA.

## 93. Common Control Selection Mistake

A common mistake is to begin with:

> "Which Annex A controls should we implement?"

A stronger approach begins with:

> "What does our organization need to manage, and which controls appropriately address those needs?"

The difference is important because ISO/IEC 42001 implementation should be driven by the organization's context, risks, impacts and requirements.

---

## 94. Common Exclusion Mistake

Another common mistake is excluding controls simply because they are inconvenient or difficult to implement.

A non-applicability decision should be based on an objective organizational rationale.

If a control is relevant but difficult to implement, difficulty alone does not make it non-applicable.

The organization should instead determine how the relevant requirement will be addressed.

---

## 95. Common Implementation Mistake

A further mistake is treating documentation as equivalent to implementation.

For example:

```text id="e8k5pw"
Policy Exists
      ≠
Control Effective
```

Implementation requires the control to operate in practice and produce appropriate evidence.

---

## 96. Common Evidence Mistake

Organizations may also collect large quantities of evidence without determining whether the evidence actually demonstrates control operation.

Effective evidence should answer:

> **What does this evidence prove about the control?**

Evidence should therefore be linked logically to the control objective and implementation.

---

## 97. Control Rationalization

Where several controls address related risks, organizations may consider how they can be implemented coherently.

For example, a single governance process may support multiple control objectives.

However, rationalization should not result in important requirements being overlooked.

The organization should demonstrate that all applicable control requirements are adequately addressed.

## 99. Control Environment Maturity

Organizations may progress through different levels of control maturity.

### Initial

Controls are largely reactive and inconsistently implemented.

### Developing

Responsibilities and procedures are being established.

### Defined

Controls are documented and consistently implemented.

### Managed

Control performance is measured and monitored.

### Optimized

Controls are continuously improved using evidence and risk information.

The maturity model should be used as an internal improvement concept rather than assumed to be an official ISO/IEC 42001 maturity scale.

## 121. Control Owner Interview Example

An assessor might ask:

> "Why is this control applicable to your organization?"

A strong answer should explain the organizational context and relevant risk or requirement.

The assessor may then ask:

> "How is the control implemented?"

The owner should explain the actual process.

The next question may be:

> "Show me evidence."

The owner should be able to provide appropriate evidence.

Finally:

> "How do you know the control is effective?"

The owner should explain the monitoring, testing or assurance mechanism.

This creates a practical audit chain:

```text

 ↓

 ↓
Show me.
 ↓
```

## 132. The Difference Between SoA and Control Register

Although they can be integrated, the two concepts serve different purposes.

### Statement of Applicability

Primarily answers:

> **Which controls are applicable and why?**

### Control Register

An organization may combine these capabilities into one governance platform while preserving the distinction between applicability and operational control management.

> Which relevant controls are selected and why?

## 134. Annex A and the Principle of Proportionality

Organizations should avoid two opposite extremes.

### Under-control

Too few or weak controls may leave significant AI risks inadequately addressed.

### Over-control

Excessive controls may create unnecessary complexity, cost and administrative burden.

A proportionate approach considers:

* Risk.
* Impact.
* Criticality.
* Regulatory obligations.
* Organizational capability.
* Business objectives.

The objective is an **effective control environment appropriate to the organization's circumstances**.

## 136. Final Principle

Annex A should ultimately be understood as a structured set of AI governance controls that supports the organization's broader AIMS.

The Statement of Applicability provides the documented bridge between:

> **organizational context and risks**

and

> **the controls selected to manage those risks and requirements.**

A strong implementation therefore does not ask only:

> **"Have we completed Annex A?"**

It asks:

> **"Have we identified the controls relevant to our AI environment, justified our decisions, implemented the applicable controls, established evidence of operation, evaluated effectiveness, and continuously improved the control environment?"**

That is the foundation of a credible, risk-based and audit-ready Annex A and Statement of Applicability process.
