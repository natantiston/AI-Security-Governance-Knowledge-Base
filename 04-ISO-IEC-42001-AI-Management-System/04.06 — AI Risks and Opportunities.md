# 04.06 — AI Risks and Opportunities

## 1. Introduction to AI Risks and Opportunities

An effective AI Management System should systematically identify, assess and address risks and opportunities associated with the organization's use and management of AI.

AI governance planning should consider both:

* **Risks** — potential events or conditions that could cause undesirable outcomes.
* **Opportunities** — potential circumstances that could improve performance, achieve objectives or create beneficial outcomes.

The AIMS should integrate both dimensions into organizational planning and decision-making.

---

## 2. Purpose of AI Risk Management

AI risk management provides a structured mechanism for understanding uncertainty associated with AI activities.

It helps the organization:

* Identify potential harmful outcomes.
* Understand sources of risk.
* Evaluate likelihood and impact.
* Determine appropriate treatments.
* Establish ownership.
* Monitor changing risk conditions.
* Support informed management decisions.

Risk management should be integrated into the AI lifecycle rather than performed only before deployment.

---

## 3. AI Risk Management in the AIMS

AI risk management should operate as part of the overall management system.

A simplified relationship is:

```text
Organizational Context
        ↓
AI Risks and Opportunities
        ↓
Risk Assessment
        ↓
Risk Treatment
        ↓
AI Controls
        ↓
Monitoring
        ↓
Risk Review
        ↓
Continual Improvement
```

This creates a continuous relationship between organizational objectives and AI risk management.

---

## 4. Definition of AI Risk

AI risk can be understood as the effect of uncertainty on objectives arising from AI-related activities, systems, technologies, data, people or dependencies.

AI risks can affect:

* Individuals.
* Customers.
* Employees.
* Organizations.
* Society.
* Business operations.
* Information assets.
* Regulatory compliance.

The nature and significance of AI risk depend on the organization's context.

---

## 5. AI Risk Sources

AI risks may originate from many different sources.

Examples include:

* AI models.
* Training data.
* Input data.
* Algorithms.
* AI applications.
* Users.
* Developers.
* Suppliers.
* Cloud infrastructure.
* External APIs.
* Business processes.
* Governance weaknesses.

Risk identification should consider the complete AI ecosystem.

---

## 6. AI Risk Categories

Organizations may categorize AI risks to improve consistency.

Possible categories include:

* Strategic risk.
* Operational risk.
* Cybersecurity risk.
* Privacy risk.
* Data risk.
* Model risk.
* Legal risk.
* Regulatory risk.
* Ethical risk.
* Safety risk.
* Reputational risk.
* Financial risk.
* Third-party risk.

Categories should be adapted to organizational needs.

---

## 7. Strategic AI Risk

Strategic AI risk can arise when AI activities conflict with organizational strategy or create unintended strategic consequences.

Examples include:

* Investing in unsuitable AI capabilities.
* Becoming excessively dependent on a provider.
* Deploying AI without sufficient organizational readiness.
* Failing to adapt to technological change.
* Pursuing AI initiatives without measurable business value.

Strategic AI risk should be considered by senior management.

---

## 8. Operational AI Risk

Operational AI risk concerns failures affecting normal business operations.

Examples include:

* AI service outages.
* Incorrect outputs.
* Workflow disruption.
* Integration failures.
* Inadequate monitoring.
* Incorrect configuration.
* Poor human oversight.

Operational risks should be evaluated according to business impact.

---

## 9. AI Cybersecurity Risk

AI systems may introduce or amplify cybersecurity risks.

Potential risks include:

* Prompt injection.
* Data poisoning.
* Model manipulation.
* Unauthorized access.
* Model theft.
* Sensitive information disclosure.
* Malicious AI-generated content.
* Compromised AI dependencies.

AI cybersecurity risk should be integrated with the organization's broader cybersecurity risk management.

---

## 10. AI Privacy Risk

AI systems may create privacy risks when processing personal information.

Examples include:

* Excessive data collection.
* Inappropriate secondary use.
* Unauthorized disclosure.
* Inadequate retention controls.
* Inference of sensitive information.
* Inadequate transparency.

Privacy risk should be evaluated according to applicable legal and organizational requirements.

---

## 11. AI Data Risk

Data-related risks may affect both AI performance and governance.

Examples include:

* Poor-quality data.
* Incomplete data.
* Inaccurate data.
* Unauthorized data.
* Biased data.
* Outdated data.
* Uncontrolled data sources.
* Insufficient data lineage.

Data risks should be considered throughout the AI lifecycle.

---

## 12. AI Model Risk

Model risk can arise when an AI model does not behave as expected.

Potential causes include:

* Incorrect assumptions.
* Inadequate training.
* Poor validation.
* Data drift.
* Model drift.
* Unexpected inputs.
* Inappropriate use.

Model risk should be evaluated according to the model's purpose and potential impact.

---

## 13. AI Performance Risk

AI systems may fail to achieve their intended performance.

Examples include:

* Low accuracy.
* High error rates.
* Inconsistent outputs.
* Poor robustness.
* Excessive false positives.
* Excessive false negatives.
* Performance degradation over time.

Performance requirements should be established and monitored where appropriate.

---

## 14. AI Bias and Fairness Risk

AI systems may produce systematically different outcomes for different groups or individuals.

Potential causes include:

* Biased training data.
* Historical discrimination.
* Unrepresentative datasets.
* Model design.
* Measurement limitations.
* Unequal error rates.

Where fairness is relevant to the AI system, associated risks should be identified and addressed.

---

## 15. AI Transparency Risk

Insufficient transparency can make it difficult for stakeholders to understand how AI is being used.

Risks may include:

* Users not knowing AI is involved.
* Unclear system limitations.
* Inadequate explanation of decisions.
* Poor documentation.
* Unclear accountability.

Transparency requirements should be proportionate to the context and potential impact.

---

## 16. AI Explainability Risk

Some AI systems may produce outputs that are difficult to explain.

This can create risks when stakeholders need to understand:

* Why an output was produced.
* What information influenced the result.
* What limitations exist.
* Who is accountable for the outcome.

Explainability requirements should be established according to use case and risk.

---

## 17. AI Human Oversight Risk

Insufficient human oversight can increase the consequences of AI errors.

Potential problems include:

* Excessive reliance on AI outputs.
* Automation bias.
* Inadequate review.
* Lack of intervention authority.
* Insufficient user competence.

Human oversight should be meaningful and appropriate to the system's risk.

---

## 18. AI Safety Risk

AI systems may create safety-related risks depending on their application.

Examples include AI used in:

* Industrial environments.
* Healthcare.
* Transportation.
* Physical security.
* Critical infrastructure.
* Autonomous systems.

Where safety is relevant, safety considerations should be incorporated into risk assessment and treatment.

---

## 19. AI Legal Risk

AI activities may create legal risks through:

* Contractual obligations.
* Intellectual property issues.
* Data use.
* Consumer protection.
* Employment considerations.
* Liability.
* Regulatory requirements.

Legal risks should be assessed in the context of applicable jurisdictions.

---

## 20. AI Regulatory Risk

Regulatory requirements affecting AI may change over time.

Organizations should therefore consider:

* Current requirements.
* Emerging requirements.
* Regulatory guidance.
* Sector-specific obligations.
* Geographic differences.

Regulatory change should be incorporated into ongoing risk management.

---

## 21. AI Compliance Risk

Compliance risk occurs when AI activities fail to satisfy applicable requirements.

Examples include:

* Missing required assessments.
* Unauthorized AI use.
* Inadequate documentation.
* Failure to implement required controls.
* Failure to meet contractual obligations.

Compliance risk should be linked to accountable owners and remediation activities.

---

## 22. AI Reputational Risk

AI failures can damage stakeholder trust.

Potential causes include:

* Harmful AI outputs.
* Privacy incidents.
* Discriminatory outcomes.
* Misleading communications.
* Major system failures.
* Poor incident response.

Reputational impact should be considered when evaluating significant AI risks.

---

## 23. AI Financial Risk

AI may create direct or indirect financial risks.

Examples include:

* Incorrect automated decisions.
* Fraud.
* Operational losses.
* Regulatory penalties.
* Litigation.
* AI infrastructure costs.
* Poor investment decisions.

Financial impact should be evaluated where relevant.

---

## 24. AI Third-Party Risk

AI systems frequently depend on external organizations.

Third-party risks may involve:

* AI providers.
* Cloud providers.
* Data providers.
* Software vendors.
* API providers.
* Model developers.

Third-party dependencies should be considered during AI risk assessment.

---

## 25. AI Supply-Chain Risk

The AI supply chain may include multiple interconnected components.

For example:

```text
Data Provider
      ↓
Dataset
      ↓
Model Provider
      ↓
Cloud Platform
      ↓
AI Application
      ↓
Business Process
      ↓
End User
```

A weakness in one component may affect the overall AI system.

---

## 26. AI Dependency Risk

AI systems may depend on technologies or services that the organization does not directly control.

Dependency risks may include:

* Provider outage.
* API changes.
* Model changes.
* Pricing changes.
* Service termination.
* Security incidents.
* Loss of technical support.

Critical dependencies should be identified and monitored.

---

## 27. AI Concentration Risk

Concentration risk can occur when many AI systems depend on the same provider or technology.

For example:

```text
AI System A ─┐
AI System B ─┼──→ Same AI Provider
AI System C ─┤
AI System D ─┘
```

A failure affecting the common provider could therefore affect multiple business processes simultaneously.

---

## 28. AI Lifecycle Risk

AI risk may change throughout the lifecycle.

A lifecycle perspective may include:

```text
Concept
  ↓
Design
  ↓
Development
  ↓
Testing
  ↓
Deployment
  ↓
Operation
  ↓
Change
  ↓
Retirement
```

Risk assessment should account for relevant lifecycle stages rather than treating deployment as the only important point.

Risk identification should systematically determine what could affect AI objectives.

Methods may include:

* Workshops.
* Interviews.
* Risk assessments.
* Threat modeling.
* Data analysis.
* Incident analysis.
* Control assessments.
* Supplier assessments.
* Historical information.

Risk identification should involve appropriate subject-matter expertise.

---

## 30. AI Risk Identification Sources

Useful sources of risk information include:

* Internal incidents.
* Audit findings.
* Security events.
* Privacy assessments.
* AI testing.
* Regulatory developments.
* Supplier information.
* Employee feedback.
* Customer complaints.
* Industry developments.

Using multiple sources improves risk visibility.

---

## 31. AI Risk Scenarios

Risk scenarios describe how a risk could occur and what could result.

A scenario may include:

```text
Threat / Cause
      ↓
AI System Weakness
      ↓
Risk Event
      ↓
Business or Stakeholder Impact
```

Scenario-based analysis helps make abstract AI risks more understandable.

---

## 32. AI Risk Event

A risk event is an occurrence that could affect objectives.

Examples include:

* AI model failure.
* Data leakage.
* Unauthorized AI deployment.
* Incorrect automated decision.
* Provider outage.
* Security compromise.

Risk events should be connected to underlying causes and potential consequences.

---

## 33. AI Risk Causes

Understanding causes helps organizations select appropriate controls.

Causes may include:

* Poor governance.
* Inadequate training.
* Weak security.
* Poor data quality.
* Inadequate testing.
* Insufficient monitoring.
* Human error.
* Supplier failure.

Addressing root causes can provide stronger risk reduction than treating symptoms alone.

---

## 34. AI Risk Consequences

Consequences describe what could happen if a risk materializes.

Potential consequences include:

* Financial loss.
* Operational disruption.
* Privacy harm.
* Security compromise.
* Legal action.
* Regulatory action.
* Customer harm.
* Reputational damage.

Multiple consequences may result from a single AI risk.

---

## 35. AI Risk Inherent Risk

Inherent risk represents the level of risk before considering the effect of controls.

A simplified representation is:

```text
AI Activity
   ↓
Threats / Vulnerabilities
   ↓
Inherent Risk
```

Inherent risk helps management understand the underlying exposure.

---

## 36. AI Risk Control Environment

Controls can reduce the likelihood or impact of AI risks.

Controls may include:

* Policies.
* Technical safeguards.
* Human oversight.
* Approval processes.
* Monitoring.
* Testing.
* Training.
* Contractual requirements.

Controls should be designed according to identified risks.

---

## 37. AI Risk Residual Risk

Residual risk is the remaining risk after considering implemented controls.

```text
Inherent Risk
      ↓
Controls
      ↓
Residual Risk
```

Residual risk should be evaluated against established risk criteria and organizational risk appetite.

---

## 38. AI Risk Appetite

Risk appetite describes the amount and type of risk the organization is willing to accept in pursuit of objectives.

AI risk appetite may vary by:

* AI use case.
* Business activity.
* Potential impact.
* Regulatory environment.
* Data sensitivity.

Risk appetite provides an important basis for AI risk decisions.

---

## 39. AI Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance establishes acceptable variation around defined objectives or risk levels.

For example, an organization may establish specific tolerance levels for:

* Model error.
* Service availability.
* Incident frequency.
* Control exceptions.

Tolerance levels should be measurable where practical.

---

## 40. AI Risk Criteria

Risk criteria provide the basis for evaluating AI risks.

Criteria may define:

* Impact categories.
* Likelihood levels.
* Risk ratings.
* Escalation thresholds.
* Acceptance authority.

Consistent criteria improve comparability between AI systems.

---

## 41. AI Risk Likelihood

Likelihood represents the possibility that a risk event will occur.

Organizations may use categories such as:

| Level          | Description                      |
| -------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Rare           | Unlikely under normal conditions |
| Unlikely       | Could occur occasionally         |
| Possible       | May occur                        |
| Likely         | Expected to occur periodically   |
| Almost Certain | Expected frequently              |

The exact methodology should be defined by the organization.

---

## 42. AI Risk Impact

Impact represents the potential consequence if an AI risk materializes.

Impact dimensions may include:

* Financial.
* Operational.
* Legal.
* Regulatory.
* Privacy.
* Security.
* Safety.
* Reputational.
* Human impact.

Impact should be evaluated according to organizational context.

---

## 43. AI Risk Scoring

Organizations may combine likelihood and impact into a risk rating.

A simplified model is:

```text
Risk Score = Likelihood × Impact
```

However, quantitative scoring is only one possible methodology and should not replace professional judgment.

## 45. AI Risk Assessment

Risk assessment generally involves:

1. Risk identification.
2. Risk analysis.
3. Risk evaluation.

The assessment should produce sufficient information for management to determine appropriate treatment.

---

## 46. AI Risk Analysis

Risk analysis examines the nature and characteristics of identified risks.

It may consider:

* Causes.
* Consequences.
* Likelihood.
* Impact.
* Existing controls.
* Control effectiveness.
* Dependencies.

Analysis provides the basis for risk evaluation.

---

## 47. AI Risk Evaluation

Risk evaluation compares analyzed risk against established criteria.

Possible outcomes include:

* Acceptable.
* Requires treatment.
* Requires escalation.
* Requires additional assessment.
* Unacceptable.

Evaluation should lead to a documented decision.

---

## 48. AI Risk Treatment

Risk treatment determines what should be done about identified risks.

Potential approaches include:

* Avoid.
* Reduce.
* Share or transfer where appropriate.
* Accept.

Treatment decisions should consider both risk and business objectives.

---

## 49. AI Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance involves changing or stopping an activity so that the risk no longer applies or is materially reduced.

Examples include:

* Not deploying a high-risk AI use case.
* Removing an unnecessary AI capability.
* Selecting a safer alternative.

Avoidance may be appropriate when risk cannot be reduced to an acceptable level.

---

## 50. AI Risk Reduction

Risk reduction involves implementing controls to reduce likelihood, impact or both.

Examples include:

* Human oversight.
* Access controls.
* Testing.
* Monitoring.
* Data controls.
* Security safeguards.
* Output validation.

Risk reduction is often central to AI risk treatment.

---

## 51. AI Risk Transfer

Some risk may be transferred or shared through contractual or other arrangements.

Examples may include:

* Supplier agreements.
* Insurance.
* Service-level commitments.
* Indemnification arrangements.

Transfer does not necessarily eliminate the organization's underlying accountability or governance obligations.

---

## 52. AI Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance occurs when authorized management decides that residual risk is within acceptable boundaries.

Acceptance should be:

* Explicit.
* Documented.
* Authorized.
* Based on defined criteria.
* Periodically reviewed.

Risk acceptance should not be used as an informal substitute for risk treatment.

---

## 53. AI Risk Treatment Selection

Treatment decisions should consider:

* Risk severity.
* Cost.
* Feasibility.
* Business objectives.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Available technology.
* Residual risk.

The selected treatment should provide an appropriate response to the identified risk.

---

## 54. AI Risk Treatment Plans

Risk treatment plans should identify the actions necessary to address risks.

A treatment plan may include:

| Element       | Example                         |
| ------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Risk          | Unauthorized AI data processing |
| Treatment     | Reduce                          |
| Control       | Data-use restriction            |
| Owner         | Data Governance                 |
| Priority      | High                            |
| Target Date   | Defined date                    |
| Residual Risk | Medium                          |

Treatment plans provide accountability for risk reduction.

---

## 55. AI Risk Monitoring

AI risks should be monitored because their characteristics can change.

Monitoring may consider:

* New threats.
* New vulnerabilities.
* AI system changes.
* Model performance.
* Regulatory developments.
* Business changes.
* Incidents.
* Control effectiveness.

Risk monitoring should be integrated with the AIMS operating model.

---

## 56. AI Risk Reassessment

Risk should be reassessed when significant changes occur.

Triggers may include:

* New AI functionality.
* Model changes.
* New data sources.
* New suppliers.
* Significant incidents.
* Regulatory changes.
* Changes in intended use.

Reassessment ensures that previous risk decisions remain appropriate.

---

## 57. AI Risk Change Triggers

Organizations should define events that automatically or routinely trigger reassessment.

Examples include:

```text
Material Model Change
        ↓
Risk Reassessment

New Data Source
        ↓
Risk Reassessment

New Intended Use
        ↓
Risk Reassessment

Major Incident
        ↓

```

Defined triggers make risk management more proactive.

---

## 58. AI Risk Ownership

Every significant AI risk should have an accountable owner.

The risk owner should have sufficient authority to:

* Understand the risk.
* Approve treatment.
* Allocate resources.
* Monitor status.
* Escalate issues.
* Accept residual risk within delegated authority.

Risk ownership should be clearly documented.

---

## 59. AI Risk Escalation

Risks exceeding established thresholds should be escalated.

Escalation may be triggered by:

* High residual risk.
* Regulatory exposure.
* Significant stakeholder impact.
* Repeated control failures.
* Material incidents.
* Risk acceptance beyond delegated authority.

Escalation ensures that significant AI risks receive appropriate management attention.

---

## 60. AI Risk Documentation

Risk information should be documented sufficiently to support governance and decision-making.

Documentation may include:

* Risk description.
* Cause.
* Consequence.
* Rating.
* Controls.
* Treatment.
* Owner.
* Acceptance.
* Review date.

Documentation should be proportionate to risk and organizational needs.

## 61. AI Risk Register

An AI risk register provides a structured record of identified AI risks and their management status.

A typical register may include:

| Field            | Description                    |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Risk ID          | Unique risk identifier         |
| AI System        | Related AI system or use case  |
| Risk Description | Statement of the risk          |
| Risk Category    | Type of AI risk                |
| Risk Owner       | Accountable individual or role |
| Inherent Risk    | Risk before controls           |
| Controls         | Existing safeguards            |
| Residual Risk    | Remaining risk                 |
| Treatment        | Selected response              |
| Status           | Current treatment status       |
| Review Date      | Next scheduled review          |

The register should remain current as AI systems and risk conditions change.

---

## 62. AI Risk Register Ownership

The organization should establish responsibility for maintaining the AI risk register.

Responsibilities may include:

* Adding newly identified risks.
* Updating existing risks.
* Recording treatment decisions.
* Tracking overdue actions.
* Escalating significant risks.
* Removing obsolete risks.
* Maintaining appropriate evidence.

Ownership should be clearly defined within the AIMS.

---

## 63. AI Risk Register Quality

A risk register should contain information that is:

* Accurate.
* Current.
* Consistent.
* Traceable.
* Complete enough for decision-making.

A large risk register is not necessarily effective if descriptions are vague, ratings are inconsistent or ownership is unclear.

Aggregation may consider:

* Shared providers.
* Shared data.

Portfolio analysis provides management with a broader view than individual system assessments.

* Customers.

* External attackers.

## 77. AI Use-Case Risk

Risk assessment should consider the specific purpose for which AI is used.

The same technology may have very different risk levels depending on its application.

For example:

```text id="d5v8hm"
AI for Drafting Internal Notes
→ Lower Potential Impact

AI for High-Impact Decisions
→ Higher Potential Impact
```

Risk should therefore be assessed in context rather than based solely on the technology.

Purpose documentation should help establish:

* Expected outputs.
* Intended users.
* Intended decisions.
* Boundaries.
* Prohibited uses.
* Applicable controls.

A clearly defined purpose provides a foundation for risk assessment.

## 79. AI Scope Creep Risk

AI systems may gradually be used beyond their originally approved purpose.

Examples include:

* New user groups.
* New datasets.
* New decisions.
* New geographic markets.
* New business processes.

Scope changes should trigger appropriate governance review.

Repurposing may change:

* Risk level.
* Data requirements.
* Stakeholder impact.
* Legal obligations.
* Security requirements.

Repurposed AI should therefore be reassessed before significant new use.

Organizations should establish mechanisms for identifying and evaluating emerging risks.
