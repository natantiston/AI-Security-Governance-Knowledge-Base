# 04.02 — Context of the Organization

## 1. Introduction

The **context of the organization** is a foundational component of an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS).

Before an organization can establish effective AI governance, it must understand the environment in which its AI activities operate.

The organization should consider:

* Internal circumstances.
* External circumstances.
* Business objectives.
* AI-related activities.
* Interested parties.
* Legal and regulatory requirements.
* Technology environment.
* Risk environment.
* Organizational structure.
* Existing management systems.

The fundamental principle is:

> **An AIMS must be designed for the organization's actual context rather than implemented as a generic collection of AI controls.**

---

## 2. Why Organizational Context Matters

Organizations use AI in very different ways.

For example:

* A bank may use AI for fraud detection and credit-related processes.
* A hospital may use AI to support clinical activities.
* A telecommunications company may use AI for network optimization.
* A manufacturer may use AI for predictive maintenance.
* A consulting company may use generative AI for knowledge work.
* A government organization may use AI for public services.

Each environment creates different:

* Risks.
* Stakeholder expectations.
* Regulatory requirements.
* Business impacts.
* Security requirements.
* Privacy considerations.
* Governance needs.

Therefore, the same AIMS design should not automatically be applied identically to every organization.

---

## 3. The Context Principle

The context of the organization establishes the environment within which the AIMS operates.

A simplified model is:

```text
                ORGANIZATIONAL CONTEXT
                         │
          ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
          ↓              ↓              ↓
       Internal        External      Interested
       Factors         Factors        Parties
          │              │              │
          └──────────────┼──────────────┘
                         ↓
                  AIMS REQUIREMENTS
                         ↓
                 AIMS DESIGN & SCOPE
                         ↓
                   AI GOVERNANCE
```

Context therefore influences virtually every subsequent element of the management system.

* Existing policies.

* Cybersecurity threats.
* AI developments.
* Economic conditions.

* Supplier ecosystem.

External factors can change over time and may require corresponding changes to the AIMS.

## 6. Strategic Context

The AIMS should be aligned with the organization's strategic direction.

For example:

```text
Business Strategy
       ↓
Digital Strategy
       ↓
AI Strategy
       ↓
AI Use Cases
       ↓
AI Governance
       ↓
AIMS
```

If the organization considers AI strategically important, the AIMS should provide sufficient governance capacity to support that strategy.

Conversely, an organization with limited AI use may require a smaller and more focused AIMS.

* Internally developed AI.

* Cloud-based AI.
* Embedded AI features.
* Generative AI.
* Machine-learning models.
* Automated decision-support systems.
* AI-enabled business applications.
* Third-party AI services.

The organization should understand not only formally designated "AI systems" but also AI capabilities embedded within broader technology services where relevant.

## 14. Organizational AI Maturity

AI maturity is another important contextual factor.

A simple maturity model might include:

### Level 1 — Experimental

AI is used informally with limited governance.

### Level 2 — Emerging

AI use cases are increasing and basic governance is being introduced.

### Level 3 — Defined

AI governance processes and responsibilities are formally established.

### Level 4 — Managed

AI governance is measured and consistently operated.

### Level 5 — Optimized

AI governance is integrated, automated where appropriate, and continually improved.

```text
Experimental
     ↓
Emerging
     ↓
Defined
     ↓
Managed
     ↓
Optimized
```

The AIMS should be appropriate to the organization's current maturity while supporting future development.

---

## 15. AI Governance Maturity Versus AI Technology Maturity

An organization can have advanced AI technology without having mature AI governance.

For example:

```text
Advanced AI Technology
        +
Weak Governance
        ↓
High Exposure
```

Conversely:

```text
Strong Governance
        +
Limited AI Adoption
        ↓
Controlled but Smaller AI Footprint
```

Therefore, organizational context should consider both:

* **AI technology maturity**, and
* **AI governance maturity**.

## 30. Centralized, Decentralized and Federated AI Governance

Three common approaches are:

### Centralized

A central function controls most AI governance activities.

```text id="t7n4y6"
Enterprise
    ↓
Central AI Governance
    ↓
All AI Systems
```

### Decentralized

Business units manage their own AI governance.

```text id="sjq3jp"
Enterprise
 ├── BU1 → AI Governance
 ├── BU2 → AI Governance
 └── BU3 → AI Governance
```

### Federated

Enterprise governance establishes common requirements while business units retain operational responsibility.

```text id="v1k1qq"
Enterprise AI Governance
          │
 ┌────────┼────────┐
 ↓        ↓        ↓
 BU1      BU2      BU3
```

The appropriate model depends on organizational context.

## 33. Organizational Culture and AI Adoption

The AIMS should therefore consider the organization's actual behavior, not merely its documented policies.

## 34. Shadow AI

Shadow AI refers to AI tools or capabilities used without appropriate organizational authorization or governance.

Examples include employees:

* Uploading company documents to public AI services.
* Using personal AI accounts for business activities.
* Deploying unauthorized AI applications.
* Connecting AI tools to enterprise systems without approval.
* Using AI for sensitive decisions without assessment.

A contextual assessment should determine whether shadow AI is a material issue for the organization.

```text id="1f1czx"
AI Demand
   ↓
Employee Adoption
   ↓
Unauthorized AI
   ↓
Data / Security / Compliance Risk
   ↓
AIMS Governance Response
```

## 62. Understanding Relevant Interested Parties

After identifying internal and external issues, the organization should determine which **interested parties** are relevant to the AIMS.

Interested parties are individuals, groups, organizations, authorities, or other entities that can affect, be affected by, or have requirements relevant to the organization's AI activities.

Examples include:

* Customers.
* Employees.
* Management.
* Regulators.
* Government authorities.
* Business partners.
* Suppliers.
* AI service providers.
* Shareholders.
* Individuals affected by AI decisions.
* Professional bodies.
* Certification bodies.

The objective is not to create an exhaustive list of every possible stakeholder.

The organization should identify interested parties whose requirements or expectations are relevant to the AIMS.

For example:

| Interested Party   | Potential AI-Related Expectation            |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |

| Regulators         | Legal and regulatory compliance             |
| Management         | Business value and controlled risk          |
| Suppliers          | Clear contractual requirements              |

| Certification Body | Demonstrable conformity                     |

These expectations can influence the organization's AIMS.

A stronger approach is to define scope based on:

* AI activities.
* Organizational responsibilities.
* Risks.
* Impacts.
* Relevant processes.
* Stakeholder requirements.

  ↓

* During management review.
* When major AI changes occur.
* Following significant incidents.
* Following regulatory changes.
* During strategic planning.

A risk-based organization may review significant contextual changes more frequently than an organization with a stable AI environment.

## 124. Context Change Management

  ├── No → Monitor

       ↓

       ↓

This provides a proportionate approach.

## 132. Common Context-Analysis Mistakes

Several mistakes can weaken the AIMS.

### Mistake 1 — Treating Context as Generic

Using a generic SWOT-style statement without linking it to AI governance provides limited value.

### Mistake 2 — Listing Everything

Documenting hundreds of irrelevant external issues creates administrative overhead.

### Mistake 3 — Ignoring Stakeholders

Focusing only on internal management can overlook important external requirements.

### Mistake 4 — Ignoring AI Dependencies

Third-party AI services may be critical to the organization's operations.

### Mistake 5 — Never Updating Context

A static context analysis can become obsolete quickly.
