# 04.15 — Internal Audit of the AIMS

## 1. Introduction to Internal Audit of the AIMS

Internal audit of the AI Management System (AIMS) provides an independent and systematic mechanism for evaluating whether the organization's AI management arrangements are appropriately implemented and maintained.

An internal audit can examine whether the AIMS:

* Conforms to established organizational requirements.
* Conforms to applicable ISO/IEC 42001 requirements.
* Is effectively implemented.
* Is maintained as intended.
* Supports achievement of AI objectives.
* Addresses identified AI risks and opportunities.
* Produces appropriate evidence.

Internal audit provides management with assurance about the effectiveness of the AIMS.

---

## 2. Purpose of AIMS Internal Audit

The purpose of an AIMS internal audit is to obtain objective evidence about the effectiveness and conformity of the AI Management System.

An audit may determine whether:

* Required processes have been established.
* Responsibilities have been assigned.
* Required controls are implemented.
* Documented requirements are followed.
* Evidence demonstrates effective operation.
* Identified deficiencies are addressed.

The audit should provide conclusions that management can use for improvement and decision-making.

---

## 3. Internal Audit as an Assurance Activity

Internal audit is an assurance mechanism within the management system.

It provides an independent perspective on whether established arrangements are operating as intended.

A simplified relationship is:

```text id="p8v3km"
AIMS Requirements
       ↓
Implementation
       ↓
Operation
       ↓
Audit Evidence
       ↓
Audit Evaluation
       ↓
Audit Findings
       ↓

```

This helps distinguish internal audit from routine operational monitoring.

The scope may specify:

* AI systems.

* Locations.
* Technologies.
* Management-system processes.
* Applicable controls.
* Time period.

The scope should be appropriate to the audit objective.

## 6. AIMS Audit Objectives

Audit objectives define what the audit is intended to determine.

Objectives may include:

* Assessing conformity with ISO/IEC 42001.
* Evaluating implementation of AI policies.
* Assessing control effectiveness.
* Evaluating risk-management processes.
* Reviewing AI impact-assessment practices.
* Verifying corrective-action effectiveness.

Clear objectives provide direction for audit planning and execution.

## 7. AIMS Audit Criteria

Audit criteria provide the requirements against which evidence is evaluated.

Criteria may include:

* ISO/IEC 42001 requirements.
* Organizational AI policies.
* Internal procedures.
* AI governance standards.
* Risk criteria.
* Contractual requirements.
* Applicable legal or regulatory requirements.

Audit findings should be based on comparison between objective evidence and defined criteria.

---

## 8. Audit Evidence

Audit evidence consists of information that supports audit conclusions.

Evidence may include:

* Policies.
* Procedures.
* Records.
* System configurations.
* Logs.
* Risk assessments.
* AI impact assessments.
* Interviews.
* Observations.
* Test results.
* Management records.

Evidence should be relevant, reliable and sufficient for the audit objective.

---

## 9. Objective Evidence

Audit conclusions should be based on objective evidence rather than assumptions or unsupported opinions.

Objective evidence may demonstrate that:

* A process exists.
* A control is implemented.
* A required activity occurred.
* A decision was approved.
* A corrective action was completed.

Statements made during interviews may require supporting evidence where appropriate.

---

## 10. Audit Independence

Internal audit activities should be conducted with an appropriate degree of independence.

Auditors should avoid auditing activities where they have direct responsibility for the subject matter being evaluated, where practical.

Independence helps reduce conflicts of interest and strengthens the credibility of audit conclusions.

---

## 11. Auditor Objectivity

Auditors should approach the audit objectively and without predetermined conclusions.

Objectivity requires:

* Evidence-based evaluation.
* Consistent application of criteria.
* Professional judgment.
* Appropriate challenge.
* Avoidance of personal bias.

Auditors should report both conformity and nonconformity based on evidence.

## 31. Audit Nonconformity

A nonconformity occurs when an applicable requirement has not been fulfilled.

Within an AIMS audit, a nonconformity may involve:

* Failure to implement a required process.
* Failure to follow an established procedure.
* Missing required evidence.
* Ineffective control implementation.
* Failure to address an identified requirement.

The finding should clearly identify the requirement, evidence and nature of the deviation.

---

## 32. Major and Minor Nonconformity

Organizations and certification bodies may classify nonconformities according to their significance.

A significant or systemic failure may be treated as a major nonconformity, while a more limited deviation may be treated as a minor nonconformity.

Classification should be based on established audit criteria and the effect of the finding on the management system.

---

## 33. Nonconformity Statement

A well-structured nonconformity statement should be:

* Specific.
* Objective.
* Evidence-based.
* Traceable.
* Clear about the requirement that was not fulfilled.

A useful structure is:

```text
Requirement
     ↓
Objective Evidence
     ↓
Observed Deviation
     ↓
Nonconformity
```

The auditor should avoid unsupported assumptions about causes.

---

## 34. Audit Observation

An observation identifies a condition that may warrant attention but does not necessarily constitute a failure to meet an audit criterion.

Examples include:

* An emerging weakness.
* An inconsistent practice.
* An area requiring clarification.
* A potential future risk.

Observations should not be used to improperly classify something that actually constitutes a nonconformity.

---

## 35. Opportunity for Improvement

An opportunity for improvement identifies a way the organization could potentially strengthen its AIMS.

Examples may include:

* Simplifying a process.
* Improving evidence collection.
* Strengthening reporting.
* Improving monitoring.
* Increasing automation.

An opportunity for improvement is different from a nonconformity because the organization is not necessarily failing to meet a requirement.

---

## 36. Positive Audit Findings

Internal audits should also identify areas where the AIMS is operating effectively.

Positive findings may include:

* Effective controls.
* Strong governance practices.
* Mature monitoring.
* Good evidence management.
* Effective risk treatment.
* Successful improvement initiatives.

Recognizing effective practices can help the organization maintain and replicate them.

---

## 37. Audit Evidence Evaluation

Auditors should evaluate evidence for relevance, reliability and sufficiency.

Evidence should be considered in relation to:

* Audit criteria.
* Audit scope.
* System context.
* Risk.
* Sampling limitations.

Evidence that is incomplete or unreliable should not be treated as conclusive without further investigation.

---

## 38. Evidence Sufficiency

Sufficient evidence means enough reliable information has been obtained to support the audit conclusion.

Evidence requirements may increase when:

* The process is high risk.
* The AI system is highly consequential.
* Previous findings exist.
* Controls are complex.
* Significant changes have occurred.

The amount of evidence should be proportionate to the audit objective.

---

## 39. Evidence Reliability

Evidence reliability concerns the confidence that can reasonably be placed in the information.

Evidence may be strengthened through:

* Independent records.
* System-generated logs.
* Controlled documents.
* Multiple corroborating sources.
* Direct observation.

Self-reported information may require additional verification where appropriate.

---

## 40. Evidence Triangulation

Evidence triangulation involves comparing information from multiple sources.

For example:

```text

  +
Interview
  +
System Record
  +
Observation
  ↓

```

Triangulation can improve confidence in audit findings.

## 41. Audit Sampling Strategy

Sampling should be planned rather than selected arbitrarily.

Factors may include:

* Population size.
* Risk.
* Audit objective.
* Previous findings.
* Variability.
* Control significance.

The sampling approach should be documented sufficiently to support the audit conclusion.

---

## 42. Audit Sample Selection

Samples may be selected using approaches such as:

* Random selection.
* Risk-based selection.
* Judgmental selection.
* Stratified selection.
* Targeted selection.

The appropriate method depends on the audit objective and population.

---

## 43. Audit Sampling Limitations

Sampling does not provide evidence about every item within a population.

Auditors should recognize that:

* Problems may exist outside the sample.
* Results may not represent the entire population.
* Sample size affects confidence.
* Selection methodology can affect conclusions.

Sampling limitations should be considered when forming audit conclusions.

## 80. Audit of Corrective Actions

Internal audit may examine whether corrective actions resulting from previous findings have been:

* Defined.
* Assigned.
* Implemented.
* Supported by evidence.
* Completed within appropriate timeframes.
* Verified for effectiveness.

Closing an action administratively does not necessarily demonstrate that the underlying issue has been resolved.

---

## 81. Audit Follow-Up

Follow-up activities determine whether previously identified issues have been appropriately addressed.

Follow-up may include:

* Reviewing implementation evidence.
* Re-testing controls.
* Interviewing responsible personnel.
* Examining updated records.
* Assessing residual risk.

The level of follow-up should reflect the significance of the original finding.

---

## 82. Effectiveness of Corrective Action

Corrective-action effectiveness should be evaluated against the original problem.

A useful model is:

```text
Finding
  ↓
Root Cause
  ↓
Corrective Action
  ↓
Implementation
  ↓
Effectiveness Verification
  ↓
Issue Resolved?
```

If the issue continues to occur, additional action may be necessary.

---

## 83. Audit Findings and Root Cause

An audit finding describes the identified deviation, while root-cause analysis attempts to explain why it occurred.

Possible root causes include:

* Inadequate procedures.
* Lack of competence.
* Insufficient resources.
* Poor system design.
* Weak governance.
* Inadequate communication.
* Ineffective monitoring.

Auditors should distinguish observed evidence from management's subsequent root-cause analysis.

---

## 84. Systemic Nonconformity

A systemic nonconformity affects broader parts of the management system rather than a single isolated activity.

Indicators may include:

* Similar findings across multiple departments.
* Repeated failures.
* Common control weaknesses.
* Widespread process deviations.

Systemic issues generally require broader corrective action than an isolated error.

---

## 85. Recurring Nonconformity

A recurring nonconformity occurs when a previously identified issue reappears.

Recurrence may indicate:

* Ineffective corrective action.
* Incorrect root-cause identification.
* Insufficient management attention.
* Weak control implementation.

Recurring findings should receive appropriate escalation.

---

## 86. Audit Reporting

* Scope.
* Criteria.
* Methodology.
* Evidence reviewed.
* Findings.
* Conclusions.
* Recommendations or opportunities for improvement.
* Required actions.

Reports should be factual and proportionate to the evidence.

## 87. Audit Report Accuracy

Audit reports should accurately represent the work performed and evidence obtained.

Reports should avoid:

* Unsupported conclusions.
* Exaggerated findings.
* Ambiguous statements.
* Misrepresentation of evidence.

Clear reporting improves trust in the internal-audit function.

---

## 88. Audit Report Distribution

Audit results should be communicated to appropriate stakeholders.

Distribution may include:

* AIMS management.
* AI system owners.
* Process owners.
* Risk management.
* Compliance.
* Senior management.

Sensitive audit information should be distributed according to appropriate access controls.

---

## 89. Audit Confidentiality

Audit information may contain sensitive organizational, technical or personal information.

Audit records should therefore be protected through appropriate:

* Access controls.
* Storage controls.
* Transmission controls.
* Retention rules.
* Confidentiality requirements.

Auditors should handle information according to applicable organizational requirements.

---

## 90. Audit Communication and Escalation

Significant findings should be communicated promptly to appropriate management rather than waiting unnecessarily for the final audit report.

Immediate escalation may be appropriate where an audit identifies:

* Significant AI risk.
* Serious security exposure.
* Material compliance concerns.
* Significant control failure.
* Potential harm to stakeholders.

The escalation mechanism should be defined by the organization's governance arrangements.

# 04.15 — Internal Audit of the AIMS

## 91. Audit Follow-Up and Verification

Internal audit should determine whether actions resulting from audit findings have been appropriately addressed.

Follow-up activities may include:

* Reviewing corrective-action evidence.
* Re-performing selected tests.
* Examining updated procedures.
* Reviewing revised controls.
* Interviewing responsible personnel.
* Evaluating subsequent monitoring results.

Follow-up should determine whether the underlying issue has been addressed rather than merely confirming that an action was marked as completed.

---

## 92. Audit Finding Closure

A finding should be considered closed only when sufficient evidence demonstrates that the required action has been implemented and the closure criteria have been satisfied.

Closure evidence may include:

* Updated documentation.
* Implemented controls.
* Completed assessments.
* System changes.
* Training records.
* Monitoring results.
* Management approval.

The closure decision should be supported by objective evidence.

---

## 93. Audit Effectiveness Verification

Corrective-action completion and corrective-action effectiveness are different concepts.

For example:

```text id="m4q8tx"
Corrective Action Completed
          ↓
Was the Action Implemented?
          ↓
Yes
          ↓
Did It Address the Root Cause?
          ↓
Did the Problem Stop Recurring?
          ↓
Effectiveness Confirmed
```

Effectiveness verification provides stronger assurance that the AIMS has actually improved.

## 104. Internal Audit Versus Certification Audit

Internal audit and external certification audit have different purposes.

| Internal Audit                                             | Certification Audit                                  |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Conducted for the organization's assurance and improvement | Conducted by an independent certification body       |
| Supports internal governance                               | Determines conformity for certification purposes     |
| Can be performed periodically                              | Conducted according to certification arrangements    |
| Focuses on improvement and assurance                       | Focuses on conformity against certification criteria |
| Results belong to internal management processes            | Results support certification decisions              |

A strong internal-audit program can improve organizational readiness without replacing an independent certification audit.

---

## 105. Internal Audit Versus Operational Monitoring

Internal audit should not be confused with routine monitoring.

**Operational monitoring** generally provides ongoing information about system and process performance.

**Internal audit** provides a structured, independent evaluation against defined criteria.

The relationship can be represented as:

```text id="u6k2rp"
Operational Monitoring
        ↓
Performance Information
        ↓
Internal Audit
        ↓
Independent Evaluation
        ↓
Audit Findings
        ↓
Improvement
```

Both activities are valuable but serve different purposes.

## 106. Internal Audit Versus Management Review

Internal audit and management review also serve different functions.

Internal audit evaluates the AIMS against defined criteria and provides audit conclusions.

Management review evaluates the overall suitability, adequacy and effectiveness of the AIMS from a management perspective.

Audit results can provide important inputs to management review.
