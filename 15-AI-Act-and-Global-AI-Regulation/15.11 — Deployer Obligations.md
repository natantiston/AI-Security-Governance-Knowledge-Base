# 15.11 — Deployer Obligations

## Part 1 — Foundations, Deployer Status, and Regulatory Responsibility

### 1. Deployer Obligations as an AI Governance Responsibility

Deployer obligations represent the organizational responsibilities that arise when an organization uses an AI system under its authority, except where the organization uses the system in a personal non-professional activity.

Under the EU AI Act, a deployer is an organization or person using an AI system under its authority, with the personal-use exception defined in the regulation.

Deployer governance is therefore different from provider governance.

A provider places an AI system on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark, while a deployer uses an AI system that has already been supplied or made available.

For an AI Security Governance program, the distinction is fundamental because an organization can be:

* a provider for one AI system;
* a deployer for another;
* both provider and deployer for the same system in different circumstances; or
* neither, depending on its role and activities.

### 2. Meaning of Deployer

The EU AI Act defines a deployer as a natural or legal person, public authority, agency or other body using an AI system under its authority, except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

The important governance concept is **use under authority**.

The organization does not necessarily need to have developed the AI system to become a deployer.

An organization may become a deployer when it:

* purchases an AI system;
* licenses an AI service;
* integrates an AI model into its operations;
* provides employees with an AI application;
* uses an AI-enabled business platform;
* incorporates AI into decision-support processes; or
* operates a high-risk AI system supplied by another provider.

### 3. Provider and Deployer Are Distinct Roles

Provider and deployer obligations should not be treated as interchangeable.

A provider is generally responsible for bringing the AI system to market or putting it into service under the provider's name or trademark.

A deployer is generally responsible for how the AI system is used within the deployer's operational environment.

This distinction creates different governance responsibilities.

For example:

| Activity                                                  | Typical primary role                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Developing an AI system for market placement              | Provider                             |
| Placing an AI system on the EU market                     | Provider                             |
| Operating a purchased high-risk AI system                 | Deployer                             |
| Configuring an AI system for internal business use        | Deployer                             |
| Developing an AI system under the organization's own name | Potentially provider                 |
| Substantially modifying certain systems                   | May create provider responsibilities |
| Using a third-party generative AI service                 | Generally deployer/user organization |

The exact legal classification depends on the circumstances and applicable provisions.

### 4. Deployer Status Is System-Specific

An organization's overall status should not be recorded simply as "AI deployer."

Deployer status should be determined for each AI system and use case.

For example, an organization may deploy:

* a high-risk recruitment system;
* a customer-service chatbot;
* an internal coding assistant;
* an AI analytics platform;
* a biometric access-control system; and
* a general-purpose AI service.

The regulatory obligations may differ substantially between these systems.

### 5. AI Use Does Not Automatically Create High-Risk Deployer Obligations

Not every AI system used by an organization is a high-risk AI system.

The organization should first determine the applicable regulatory classification.

Potential categories include:

* prohibited AI practices;
* high-risk AI systems;
* transparency-related systems;
* GPAI-related activities;
* minimal-risk AI systems; and
* other AI systems subject to specific legal requirements.

Deployer governance should therefore begin with classification rather than assuming that every AI tool creates the same compliance obligations.

### 6. Intended Purpose and Operational Context

The intended purpose of an AI system is important when determining applicable obligations.

The deployer should understand:

* what the provider designed the system to do;
* what decisions or activities the system supports;
* who uses it;
* who is affected;
* what data it processes;
* where it operates;
* how outputs are used; and
* whether the organization intends to use it differently from the provider's documented purpose.

A change in operational context can materially alter the organization's risk.

### 7. Deployer Use-Case Assessment

Before introducing an AI system into a material business process, the organization should perform a use-case assessment.

The assessment should document:

* business purpose;
* AI functionality;
* users;
* affected individuals;
* data;
* decision impact;
* provider;
* deployment environment;
* regulatory classification;
* security risks;
* privacy implications;
* human oversight;
* applicable policies; and
* approval requirements.

This assessment should become part of the AI inventory.

### 8. Deployer Governance Scope

A mature deployer governance framework should cover the complete operational lifecycle:

**AI acquisition → assessment → approval → configuration → deployment → monitoring → incident handling → change → reassessment → retirement.**

Governance should therefore begin before employees or business units start using an AI service.

### 9. Shadow AI and Unauthorized Deployment

One of the most significant deployer governance challenges is unauthorized AI adoption.

Employees may independently introduce:

* public generative AI tools;
* AI browser extensions;
* AI coding assistants;
* AI meeting transcription tools;
* AI productivity platforms;
* AI document-processing services; or
* AI APIs.

These activities may create security, privacy, regulatory, intellectual-property and data-governance risks.

A deployer framework should therefore address **shadow AI**.

### 10. AI Inventory for Deployer Activities

The organization should maintain an inventory of AI systems it uses.

The inventory should include, where relevant:

* AI system name;
* provider;
* system version;
* business owner;
* technical owner;
* deployment environment;
* users;
* affected populations;
* intended purpose;
* regulatory classification;
* data categories;
* risk rating;
* contractual status;
* security assessment;
* privacy assessment;
* approval status; and
* lifecycle status.

### 11. Deployer Role in the AI Supply Chain

The deployer often operates downstream from the provider.

The supply chain may contain:

**Foundation model provider → application provider → platform → integrator → deployer → end user.**

The deployer should understand which responsibilities remain with the provider and which responsibilities arise from the deployer's own use.

Contracts should not be used to assume that all regulatory responsibility has been transferred to another party.

### 12. AI-DEP-01 — Deployer Role Determination

The organization should establish a formal control for determining whether it acts as a deployer.

The assessment should consider:

* whether the organization uses the AI system;
* whether use occurs under its authority;
* whether the use is professional;
* whether another role also applies;
* whether the organization modified the system;
* whether the organization markets the system under its own name; and
* whether specific provisions of the AI Act apply.

The determination should be documented.

### 13. Deployer Role Record

Each material AI system should have a documented role record.

The record should identify:

* organization;
* AI system;
* provider;
* organizational role;
* use case;
* authority for use;
* regulatory classification;
* responsible owner;
* date of determination; and
* reassessment triggers.

### 14. Deployer Accountability

The business function using the AI system should not assume that responsibility belongs exclusively to IT, Legal, Compliance or the external provider.

Deployer accountability should be distributed across:

* business owners;
* AI governance;
* IT;
* cybersecurity;
* privacy;
* legal;
* compliance;
* procurement;
* risk management;
* human resources; and
* internal audit.

### 15. AI-DEP-02 — Deployer Accountability Matrix

The organization should establish a RACI or equivalent accountability matrix.

The matrix should assign responsibility for:

* AI approval;
* risk assessment;
* data governance;
* security;
* privacy;
* human oversight;
* user training;
* monitoring;
* incident management;
* supplier management;
* regulatory reporting;
* corrective action; and
* retirement.

### 16. Deployer Governance Committee

Organizations with material AI usage should establish appropriate governance oversight.

Depending on organizational size, this may be:

* an AI Governance Committee;
* Technology Risk Committee;
* Model Risk Committee;
* Data Governance Committee;
* AI Security Committee; or
* another established governance body.

The committee should not replace accountable business owners.

### 17. AI-DEP-03 — Deployer Governance Charter

The governance charter should define:

* scope;
* authority;
* responsibilities;
* decision rights;
* escalation;
* risk thresholds;
* reporting;
* exceptions;
* meeting frequency; and
* relationship with existing governance bodies.

### 18. Three Lines of Defense

The Three Lines model can provide an effective governance structure.

**First Line — Business and Technology**

Own AI use, risks and operational controls.

**Second Line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy and Security**

Establish standards, challenge first-line decisions and monitor compliance.

**Third Line — Internal Audit**

Provides independent assurance.

This model should preserve accountability rather than transferring operational ownership to second- or third-line functions.

### 19. Deployer Risk Appetite

The organization should define its tolerance for AI deployment risk.

Risk appetite may address:

* prohibited uses;
* high-impact decisions;
* autonomous actions;
* sensitive information;
* security exposure;
* privacy;
* discrimination;
* unacceptable error rates;
* human oversight;
* third-party dependency; and
* regulatory uncertainty.

Risk appetite should influence approval thresholds.

### 20. AI-DEP-04 — Deployer Risk Assessment

A risk assessment should be performed before material AI deployment.

The assessment should consider:

* regulatory risk;
* security risk;
* privacy risk;
* operational risk;
* legal risk;
* financial risk;
* reputational risk;
* discrimination risk;
* safety risk;
* human-rights implications; and
* third-party risk.

### 21. Deployer Risk Classification

Internal risk classification should complement, not replace, legal classification.

An organization may classify a system as:

* low;
* moderate;
* high; or
* critical

for internal governance purposes even when the system is not legally classified as "high-risk" under the AI Act.

This allows the organization to apply stronger controls where business impact warrants them.

### 22. AI-DEP-05 — Deployer Prohibited-Practice Screening

Before deployment, the organization should assess whether the intended use could fall within prohibited AI practices.

The screening should examine:

* manipulation;
* exploitation of vulnerabilities;
* prohibited social scoring;
* prohibited biometric practices;
* prohibited emotion-recognition use;
* prohibited criminal-risk practices;
* prohibited biometric identification activities; and
* other applicable Article 5 restrictions.

A provider's statement that a product is "compliant" should not automatically substitute for the deployer's own use-case assessment.

### 23. AI-DEP-06 — High-Risk Deployer Assessment

Where a high-risk AI system is deployed, the organization should identify the specific deployer obligations applicable to its use.

The assessment should address:

* provider information;
* instructions for use;
* human oversight;
* monitoring;
* logging;
* data;
* cybersecurity;
* incident response;
* fundamental rights;
* organizational procedures; and
* applicable registration or reporting responsibilities.

### 24. High-Risk Deployer Responsibilities

For high-risk AI systems, the AI Act establishes specific deployer obligations.

Depending on the applicable provision, deployers may need to:

* use systems according to instructions;
* assign appropriate human oversight;
* monitor operation;
* maintain logs under their control;
* use relevant input data appropriately;
* conduct workplace-related measures where applicable;
* inform affected persons in applicable circumstances;
* perform fundamental-rights impact assessments where required; and
* take corrective measures when risks or incidents arise.

The exact requirements depend on the system and deployer circumstances.

### 25. AI-DEP-07 — Instructions-for-Use Control

The deployer should obtain and review provider instructions before deployment.

The review should determine:

* intended purpose;
* limitations;
* performance characteristics;
* known risks;
* human oversight requirements;
* operating conditions;
* data requirements;
* maintenance;
* monitoring;
* expected input;
* prohibited use; and
* incident procedures.

Instructions should be incorporated into operational procedures.

### 26. AI-DEP-08 — Operational Use Conformance

The deployer should verify that actual use conforms to the approved use case and provider instructions.

Monitoring should identify:

* unauthorized use;
* expanded user groups;
* changed decision purposes;
* changed data;
* changed integrations;
* increased autonomy;
* use outside documented conditions; and
* prohibited or restricted applications.

### 27. Human Oversight as a Deployer Responsibility

Human oversight is particularly important when AI systems support or influence consequential decisions.

The deployer should determine:

* who supervises the system;
* what information the human receives;
* what authority the human has;
* when intervention is mandatory;
* how outputs can be challenged;
* how overrides are recorded; and
* what training is required.

### 28. AI-DEP-09 — Human Oversight Assignment

Each material AI deployment should identify named or role-based human overseers.

The assignment should specify:

* responsibility;
* authority;
* competence;
* escalation;
* intervention rights;
* expected response time; and
* documentation requirements.

### 29. Human Oversight Competence

Human oversight cannot be effective if the responsible personnel lack appropriate understanding.

Training should address:

* AI capabilities;
* limitations;
* uncertainty;
* errors;
* automation bias;
* hallucination;
* security threats;
* privacy;
* escalation;
* override procedures; and
* applicable organizational policies.

This aligns with the broader AI literacy requirements under Article 4.

### 30. AI-DEP-10 — Automation-Bias Control

The organization should assess the risk that users will accept AI outputs without adequate review.

Controls may include:

* mandatory review;
* confidence indicators;
* second-level approval;
* randomized quality checks;
* override mechanisms;
* decision documentation;
* user training; and
* monitoring of acceptance rates.

### 31. Deployer Data Governance

The deployer is responsible for governing the data it provides to AI systems within the scope of applicable law and contractual arrangements.

Controls should address:

* data classification;
* lawful use;
* access;
* accuracy;
* minimization;
* retention;
* transmission;
* security;
* sensitive data;
* data residency where relevant; and
* deletion.

### 32. AI-DEP-11 — Input Data Control

Organizations should define which data may be entered into each AI system.

The policy should identify:

* public information;
* internal information;
* confidential information;
* personal data;
* special-category data;
* credentials;
* source code;
* customer information;
* regulated information; and
* prohibited data.

Technical controls should enforce these rules where feasible.

### 33. Data Leakage Prevention

AI deployment should be integrated with data-loss-prevention controls.

Potential safeguards include:

* DLP;
* CASB;
* API gateways;
* access controls;
* content inspection;
* tenant restrictions;
* endpoint controls;
* logging; and
* approved enterprise AI interfaces.

### 34. AI-DEP-12 — Privacy Assessment

Where personal data is processed, the organization should perform appropriate privacy assessment under applicable data-protection requirements.

Depending on the use case, this may involve:

* data-protection impact assessment;
* lawful-basis analysis;
* data-processing agreements;
* processor/controller assessment;
* retention controls;
* transparency;
* data-subject rights; and
* privacy-by-design measures.

AI Act compliance does not replace GDPR or other applicable privacy obligations.

### 35. AI-DEP-13 — Security Assessment

Material AI deployments should undergo cybersecurity assessment before production use.

The assessment should examine:

* authentication;
* authorization;
* encryption;
* API security;
* prompt injection;
* data leakage;
* model access;
* integrations;
* logging;
* monitoring;
* vulnerabilities;
* supply chain; and
* incident response.

### 36. AI-DEP-14 — Third-Party AI Security Assurance

Before adopting an external AI service, the deployer should assess the provider's security posture.

Evidence may include:

* security certifications;
* independent assurance reports;
* penetration-testing information;
* vulnerability-management practices;
* incident history;
* security architecture;
* encryption;
* access control;
* data-handling practices; and
* contractual security commitments.

### 37. Deployer Procurement Governance

AI procurement should include regulatory and security requirements before contract execution.

Procurement should involve appropriate:

* business;
* legal;
* privacy;
* cybersecurity;
* compliance;
* architecture;
* procurement; and
* risk-management functions.

### 38. AI-DEP-15 — AI Vendor Assessment

The vendor assessment should consider:

* provider identity;
* regulatory status;
* AI classification;
* documentation;
* security;
* privacy;
* data usage;
* model training;
* subcontractors;
* geographic processing;
* service availability;
* incident notification;
* audit rights; and
* termination arrangements.

### 39. Contractual AI Governance

Contracts with AI providers should address relevant responsibilities.

Depending on the service, contracts may cover:

* intended use;
* prohibited use;
* security;
* privacy;
* data ownership;
* model training;
* confidentiality;
* incident notification;
* regulatory cooperation;
* service changes;
* subcontractors;
* audit rights;
* business continuity; and
* termination.

### 40. AI-DEP-16 — Material Supplier Change Control

The deployer should receive notification of material changes to externally supplied AI systems where practicable.

Material changes may include:

* model replacement;
* major version;
* new capabilities;
* changed data practices;
* changed hosting;
* changed subcontractors;
* changed security controls;
* changed terms;
* changed intended purpose; or
* changed regulatory classification.

### 41. Deployer Change Management

Changes to an AI deployment should be subject to controlled change management.

Changes may include:

* model version;
* configuration;
* prompt;
* system integration;
* data;
* user population;
* business process;
* autonomy;
* decision authority; or
* provider.

Material changes should trigger reassessment.

### 42. AI-DEP-17 — Change-Impact Assessment

The impact assessment should determine whether the change affects:

* legal classification;
* risk rating;
* privacy;
* cybersecurity;
* human oversight;
* performance;
* transparency;
* documentation;
* contractual obligations;
* fundamental rights; or
* operational procedures.

### 43. Deployer Monitoring

AI systems should be monitored after deployment.

Monitoring should consider:

* performance;
* errors;
* security events;
* user behavior;
* misuse;
* complaints;
* unexpected outputs;
* data changes;
* system changes; and
* regulatory concerns.

Monitoring intensity should be proportional to risk.

### 44. AI-DEP-18 — Operational Performance Monitoring

For material systems, performance should be monitored against approved expectations.

Metrics may include:

* accuracy;
* false-positive rate;
* false-negative rate;
* availability;
* latency;
* error rate;
* override rate;
* escalation rate; and
* user complaints.

### 45. AI-DEP-19 — AI Output Quality Monitoring

The organization should periodically test whether AI outputs remain suitable for their intended use.

Testing may include:

* sampling;
* human review;
* benchmark comparisons;
* scenario testing;
* adversarial testing;
* subgroup testing; and
* production-quality analysis.

### 46. Deployer Incident Management

AI incidents should be integrated into the organization's existing incident-management framework.

Potential incidents include:

* harmful output;
* discriminatory outcome;
* privacy breach;
* security compromise;
* incorrect decision;
* unauthorized use;
* system failure;
* model manipulation;
* data leakage; and
* regulatory non-compliance.

### 47. AI-DEP-20 — AI Incident Classification

The organization should establish an AI-specific incident taxonomy.

Incidents may be categorized as:

* security;
* privacy;
* safety;
* quality;
* discrimination;
* compliance;
* operational;
* reputational; or
* fundamental-rights related.

Severity should determine escalation.

### 48. AI-DEP-21 — AI Incident Escalation

Material AI incidents should have predefined escalation paths.

Escalation may involve:

* business owner;
* cybersecurity;
* privacy;
* legal;
* compliance;
* AI governance;
* senior management; and
* regulators where legally required.

### 49. Provider Incident Coordination

When an incident involves a third-party AI system, the deployer should coordinate with the provider.

Coordination may involve:

* technical investigation;
* logs;
* system version;
* affected users;
* affected data;
* mitigation;
* corrective action;
* customer communications; and
* regulatory obligations.

### 50. AI-DEP-22 — Deployer Corrective Action

Corrective action should address the underlying cause of AI incidents.

Actions may include:

* disabling a feature;
* restricting access;
* changing configuration;
* retraining users;
* changing data;
* replacing a model;
* increasing human oversight;
* suspending deployment; or
* terminating the service.

Corrective action should be tracked through formal issue management.

### 51. Deployer Fundamental-Rights Governance

Certain AI deployments may significantly affect individuals or groups.

Governance should consider potential impacts involving:

* equality;
* non-discrimination;
* privacy;
* dignity;
* access to services;
* employment;
* education;
* safety;
* due process; and
* other applicable fundamental rights.

Where the AI Act requires a fundamental-rights impact assessment for a deployer, that legal requirement should be incorporated into the organization's governance process.

### 52. AI-DEP-23 — Fundamental-Rights Impact Assessment

Where applicable, the deployer should establish a controlled process for conducting and maintaining the required fundamental-rights impact assessment.

The assessment should consider:

* intended use;
* affected persons;
* foreseeable impacts;
* risk mitigation;
* human oversight;
* governance measures;
* monitoring; and
* residual risk.

### 53. Deployer Transparency

The deployer should ensure that users and affected persons receive information required by applicable law.

Transparency may involve:

* notifying individuals that they are interacting with AI;
* explaining relevant AI-supported processes;
* identifying AI-generated or manipulated content where required;
* providing instructions;
* documenting human involvement; and
* communicating applicable rights or complaint mechanisms.

### 54. AI-DEP-24 — Deployer Transparency Control

A deployer transparency control should verify:

* whether disclosure is legally required;
* who must receive it;
* when disclosure must occur;
* required wording or information;
* accessibility;
* consistency across channels;
* technical implementation; and
* evidence of implementation.

### 55. Deployer Compliance Gate

Before a material AI system enters production, the organization should confirm that:

* the deployer role is established;
* regulatory classification is documented;
* prohibited practices have been screened;
* risk assessment is complete;
* security assessment is complete;
* privacy requirements are addressed;
* provider documentation has been reviewed;
* human oversight is assigned;
* users have appropriate AI literacy;
* contractual requirements are complete;
* monitoring is configured;
* incident procedures are established; and
* required approvals have been obtained.

The production decision should be documented and traceable to the accountable business owner and relevant control functions.
