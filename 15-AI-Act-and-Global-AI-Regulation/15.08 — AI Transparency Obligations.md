# 15.08 — AI Transparency Obligations

# Part 1 — Foundations of AI Transparency Obligations

1. **Introduction to AI Transparency Obligations**

AI transparency is a core component of responsible AI governance and a specific regulatory requirement under the EU AI Act.

Transparency obligations are intended to ensure that relevant persons understand when they are interacting with, receiving content from, or being materially affected by an AI system where the AI Act requires disclosure.

Transparency should therefore be treated as a **control objective**, not merely as a communication or user-experience feature.

An enterprise transparency program should establish:

* what must be disclosed;
* to whom;
* when;
* through which mechanism;
* in what form;
* who is responsible;
* what evidence demonstrates compliance.

2. **Transparency as a Regulatory Control**

AI transparency should be distinguished from general principles such as explainability, accountability and ethical AI.

These concepts are related but not interchangeable.

For GRC purposes:

**Transparency** concerns communicating specified information about AI use or AI-generated/manipulated content.

**Explainability** concerns understanding how or why an AI system produces a result.

**Accountability** concerns assigning responsibility for decisions and outcomes.

**Interpretability** concerns the technical ability to understand model behavior.

A system may therefore satisfy a disclosure obligation while remaining technically difficult to interpret.

3. **EU AI Act Transparency Architecture**

The EU AI Act contains transparency requirements across multiple contexts rather than one universal transparency obligation.

Relevant provisions include:

* Article 13 for certain high-risk AI systems;
* Article 26 for certain deployer obligations;
* Article 50 for specified AI systems and AI-generated or manipulated content;
* other provisions addressing documentation, information and communication throughout the AI lifecycle.

The organization should therefore avoid creating a single generic "AI transparency" control and assuming that it satisfies every regulatory requirement.

4. **Transparency Obligation Mapping**

A mature GRC framework should map each AI use case to its applicable transparency requirements.

The mapping should consider:

* AI system classification;
* provider/deployer role;
* intended purpose;
* affected persons;
* users;
* deployment environment;
* type of AI output;
* whether people interact directly with the system;
* whether content is synthetically generated or manipulated;
* whether the system is high-risk;
* whether special disclosure rules apply.

The resulting assessment should identify the exact transparency controls applicable to the use case.

5. **Article 50 as a Key Transparency Layer**

Article 50 establishes specific transparency obligations for certain AI systems and AI-generated or manipulated content.

The relevant scenarios include:

* AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons;
* systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text;
* systems performing emotion recognition or biometric categorisation;
* systems generating or manipulating deepfake content;
* certain AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest.

These obligations differ according to the technology, purpose and circumstances of use.

6. **Transparency Does Not Mean Universal Disclosure**

The AI Act does not require every use of AI to carry the same disclosure.

For example, an organization should distinguish between:

* internal AI-assisted drafting;
* an AI chatbot directly interacting with customers;
* synthetic marketing imagery;
* AI-generated public-interest content;
* a deepfake;
* an emotion-recognition system;
* a high-risk AI system used in an employment process.

Each scenario may have different transparency requirements.

7. **Provider and Deployer Responsibilities**

Transparency responsibilities may fall on the provider, deployer or both depending on the applicable AI Act provision.

The organization should therefore determine:

1. Who provides the AI system?
2. Who deploys it?
3. Who controls the relevant user interaction?
4. Who generates or publishes the content?
5. Who has the technical capability to implement the disclosure?
6. Which party is legally responsible for the relevant obligation?

This allocation should be documented in the AI governance record.

8. **Transparency by Design**

Transparency should be incorporated into AI system architecture from the beginning.

Design requirements may include:

* AI disclosure interfaces;
* system-generated labels;
* metadata;
* content provenance;
* user notifications;
* machine-readable markings;
* audit logs;
* publication workflows;
* human-review gates.

Retrofitting transparency after deployment can be difficult, particularly where AI-generated content passes through multiple systems.

9. **Transparency Risk Assessment**

Each material AI system should undergo a transparency assessment.

The assessment should determine:

* whether a disclosure requirement applies;
* who must receive the disclosure;
* the timing;
* required content;
* delivery mechanism;
* applicable exceptions;
* accessibility requirements;
* technical implementation;
* monitoring;
* evidence.

A transparency assessment should be revisited after material changes to the AI system or its use.

10. **Direct Human Interaction**

Where an AI system is intended to interact directly with natural persons, the organization must assess whether the applicable AI Act transparency requirement applies.

A practical assessment should identify:

* chatbot interactions;
* voice assistants;
* customer-service agents;
* AI sales agents;
* virtual assistants;
* AI-powered support systems;
* conversational interfaces.

The organization should determine whether the person is likely to believe they are communicating with a natural person.

11. **AI Disclosure at First Interaction**

For systems covered by the relevant Article 50 requirement, users should generally be informed that they are interacting with an AI system.

The disclosure should occur in a timely manner, taking into account the circumstances and capabilities of the affected person.

From a GRC perspective, the organization should test the actual user journey rather than merely verify that disclosure language exists somewhere in the application's documentation.

12. **Obvious AI Interaction Exception**

The AI Act recognizes circumstances where it may be obvious from the circumstances and context that the interaction is with an AI system.

This does not justify automatically removing disclosure from every modern interface.

The organization should document why an interaction is considered sufficiently obvious and should consider:

* user population;
* interface design;
* branding;
* conversational behavior;
* context;
* accessibility;
* likelihood of confusion.

Legal interpretation should be obtained where the boundary is uncertain.

13. **Human-Readable Transparency**

Transparency information should be understandable to the intended recipient.

The organization should consider:

* language;
* terminology;
* readability;
* placement;
* timing;
* accessibility;
* user comprehension;
* cultural context.

A disclosure hidden in a lengthy privacy policy may not achieve the same practical transparency as an appropriate notice presented at the point of interaction.

14. **Accessibility of AI Disclosures**

AI transparency mechanisms should be designed to remain accessible to persons with disabilities.

Relevant considerations may include:

* screen-reader compatibility;
* accessible text;
* sufficient contrast;
* keyboard navigation;
* audio alternatives;
* captioning;
* understandable language.

Accessibility should be incorporated into transparency testing rather than treated as a separate afterthought.

15. **Synthetic Content Transparency**

AI-generated or manipulated content creates a separate transparency challenge.

Synthetic content may include:

* generated images;
* generated video;
* generated audio;
* synthetic speech;
* AI-generated music;
* generated text;
* manipulated media.

The organization should determine whether the applicable AI Act transparency requirement requires the content to be marked or otherwise disclosed.

16. **Machine-Readable Marking**

For certain AI-generated or manipulated content, the AI Act requires applicable providers to ensure outputs are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated.

This creates a technical control requirement rather than merely a user-interface requirement.

Potential implementation mechanisms may include:

* metadata;
* provenance information;
* embedded markers;
* cryptographic mechanisms;
* content credentials;
* standardized technical formats.

The implementation should preserve the marking through relevant processing and distribution stages where technically and legally required.

17. **Human-Readable Versus Machine-Readable Transparency**

These two mechanisms serve different purposes.

**Human-readable disclosure** is designed for people.

**Machine-readable marking** enables technical systems to identify AI-generated or manipulated content.

An organization should not assume that one automatically satisfies the other.

The control architecture should identify:

* human-facing disclosure;
* machine-readable marking;
* provenance;
* detection;
* validation.

18. **Marking Integrity**

AI-generated-content marking should be protected against accidental or deliberate removal.

The organization should consider:

* transformation of media;
* format conversion;
* compression;
* editing;
* transcoding;
* copying;
* publication platforms;
* third-party processing.

Where marking is required, the organization should test whether the marking survives the actual production workflow.

19. **Synthetic Audio Transparency**

AI-generated audio may create risks of:

* impersonation;
* fraud;
* misinformation;
* social engineering;
* unauthorized identity representation.

Where an applicable transparency obligation exists, organizations should ensure that synthetic audio is appropriately marked or disclosed.

Use cases should include:

* synthetic customer-service voices;
* generated announcements;
* AI-generated podcasts;
* synthetic political or public-interest communications;
* voice cloning.

20. **Synthetic Image Transparency**

AI-generated or manipulated images should be assessed for applicable marking or disclosure obligations.

Relevant use cases include:

* advertising;
* marketing;
* social-media publishing;
* product imagery;
* corporate communications;
* training material;
* public information.

The organization should distinguish ordinary image editing from AI-generated or AI-manipulated content where the AI Act establishes specific obligations.

21. **Synthetic Video Transparency**

AI-generated or manipulated video presents heightened transparency challenges because realistic video can create a strong perception of authenticity.

Governance should assess:

* fully synthetic video;
* face replacement;
* voice replacement;
* identity manipulation;
* AI-assisted video generation;
* realistic virtual persons.

The organization should establish controlled publishing processes for content subject to applicable disclosure obligations.

22. **Deepfake Governance**

Deepfakes are AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video content that can falsely appear authentic.

The AI Act establishes specific transparency requirements for deepfake content, subject to the applicable legal conditions and exceptions.

Organizations should therefore maintain a **deepfake assessment control** that evaluates:

* whether the content is artificially generated or manipulated;
* whether it depicts existing persons, objects or events;
* whether the content qualifies as a deepfake;
* whether an exception applies;
* what disclosure is required;
* who must implement it.

23. **Creative, Artistic and Fictional Content**

Transparency requirements concerning deepfakes recognize specific treatment for artistic, creative, satirical and fictional contexts.

This does not mean that all creative content is automatically exempt from every transparency requirement.

The organization should assess:

* purpose;
* context;
* audience;
* presentation;
* degree of manipulation;
* whether disclosure would interfere with the nature of the work;
* whether the applicable statutory condition for an exception is satisfied.

24. **Emotion Recognition Transparency**

AI systems that infer emotions or intentions from biometric data may be subject to specific transparency requirements.

Examples include systems attempting to infer:

* emotional state;
* stress;
* attention;
* engagement;
* behavioral tendencies.

Organizations should distinguish emotion recognition from:

* ordinary sentiment analysis;
* explicit user feedback;
* non-biometric text classification;
* conventional customer analytics.

The regulatory assessment should focus on the actual technical function and data used.

25. **Biometric Categorisation Transparency**

Certain biometric categorisation systems are subject to transparency obligations.

Governance should identify systems that classify natural persons based on biometric data into categories.

Potential categories may include characteristics related to:

* physical traits;
* behavioral characteristics;
* identity-related attributes.

The organization should conduct a legal assessment because biometric categorisation can intersect with both transparency and prohibited/high-risk AI requirements.

26. **Relationship Between Transparency and Prohibited AI**

Transparency does not legalize an otherwise prohibited AI practice.

If an AI practice falls within Article 5, simply informing the affected person does not make the practice permissible.

Therefore:

**Prohibition assessment → first**

**Transparency assessment → separately**

This distinction should be embedded into the AI governance workflow.

27. **Relationship Between Transparency and High-Risk AI**

A high-risk AI system may have transparency requirements that are different from Article 50 obligations.

High-risk governance can require information concerning:

* capabilities;
* limitations;
* intended purpose;
* operation;
* human oversight;
* performance;
* risks.

Article 50 transparency obligations may additionally apply depending on how the system interacts with people or generates/manipulates content.

Organizations should therefore map both layers.

28. **Transparency and Privacy**

AI transparency should not be confused with privacy compliance.

A transparency notice may explain:

> "You are interacting with an AI system."

A privacy notice may explain:

* what personal data is processed;
* why it is processed;
* legal basis;
* retention;
* data-sharing;
* rights.

Both may be necessary.

One should not be used as a substitute for the other.

29. **Transparency and Security**

Security controls should protect transparency mechanisms from manipulation.

Threats may include:

* removal of AI labels;
* spoofed provenance;
* false AI markings;
* metadata manipulation;
* disclosure suppression;
* interface tampering;
* unauthorized content publication.

Transparency controls should therefore be included in the security threat model.

30. **Transparency and Content Authenticity**

AI transparency increasingly interacts with content provenance and authenticity technologies.

A mature governance architecture may combine:

**AI Generation → Provenance → Machine-Readable Marking → Human Disclosure → Publication → Detection → Monitoring**

The objective is to preserve reliable information about the origin or AI involvement of content throughout its lifecycle.

31. **Public-Interest Text**

The AI Act contains specific transparency requirements for AI-generated or manipulated text that is published to inform the public on matters of public interest.

The organization should identify relevant activities such as:

* public announcements;
* news-like content;
* public policy information;
* crisis communications;
* public-interest reporting;
* informational websites.

The assessment should determine whether the statutory conditions apply.

32. **Human Review for Public-Interest Text**

The AI Act provides specific treatment where AI-generated or manipulated text has undergone human review or editorial control and a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility.

Organizations should therefore establish an evidence-based editorial process.

Evidence may include:

* named reviewer;
* review timestamp;
* source verification;
* edits;
* approval;
* publication authorization;
* version history.

33. **Editorial Responsibility**

Where AI is used in public-interest communications, the organization should clearly identify who is accountable for publication.

The presence of a human editor should not be assumed merely because a person clicked "publish."

Meaningful editorial responsibility should include appropriate review of:

* factual accuracy;
* context;
* material omissions;
* misleading content;
* harmful implications;
* AI-generated claims.

34. **Transparency Exceptions Governance**

Exceptions to transparency obligations should be controlled.

The organization should establish:

* exception criteria;
* legal interpretation;
* approval authority;
* evidence requirements;
* duration;
* review;
* reassessment triggers.

An exception should never be implemented solely because disclosure is inconvenient.

35. **Transparency Risk Register**

Material transparency risks should be recorded in the AI risk register.

Examples include:

* users unaware they are interacting with AI;
* synthetic content published without required marking;
* deepfake disclosure failure;
* broken machine-readable metadata;
* inaccessible disclosure;
* inaccurate transparency statement;
* vendor failure to provide required information;
* obsolete disclosure following model changes.

36. **Transparency Control Objectives**

A transparency control framework should pursue six primary objectives:

1. **Identification** — identify AI systems subject to transparency obligations.

2. **Classification** — determine which obligation applies.

3. **Implementation** — implement appropriate disclosure or marking.

4. **Integrity** — prevent or detect removal or manipulation.

5. **Monitoring** — verify that transparency remains effective.

6. **Evidence** — retain proof of compliance.

7. **Transparency by Lifecycle Stage**

Transparency should be assessed at every major lifecycle stage.

| Lifecycle Stage | Transparency Question        |
| --------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Design          | What disclosure is required? |
| Development     | How will it be implemented?  |
| Testing         | Does the disclosure work?    |
| Deployment      | Is it active?                |
| Operation       | Can users understand it?     |
| Change          | Has the obligation changed?  |
| Incident        | Did transparency fail?       |
| Retirement      | Are records retained?        |

38. **Transparency Inventory**

The enterprise AI inventory should contain transparency attributes.

Each system should identify:

* direct human interaction;
* synthetic content generation;
* emotion recognition;
* biometric categorisation;
* deepfake capability;
* public-interest content generation;
* high-risk status;
* provider/deployer role;
* applicable transparency provision;
* disclosure method;
* machine-readable marking;
* human-review requirement;
* responsible owner.

39. **Transparency Responsibility Matrix**

Responsibility should be allocated across functions.

| Function          | Typical Responsibility         |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------ |
| AI Governance     | Framework and applicability    |
| Legal             | Regulatory interpretation      |
| Product           | User-facing implementation     |
| Engineering       | Technical controls             |
| Security          | Integrity and abuse protection |
| Communications    | Public disclosure              |
| Content/Editorial | Human review                   |
| Compliance        | Control monitoring             |
| Internal Audit    | Independent assurance          |

40. **Transparency Control Design**

Each transparency control should specify:

* objective;
* applicable requirement;
* population;
* control activity;
* owner;
* frequency;
* evidence;
* test method;
* exception process;
* escalation threshold.

This allows transparency to be incorporated into the organization's broader GRC control library.

41. **Transparency Testing**

Testing should evaluate the actual user or content lifecycle.

Examples include:

* interacting with a chatbot as a first-time user;
* testing disclosure visibility;
* testing screen-reader accessibility;
* generating synthetic content;
* transferring marked content;
* editing marked content;
* publishing content;
* testing metadata persistence.

Testing should reproduce realistic operating conditions.

42. **Negative Testing**

Transparency controls should include negative tests designed to determine whether the control can fail.

Examples include:

* bypassing the AI disclosure;
* removing metadata;
* changing file formats;
* copying generated content;
* manipulating interface code;
* publishing through an unapproved channel;
* disabling the transparency feature.

Negative testing is particularly important where transparency relies on technical mechanisms.

43. **Transparency Incident Management**

A transparency failure should be treated as a potential compliance incident.

Examples include:

* missing AI disclosure;
* incorrect disclosure;
* broken machine-readable marking;
* undisclosed deepfake;
* publication without required transparency;
* inaccessible disclosure;
* vendor failure.

The incident should be assessed for regulatory, reputational, consumer, privacy and fundamental-rights implications.

44. **Transparency Evidence**

Evidence should demonstrate that transparency controls operated as intended.

Potential evidence includes:

* screenshots;
* interface configurations;
* system logs;
* metadata;
* provenance records;
* publication approvals;
* test results;
* accessibility tests;
* vendor attestations;
* incident records.

45. **Transparency Auditability**

A regulator or auditor should be able to trace:

**AI System → Applicable Requirement → Disclosure Control → Implementation → Test → Evidence → Owner**

The inability to establish this chain represents a governance weakness.

46. **Third-Party Transparency**

Where a third-party provider supplies AI functionality, the organization should determine which transparency obligations remain with the organization and which are implemented by the provider.

Contracts should address:

* disclosure functionality;
* marking;
* provenance;
* model changes;
* documentation;
* incident notification;
* evidence;
* service changes.

47. **Vendor Transparency Due Diligence**

Before adopting a third-party AI service, the organization should determine:

* whether the service provides required AI disclosures;
* whether disclosure can be configured;
* whether machine-readable marking is supported;
* whether provenance survives downstream processing;
* whether model changes can alter transparency obligations;
* whether the provider supplies sufficient documentation.

48. **Transparency Change Management**

Any material change to:

* model;
* user interface;
* output format;
* content pipeline;
* publication workflow;
* provider;
* intended purpose;
* target audience

should trigger a transparency reassessment.

A technically minor change may still create a significant compliance impact.

49. **Transparency Compliance Status**

Each applicable AI system should have an explicit transparency status:

* **Compliant**
* **Compliant with conditions**
* **Assessment required**
* **Remediation required**
* **Exception approved**
* **Restricted**
* **Blocked**

Status should be based on documented control criteria.

50. **Management Transparency Metrics**

Management reporting should include indicators such as:

* percentage of AI systems assessed for transparency;
* percentage with required disclosures implemented;
* percentage with tested machine-readable marking;
* number of transparency incidents;
* number of overdue remediation actions;
* number of third-party transparency deficiencies;
* accessibility-test coverage;
* percentage of material changes triggering reassessment.

51. **Transparency Governance Review**

The transparency framework should be reviewed periodically and after material regulatory, technical or operational changes.

Review triggers should include:

* new AI Act guidance;
* new technical standards;
* new AI capabilities;
* new content-generation workflows;
* major model upgrades;
* new jurisdictions;
* significant incidents.

52. **Regulatory Interpretation Governance**

Where the applicability of a transparency requirement is uncertain, the organization should document the legal interpretation.

The record should identify:

* factual scenario;
* applicable provision;
* relevant definitions;
* interpretation;
* assumptions;
* exceptions considered;
* evidence;
* decision;
* reviewer;
* date.

53. **Transparency and Fundamental Rights**

Transparency can contribute to the protection of fundamental rights by enabling people to understand when AI is involved in interactions or content.

However, transparency alone does not eliminate:

* discrimination;
* privacy violations;
* manipulation;
* inaccurate decisions;
* unsafe AI behavior;
* unlawful processing.

The organization should therefore treat transparency as one component of a broader fundamental-rights control framework.

54. **Transparency and Human Agency**

Effective transparency should support meaningful human agency.

Users should have sufficient awareness to determine:

* whether they are interacting with AI;
* whether content was generated or manipulated;
* whether they should independently verify information;
* whether additional human assistance is available where appropriate.

55. **Transparency as a Lifecycle Control**

The fundamental governance model should therefore be:

**Identify → Determine Obligation → Design Disclosure → Implement → Test → Monitor → Reassess → Evidence**

Transparency should remain active throughout the AI lifecycle rather than being treated as a one-time compliance exercise.

# Part 2 — AI Transparency Control Implementation and Compliance Management

56. **AI Transparency Applicability Assessment**

The organization should establish a standardized process for determining whether a transparency obligation applies to an AI system.

The assessment should begin with the system's:

* provider/deployer role;
* intended purpose;
* actual deployment;
* user population;
* affected persons;
* AI capabilities;
* output types;
* interaction mechanisms;
* content publication channels.

The assessment should then map the system against the relevant AI Act provisions.

57. **Transparency Decision Matrix**

A practical decision matrix should distinguish the principal scenarios.

| Scenario                             | Primary Assessment                              |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| AI directly interacts with people    | AI interaction disclosure                       |
| AI generates synthetic content       | Marking/disclosure assessment                   |
| AI generates/manipulates deepfakes   | Deepfake transparency                           |
| AI performs emotion recognition      | Specific disclosure                             |
| AI performs biometric categorisation | Specific disclosure                             |
| AI generates public-interest text    | Public-interest transparency                    |
| High-risk AI system                  | High-risk information/transparency requirements |
| Prohibited AI practice               | Prohibition assessment first                    |

The matrix should be integrated into the AI intake process.

58. **Transparency Role Determination**

The organization should formally identify whether it acts as:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* downstream provider;
* content publisher;
* application operator.

The applicable transparency responsibility should then be mapped to the relevant role.

59. **Transparency Requirement Register**

Each AI system should have a requirement register identifying:

* legal provision;
* obligation;
* responsible party;
* implementation mechanism;
* effective date;
* exception;
* control;
* evidence;
* testing method.

This creates traceability between law and operational implementation.

60. **AI Interaction Disclosure Control**

**Control AI-TR-01 — Direct AI Interaction Disclosure**

Where an applicable obligation exists, the system should inform natural persons that they are interacting with an AI system.

The control should verify:

* disclosure occurs at the appropriate point;
* the disclosure is understandable;
* it is not misleading;
* it is visible to the intended user;
* it remains active after system changes.

61. **First-Interaction Testing**

Testing should confirm the disclosure from the perspective of a new user.

Testers should determine:

* what the user sees first;
* whether AI involvement is apparent;
* whether disclosure occurs before or at the required point of interaction;
* whether the user can reasonably understand the disclosure;
* whether accessibility mechanisms function.

62. **Conversational Interface Control**

AI chat interfaces should have controlled disclosure behavior.

The organization should test:

* web chat;
* mobile applications;
* voice interfaces;
* messaging channels;
* embedded customer-service interfaces;
* AI agents.

The disclosure should not disappear merely because the conversation becomes lengthy or changes context.

63. **Voice AI Disclosure**

Voice-based AI systems require specific consideration because users may initially assume they are speaking with a human.

Testing should examine:

* first voice interaction;
* call transfers;
* interruptions;
* multilingual interaction;
* accessibility;
* disclosure after reconnecting;
* voice-agent upgrades.

64. **Accessibility Control**

**Control AI-TR-02 — Accessible AI Disclosure**

AI transparency information should be accessible to the intended population.

Testing should cover:

* screen readers;
* keyboard navigation;
* captions;
* alternative text;
* audio presentation;
* language accessibility;
* understandable terminology.

65. **Synthetic Content Marking Control**

**Control AI-TR-03 — Synthetic Content Marking**

Where the applicable AI Act requirement requires AI-generated or manipulated content to be marked, the organization should ensure that the marking is implemented in an appropriate machine-readable format.

The control should cover:

* generation;
* transformation;
* storage;
* transfer;
* publication.

66. **Marking at Generation**

The preferred control architecture is to mark content at the point of generation rather than attempting to identify it later.

The generation pipeline should associate:

* model identity;
* model version;
* generation timestamp;
* content identifier;
* applicable marking;
* provenance information.

67. **Machine-Readable Marking Validation**

Automated validation should verify that required markings exist.

Validation may occur:

* immediately after generation;
* before storage;
* before publication;
* after transformation;
* during content distribution.

Failures should generate an exception or publication block where appropriate.

68. **Content Provenance**

Provenance mechanisms should preserve relevant information concerning the origin and processing history of AI-generated or manipulated content.

Potential information includes:

* origin;
* generating system;
* model;
* creation time;
* modification history;
* responsible organization.

Provenance should not be treated as a substitute for all legally required transparency measures.

69. **Provenance Integrity**

Provenance controls should protect against:

* unauthorized alteration;
* deletion;
* spoofing;
* false attribution;
* metadata stripping.

Security controls should therefore be applied to provenance infrastructure.

70. **Content Transformation Testing**

The organization should test whether required markings survive:

* resizing;
* compression;
* transcoding;
* editing;
* format conversion;
* file transfer;
* content-management systems;
* social-media publication.

Where a transformation removes a required marking, the workflow should detect and remediate the problem.

71. **Human-Readable Synthetic Content Disclosure**

Where human-readable disclosure is required, it should be:

* clear;
* understandable;
* appropriately placed;
* sufficiently visible;
* available at the relevant point of consumption.

The organization should test disclosure in the actual user environment.

72. **Deepfake Assessment Control**

**Control AI-TR-04 — Deepfake Transparency Assessment**

The organization should assess whether AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video qualifies for the applicable deepfake transparency requirement.

The assessment should document:

* content type;
* AI involvement;
* degree of manipulation;
* depiction of persons, objects or events;
* purpose;
* publication context;
* applicable exception;
* required disclosure.

73. **Deepfake Publication Gate**

Potentially regulated deepfake content should pass a controlled publication gate.

The gate should verify:

* legal classification;
* disclosure;
* marking;
* provenance;
* human approval;
* publication channel.

Unresolved transparency questions should prevent publication where the risk is material.

74. **Creative-Content Exception Control**

Where creative, artistic, satirical or fictional treatment may affect the transparency requirement, the organization should document the applicable legal basis.

The assessment should identify:

* nature of the work;
* audience;
* context;
* statutory condition;
* disclosure impact;
* approval.

75. **Emotion Recognition Disclosure Control**

**Control AI-TR-05 — Emotion Recognition Transparency**

Where applicable, persons exposed to an emotion-recognition system should receive the required information.

The organization should document:

* biometric input;
* inferred characteristic;
* system purpose;
* affected persons;
* disclosure method;
* legal assessment;
* applicable exceptions.

76. **Biometric Categorisation Disclosure Control**

**Control AI-TR-06 — Biometric Categorisation Transparency**

Where applicable, the organization should disclose the use of biometric categorisation systems according to the AI Act requirements.

The assessment should distinguish biometric categorisation from:

* ordinary identity verification;
* authentication;
* non-biometric classification;
* statistical segmentation.

77. **Public-Interest Text Assessment**

**Control AI-TR-07 — Public-Interest Text Assessment**

Where AI-generated or manipulated text is intended to inform the public on matters of public interest, the organization should determine whether the Article 50 requirement applies.

The assessment should document:

* subject matter;
* intended audience;
* publication channel;
* AI involvement;
* human review;
* editorial control;
* publication responsibility.

78. **Editorial Review Control**

Where the applicable legal conditions depend on human review or editorial control, the organization should implement a documented review process.

The reviewer should assess, as appropriate:

* factual accuracy;
* source reliability;
* material AI-generated claims;
* misleading presentation;
* context;
* material omissions;
* publication suitability.

79. **Editorial Evidence**

The organization should retain evidence demonstrating that meaningful review occurred.

Evidence may include:

* reviewer identity;
* review timestamp;
* source material;
* tracked edits;

# Part 2 — AI Transparency Control Implementation and Compliance Management

56. **AI Transparency Applicability Assessment**

The organization should establish a standardized process for determining whether a transparency obligation applies to an AI system.

The assessment should begin with the system's:

* provider/deployer role;
* intended purpose;
* actual deployment;
* user population;
* affected persons;
* AI capabilities;
* output types;
* interaction mechanisms;
* content publication channels.

The assessment should then map the system against the relevant AI Act provisions.

57. **Transparency Decision Matrix**

A practical decision matrix should distinguish the principal scenarios.

| Scenario                             | Primary Assessment                              |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| AI directly interacts with people    | AI interaction disclosure                       |
| AI generates synthetic content       | Marking/disclosure assessment                   |
| AI generates/manipulates deepfakes   | Deepfake transparency                           |
| AI performs emotion recognition      | Specific disclosure                             |
| AI performs biometric categorisation | Specific disclosure                             |
| AI generates public-interest text    | Public-interest transparency                    |
| High-risk AI system                  | High-risk information/transparency requirements |
| Prohibited AI practice               | Prohibition assessment first                    |

The matrix should be integrated into the AI intake process.

58. **Transparency Role Determination**

The organization should formally identify whether it acts as:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* downstream provider;
* content publisher;
* application operator.

The applicable transparency responsibility should then be mapped to the relevant role.

59. **Transparency Requirement Register**

Each AI system should have a requirement register identifying:

* legal provision;
* obligation;
* responsible party;
* implementation mechanism;
* effective date;
* exception;
* control;
* evidence;
* testing method.

This creates traceability between law and operational implementation.

60. **AI Interaction Disclosure Control**

**Control AI-TR-01 — Direct AI Interaction Disclosure**

Where an applicable obligation exists, the system should inform natural persons that they are interacting with an AI system.

The control should verify:

* disclosure occurs at the appropriate point;
* the disclosure is understandable;
* it is not misleading;
* it is visible to the intended user;
* it remains active after system changes.

61. **First-Interaction Testing**

Testing should confirm the disclosure from the perspective of a new user.

Testers should determine:

* what the user sees first;
* whether AI involvement is apparent;
* whether disclosure occurs before or at the required point of interaction;
* whether the user can reasonably understand the disclosure;
* whether accessibility mechanisms function.

62. **Conversational Interface Control**

AI chat interfaces should have controlled disclosure behavior.

The organization should test:

* web chat;
* mobile applications;
* voice interfaces;
* messaging channels;
* embedded customer-service interfaces;
* AI agents.

The disclosure should not disappear merely because the conversation becomes lengthy or changes context.

63. **Voice AI Disclosure**

Voice-based AI systems require specific consideration because users may initially assume they are speaking with a human.

Testing should examine:

* first voice interaction;
* call transfers;
* interruptions;
* multilingual interaction;
* accessibility;
* disclosure after reconnecting;
* voice-agent upgrades.

64. **Accessibility Control**

**Control AI-TR-02 — Accessible AI Disclosure**

AI transparency information should be accessible to the intended population.

Testing should cover:

* screen readers;
* keyboard navigation;
* captions;
* alternative text;
* audio presentation;
* language accessibility;
* understandable terminology.

65. **Synthetic Content Marking Control**

**Control AI-TR-03 — Synthetic Content Marking**

Where the applicable AI Act requirement requires AI-generated or manipulated content to be marked, the organization should ensure that the marking is implemented in an appropriate machine-readable format.

The control should cover:

* generation;
* transformation;
* storage;
* transfer;
* publication.

66. **Marking at Generation**

The preferred control architecture is to mark content at the point of generation rather than attempting to identify it later.

The generation pipeline should associate:

* model identity;
* model version;
* generation timestamp;
* content identifier;
* applicable marking;
* provenance information.

67. **Machine-Readable Marking Validation**

Automated validation should verify that required markings exist.

Validation may occur:

* immediately after generation;
* before storage;
* before publication;
* after transformation;
* during content distribution.

Failures should generate an exception or publication block where appropriate.

68. **Content Provenance**

Provenance mechanisms should preserve relevant information concerning the origin and processing history of AI-generated or manipulated content.

Potential information includes:

* origin;
* generating system;
* model;
* creation time;
* modification history;
* responsible organization.

Provenance should not be treated as a substitute for all legally required transparency measures.

69. **Provenance Integrity**

Provenance controls should protect against:

* unauthorized alteration;
* deletion;
* spoofing;
* false attribution;
* metadata stripping.

Security controls should therefore be applied to provenance infrastructure.

70. **Content Transformation Testing**

The organization should test whether required markings survive:

* resizing;
* compression;
* transcoding;
* editing;
* format conversion;
* file transfer;
* content-management systems;
* social-media publication.

Where a transformation removes a required marking, the workflow should detect and remediate the problem.

71. **Human-Readable Synthetic Content Disclosure**

Where human-readable disclosure is required, it should be:

* clear;
* understandable;
* appropriately placed;
* sufficiently visible;
* available at the relevant point of consumption.

The organization should test disclosure in the actual user environment.

72. **Deepfake Assessment Control**

**Control AI-TR-04 — Deepfake Transparency Assessment**

The organization should assess whether AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video qualifies for the applicable deepfake transparency requirement.

The assessment should document:

* content type;
* AI involvement;
* degree of manipulation;
* depiction of persons, objects or events;
* purpose;
* publication context;
* applicable exception;
* required disclosure.

102. **Transparency Compliance Evidence Chain**

The organization should maintain:

**Requirement → Applicability → Control → Implementation → Test → Evidence → Finding → Remediation**

This chain should be available for each material transparency obligation.

103. **Transparency Control Testing Program**

Testing should cover both:

**Design effectiveness**

and:

**Operating effectiveness.**

Design testing determines whether the control is capable of satisfying the requirement.

Operating testing determines whether it actually operated during the review period.

104. **Sampling Methodology**

Sampling should consider:

* AI system criticality;
* number of interactions;
* content volume;
* publication channels;
* geographic scope;
* model versions;
* incident history.

High-risk or high-volume systems should receive increased testing.

105. **Automated Compliance Testing**

Automated testing can continuously verify:

* required disclosure enabled;
* marking present;
* provenance available;
* correct model version;
* publication gate active;
* approved configuration deployed.

Automation should produce evidence suitable for GRC review.

106. **Manual Compliance Testing**

Manual testing remains useful for assessing:

* user comprehension;
* disclosure clarity;
* accessibility;
* actual interface behavior;
* editorial judgment;
* exception applicability.

Technical automation cannot fully replace human assessment of whether a disclosure is understandable.

107. **User-Comprehension Testing**

Where transparency effectiveness depends on user understanding, organizations should conduct user testing.

Testing may ask whether users understand:

* they are interacting with AI;
* content was AI-generated;
* content was manipulated;
* a human reviewed the content;
* what the disclosure means.

108. **Transparency Effectiveness**

A disclosure should not be considered effective merely because it exists.

Effectiveness should consider:

**Visibility + Timing + Clarity + Accessibility + Integrity + User Understanding**

This provides a more meaningful control objective.

109. **Transparency Dashboard**

The GRC dashboard should display:

* systems requiring transparency controls;
* compliant systems;
* systems under remediation;
* disclosure failures;
* marking failures;
* provenance failures;
* vendor deficiencies;
* open exceptions;
* overdue reviews;
* transparency incidents.

111. **Control Ownership**

Each transparency control should have:

* accountable owner;
* responsible operator;
* reviewer;
* evidence owner;
* testing owner;
* escalation authority.

This prevents transparency controls from becoming organizationally ambiguous.

112. **Segregation of Duties**

For material transparency controls, responsibility should be appropriately separated.

For example:

**Engineering**
implements the mechanism.

**Product**
owns user experience.

**Legal**
interprets applicability.

**Compliance**
monitors control effectiveness.

**Internal Audit**
provides independent assurance.

113. **Transparency Change-Impact Assessment**

Material changes should trigger an impact assessment covering:

* new AI capability;
* new content type;
* new user group;
* new jurisdiction;
* new publication channel;
* new provider;
* new model;
* new integration.

The assessment should determine whether existing transparency controls remain adequate.

114. **Transparency Release Gate**

Before production release, the organization should verify:

* transparency assessment completed;
* required disclosure implemented;
* machine-readable marking tested where applicable;
* accessibility tested;
* provenance tested;
* exceptions approved;
* evidence captured.

115. **Transparency Deployment Blocking**

Deployment should be blocked when:

* a mandatory disclosure is missing;
* required marking is not functioning;
* material transparency testing has failed;
* legal applicability is unresolved for a material use case;
* required human review cannot be implemented;
* a critical vendor transparency dependency has failed.

116. **Transparency Production Monitoring**

After deployment, monitoring should verify:

* disclosure remains enabled;
* content marking remains functional;
* metadata survives processing;
* publication controls remain active;
* vendor functionality remains consistent.

117. **Transparency Review Frequency**

Periodic reviews should be risk-based.

Higher-risk systems should receive more frequent reviews, especially where they:

* interact with large populations;
* generate public content;
* generate realistic synthetic media;
* involve biometric technologies;
* support material business processes.

118. **Transparency Reassessment Triggers**

Immediate reassessment should occur following:

* major model upgrade;
* new AI modality;
* new content type;
* significant UI change;
* new provider;
* new publication channel;
* regulatory change;
* material transparency incident.

119. **Transparency Regulatory Change Control**

When a regulatory requirement changes, the organization should determine:

* affected AI systems;
* affected controls;
* policy changes;
* technical changes;
* testing requirements;
* contractual changes;
* implementation deadline.

120. **Transparency Regulatory Evidence**

Regulatory assessments should retain:

* legal interpretation;
* source provision;
* applicability analysis;
* control mapping;
* implementation evidence;
* test results;
* approval.

121. **Transparency Compliance Attestation**

For material AI systems, management may periodically attest that:

* applicable transparency obligations were assessed;
* controls remain implemented;
* material incidents were disclosed internally;
* known exceptions are documented;
* material changes were reassessed.

The attestation should be supported by evidence.

122. **Transparency Assurance**

Independent assurance should evaluate:

* completeness of the AI inventory;
* correctness of applicability assessments;
* control design;
* control operation;
* evidence quality;
* exception management;
* incident handling.

123. **Internal Audit Testing**

Internal Audit may select AI systems and verify:

**Inventory → Legal Assessment → Control → Configuration → Actual User Experience → Evidence → Monitoring**

This provides assurance that documented compliance corresponds to actual operation.

124. **Transparency Audit Findings**

Audit findings should be classified according to:

* regulatory impact;
* affected population;
* duration;
* frequency;
* likelihood;
* severity;
* compensating controls.

125. **Remediation Governance**

Each material finding should have:

* owner;
* action plan;
* target date;
* risk rating;
* interim control;
* validation;
* closure approval.

126. **Transparency Regulatory Examination Readiness**

The organization should be able to demonstrate:

* which systems are subject to transparency requirements;
* which provisions apply;
* how disclosures are implemented;
* how content is marked;
* how exceptions are justified;
* how controls are tested;
* how incidents are handled.

127. **Evidence Retrieval Testing**

The organization should periodically test whether transparency evidence can be retrieved quickly.

A useful test is:

> Select a model version at random and reconstruct the transparency decision, implementation and testing evidence without relying on undocumented institutional knowledge.

128. **Transparency Records Management**

Transparency records should be maintained under controlled retention and access requirements.

Records should remain linked to the relevant:

* AI system;
* model;
* version;
* deployment;
* publication;
* user-facing implementation.

129. **Transparency Data Lineage**

Where transparency depends on metadata or provenance, the organization should maintain data lineage.

Example:

**Model → Generated Artifact → Metadata → Content Management → Distribution → Publication**

Each transition should be evaluated for potential loss of transparency information.

130. **Transparency Supply-Chain Testing**

Where multiple vendors process AI-generated content, the organization should test the complete supply chain.

Testing should determine whether required disclosure or marking survives:

* generation;
* storage;
* transformation;
* distribution;
* publication.

131. **Transparency Control Failure Escalation**

A systemic failure affecting a material population should be escalated beyond the individual application owner.

Escalation should involve:

* AI Governance;
* Compliance;
* Legal;
* Security where integrity is affected;
* executive management where material.

132. **Transparency Governance Committee Review**

The AI Governance Committee should periodically review:

* transparency incidents;
* major exceptions;
* regulatory changes;
* high-volume disclosure failures;
* vendor deficiencies;
* audit findings;
* systemic control weaknesses.

133. **Transparency Program Metrics**

Program metrics should include both compliance and effectiveness measures.

Examples:

* disclosure implementation rate;
* marking validation success rate;
* provenance integrity rate;
* accessibility-test coverage;
* user-comprehension results;
* transparency incident frequency;
* remediation time;
* exception aging.

134. **Transparency Operational Readiness**

An AI system should not be considered operationally ready until:

* applicable transparency obligations are identified;
* required disclosures are implemented;
* technical marking is validated where applicable;
* accessibility is tested;
* exceptions are approved;
* monitoring is active;
* evidence is available;
* ownership is established.

135. **Transparency Continuous-Control Model**

The operational transparency control loop should be:

**Identify → Assess → Implement → Validate → Monitor → Detect → Correct → Reassess**

The control loop should operate continuously throughout the AI system lifecycle rather than only at initial deployment.
