# 15.10 — Provider Obligations

## Part 1 — Foundations, Provider Status, and Regulatory Responsibility

### 1. Provider Obligations as a Core AI Governance Domain

Provider obligations are among the most important accountability mechanisms established by the EU AI Act.

The regulatory responsibilities of an organization depend significantly on the role it performs within the AI value chain. An organization may be:

* a provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* product manufacturer;
* authorized representative;
* provider of a general-purpose AI model; or
* more than one of these simultaneously.

The organization must therefore determine its legal role before determining its compliance obligations.

Provider status is particularly important because providers can carry extensive responsibilities for ensuring that regulated AI systems are designed, developed, documented, assessed, placed on the market, operated, monitored, and corrected in accordance with applicable requirements.

### 2. Meaning of an AI System Provider

Under the EU AI Act, a provider is broadly the natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body that:

* develops an AI system or a general-purpose AI model; or
* has an AI system or general-purpose AI model developed;

and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge.

Provider status therefore does not depend exclusively on who wrote the software.

An organization may become the provider even where substantial technical development was outsourced.

### 3. Development Does Not Determine Provider Status Alone

A common governance mistake is to assume:

> “We did not build the model, therefore we cannot be the provider.”

This is not necessarily correct.

An organization can procure:

* external models;
* cloud AI services;
* machine-learning components;
* software libraries;
* training services;
* system integration;
* development services; or
* complete AI applications

and still potentially act as the provider of the resulting AI system.

Legal-role assessment should therefore consider the complete commercialization and deployment arrangement rather than simply identifying the original developer.

### 4. Provider Status and Own Name or Trademark

Placing an AI system on the market or putting it into service under an organization's own name or trademark can be a significant element of provider status.

This is particularly important for:

* white-label AI products;
* outsourced AI development;
* embedded AI;
* customized vendor systems;
* SaaS products incorporating third-party models;
* private-label AI services; and
* enterprise-developed AI systems.

Organizations should therefore include provider-role analysis in product and procurement governance.

### 5. Provider Versus Deployer

Provider and deployer are distinct regulatory roles.

A **provider** generally develops or has an AI system developed and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

A **deployer** generally uses an AI system under its authority, except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity.

An enterprise can therefore purchase an AI system from another organization and operate as its deployer.

However, modifications, rebranding, changes of intended purpose, or other circumstances may alter the organization's regulatory role.

### 6. Provider and Deployer Roles Can Coexist

An organization can simultaneously perform multiple AI Act roles.

For example, a company may:

* provide an AI platform to customers;
* deploy the same platform internally;
* integrate a third-party GPAI model;
* import another AI component;
* distribute another provider's AI system; and
* develop its own internal AI applications.

AI governance should therefore determine roles **per AI system and per activity**, not assign one permanent legal role to the entire enterprise.

### 7. Provider Status Is System-Specific

The enterprise AI inventory should record provider status at the individual AI-system level.

For example:

| AI asset                                          | Organizational role                    |
| ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Internal purchased chatbot                        | Deployer                               |
| AI product developed for customers                | Provider                               |
| White-label AI application                        | Potential provider                     |
| Imported third-party AI system                    | Importer/deployer                      |
| Proprietary foundation model                      | GPAI provider where applicable         |
| Third-party model integrated into company product | Requires value-chain/provider analysis |

The role determination should be documented and periodically reassessed.

### 8. Provider Status and Outsourced Development

Outsourcing development does not necessarily transfer regulatory responsibility.

A company may commission a contractor to build an AI system while retaining:

* product ownership;
* intended-purpose authority;
* branding;
* deployment authority;
* commercialization rights; and
* control over placing the system on the market.

The contracting organization may therefore remain the provider.

Contracts should clearly distinguish technical development responsibilities from regulatory accountability.

### 9. Provider Status and Internal AI Systems

Provider status can also arise when an organization develops an AI system and puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

Organizations should therefore not assume that the absence of external customers eliminates provider obligations.

Internal AI development should be included in regulatory-role assessment.

### 10. Provider Status and AI Supply Chains

Modern AI systems frequently involve complex supply chains.

A single AI service may involve:

**data provider → GPAI provider → cloud provider → application developer → system integrator → enterprise provider → deployer → end user.**

The existence of upstream providers does not automatically eliminate downstream provider responsibilities.

Each organization must determine its own role.

### 11. Provider Status and General-Purpose AI

The AI Act distinguishes between:

* providers of AI systems; and
* providers of general-purpose AI models.

GPAI providers are subject to a distinct regulatory framework, including obligations concerning technical documentation, downstream information, copyright policy, and information concerning training content, with additional requirements for GPAI models with systemic risk.

GPAI provider obligations have applied since **2 August 2025**, subject to applicable transitional arrangements for models already placed on the market.

Organizations should therefore avoid assuming that the high-risk AI provider framework and the GPAI provider framework are identical.

### 12. Provider Status and Significant Modification

An organization that did not originally provide an AI system can potentially acquire provider responsibilities through its subsequent actions.

This makes **significant modification** an important AI governance issue.

Organizations modifying third-party AI systems should determine whether the modification affects:

* intended purpose;
* regulatory classification;
* system behavior;
* performance;
* safety;
* fundamental-rights risk;
* compliance characteristics; or
* other legally relevant characteristics.

Modification governance should therefore include regulatory-role reassessment.

### 13. Provider Status Through Changes to Intended Purpose

Changes to intended purpose can materially affect regulatory responsibility.

For example, an AI system originally designed for ordinary document classification might later be adapted for:

* employee evaluation;
* credit decisions;
* educational admissions;
* biometric processing; or
* another regulated use.

The downstream organization cannot assume that the original provider's classification and compliance assessment remain sufficient.

Change-of-purpose governance should trigger a new AI Act assessment.

### 14. Provider Status and Rebranding

Organizations should assess provider implications before rebranding third-party AI systems.

This is particularly relevant where an organization:

* removes the original provider's branding;
* sells the system under its own brand;
* bundles the system into another product;
* markets the AI functionality as its own;
* modifies instructions;
* changes intended purpose; or
* controls the final product configuration.

Legal review should occur before commercialization.

### 15. Provider Determination Control

The organization should establish a formal control:

**AI-PRV-01 — Provider Role Determination**

Control objective:

> Determine and document whether the organization acts as a provider for each AI system or GPAI model before placing it on the market or putting it into service.

The control should operate during:

* AI intake;
* product design;
* procurement;
* acquisition;
* development;
* integration;
* commercialization;
* material modification; and
* change of intended purpose.

### 16. Provider Role Determination Record

The provider determination should document:

* AI system identifier;
* system owner;
* developer;
* commissioning organization;
* model provider;
* product owner;
* trademark or branding;
* intended purpose;
* market-placement arrangement;
* internal-service arrangement;
* modifications;
* downstream users;
* contractual relationships;
* legal analysis;
* determined regulatory role;
* approver; and
* review date.

This becomes part of the system's regulatory evidence package.

### 17. Provider Obligations Are Risk-Dependent

Provider obligations are not identical for every AI system.

The applicable requirements depend on factors such as:

* whether the system is prohibited;
* whether it is high-risk;
* whether Article 50 transparency obligations apply;
* whether a GPAI model is involved;
* whether the GPAI model presents systemic risk;
* whether sector-specific legislation applies;
* whether product-safety legislation applies; and
* when the relevant provisions become applicable.

The organization should therefore perform **obligation mapping after role determination and regulatory classification**.

### 18. Provider Obligations for High-Risk AI

Providers of high-risk AI systems carry extensive obligations.

These include, where applicable, ensuring compliance with requirements concerning:

* risk management;
* data and data governance;
* technical documentation;
* record keeping;
* transparency and instructions for use;
* human oversight;
* accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity;
* quality management;
* conformity assessment;
* registration;
* corrective action;
* cooperation with authorities;
* post-market monitoring; and
* serious-incident reporting.

The Commission describes providers as remaining responsible for the safety and compliance of high-risk AI systems throughout their lifecycle.

### 19. High-Risk Provider Obligations and Article 16

Article 16 forms a central part of the provider accountability framework for high-risk AI systems.

The provider must ensure that applicable high-risk AI requirements are satisfied before and after market placement or putting the system into service.

Provider compliance should therefore be treated as an end-to-end lifecycle responsibility rather than a one-time certification exercise.

### 20. Provider Responsibility for Compliance by Design

Provider obligations begin during design and development.

A provider should not attempt to create compliance documentation only after technical development has been completed.

Requirements should be translated into engineering and governance controls during:

* requirements definition;
* architecture;
* data selection;
* model development;
* testing;
* validation;
* cybersecurity design;
* human-oversight design;
* documentation; and
* release management.

This approach can be described as **AI compliance by design**.

### 21. Provider Responsibility for Risk Management

High-risk AI systems require a risk-management system.

The provider should establish a continuous iterative process addressing risks throughout the lifecycle.

The risk-management process should identify and address reasonably foreseeable risks associated with:

* intended use;
* reasonably foreseeable misuse;
* health;
* safety;
* fundamental rights;
* system performance;
* human interaction;
* cybersecurity;
* data;
* operational environment; and
* post-market experience.

Risk management should be integrated into product development rather than maintained as a separate compliance document.

### 22. Provider Responsibility for Data Governance

Where relevant high-risk requirements apply, providers must establish appropriate data and data-governance practices.

Governance may need to address:

* data collection;
* data preparation;
* labeling;
* cleaning;
* updating;
* enrichment;
* aggregation;
* assumptions;
* availability;
* quantity;
* suitability;
* representativeness;
* bias;
* data gaps; and
* statistical characteristics.

Provider governance should preserve evidence explaining how data-related decisions were made.

### 23. Provider Responsibility for Technical Documentation

Providers of high-risk AI systems must prepare technical documentation in accordance with applicable AI Act requirements.

Technical documentation supports:

* conformity assessment;
* regulatory review;
* system understanding;
* accountability;
* change management;
* incident investigation; and
* post-market monitoring.

Documentation should be created throughout development rather than reconstructed after deployment.

### 24. Provider Responsibility for Record Keeping

High-risk AI systems must support appropriate logging capabilities.

Providers should ensure that system design enables the automatic recording of events where required.

Logging should support:

* traceability;
* monitoring;
* investigation;
* human oversight;
* incident analysis;
* performance evaluation; and
* regulatory evidence.

Logging requirements should be considered during architecture design.

### 25. Provider Responsibility for Transparency

Providers must ensure that relevant AI systems satisfy applicable transparency requirements.

For high-risk AI, this includes providing sufficient information and instructions to enable deployers to understand and use the system appropriately.

Provider transparency may therefore include information concerning:

* intended purpose;
* capabilities;
* limitations;
* performance;
* human oversight;
* required inputs;
* expected outputs;
* foreseeable circumstances of misuse;
* maintenance;
* monitoring; and
* operational requirements.

Transparency should support safe downstream operation.

### 26. Provider Responsibility for Instructions for Use

Instructions for use are a significant provider control.

Instructions should be:

* accurate;
* understandable;
* complete;
* accessible;
* consistent with intended purpose;
* aligned with tested capabilities; and
* maintained when the system changes.

Instructions should not overstate system capabilities or conceal material limitations.

### 27. Provider Responsibility for Human Oversight Design

Providers of high-risk AI systems should design systems so that appropriate human oversight can be implemented.

The provider should consider:

* what humans need to understand;
* what information they receive;
* when intervention is possible;
* how the system can be overridden;
* when operation should be stopped;
* how automation bias can be reduced; and
* what competencies human overseers require.

Human oversight should be technically meaningful rather than purely procedural.

### 28. Provider Responsibility for Accuracy

Providers should establish and document appropriate levels of accuracy for regulated high-risk systems.

Accuracy governance should consider:

* intended purpose;
* relevant populations;
* operating conditions;
* error rates;
* false positives;
* false negatives;
* performance degradation;
* monitoring;
* testing; and
* known limitations.

Accuracy should not be communicated through misleading or unsupported performance claims.

### 29. Provider Responsibility for Robustness

Providers should design high-risk AI systems with appropriate robustness.

Robustness includes the system's ability to maintain appropriate performance when confronted with:

* expected variation;
* errors;
* faults;
* environmental changes;
* unusual inputs;
* system dependencies; and
* reasonably foreseeable operational conditions.

Testing should reflect actual deployment environments where practicable.

### 30. Provider Responsibility for Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is a direct component of high-risk AI provider compliance.

Provider security governance should consider AI-specific threats such as:

* data poisoning;
* model manipulation;
* adversarial inputs;
* model extraction;
* model inversion;
* prompt injection;
* malicious integrations;
* supply-chain compromise;
* unauthorized model access;
* credential compromise; and
* manipulation of AI outputs.

Security controls should correspond to the system's risks and architecture.

### 31. Provider Quality Management System

Providers of high-risk AI systems are required to establish a quality management system appropriate to the applicable AI Act requirements.

The QMS provides the organizational framework for ensuring consistent compliance throughout the AI lifecycle.

It should connect:

* regulatory strategy;
* design controls;
* development;
* testing;
* risk management;
* data governance;
* documentation;
* conformity assessment;
* change management;
* incident handling;
* post-market monitoring; and
* corrective action.

The Commission's standardisation work specifically identifies Article 17 quality-management requirements as an important implementation area.

### 32. Quality Management Is More Than Documentation

A provider QMS should not consist solely of policies stored in a repository.

The QMS should establish operational processes defining:

* who performs activities;
* who approves them;
* what evidence is required;
* what controls apply;
* what happens when controls fail;
* how changes are managed;
* how incidents are escalated; and
* how management verifies effectiveness.

The system should produce evidence of actual operation.

### 33. Provider Conformity Assessment Responsibility

Where conformity assessment is required, the provider must ensure that the relevant procedure is completed before the regulated AI system is placed on the market or put into service.

The applicable assessment pathway depends on the system and applicable legal framework.

Provider governance should therefore determine:

* applicable conformity-assessment procedure;
* internal versus third-party assessment requirements;
* applicable harmonised standards or common specifications;
* technical evidence;
* testing requirements;
* notified-body involvement where applicable; and
* required approvals.

### 34. EU Declaration of Conformity

Where required, the provider must draw up an EU declaration of conformity.

The declaration represents a formal statement that applicable requirements have been addressed.

Governance should control:

* preparation;
* approval;
* signing authority;
* versioning;
* retention;
* applicable legislation;
* system identification; and
* supporting evidence.

A declaration should never be issued before the required compliance evidence exists.

### 35. CE Marking

Where applicable under the AI Act framework, the provider must ensure that required CE marking is affixed appropriately.

CE marking should be connected to:

* conformity assessment;
* technical documentation;
* declaration of conformity;
* system identification; and
* release approval.

Product teams should not treat CE marking as merely a marketing label.

### 36. Registration of High-Risk AI Systems

Providers of applicable high-risk AI systems must register required information in the EU database before relevant market placement or putting into service.

The Commission emphasizes registration as part of the transparency and supervision framework for high-risk AI.

Registration should therefore be incorporated into the provider deployment gate.

### 37. Provider Identification

Providers should ensure that required identifying information accompanies regulated AI systems.

This supports:

* traceability;
* regulatory supervision;
* downstream accountability;
* incident investigation; and
* market surveillance.

Organizational identity, contact information, system identification, and applicable regulatory information should remain accurate.

### 38. Provider Responsibility After Market Placement

Provider responsibility does not end when the AI system is released.

Post-market responsibilities can include:

* monitoring;
* collecting operational information;
* analyzing performance;
* identifying emerging risks;
* managing incidents;
* correcting non-conformities;
* cooperating with authorities;
* updating documentation; and
* implementing corrective actions.

The provider should therefore maintain operational governance throughout the system lifecycle.

### 39. Post-Market Monitoring

Providers of high-risk AI systems must establish a post-market monitoring system appropriate to the nature of the AI technology and its risks.

The monitoring framework should collect and analyze relevant information concerning system performance throughout its lifetime.

Monitoring should identify:

* unexpected behavior;
* performance degradation;
* new risks;
* misuse patterns;
* security vulnerabilities;
* human-oversight problems;
* data drift;
* incidents; and
* compliance failures.

### 40. Provider Responsibility for Corrective Action

When a provider has reason to consider that a regulated AI system is not in conformity, appropriate corrective action should be initiated.

Depending on circumstances, action may include:

* correcting the system;
* restricting use;
* disabling functionality;
* withdrawing the system;
* recalling the system;
* updating instructions;
* notifying affected parties;
* updating documentation; or
* escalating to competent authorities.

Corrective action should be proportionate to the identified risk.

### 41. Serious-Incident Responsibilities

Providers of high-risk AI systems have responsibilities concerning serious incidents under the AI Act.

The incident-management capability should therefore determine:

* whether an event is a serious incident;
* when it became known;
* applicable reporting timelines;
* competent authority;
* investigation requirements;
* evidence preservation;
* corrective action; and
* follow-up reporting.

The Commission has developed guidance and reporting mechanisms specifically for serious incidents involving high-risk AI systems.

### 42. Provider Cooperation with Authorities

Providers should establish procedures for cooperating with competent authorities.

This may include responding to requests concerning:

* technical documentation;
* conformity evidence;
* logs;
* risk assessments;
* testing;
* quality management;
* incidents;
* corrective actions; and
* post-market monitoring.

Regulatory-response responsibilities should be assigned before an authority request occurs.

### 43. Provider Regulatory Evidence

Provider compliance should be evidence-based.

Relevant evidence may include:

* provider-role assessment;
* regulatory classification;
* risk-management records;
* data-governance records;
* technical documentation;
* test results;
* security assessments;
* human-oversight design;
* instructions for use;
* QMS records;
* conformity-assessment evidence;
* declaration of conformity;
* registration;
* post-market monitoring;
* incident records;
* corrective actions; and
* regulatory communications.

Evidence should be traceable to the relevant system version.

### 44. Provider Obligations and Version Control

AI systems change frequently.

Provider governance should maintain traceability between:

* system version;
* model version;
* dataset version;
* technical documentation;
* testing;
* risk assessment;
* instructions;
* conformity assessment;
* declaration;
* registration;
* monitoring; and
* incidents.

Version mismatches can undermine regulatory evidence.

### 45. Provider Obligations and Change Management

Material AI changes should trigger provider compliance reassessment.

Changes may include:

* new model;
* new training data;
* new intended purpose;
* expanded capabilities;
* increased autonomy;
* new integration;
* new user population;
* new geography;
* new security architecture; or
* changed performance.

The organization should determine whether existing conformity evidence remains valid.

### 46. Provider Obligations and Third-Party Components

Providers should govern third-party components used within their AI systems.

Relevant components may include:

* GPAI models;
* datasets;
* APIs;
* cloud services;
* libraries;
* plugins;
* AI agents;
* security tools; and
* external software.

The provider should understand how third-party dependencies affect its ability to demonstrate compliance.

### 47. Provider Dependency Mapping

Each material AI system should maintain a dependency map showing:

**provider system → models → data → software → infrastructure → APIs → external services → downstream deployment.**

The map supports:

* supply-chain risk management;
* security;
* change impact;
* incident investigation;
* regulatory responsibility; and
* business continuity.

### 48. Provider and Downstream Information

Providers should ensure that downstream actors receive information necessary to fulfil their responsibilities where required.

Information may concern:

* intended purpose;
* operating conditions;
* limitations;
* required human oversight;
* data requirements;
* performance;
* monitoring;
* security;
* maintenance;
* known risks; and
* changes.

Information should remain synchronized with the actual system.

### 49. Provider and Contractual Governance

Contracts should support, rather than undermine, provider compliance.

Contracts with developers, model providers, integrators, cloud providers, and other suppliers should address where relevant:

* access to technical information;
* change notification;
* security requirements;
* incident notification;
* audit rights;
* regulatory cooperation;
* documentation;
* model versions;
* data responsibilities;
* intellectual property;
* service termination; and
* evidence preservation.

Contractual allocation does not necessarily eliminate statutory responsibility.

### 50. Provider and AI Literacy

Provider personnel should possess AI literacy appropriate to their responsibilities.

Different competency requirements may apply to:

* developers;
* product managers;
* data scientists;
* security engineers;
* compliance personnel;
* legal personnel;
* quality personnel;
* incident responders;
* executives; and
* regulatory specialists.

Provider compliance depends on competent personnel operating the relevant controls.

### 51. Provider Accountability Model

A provider should establish clear accountability across the lifecycle.

A practical model may include:

* **Board:** oversight of material AI risk;
* **Executive Management:** accountability;
* **AI Governance:** regulatory framework;
* **Product Owner:** system accountability;
* **Engineering:** technical compliance;
* **Data Governance:** data controls;
* **Security:** cybersecurity;
* **Privacy:** personal-data governance;
* **Legal/Compliance:** regulatory interpretation;
* **Quality:** QMS and conformity processes;
* **Operations:** post-market monitoring;
* **Incident Response:** incident management; and
* **Internal Audit:** independent assurance.

### 52. Provider Obligations and the Three Lines Model

Provider governance can be aligned with the Three Lines Model.

**First Line**

Engineering, Product, Data, and Operations design, build, test, operate, and monitor compliant AI systems.

**Second Line**

AI Governance, Risk, Compliance, Legal, Privacy, Security, and Quality establish requirements and provide challenge and oversight.

**Third Line**

Internal Audit independently evaluates the effectiveness of provider governance and controls.

### 53. Provider Compliance by Lifecycle

Provider compliance should operate through lifecycle gates:

**concept → classification → design → development → validation → conformity → release → registration → deployment → monitoring → incident management → change → retirement.**

Each gate should define:

* required controls;
* accountable owners;
* evidence;
* approvals;
* exceptions; and
* blocking criteria.

### 54. Provider Deployment Gate

A regulated AI system should not be released until required provider obligations have been satisfied.

The deployment gate should verify, where applicable:

* provider role;
* regulatory classification;
* prohibited-practice screening;
* risk management;
* data governance;
* technical documentation;
* logging;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity;
* QMS;
* conformity assessment;
* declaration of conformity;
* CE marking;
* registration;
* post-market monitoring readiness; and
* incident-response readiness.

### 55. Provider Obligations as Continuous Compliance

Provider obligations should ultimately be managed as a continuous compliance capability.

The provider should maintain traceability across:

**legal requirement → AI system → regulatory role → risk classification → control → technical implementation → evidence → conformity → release → monitoring → incident → corrective action → reassessment.**

This lifecycle approach is particularly important because providers of high-risk AI systems remain responsible for safety and compliance beyond initial deployment, including monitoring, corrective action, serious-incident handling, and cooperation with market-surveillance authorities.

As of the current 2026 implementation framework, the Commission indicates that the high-risk rules for specified Annex III use cases are scheduled to apply from **2 December 2027**, while rules for high-risk systems embedded in products covered by relevant Annex I legislation are scheduled for **2 August 2028**.


# Part 2 — Provider Compliance Assessment, Controls, and Implementation

### 56. Provider Compliance Assessment

A provider should establish a formal assessment process to determine which AI Act obligations apply to each AI system for which it may have provider responsibility.

The assessment should establish:

* organizational role;
* AI system identity;
* intended purpose;
* deployment model;
* market-placement model;
* regulatory classification;
* applicable AI Act obligations;
* applicable sectoral legislation;
* applicable conformity requirements;
* relevant third-party dependencies;
* required evidence;
* accountable owners; and
* applicable compliance deadlines.

The assessment should be documented and retained as part of the AI system's regulatory record.

### 57. Provider Obligation Matrix

The organization should maintain a provider obligation matrix connecting legal requirements to operational controls.

A practical matrix may contain:

| Requirement area        | Applicability                         | Control                     | Owner                 | Evidence            |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | --------------------- | ------------------- |
| Provider identification | Applicable where provider role exists | Provider-role determination | AI Governance         | Role assessment     |
| Risk management         | High-risk                             | AI risk-management process  | Risk/Product          | Risk records        |
| Data governance         | High-risk                             | Data governance control     | Data Governance       | Data records        |
| Technical documentation | High-risk                             | Documentation control       | Engineering           | Technical file      |
| Logging                 | High-risk                             | Logging control             | Engineering           | Logs                |
| Transparency            | Applicable requirements               | Transparency control        | Product               | Disclosure evidence |
| Human oversight         | High-risk                             | Oversight control           | Business/Product      | Procedures          |
| Accuracy                | High-risk                             | Performance control         | Engineering           | Test results        |
| Cybersecurity           | High-risk                             | AI security control         | Security              | Security evidence   |
| QMS                     | High-risk                             | Quality management          | Quality               | QMS records         |
| Conformity assessment   | Applicable systems                    | Conformity control          | Compliance/Quality    | Assessment          |
| Post-market monitoring  | Applicable systems                    | Monitoring control          | Operations            | Monitoring records  |
| Incident reporting      | Applicable systems                    | Incident control            | Compliance/Operations | Reports             |

The matrix should distinguish legally mandatory requirements from internal enhancements.

### 58. Provider Applicability Decision

The provider should formally determine whether each requirement applies.

The decision should consider:

* AI system type;
* regulatory classification;
* intended purpose;
* provider role;
* market placement;
* putting into service;
* modifications;
* geographic scope;
* product integration;
* applicable exceptions; and
* transition provisions.

The determination should be approved by an appropriately authorized function.

### 59. AI-PRV-02 — Regulatory Obligation Mapping

The organization should maintain a control that maps each applicable AI Act requirement to:

* legal provision;
* AI system;
* responsible function;
* control;
* implementation status;
* evidence;
* testing method;
* review date; and
* regulatory source.

This provides traceability from law to operational implementation.

### 60. AI-PRV-03 — Intended-Purpose Control

The intended purpose of the AI system should be formally documented.

The record should define:

* intended users;
* intended environment;
* intended tasks;
* expected outputs;
* decision role;
* prohibited uses;
* foreseeable misuse;
* affected persons; and
* applicable restrictions.

The intended purpose should be controlled through product and change management.

### 61. Intended Purpose and Regulatory Classification

Provider classification should not be separated from intended purpose.

A change from:

> “Assists employees with document search”

to:

> “Evaluates applicants and recommends employment decisions”

may materially alter regulatory exposure.

The provider governance process should therefore require reassessment whenever intended purpose changes.

### 62. AI-PRV-04 — Regulatory Classification Control

The organization should establish a formal classification control covering:

* prohibited practices;
* high-risk AI;
* transparency obligations;
* GPAI;
* systemic-risk GPAI;
* minimal-risk systems; and
* applicable sector-specific requirements.

The classification should be documented with supporting legal and technical evidence.

### 63. AI-PRV-05 — Prohibited-Practice Screening

Before placing an AI system on the market or putting it into service, the provider should screen the system and intended uses for prohibited AI practices.

The screening should consider:

* intended purpose;
* actual deployment;
* reasonably foreseeable misuse;
* affected populations;
* biometric functionality;
* emotion recognition;
* social scoring;
* manipulation;
* exploitation of vulnerabilities;
* prohibited biometric identification; and
* other applicable Article 5 restrictions.

Where legal uncertainty exists, the deployment should be escalated.

### 64. AI-PRV-06 — High-Risk Assessment

The provider should establish a formal high-risk determination process.

The assessment should evaluate:

* Annex I pathway;
* Annex III pathway;
* product integration;
* intended purpose;
* applicable exceptions;
* profiling;
* sector;
* affected individuals;
* system functionality; and
* applicable transition provisions.

The determination should be reviewed by appropriate Legal, Compliance, and AI Governance personnel.

### 65. AI-PRV-07 — GPAI Provider Assessment

Where the organization provides a general-purpose AI model, it should perform a separate GPAI applicability assessment.

The assessment should consider:

* whether the model meets the GPAI definition;
* whether the organization is the provider;
* whether obligations under the GPAI framework apply;
* whether an open-source exemption is relevant;
* whether systemic-risk requirements apply;
* whether the model has been significantly modified; and
* whether downstream responsibilities are affected.

GPAI provider governance should not be incorrectly combined with ordinary AI-system provider controls.

### 66. AI-PRV-08 — Systemic-Risk Assessment

Providers of GPAI models should establish a process for determining whether a model presents systemic risk under the applicable AI Act framework.

The assessment should consider relevant legal criteria, including:

* high-impact capabilities; and
* the applicable training-compute threshold.

The current AI Act framework provides a rebuttable presumption of systemic risk where cumulative training compute exceeds **10^25 floating-point operations**, while the Commission may designate models based on other criteria and can update relevant thresholds through the regulatory mechanisms established by the Act.

**Third Line**

The model should preserve sufficient independence between control execution and assurance.
