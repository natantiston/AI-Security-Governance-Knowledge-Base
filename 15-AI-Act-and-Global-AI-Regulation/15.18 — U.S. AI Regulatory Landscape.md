# 15.18 — U.S. AI Regulatory Landscape — Part 1

## Part 1 — Foundations, Regulatory Structure, Scope, and Governance

1. **U.S. AI Regulatory Landscape**
   The United States does not operate under a single comprehensive federal AI statute equivalent to the EU AI Act. As of September 2026, the U.S. AI regulatory environment consists of federal statutes and agency authorities, executive actions and federal policy, sector-specific regulation, state legislation, voluntary frameworks and standards, and developing federal legislative proposals. This fragmented structure requires organizations to determine requirements according to jurisdiction, sector, use case, AI system, and applicable legal authority. The Congressional legislative landscape should therefore be distinguished from executive policy and existing enforceable law.

2. **U.S. AI Regulation as a Multi-Layer Framework**
   U.S. AI governance should be analyzed across multiple regulatory layers rather than treated as one regulatory regime.

3. **Federal Statutory Layer**
   Federal statutes can impose legally enforceable obligations on AI-related activities even when they do not constitute a comprehensive AI-specific law.

4. **Federal Agency Layer**
   Federal agencies may regulate AI applications under existing statutory authority within their respective mandates.

5. **Executive Policy Layer**
   Presidential executive orders and related administration policies can direct federal agencies and shape federal AI policy, but an executive order should not automatically be treated as equivalent to an Act of Congress or a generally applicable private-sector statute.

6. **State Legislative Layer**
   States may enact AI-specific requirements addressing areas such as automated decision-making, consumer protection, privacy, employment, discrimination, synthetic media, and other AI-related risks.

7. **Sectoral Regulatory Layer**
   Organizations may face AI requirements through sector-specific regimes covering financial services, healthcare, employment, telecommunications, critical infrastructure, consumer protection, and other regulated activities.

8. **Voluntary Standards Layer**
   Frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework provide structured risk-management guidance but are not, by themselves, generally applicable federal statutes. NIST expressly describes the AI RMF as intended for voluntary use.

9. **AI-REG-US-01 — Regulatory-Layer Classification Control**
   Organizations should classify each U.S. AI requirement according to its legal or governance source, such as federal statute, federal regulation, agency authority, executive action, state law, contractual requirement, voluntary framework, or internal policy.

10. **Federal AI Legislation Status**
    The existence of congressional proposals should be distinguished from enacted federal legislation. The current U.S. landscape should not be represented as having a single enacted federal comprehensive AI law equivalent to the EU AI Act. A January 2026 Colorado Legislative Council assessment likewise described the national landscape as lacking enacted comprehensive federal AI legislation at that time.

11. **AI-REG-US-02 — Federal Legislative Monitoring Control**
    Organizations should monitor enacted federal legislation, proposed legislation, amendments, and authoritative legislative developments relevant to their AI activities.

12. **Federal Regulatory Authorities**
    AI-related regulatory responsibility can be distributed across multiple federal agencies depending on the conduct and sector involved.

13. **Federal Trade Commission**
    The Federal Trade Commission can apply the Federal Trade Commission Act to conduct involving AI where its statutory jurisdiction and applicable legal standards are satisfied. In 2026, the FTC was also developing policy concerning AI accuracy and potentially deceptive conduct.

14. **AI-REG-US-03 — FTC Applicability Assessment**
    Organizations should assess whether AI-related products, services, representations, advertising, or business practices fall within applicable FTC authority.

15. **Consumer Protection and AI**
    AI systems can create consumer-protection issues involving deception, unfairness, representations about AI capabilities, automated interactions, pricing, recommendations, or other consumer-facing practices.

16. **AI-REG-US-04 — AI Consumer-Protection Control**
    Organizations should identify AI-related consumer claims and representations that could create exposure under applicable consumer-protection law.

17. **Federal Financial Regulation**
    AI used by financial institutions may be subject to existing financial-sector laws and supervisory expectations even where the underlying technology is AI.

18. **AI-REG-US-05 — Financial AI Regulatory Mapping**
    Financial institutions should map AI use cases to applicable federal financial regulators and sector-specific requirements.

19. **Healthcare AI Regulation**
    AI used in healthcare can implicate existing federal healthcare, medical-device, privacy, reimbursement, and safety requirements depending on the system and use case.

20. **AI-REG-US-06 — Healthcare AI Regulatory Mapping**
    Healthcare organizations should identify the legal and regulatory requirements applicable to each AI-enabled clinical, administrative, diagnostic, or decision-support use case.

21. **Employment and Workplace AI**
    AI used in recruitment, selection, performance management, workforce monitoring, promotion, compensation, or termination can interact with existing employment and anti-discrimination requirements.

22. **AI-REG-US-07 — Employment AI Compliance Mapping**
    Organizations should assess AI-enabled employment processes against applicable federal and state employment, discrimination, privacy, and labor requirements.

23. **Civil Rights and Algorithmic Decision-Making**
    Existing civil-rights laws can become relevant when automated or AI-supported decisions affect protected activities or legally protected groups.

24. **AI-REG-US-08 — Algorithmic Discrimination Assessment**
    Organizations should assess AI-supported decisions for applicable discrimination and civil-rights risks.

25. **Federal Procurement and Government AI**
    Federal agencies can be subject to government-specific AI policies, procurement requirements, standards, and executive directives that differ from requirements applicable to private-sector organizations.

26. **AI-REG-US-09 — Federal Government AI Applicability Control**
    Organizations supplying AI products or services to the U.S. federal government should separately assess federal procurement, contracting, security, privacy, and AI-specific requirements.

27. **NIST's Role**
    NIST has a statutory role in developing AI standards, guidelines, measurement approaches, and risk-management resources. Federal law directs NIST to advance collaborative AI frameworks and standards, support AI risk-mitigation frameworks, and develop standards and guidelines for trustworthy AI and bias testing.

28. **AI-REG-US-10 — NIST Framework Assessment Control**
    Organizations should determine whether NIST AI resources are relevant to their AI governance, risk-management, security, procurement, or assurance programs.

29. **NIST AI Risk Management Framework**
    The NIST AI RMF 1.0 provides a voluntary, non-sector-specific framework for managing AI risks and promoting trustworthy AI.

30. **AI-REG-US-11 — AI RMF Adoption Control**
    Organizations may adopt the NIST AI RMF as an internal AI risk-management framework where appropriate to their risk profile and governance objectives.

31. **AI RMF Core Functions**
    The NIST AI RMF organizes AI risk management around the functions **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**.

32. **AI-REG-US-12 — Govern Function Control**
    Organizations implementing the AI RMF should establish governance structures, accountability, policies, and organizational processes for AI risk.

33. **AI-REG-US-13 — Map Function Control**
    Organizations should identify AI context, intended use, affected stakeholders, risks, limitations, and potential impacts.

34. **AI-REG-US-14 — Measure Function Control**
    Organizations should establish appropriate measurement and evaluation processes for identified AI risks.

35. **AI-REG-US-15 — Manage Function Control**
    Organizations should prioritize and manage identified AI risks through appropriate treatment, monitoring, and response mechanisms.

36. **NIST Generative AI Profile**
    NIST published the AI RMF Generative AI Profile to address risks specific to generative AI. It provides implementation guidance rather than creating a generally applicable federal statutory obligation.

37. **AI-REG-US-16 — Generative AI Risk Framework Control**
    Organizations using generative AI should consider whether the NIST Generative AI Profile provides useful controls for their risk-management program.

38. **NIST AI RMF Revision**
    NIST is revising AI RMF 1.0, meaning organizations using the framework should monitor future revisions and assess their potential effect on internal AI governance.

39. **AI-REG-US-17 — NIST Framework Change Monitoring**
    Organizations using NIST AI guidance should monitor material revisions and determine whether internal controls require updates.

40. **NIST AI Standards Development**
    NIST participates in and coordinates AI standards activities across government and industry and supports development of technical standards concerning AI data, performance, governance, and trustworthy AI.

41. **AI-REG-US-18 — AI Standards Monitoring Control**
    Organizations should monitor relevant U.S. and international AI standards where those standards are incorporated into contracts, regulations, procurement requirements, or internal governance.

42. **Voluntary Frameworks vs Legal Requirements**
    Adoption of NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, industry standards, or similar frameworks should not automatically be represented as a legal requirement merely because an organization uses them for governance.

43. **AI-REG-US-19 — Legal-Status Classification Control**
    Every AI governance requirement should identify whether it is legally mandatory, contractually mandatory, regulatorically expected, voluntarily adopted, or internally established.

44. **Executive Orders and AI Policy**
    Executive orders can materially influence federal AI policy and agency activity. However, their legal effect depends on the authority, scope, implementation, and affected entities; organizations should not automatically treat every executive-order provision as a private-sector compliance obligation.

45. **AI-REG-US-20 — Executive-Order Applicability Control**
    Organizations should assess whether an executive order applies directly to their organization, affects a federal agency or contractor relationship, or represents policy direction without creating a direct private-sector obligation.

46. **2025 Federal AI Policy Direction**
    Executive Order 14179, issued January 23, 2025, directed development of an action plan intended to sustain and enhance U.S. leadership in AI and directed review of actions associated with the previous administration's AI policy.

47. **2025 AI Action Plan**
    The 2025 U.S. AI Action Plan established policy priorities including AI infrastructure, innovation, evaluation, cybersecurity, workforce development, and federal AI capabilities. NIST identifies the plan as a major source of current federal AI policy direction.

48. **AI-REG-US-21 — Federal AI Policy Monitoring Control**
    Organizations operating in federally regulated or government-facing environments should monitor federal AI policy developments that could affect procurement, security, technical standards, contracting, or regulatory expectations.

49. **2026 Federal AI Legislative Framework**
    In March 2026, the White House released a national AI legislative framework and stated that the Administration intended to work with Congress to turn the framework into legislation. This should be distinguished from enacted federal legislation.

50. **AI-REG-US-22 — Legislative Proposal Status Control**
    Organizations should distinguish proposals, frameworks, bills, executive actions, regulations, and enacted statutes when determining whether an AI requirement is legally binding.

51. **Federal-State Regulatory Tension**
    Federal AI policy has increasingly addressed the relationship between federal objectives and state AI regulation. The Administration's 2026 legislative framework expressly called for a more uniform national policy and raised concerns about conflicting state requirements.

52. **AI-REG-US-23 — Federal-State Conflict Monitoring**
    Organizations operating across multiple states should monitor potential conflicts, preemption questions, and litigation affecting AI requirements.

53. **State AI Regulation**
    State governments have become a major source of AI-specific regulatory requirements. State requirements can differ substantially in scope, definitions, effective dates, covered entities, prohibited conduct, disclosure requirements, impact assessments, and enforcement mechanisms.

54. **AI-REG-US-24 — State AI Applicability Register**
    Organizations operating in the United States should maintain a state-by-state AI regulatory applicability register.

55. **State Law Does Not Equal Federal Law**
    A state AI statute applies according to its own jurisdictional and substantive requirements and should not be represented as a nationwide U.S. requirement.

56. **AI-REG-US-25 — State-Jurisdiction Control**
    Organizations should identify the states in which an AI system is developed, offered, deployed, used, or affects individuals and assess applicable state requirements.

57. **Colorado AI Act**
    Colorado provides an important example of comprehensive state-level AI regulation. The Colorado AI Act established obligations concerning high-risk AI systems and algorithmic discrimination and was designed as a broad state framework rather than a federal law.

58. **AI-REG-US-26 — Colorado AI Act Applicability Control**
    Organizations conducting covered AI activities involving Colorado should assess the statute, applicable amendments, effective dates, regulations, and official guidance rather than relying on a generalized description of the law.

59. **California AI Regulation**
    California has developed multiple AI-related legislative and regulatory measures addressing different aspects of AI, including privacy, automated decision-making, transparency, synthetic media, and other AI-related activities.

60. **AI-REG-US-27 — California AI Regulatory Mapping Control**
    Organizations operating in California should assess applicable AI-related statutes and regulations individually rather than treating “California AI law” as one unified requirement.

61. **State Privacy Laws and AI**
    State comprehensive privacy laws can affect AI systems through requirements concerning personal data, sensitive data, profiling, automated decision-making, consumer rights, and data governance.

62. **AI-REG-US-28 — State Privacy-AI Mapping Control**
    Organizations should map AI data processing activities against applicable state privacy requirements.

63. **State Automated Decision-Making Requirements**
    Certain state laws address automated decision-making or profiling directly or indirectly through privacy, consumer-protection, employment, or sector-specific requirements.

64. **AI-REG-US-29 — Automated Decision-Making Applicability Control**
    Organizations should identify AI systems that make or materially support decisions concerning individuals and assess applicable jurisdiction-specific requirements.

65. **State Synthetic-Media Requirements**
    Certain state laws address deepfakes, election-related synthetic media, impersonation, deceptive content, or other forms of AI-generated media.

66. **AI-REG-US-30 — Synthetic-Media Legal Mapping Control**
    Organizations producing or distributing synthetic media should assess jurisdiction-specific disclosure, labeling, consent, impersonation, election, and consumer-protection requirements.

67. **State Employment AI Requirements**
    State and local jurisdictions may impose additional requirements affecting AI-assisted employment decisions.

68. **AI-REG-US-31 — State Employment AI Mapping Control**
    Organizations should assess state and local requirements affecting AI-assisted recruitment, hiring, promotion, evaluation, monitoring, and termination.

69. **State Enforcement Authorities**
    State attorneys general, consumer-protection authorities, civil-rights agencies, labor authorities, privacy regulators, and other designated bodies may have enforcement roles depending on the applicable law.

70. **AI-REG-US-32 — State Enforcement Authority Register**
    Organizations should maintain a register of relevant state regulatory and enforcement authorities.

71. **Federal Agency Enforcement vs State Enforcement**
    A single AI system can potentially be subject to multiple enforcement authorities because different laws may apply simultaneously.

72. **AI-REG-US-33 — Multi-Authority Enforcement Mapping**
    Organizations should map AI systems to potentially applicable federal, state, sectoral, and contractual enforcement authorities.

73. **AI Regulatory Preemption**
    Questions concerning whether federal law or policy preempts state AI requirements can be legally complex and may depend on statutory text, federal authority, conflict analysis, and judicial decisions.

74. **AI-REG-US-34 — Preemption Legal Review Control**
    Organizations should obtain appropriate legal analysis before treating a federal policy, statute, or regulation as displacing a state AI requirement.

75. **Regulatory Uncertainty Management**
    Rapid changes in federal and state AI policy create a requirement for disciplined regulatory horizon scanning.

76. **AI-REG-US-35 — AI Regulatory Horizon-Scanning Control**
    Organizations should monitor legislation, regulations, agency actions, executive actions, enforcement developments, court decisions, standards, and official guidance.

77. **Effective-Date Management**
    AI requirements should be tracked according to enactment, effective, compliance, enforcement, and transition dates rather than a single “law date.”

78. **AI-REG-US-36 — Effective-Date Register**
    Organizations should maintain effective dates and transition periods for applicable U.S. AI requirements.

79. **Regulatory Applicability by AI Lifecycle**
    U.S. AI regulatory analysis should consider the complete AI lifecycle, including design, development, training, testing, deployment, use, monitoring, modification, retirement, and post-deployment activity.

80. **AI-REG-US-37 — Lifecycle Regulatory Mapping Control**
    Each material AI system should have regulatory applicability mapped across its lifecycle.

81. **Regulatory Applicability by AI Role**
    A U.S. organization may simultaneously act as an AI developer, deployer, service provider, data controller, employer, contractor, or regulated entity depending on the use case.

82. **AI-REG-US-38 — Role-Based Regulatory Mapping Control**
    Organizations should identify their legal and operational role for each material AI activity.

83. **AI Regulatory Inventory**
    An AI regulatory inventory should connect AI systems to jurisdictions, laws, agencies, obligations, controls, risks, evidence, and owners.

84. **AI-REG-US-39 — U.S. AI Regulatory Inventory Control**
    Organizations should maintain a controlled inventory of applicable federal, state, sectoral, and contractual AI requirements.

85. **Regulatory Obligation Register**
    The obligation register should distinguish binding requirements from frameworks, guidance, standards, and internal controls.

86. **AI-REG-US-40 — Regulatory Obligation Classification Control**
    Each obligation should record its source, legal status, jurisdiction, applicability, effective date, owner, and implementation status.

87. **Legal Interpretation Governance**
    Ambiguous AI regulatory requirements should be subject to documented legal interpretation rather than informal assumptions.

88. **AI-REG-US-41 — AI Regulatory Legal Review Control**
    Material questions concerning applicability, preemption, enforcement authority, exemptions, or statutory interpretation should receive appropriate legal review.

89. **Regulatory Change Management**
    Changes to U.S. AI requirements should flow into organizational change management.

90. **AI-REG-US-42 — Regulatory Change Impact Assessment**
    Material changes should be assessed for impact on AI systems, controls, contracts, policies, technical documentation, training, and monitoring.

91. **Evidence-Based Regulatory Compliance**
    Organizations should maintain evidence demonstrating how applicable U.S. AI requirements have been identified, interpreted, implemented, and monitored.

92. **AI-REG-US-43 — Regulatory Evidence Control**
    Regulatory applicability assessments, legal interpretations, control mappings, approvals, testing results, and remediation records should be retained according to applicable requirements.

93. **U.S. AI Governance and GRC Integration**
    U.S. AI regulatory compliance should be integrated with enterprise GRC rather than managed as an isolated legal activity.

94. **AI-REG-US-44 — GRC Regulatory Mapping Control**
    U.S. AI obligations should map to organizational risks, controls, systems, owners, evidence, findings, and remediation.

95. **AI-REG-US-45 — Regulatory Risk Integration Control**
    Material U.S. AI regulatory risks should be incorporated into the organization's AI and enterprise risk-management processes.

96. **AI-REG-US-46 — Regulatory Control Ownership**
    Each material U.S. AI compliance control should have an accountable owner.

97. **AI-REG-US-47 — Regulatory Assurance Control**
    Material AI regulatory controls should be periodically assessed for design and operating effectiveness.

98. **AI-REG-US-48 — Regulatory Documentation Control**
    Technical and governance documentation should identify applicable U.S. regulatory requirements and their implementation status where relevant.

99. **AI-REG-US-49 — Regulatory Monitoring Control**
    Organizations should continuously monitor changes affecting the applicability of U.S. AI requirements.

100. **AI-REG-US-50 — U.S. AI Regulatory Governance Control**
     The organization should maintain an integrated U.S. AI regulatory governance model that distinguishes federal law, federal agency authority, executive policy, state legislation, sectoral regulation, voluntary frameworks, and internal controls, while continuously assessing applicability, effective dates, legal interpretation, evidence, and regulatory change.
