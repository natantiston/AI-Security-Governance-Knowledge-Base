# 15.07 — General-Purpose AI and GPAI

## Part 1 — Regulatory Foundations, Scope, Classification and Governance Context

### 1. Introduction

General-Purpose AI (GPAI) represents a distinct regulatory category within the EU AI Act.

Unlike a conventional AI system developed for a narrowly defined business function, a **general-purpose AI model** is capable of performing a wide range of tasks and can serve as the technological foundation for many downstream AI systems.

Examples can include models capable of:

* generating and understanding text;
* generating or understanding images;
* generating or processing audio;
* generating or processing video;
* reasoning;
* coding;
* summarization;
* translation;
* question answering;
* multimodal interaction.

The EU AI Act therefore establishes a dedicated framework for GPAI models rather than treating them exclusively under the rules applicable to individual downstream AI systems.

This distinction is fundamental to AI governance because an organization may simultaneously operate as:

* provider of a GPAI model;
* provider of a downstream AI system built on a GPAI model;
* deployer of a downstream AI system;
* distributor or importer;
* consumer of a third-party GPAI service.

The regulatory responsibilities can differ substantially depending on the organization's role.

---

### 2. GPAI as a Distinct Regulatory Layer

The AI Act contains a dedicated **Chapter V — General-Purpose AI Models**.

The GPAI framework is principally concerned with the obligations of **providers of general-purpose AI models**.

The regulatory structure distinguishes between:

1. general-purpose AI models;
2. general-purpose AI models with systemic risk;
3. downstream AI systems built using GPAI models.

The first category is subject to baseline obligations.

The second category is subject to additional requirements because of the potential for systemic effects.

The third category may be subject to other AI Act requirements depending on the characteristics and intended purpose of the downstream AI system.

This means that:

> **Using a GPAI model does not automatically make the downstream AI system a high-risk AI system.**

Likewise:

> **A GPAI model is not itself automatically a high-risk AI system merely because it is general-purpose.**

GPAI governance and high-risk AI governance must therefore be treated as related but distinct regulatory layers.

---

### 3. Model-Level Versus System-Level Regulation

One of the most important concepts in GPAI governance is the distinction between a **model** and an **AI system**.

A model may provide general capabilities that are subsequently integrated into many different AI systems.

For example:

**GPAI model**

→ integrated into a recruitment application
→ integrated into a customer-service chatbot
→ integrated into a coding assistant
→ integrated into a medical information application
→ integrated into a document-processing platform.

The same underlying model can therefore support systems with very different:

* intended purposes;
* users;
* risks;
* sectors;
* regulatory classifications.

The AI Act consequently applies certain obligations at the **model provider level**, while other obligations arise at the **downstream AI-system level**.

A mature GRC architecture should maintain both records.

---

### 4. Definition of General-Purpose AI Model

The AI Act defines a general-purpose AI model based on its generality and capability to competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks.

The Commission's guidelines explain that GPAI models include models with significant computational training resources and broad capabilities, with the Commission's current interpretation using technical criteria to help determine whether a model falls within the GPAI obligations.

The regulatory analysis should therefore consider:

* model capability;
* intended and reasonably foreseeable use;
* generality;
* training methodology;
* computational resources;
* ability to perform multiple distinct tasks;
* whether the model is placed on the EU market;
* whether the organization qualifies as the provider.

The legal determination should not be made solely from the product name.

---

### 5. The Importance of the Provider Concept

The GPAI obligations primarily attach to the **provider of the general-purpose AI model**.

The provider concept is therefore a critical GRC determination.

The organization should establish:

* who developed the model;
* who commissioned development;
* who owns or controls the model;
* who places the model on the EU market;
* under whose name or trademark the model is made available;
* whether a substantial modification has occurred;
* whether another organization has assumed provider responsibilities.

The Commission's GPAI guidelines clarify concepts such as provider, placing on the market and significant modification to help determine which actors fall within the GPAI obligations.

---

### 6. GPAI Provider Versus Downstream Provider

An organization consuming a third-party GPAI model is not automatically the provider of that underlying model.

For example:

**Company A**

Develops and places a GPAI model on the EU market.

→ Company A may be the GPAI provider.

**Company B**

Uses Company A's model through an API to build a customer-service application.

→ Company B may be a downstream AI-system provider or deployer depending on its role and circumstances.

The responsibilities of Company A and Company B are therefore different.

This distinction is particularly important for procurement and enterprise AI governance.

---

### 7. Significant Modification

Organizations that substantially modify an existing GPAI model must carefully assess whether they have assumed provider-level responsibilities.

The Commission's guidance clarifies that significant modifications can result in an actor being treated as a provider of the modified GPAI model, while minor modifications do not automatically create the same status.

Potentially relevant changes can include:

* substantial retraining;
* major modification of model capabilities;
* significant changes to architecture;
* major changes to model parameters;
* transformation into a materially different general-purpose model.

The assessment should be documented rather than determined solely by engineering terminology.

---

### 8. GPAI Obligations Entered Into Application in 2025

The GPAI obligations entered into application on **2 August 2025**.

For GPAI models placed on the market before that date, the AI Act provides a transition period, with compliance required by **2 August 2027** under the applicable transitional provisions.

The Commission began enforcement of the GPAI obligations from **2 August 2026**, including the possibility of fines for non-compliance.

Therefore, as of September 2026, GPAI governance is an active regulatory requirement rather than a future preparation exercise.

---

### 9. Baseline GPAI Obligations

Providers of GPAI models are subject to several core obligations.

The Commission identifies the principal obligations as including:

* technical documentation;
* information and documentation for downstream AI-system providers;
* a policy to comply with Union copyright law;
* publication of a sufficiently detailed summary of training content;
* appointment of an authorised representative in the Union for providers established outside the EU, where required.

These requirements create a model-level governance framework covering:

**Documentation → Downstream Transparency → Copyright → Training-Data Transparency → EU Representation**

The obligations should be implemented through formal controls rather than informal documentation practices.

---

### 10. Technical Documentation

GPAI providers must prepare and maintain technical documentation concerning the model.

The documentation should provide sufficient information to enable the AI Office and relevant authorities to understand the model and its development.

Depending on the applicable requirements, documentation can include information concerning:

* model architecture;
* development process;
* training methodology;
* computational resources;
* model capabilities;
* evaluation;
* testing;
* limitations;
* intended use;
* foreseeable misuse;
* applicable risk controls.

The Commission specifically identifies technical documentation as a core obligation for GPAI providers.

Technical documentation should be treated as a controlled regulatory record.

---

### 11. Downstream Information Obligations

GPAI providers must provide information and documentation to downstream AI-system providers to enable them to understand the model's capabilities and limitations and to meet their own AI Act obligations.

This creates an important supply-chain governance principle:

> **The GPAI provider must support downstream regulatory compliance through appropriate technical and usage information.**

Downstream information should address matters such as:

* capabilities;
* limitations;
* intended functionality;
* known risks;
* integration considerations;
* relevant performance characteristics;
* applicable restrictions;
* model documentation.

The exact information should be proportionate to the model and the applicable regulatory requirements.

---

### 12. Copyright Compliance

GPAI providers must put in place a policy to comply with applicable Union copyright law and related rights.

The Commission identifies this as one of the core GPAI obligations.

The policy should address:

* copyright compliance;
* rights reservations;
* training-data acquisition;
* content ingestion;
* automated data collection;
* web crawling;
* rights-management signals;
* documentation;
* governance;
* monitoring.

This creates an important intersection between:

* AI governance;
* legal;
* data governance;
* intellectual property;
* engineering;
* procurement.

---

### 13. Training-Content Summary

GPAI providers must publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used to train the model.

The Commission has provided a mandatory template intended to facilitate compliance with this requirement.

The objective is not necessarily to publish the complete training dataset.

Instead, the requirement establishes a transparency mechanism concerning the categories and sources of training content.

From a GRC perspective, the organization should maintain internal evidence supporting the published summary.

The internal record should allow the provider to demonstrate:

* how the summary was prepared;
* what sources were considered;
* who reviewed it;
* what methodology was used;
* when it was published;
* what version of the model it covers.

---

### 14. Authorised Representative for Certain Non-EU Providers

A provider of a GPAI model established outside the EU must appoint an authorised representative in the Union before placing the model on the EU market, subject to the applicable legal requirements and exemptions.

The representative serves as an important regulatory interface.

The governance framework should document:

* representative identity;
* mandate;
* responsibilities;
* contact information;
* regulatory communications;
* documentation access;
* authority interaction;
* termination procedures.

The appointment should be treated as a controlled regulatory requirement rather than a purely contractual relationship.

---

### 15. Open-Source Considerations

The AI Act provides specific treatment for certain GPAI models released under free and open-source licences.

Under the applicable conditions, some documentation obligations do not apply to providers of qualifying open-source GPAI models.

However, the exemption is **not universal**.

The AI Act maintains additional requirements where a GPAI model presents systemic risks.

The organization's assessment should therefore determine:

* whether the model qualifies as GPAI;
* whether it qualifies for the open-source treatment;
* whether the licence satisfies the legal conditions;
* whether parameters and relevant information are publicly available;
* whether the model presents systemic risk.

"Open source" should not be treated as a blanket exemption from the AI Act.

---

### 16. Systemic-Risk GPAI

The AI Act establishes a separate category of **general-purpose AI models with systemic risk**.

These models are subject to additional obligations because their capabilities or potential impact can create risks extending beyond individual deployments.

A GPAI model is classified as having systemic risk if it meets the relevant conditions in Article 51.

One statutory presumption applies where the cumulative computational resources used to train the model exceed **10²⁵ floating-point operations (FLOP)**, subject to the Act's rules and potential updates to the threshold.

However, systemic risk is not limited to a simple computational threshold.

The Commission may also designate a GPAI model as presenting systemic risk based on the applicable criteria and procedures.

---

### 17. Systemic Risk Is Not the Same as High-Risk AI

This distinction is essential.

**High-risk AI system**

→ classification primarily concerns the use and context of an AI system.

**GPAI model with systemic risk**

→ classification concerns the capabilities and potential systemic impact of a general-purpose model.

A GPAI model may therefore:

* not itself be classified as a high-risk AI system;
* nevertheless be a GPAI model with systemic risk;
* provide capabilities used within many downstream high-risk systems.

These regulatory layers can coexist.

The organization's GRC architecture should therefore avoid using a single "AI risk level" field to represent both concepts.

---

### 18. Systemic-Risk Assessment

Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must establish processes to assess and mitigate systemic risks.

The AI Act requires additional measures including:

* model evaluation;
* adversarial testing;
* systemic-risk assessment and mitigation;
* serious-incident tracking and reporting;
* cybersecurity protections.

Systemic-risk governance should therefore examine risks that can arise from:

* model capabilities;
* scale;
* widespread deployment;
* misuse;
* failures;
* security compromise;
* cascading effects;
* concentration;
* loss of control;
* impacts across multiple downstream systems.

---

### 19. Model Evaluation

Systemic-risk GPAI providers must perform model evaluations using appropriate standardized protocols and state-of-the-art tools.

This includes adversarial testing designed to identify and mitigate systemic risks.

A mature evaluation program should address:

* dangerous capabilities;
* misuse;
* adversarial robustness;
* model autonomy where relevant;
* cyber capabilities;
* deception;
* manipulation;
* privacy risks;
* harmful content;
* security vulnerabilities;
* failure modes.

Evaluation results should be formally documented and linked to mitigation decisions.

---

### 20. Adversarial Testing

Adversarial testing is particularly important for systemic-risk GPAI models.

Testing should attempt to identify:

* unsafe capabilities;
* unexpected behavior;
* circumvention of safeguards;
* prompt-based attacks;
* jailbreaks;
* model manipulation;
* harmful capability elicitation;
* security weaknesses;
* emergent behaviors.

Testing should be performed using controlled methodologies and documented evidence.

Where significant vulnerabilities are discovered, the provider should establish:

**Finding → Risk Assessment → Mitigation → Retest → Residual Risk Decision**

---

### 21. Systemic-Risk Mitigation

Systemic-risk mitigation should not depend on a single safeguard.

Controls may include:

* model-level safeguards;
* access controls;
* deployment restrictions;
* monitoring;
* abuse detection;
* red teaming;
* capability evaluations;
* incident response;
* model update controls;
* security architecture;
* infrastructure protections;
* downstream-use restrictions.

Mitigation should address both:

**known risks**

and

**emerging risks identified through evaluation and monitoring.**

---

### 22. Serious-Incident Management for Systemic-Risk GPAI

Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must track, document and report relevant information concerning serious incidents and possible corrective measures without undue delay to the AI Office and, as appropriate, national competent authorities.

The incident-management framework should therefore include:

* incident identification;
* severity classification;
* evidence preservation;
* technical investigation;
* systemic-risk assessment;
* corrective action;
* reporting determination;
* regulatory communication;
* post-incident review.

This should be integrated with the organization's existing AI security incident-management framework.

---

### 23. Cybersecurity of Systemic-Risk GPAI

The AI Act requires providers of GPAI models with systemic risk to ensure an adequate level of cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure.

This creates a model-level cybersecurity obligation that extends beyond ordinary application security.

Relevant controls may include:

* model-weight protection;
* secure development;
* infrastructure security;
* access control;
* secrets management;
* supply-chain security;
* insider-threat controls;
* vulnerability management;
* secure deployment;
* monitoring;
* incident response;
* backup and recovery;
* physical security.

The objective is to reduce the possibility that compromise of the model or infrastructure creates widespread consequences.

---

### 24. GPAI Code of Practice

The European Commission has endorsed the **General-Purpose AI Code of Practice** as a voluntary tool that providers can use to demonstrate compliance with relevant AI Act obligations.

The Code contains chapters covering:

* transparency;
* copyright;
* safety and security for systemic-risk GPAI.

The Code is not itself the AI Act.

The distinction should be maintained:

**AI Act**

→ legally binding regulation.

**GPAI Code of Practice**

→ voluntary compliance-support mechanism.

Providers that do not rely on the Code must still demonstrate compliance through other appropriate means.

---

### 25. Governance Significance of the GPAI Code

From a GRC perspective, the Code can provide:

* standardized control expectations;
* documentation structure;
* implementation guidance;
* evidence expectations;
* risk-management practices;
* greater consistency across providers.

However, organizations should avoid treating Code adherence as an automatic legal safe harbor.

The underlying statutory requirements remain the governing legal framework.

The Commission confirms that the Code is intended to facilitate compliance and provide a practical way for providers to demonstrate compliance with relevant obligations.

---

### 26. GPAI Governance as a Supply-Chain Control

GPAI models create a new layer of technology supply-chain risk.

A downstream organization may depend on a model provider for:

* model availability;
* model behavior;
* documentation;
* training-data transparency;
* copyright compliance;
* security;
* incident information;
* model changes;
* limitations;
* technical specifications.

Therefore, enterprise AI governance should treat GPAI providers as strategic technology suppliers where their models are embedded into material business processes.

---

---

### 28. Model-Level Regulatory Classification

The organization should avoid classifying GPAI models using only traditional enterprise risk ratings such as:

* low;
* medium;
* high;
* critical.

Instead, regulatory classification should separately record:

* GPAI status;
* systemic-risk status;
* provider status;
* open-source status;
* downstream-system status;
* high-risk status of any downstream AI system;
* Article 50 applicability;
* other relevant regulatory classifications.

This prevents different legal concepts from being collapsed into one risk score.

---

### 29. Relationship Between GPAI and Downstream High-Risk AI

A GPAI model can serve as a component of a downstream high-risk AI system.

For example:

**GPAI model**

→ integrated into recruitment decision-support system

→ downstream system assessed under the AI Act

→ potentially high-risk depending on its intended purpose and applicable Article 6/Annex III criteria.

The GPAI provider and downstream provider can therefore have separate obligations.

The downstream provider should not assume that compliance documentation supplied by the GPAI provider automatically satisfies the downstream system's own obligations.

---

### 30. Governance Principle

GPAI governance should be based on the following principle:

> **Regulate the model according to its role and capabilities, and regulate the downstream AI system according to its own intended purpose, context and regulatory classification.**

This requires organizations to maintain a clear separation between:

* model governance;
* system governance;
* deployment governance;
* provider obligations;
* deployer obligations;
* systemic-risk governance;
* transparency obligations;
* downstream high-risk obligations.

The result is a layered AI GRC model capable of following responsibility and risk across the AI value chain.

# Part 2 — GPAI Classification, Applicability and Control Requirements

31. **GPAI Regulatory Applicability Assessment**

An organization should not assume that an AI model is outside the AI Act merely because it is obtained from a third-party vendor, accessed through an API, hosted in a cloud environment, or embedded within another application.

The first governance question should be:

> **Does the organization develop, place on the market, distribute, significantly modify, or integrate a general-purpose AI model, and what regulatory role does it have in relation to that model?**

The applicability assessment should distinguish at least:

* GPAI model provider;
* downstream AI-system provider;
* deployer;
* distributor;
* importer;
* authorized representative;
* organization making a significant modification to an existing GPAI model;
* organization merely using a third-party GPAI-enabled application.

This role determination is critical because obligations under the GPAI chapter are primarily directed toward **providers of GPAI models**, while organizations integrating those models into AI systems have separate responsibilities under other parts of the AI Act.

The European Commission's GPAI guidelines specifically address scope, provider status, significant modification and open-source considerations.

32. **GPAI Model Identification**

The organization should establish a repeatable methodology for determining whether a model qualifies as a GPAI model.

The assessment should consider:

* the model's intended and reasonably foreseeable capabilities;
* whether it can competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks;
* the modalities supported;
* the model's ability to be integrated into different AI systems;
* the model's distribution method;
* whether it is offered through an API, downloadable weights, software package, hosted service or another mechanism;
* whether the organization is providing the model itself or only an application built on top of it.

A model should therefore be assessed at the **model layer**, rather than simply by examining the business application in which it is used.

The AI Act deliberately distinguishes GPAI models from AI systems. A GPAI model can become part of many different AI systems, but the model itself is not necessarily an AI system.

33. **Model Versus AI System Boundary**

A key GRC control is maintaining a clear boundary between:

**Model layer**

* foundation model;
* pretrained model;
* fine-tuned model;
* model weights;
* model architecture;
* model training and evaluation.

and:

**AI-system layer**

* application;
* user interface;
* workflow;
* business logic;
* retrieval system;
* external tools;
* decision process;
* human oversight;
* deployment environment.

This distinction prevents organizations from assuming that compliance at one layer automatically establishes compliance at another.

For example, a GPAI provider may satisfy its obligations concerning technical documentation, copyright policy and training-content summary, while a downstream organization may still need to conduct its own assessment of the AI system into which that model has been integrated.

34. **Provider Determination**

Provider determination should be formally documented.

An organization may become a provider where it develops a GPAI model and places it on the market under its own name or trademark. Provider status should also be reassessed when an organization makes a **significant modification** to an existing GPAI model.

The European Commission's guidelines clarify that significant modification is relevant to determining whether the modifying organization becomes subject to provider obligations, while minor modifications do not automatically create the same status.

The governance record should therefore identify:

* original model provider;
* current model owner;
* organization performing modifications;
* nature of modifications;
* significance assessment;
* new model/version identity;
* applicable regulatory role;
* resulting obligations.

35. **Significant Modification Assessment**

A significant-modification assessment should be performed whenever an organization:

* substantially retrains a model;
* materially changes model architecture;
* performs substantial fine-tuning;
* changes important model capabilities;
* materially changes model behavior;
* adds capabilities that significantly expand the model's intended use;
* combines model components in a way that creates a substantially different model;
* changes the model's risk profile materially.

The assessment should not rely solely on the technical team's description of the change.

GRC should evaluate whether the modification changes:

* capabilities;
* intended purpose;
* foreseeable uses;
* risk profile;
* model performance;
* safety characteristics;
* cybersecurity exposure;
* systemic-risk indicators;
* regulatory classification.

36. **GPAI Applicability Decision Record**

Every potentially regulated model should have a formal applicability record containing:

| Assessment Element       | Required Determination           |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------- |
| Model identity           | Unique model/version             |
| Provider                 | Legal entity                     |
| Market status            | Placed on EU market / not placed |
| GPAI determination       | Yes / No / Uncertain             |
| Provider status          | Yes / No / Uncertain             |
| Significant modification | Yes / No                         |
| Systemic-risk status     | Yes / No / Under assessment      |
| Open-source status       | Qualifying / Non-qualifying      |
| Applicable obligations   | Mapped requirements              |
| Evidence owner           | Responsible function             |
| Legal review             | Completed / Required             |
| Approval status          | Approved / Escalated / Blocked   |

This record should become part of the AI governance evidence repository.

37. **GPAI Classification Is Not the Same as Systemic Risk**

A GPAI model may be subject to the general GPAI obligations without being classified as a GPAI model with systemic risk.

The governance model should therefore maintain two separate determinations:

**Determination A — Is the model GPAI?**

and

**Determination B — Does the model present systemic risk?**

The second determination triggers substantially stronger requirements.

The AI Act provides that a GPAI model is classified as presenting systemic risk when it has high-impact capabilities or is designated by the European Commission based on the statutory criteria. A presumption applies when cumulative training computation exceeds **10²⁵ floating-point operations**, subject to the statutory framework and future regulatory adjustment.

38. **Systemic-Risk Assessment**

Systemic-risk assessment should not be treated as a simple computational threshold test.

The organization should evaluate:

* training computation;
* model capability;
* scale;
* autonomy-related capabilities;
* potential for widespread misuse;
* ability to cause large-scale harm;
* cybersecurity implications;
* biological, chemical or other dangerous capability concerns;
* potential for loss of control;
* harmful manipulation;
* large-scale fundamental-rights impacts;
* potential impact on critical systems;
* concentration and dependency risks;
* model proliferation and downstream reach.

The organization should retain evidence supporting the assessment.

39. **Training-Compute Monitoring**

Organizations developing GPAI models should maintain reliable records of cumulative training computation.

The record should include, where applicable:

* training runs;
* compute resources;
* accelerator types;
* number of accelerators;
* training duration;
* computational methodology;
* floating-point operation estimates;
* major retraining events;
* continuation or extension of training;
* material post-training computation;
* assumptions used in the calculation.

This is important because the AI Act uses cumulative training computation as one statutory trigger for presuming high-impact capabilities.

40. **Systemic-Risk Notification Control**

Where the statutory conditions for systemic risk are met, the provider must notify the European Commission without delay and, under Article 52, within two weeks after the condition is met or becomes known to the provider.

The organization should therefore establish:

**Control AI-GPAI-01 — Systemic-Risk Notification**

The control should ensure:

1. compute and capability indicators are monitored;

2. potential threshold crossings are escalated;

3. Legal and AI Governance review the determination;

4. required notification information is assembled;

5. notification deadlines are tracked;

6. submission evidence is preserved;

7. subsequent Commission correspondence is retained.

8. **Commission Designation Risk**

A provider should not assume that remaining below the computational presumption eliminates systemic-risk exposure.

The Commission may designate a GPAI model as presenting systemic risk based on the statutory criteria, including through the mechanisms established by Article 52.

Therefore, internal governance should monitor:

* Commission designations;
* regulatory communications;
* scientific-panel alerts;
* relevant technical developments;
* new benchmarks;
* evolving systemic-risk indicators.

The AI Act also permits reassessment mechanisms under specified conditions.

42. **Systemic-Risk Reassessment**

Where a model has been designated as presenting systemic risk, governance should maintain a reassessment process.

The reassessment file should document:

* original designation;
* reasons for designation;
* subsequent technical changes;
* new evaluation results;
* mitigation improvements;
* changes in model capabilities;
* changes in deployment scale;
* new evidence relevant to Annex XIII criteria;
* reasons supporting continued designation or reassessment.

A provider requesting reassessment must provide objective, detailed and new reasons arising since the designation, and statutory timing restrictions apply.

43. **Technical Documentation Control**

GPAI providers must create and maintain technical documentation concerning the model, including its training and testing processes and evaluation results. The documentation must contain at least the information specified in Annex XI and be available to the AI Office and national competent authorities as required.

A mature documentation framework should cover:

* model description;
* architecture;
* parameter information;
* modalities;
* intended tasks;
* distribution methods;
* training methodology;
* training data information;
* computational resources;
* testing;
* validation;
* evaluation;
* known limitations;
* acceptable-use policies;
* cybersecurity;
* release history;
* licensing;
* model versions.

44. **Technical Documentation as a Controlled Record**

Technical documentation should not be treated as a static engineering document.

It should have:

* document ownership;
* version control;
* approval;
* change history;
* review frequency;
* evidence references;
* confidentiality classification;
* access control;
* retention requirements;
* regulatory retrieval procedures.

Every material model release should trigger a documentation review.

45. **Downstream Information Obligation**

GPAI providers must provide information and documentation to providers of AI systems intending to integrate the GPAI model.

The information must enable downstream providers to understand the model's capabilities and limitations and to comply with their own AI Act obligations.

The downstream information package should therefore provide meaningful information rather than merely marketing descriptions.

46. **Downstream Documentation Content**

The downstream information package should address, as applicable:

* model capabilities;
* limitations;
* intended tasks;
* known failure modes;
* input requirements;
* output characteristics;
* supported modalities;
* integration requirements;
* relevant performance information;
* evaluation results;
* foreseeable limitations;
* acceptable-use restrictions;
* known security considerations;
* relevant training-data information;
* operational dependencies;
* version information;
* material changes.

The Commission's guidance specifically identifies documentation concerning intended tasks, technical integration requirements, input/output specifications and training information as relevant to downstream providers.

47. **Downstream Information Quality**

The provider should evaluate downstream documentation using a quality framework.

Information should be:

* accurate;
* current;
* understandable;
* sufficiently detailed;
* technically meaningful;
* consistent with actual model behavior;
* traceable to evidence;
* updated after material model changes.

A documentation package that technically exists but fails to communicate important limitations should be treated as a governance deficiency.

48. **Copyright Policy Control**

GPAI providers must implement a policy to comply with applicable Union copyright law and related rights, including use of state-of-the-art technologies to identify and respect rights reservations where applicable.

**Control AI-GPAI-02 — Copyright Governance** should establish:

* ownership of the copyright policy;
* training-data governance;
* rights-reservation handling;
* technical mechanisms for respecting reservations;
* legal review;
* supplier controls;
* evidence of implementation;
* periodic policy review.

49. **Training-Content Summary**

GPAI providers must publish a sufficiently detailed summary of content used for training the model.

The governance process should distinguish this public summary from:

* confidential training datasets;
* trade secrets;
* internal data catalogs;
* security-sensitive information;
* detailed dataset-level evidence.

The objective is regulatory transparency without unnecessarily disclosing protected information.

50. **Training-Content Governance**

The training-content summary should be supported by an internal evidence chain.

The organization should maintain:

* training-data inventories;
* dataset sources;
* acquisition method;
* licensing information;
* rights restrictions;
* data categories;
* filtering procedures;
* preprocessing;
* deduplication;
* quality controls;
* documentation of relevant exclusions;
* legal review;
* published-summary approval.

This allows the public summary to be supported by underlying evidence.

51. **Authorized Representative Control**

Providers established outside the EU must appoint an authorized representative established in the Union before placing a GPAI model on the Union market, subject to the AI Act's applicable rules.

The authorized representative governance record should include:

* legal entity;
* written mandate;
* scope of responsibilities;
* effective date;
* contact details;
* document-retention responsibilities;
* communication procedures;
* replacement procedures;
* termination procedures.

52. **Open-Source Assessment**

Open-source status should be treated as a legal qualification requiring evidence rather than as a label chosen by the development team.

The assessment should verify:

* applicable licence;
* accessibility of model parameters;
* availability of weights where required;
* architecture information;
* usage information;
* rights to access;
* rights to use;
* rights to modify;
* rights to distribute.

The organization should document whether the model meets the statutory conditions for the relevant exemption.

53. **Limits of the Open-Source Exemption**

The open-source treatment does not mean that a qualifying model is completely outside the AI Act.

The statutory exemption applies to specified GPAI obligations, while GPAI models presenting systemic risks remain subject to systemic-risk obligations.

Therefore, the compliance assessment should explicitly identify:

* obligations that remain applicable;
* obligations for which an exemption is available;
* evidence supporting the exemption;
* systemic-risk status;
* downstream obligations;
* other AI Act obligations applicable to resulting AI systems.

54. **GPAI Code of Practice as an Implementation Mechanism**

The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice is a voluntary practical tool designed to help providers demonstrate compliance with the AI Act's GPAI obligations.

It should not be confused with the AI Act itself.

A governance assessment should distinguish:

**Mandatory legal requirements**

from

**voluntary implementation mechanisms**

such as the Code of Practice.

The European Commission describes the Code as a practical tool complementing the AI Act and its GPAI guidelines.

55. **Code-of-Practice Governance**

Organizations using the Code of Practice should document:

* which commitments are adopted;
* which controls implement each commitment;
* responsible control owners;
* evidence produced;
* exceptions;
* gaps;
* compensating controls;
* management approval.

Where an organization chooses not to rely on the Code, it should still maintain a documented compliance approach demonstrating how the mandatory requirements are satisfied.

56. **Systemic-Risk Model Evaluation**

Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk have additional obligations concerning standardized evaluation and adversarial testing.

The AI Act requires such providers to perform and document model evaluations using appropriate standardized protocols and tools, including adversarial testing intended to identify and mitigate systemic risks.

Evaluation governance should therefore include:

* defined evaluation methodology;
* benchmark selection;
* capability testing;
* adversarial testing;
* red-team methodology;
* test coverage;
* test results;
* residual-risk analysis;
* mitigation decisions;
* retesting;
* management approval.

57. **Adversarial Testing Governance**

Adversarial testing should address both technical weaknesses and harmful capability scenarios.

Testing may consider:

* jailbreak resistance;
* dangerous capability elicitation;
* cyber abuse;
* harmful manipulation;
* privacy attacks;
* model extraction;
* prompt injection;
* tool abuse;
* unsafe autonomy;
* loss-of-control scenarios;
* high-impact misuse;
* large-scale abuse pathways.

Testing should be risk-based and proportionate to the model's capabilities.

58. **Systemic-Risk Mitigation**

Systemic-risk providers must establish processes to assess and mitigate systemic risks.

Mitigation should be linked to identified risks rather than implemented as generic security controls.

A mitigation record should identify:

* risk;
* causal mechanism;
* affected stakeholders;
* likelihood;
* severity;
* existing safeguards;
* proposed mitigation;
* residual risk;
* control owner;
* verification method;
* acceptance authority.

59. **Serious-Incident Governance**

Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk must maintain processes for tracking and reporting serious incidents.

The incident framework should integrate:

* AI safety;
* cybersecurity;
* privacy;
* fundamental rights;
* model behavior;
* infrastructure;
* downstream impact;
* regulatory reporting.

Incident records should preserve the relationship between:

**model → version → deployment → event → impact → mitigation → notification.**

60. **Cybersecurity of GPAI Models**

Systemic-risk GPAI providers must ensure an adequate level of cybersecurity protection for the model and its physical infrastructure.

The cybersecurity program should address:

* model weights;
* training infrastructure;
* inference infrastructure;
* APIs;
* development environments;
* secrets;
* privileged access;
* model repositories;
* supply chain;
* build pipelines;
* deployment infrastructure;
* monitoring;
* incident response.

61. **Model-Weight Protection**

For advanced GPAI models, model weights can represent a highly valuable and security-sensitive asset.

Controls should include:

* strong authentication;
* privileged-access management;
* encryption;
* repository protection;
* network segmentation;
* controlled export;
* integrity verification;
* insider-risk controls;
* logging;
* anomaly detection;
* secure backup;
* recovery procedures.

62. **GPAI Supply-Chain Governance**

GPAI governance should extend beyond the immediate model provider.

The supply chain may include:

* dataset providers;
* cloud infrastructure;
* accelerator suppliers;
* model hosting providers;
* evaluation providers;
* safety-testing organizations;
* open-source components;
* API providers;
* downstream AI-system providers.

Contracts and assurance mechanisms should establish responsibilities for:

* security;
* incident notification;
* material changes;
* documentation;
* service availability;
* compliance support;
* evidence provision.

63. **Model-Version Governance**

Every production GPAI model should have a controlled version identity.

The organization should record:

* model name;
* version;
* release date;
* architecture;
* parameter configuration;
* training state;
* fine-tuning state;
* evaluation state;
* applicable regulatory classification;
* systemic-risk status;
* documentation version;
* security status.

This prevents evidence for one model version from being incorrectly applied to another.

64. **Change-Control Trigger**

The following events should automatically trigger a GPAI regulatory reassessment:

* new model release;
* major retraining;
* significant fine-tuning;
* architecture changes;
* new modality;
* new capability;
* substantial performance improvement;
* increased compute;
* new deployment scale;
* material change in intended use;
* material cybersecurity change;
* new systemic-risk evidence;
* change in legal requirements.

65. **GPAI Compliance Evidence Repository**

The organization should maintain a dedicated evidence repository containing:

* applicability assessments;
* provider determinations;
* technical documentation;
* downstream documentation;
* copyright policy;
* training-content summary;
* open-source analysis;
* systemic-risk assessment;
* compute evidence;
* evaluation results;
* adversarial testing;
* mitigation records;
* incident reports;
* cybersecurity evidence;
* regulatory correspondence;
* Code-of-Practice mapping;
* approvals and exceptions.

66. **Evidence Traceability**

Every significant GPAI compliance assertion should be traceable to evidence.

For example:

**Requirement → Control → Procedure → Evidence → Owner → Test → Result**

This structure allows Internal Audit, Compliance, Legal, regulators and management to determine not only whether a control exists, but whether it operates effectively.

68. **Control Testing**

GPAI controls should be tested for both design effectiveness and operating effectiveness.

Testing may include:

* document inspection;
* sample-based testing;
* technical validation;
* configuration review;
* interview;
* walkthrough;
* adversarial testing;
* evidence reconciliation;
* version comparison;
* incident-record review.

A control should not be considered effective merely because a policy exists.

69. **GPAI Compliance Dashboard**

Management reporting should provide visibility into:

* number of GPAI models;

* systemic-risk models;
* models under classification;

70. **Regulatory Change Monitoring**

Because GPAI regulation is technically dynamic, the governance function should monitor:

* EUR-Lex amendments;
* European Commission guidance;
* AI Office communications;
* delegated acts;
* implementing acts;
* AI Board outputs;
* scientific-panel developments;
* GPAI Code-of-Practice updates;
* changes to benchmarks and technical thresholds.

The AI Act expressly allows certain systemic-risk thresholds and benchmarks to be updated through delegated acts as technology evolves.

71. **Enforcement Readiness**

GPAI providers should operate on the assumption that regulatory compliance may need to be demonstrated to the AI Office or competent authorities.

As of **2 August 2026**, the Commission's enforcement powers concerning GPAI obligations are applicable, including enforcement involving fines.

Therefore, evidence should be maintained continuously rather than assembled only after receiving a regulatory request.

72. **Downstream Complaint and Escalation Mechanism**

Downstream providers have mechanisms under the AI Act to raise complaints concerning alleged infringements of GPAI provider obligations.

Organizations should therefore maintain an internal escalation process for disputes concerning:

* missing documentation;
* inadequate downstream information;
* copyright compliance;
* training-content transparency;
* systemic-risk obligations;
* serious incidents;
* cybersecurity.

The European Commission has established information concerning a complaint channel for downstream providers.

73. **GPAI Vendor Due Diligence**

Organizations consuming third-party GPAI models should evaluate vendors before production use.

Due diligence should examine:

* provider identity;
* model classification;
* regulatory role;
* technical documentation;
* security documentation;
* model limitations;
* training-content information;
* copyright policy;
* incident management;
* systemic-risk status;
* versioning;
* change notification;
* contractual commitments;
* regulatory cooperation.

74. **Contractual GPAI Controls**

Contracts with GPAI providers should address, where relevant:

* regulatory compliance;
* documentation availability;
* material-change notification;
* security incidents;
* serious incidents;
* service changes;
* model retirement;
* version changes;
* audit cooperation;
* evidence requests;
* data handling;
* confidentiality;
* intellectual-property responsibilities;
* regulatory requests.

75. **Downstream Risk Ownership**

The existence of a compliant GPAI provider does not eliminate downstream risk.

The downstream organization remains responsible for assessing its own AI system, including:

* intended purpose;
* deployment context;
* users;
* affected persons;
* human oversight;
* privacy;
* cybersecurity;
* fundamental rights;
* transparency;
* applicable high-risk classification;
* operational controls.

This creates an important governance boundary:

> **GPAI provider compliance is a supply-chain input, not a substitute for downstream AI governance.**

76. **Enterprise GPAI Intake Process**

A practical enterprise intake workflow should be:

**AI proposal → model identification → GPAI assessment → provider/deployer role → systemic-risk assessment → vendor assessment → applicable obligations → risk assessment → control mapping → approval → deployment → monitoring.**

Every stage should have a named owner and evidence requirement.

77. **GPAI Deployment Gate**

Before an enterprise approves a GPAI model for material production use, the deployment gate should verify:

* model identity;
* provider identity;
* regulatory classification;
* documentation;
* security review;
* privacy review;
* legal review;
* acceptable-use restrictions;
* downstream risk assessment;
* human oversight;
* incident response;
* vendor controls;
* change-management arrangements.

High-impact or material deployments should require additional governance approval.

78. **Residual-Risk Management**

Even after GPAI compliance controls are implemented, residual risks may remain.

Residual-risk decisions should document:

* unresolved risk;
* business impact;
* regulatory implications;
* affected stakeholders;
* existing safeguards;
* compensating controls;
* risk owner;
* approval authority;
* review date.

Regulatory compliance should not be interpreted as evidence that all technical or business risks have been eliminated.

79. **GPAI Governance RACI**

A practical accountability model may assign:

| Function                | Primary Responsibility                   |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Board                   | Oversight and risk accountability        |
| AI Governance Committee | Regulatory governance and escalation     |
| Legal                   | Legal interpretation                     |
| Compliance              | Regulatory control oversight             |
| AI/ML Engineering       | Model development and technical evidence |
| Security                | Cybersecurity and adversarial testing    |
| Privacy                 | Data and privacy governance              |
| Procurement             | Vendor governance                        |
| Business Owner          | Intended use and operational risk        |
| Internal Audit          | Independent assurance                    |

80. **GPAI Governance Principle**

GPAI governance should operate as a controlled lifecycle rather than a one-time legal assessment.

The minimum lifecycle should connect:

**Model identification → regulatory role → GPAI classification → systemic-risk assessment → documentation → copyright governance → training-content transparency → downstream information → security → evaluation → incident management → change management → regulatory assurance.**
