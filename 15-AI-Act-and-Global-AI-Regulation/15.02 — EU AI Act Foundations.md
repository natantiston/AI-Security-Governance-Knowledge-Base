# 15.02 — EU AI Act Foundations

## Part 1 — Foundations

### 2.1 The European Union Artificial Intelligence Act

The **European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act)** is the European Union's comprehensive legal framework for artificial intelligence.

It was adopted as **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689** on 13 June 2024 and published in the Official Journal on 12 July 2024. The regulation establishes harmonized rules for AI and is designed to promote human-centric and trustworthy AI while protecting health, safety, fundamental rights, democracy, the rule of law, and the environment.

The EU AI Act is particularly important for AI Security Governance because it transforms many AI governance principles into legally enforceable requirements.

For GRC professionals, the regulation should therefore be understood not merely as an "AI ethics law," but as a comprehensive regulatory framework affecting:

* AI providers;
* AI deployers;
* importers;
* distributors;
* product manufacturers;
* authorized representatives;
* GPAI model providers;
* public authorities;
* organizations placing AI systems on the EU market;
* organizations using AI systems in the EU.

The official consolidated regulation should be treated as the authoritative legal source because the regulation has subsequently been amended. The current consolidated version published by EUR-Lex reflects amendments through **27 July 2026**.

---

## 2.2 Purpose of the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act has several interconnected objectives.

The regulation seeks to:

1. Establish harmonized rules for AI within the EU internal market.
2. Promote trustworthy and human-centric AI.
3. Protect health and safety.
4. Protect fundamental rights.
5. Protect democracy and the rule of law.
6. Address harmful AI practices.
7. Establish governance requirements for AI providers and deployers.
8. Promote innovation and experimentation.
9. Provide greater legal certainty for organizations developing and using AI.

The regulation therefore attempts to balance two objectives:

**AI Innovation**

and

**AI Risk Protection**

This balance is important because the EU AI Act is not intended simply to restrict AI.

Its regulatory philosophy is based on the idea that AI should be developed and deployed in a manner compatible with European values and fundamental rights while allowing beneficial AI innovation.

---

## 2.3 The EU AI Act as a Regulation

The EU AI Act is a **Regulation**, not a Directive.

This distinction is important for GRC professionals.

An EU regulation generally becomes directly applicable within the EU legal order without requiring each Member State to transpose the entire instrument into separate national legislation.

The EU AI Act therefore creates a harmonized legal framework across the EU.

However, this does **not** mean that all AI-related regulation in Europe comes exclusively from the AI Act.

Organizations must still consider other applicable legal regimes, including:

* GDPR;
* product safety legislation;
* cybersecurity legislation;
* employment law;
* consumer-protection law;
* medical-device regulation;
* financial-sector regulation;
* competition law;
* sector-specific national requirements.

The AI Act should therefore be treated as an important layer within a broader European regulatory ecosystem.

---

## 2.4 The AI Act Is Risk-Based

The central architecture of the EU AI Act is a **risk-based approach**.

The regulation does not impose identical requirements on every AI system.

Instead, regulatory obligations increase according to the risks associated with particular AI practices and systems.

At a conceptual level, the framework can be represented as:

**Unacceptable Risk**

→ prohibited practices

**High Risk**

→ extensive governance and compliance requirements

**Specific Transparency Risk**

→ transparency obligations

**Minimal or Limited Risk**

→ generally fewer mandatory requirements, although other laws and voluntary governance measures may still apply

This risk-based structure is one of the most important concepts in the entire EU AI Act.

The organization must therefore determine **what the AI system does and how it is used**, rather than assuming that all AI systems are regulated identically.

---

## 2.5 Risk Is Determined by Use, Not Simply by Technology

An important GRC principle is that the regulatory significance of AI cannot be determined solely by the underlying model.

Consider a general-purpose model used for:

* drafting an internal email;
* generating marketing content;
* assisting a software developer;
* supporting recruitment;
* assisting a medical decision.

The underlying model may be the same.

However, the regulatory consequences can be dramatically different because the **use case, context, affected persons, and potential harm** differ.

Consequently, AI regulatory classification requires examination of:

* intended purpose;
* function;
* deployment context;
* affected individuals;
* degree of autonomy;
* potential harm;
* applicable sector;
* organizational role.

This principle becomes particularly important when determining whether an AI system falls within a high-risk category.

---

## 2.6 Scope of the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act establishes rules concerning the placing on the market, putting into service, and use of AI systems within the Union.

Its scope also has cross-border implications.

An organization located outside the EU cannot automatically assume that the regulation is irrelevant merely because it has no EU headquarters.

The applicability analysis must consider the circumstances described by the regulation, including whether AI systems or outputs are placed on the EU market or used in contexts covered by the Act.

For GRC purposes, geographic scope should therefore be assessed based on:

**Entity Location + Market Activity + AI Deployment + Affected Persons + Role + Output Location**

rather than simply:

> "Where is our company headquartered?"

---

## 2.7 The Definition of an AI System

The definition of an **AI system** is fundamental because the regulatory obligations generally begin with determining whether the technology falls within the regulation's scope.

The AI Act uses a technology-neutral definition centered on a machine-based system that operates with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and which, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from input how to generate outputs such as:

* predictions;
* content;
* recommendations;
* decisions.

The outputs can influence physical or virtual environments.

The definition is important because it avoids limiting the regulation to one particular AI technology.

It can therefore potentially cover systems using different technical approaches.

The European Commission has also published guidance on the definition of AI systems to help organizations determine whether particular software falls within the scope of the Act.

---

## 2.8 AI System Versus AI Model

The AI Act distinguishes between concepts relating to **AI systems** and **AI models**, particularly in the context of general-purpose AI.

This distinction is important for GRC.

An AI model can be a component of an AI system.

For example:

**Foundation Model**

↓

**AI Application**

↓

**Enterprise Workflow**

↓

**Business Decision**

The regulatory obligations may differ depending on which layer the organization occupies.

An organization developing a general-purpose AI model may have one set of obligations.

An organization integrating that model into a recruitment application may have another.

A third organization using that application may have deployer responsibilities.

Therefore:

> **AI governance must identify both the technology layer and the organizational role.**

---

## 2.9 Providers and Deployers

The EU AI Act establishes important distinctions between different actors.

Two particularly important categories are:

### Provider

An organization that develops an AI system or general-purpose AI model with a view to placing it on the market or putting it into service under its name or trademark.

### Deployer

An organization or person using an AI system under its authority, except where the use is purely personal and non-professional.

These definitions are central to regulatory responsibility.

An organization that purchases an AI system from a vendor may not be the provider, but it can still have significant obligations as a deployer.

This means:

> **Vendor compliance does not automatically equal customer compliance.**

The customer must determine which obligations apply to its own role.

---

## 2.10 Other Actors in the AI Supply Chain

The AI Act also recognizes additional actors.

Depending on the circumstances, organizations may operate as:

* providers;
* deployers;
* importers;
* distributors;
* product manufacturers;
* authorized representatives;
* GPAI model providers.

A company can potentially occupy more than one role depending on its activities.

For example, an organization might:

* develop an AI system internally;
* provide it to customers;
* use another vendor's GPAI model;
* integrate AI into a physical product;
* distribute an AI-enabled product.

Its regulatory responsibilities may therefore vary from system to system.

---

## 2.11 Fundamental Rights as a Regulatory Foundation

The EU AI Act is strongly connected to the protection of fundamental rights.

Relevant concerns include:

* human dignity;
* privacy;
* data protection;
* non-discrimination;
* freedom of expression;
* access to justice;
* democratic processes;
* individual autonomy.

This is important because AI can create harms that are not captured by conventional cybersecurity metrics.

For example:

**Confidentiality = Protected**

does not necessarily mean:

**Fundamental Rights = Protected**

Similarly:

**Model Accuracy = High**

does not necessarily mean:

**Fairness = Acceptable**

AI governance must therefore incorporate both technical and rights-based risk considerations.

---

## 2.12 Human-Centric AI

The EU AI Act promotes a human-centric approach to AI.

This means AI should not be viewed solely as a mechanism for maximizing automation.

Governance should consider:

* human autonomy;
* human oversight;
* safety;
* accountability;
* transparency;
* appropriate intervention.

This becomes especially important for high-risk AI systems.

An organization should be able to determine:

* who is accountable for the AI system;
* who monitors it;
* who can intervene;
* who can override it;
* what happens when the AI behaves unexpectedly;
* how affected persons can be protected.

Human involvement should therefore be designed as an operational control, not merely described in a policy.

---

## 2.13 The EU AI Act and the AI Lifecycle

The regulation should be understood as a lifecycle-oriented framework.

A simplified lifecycle is:

**Planning**

↓

**Design**

↓

**Development**

↓

**Testing**

↓

**Market Placement / Deployment**

↓

**Operation**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Incident Management**

↓

**Modification**

↓

**Retirement**

Different requirements apply at different stages.

For example:

* classification may occur during design;
* risk management may occur throughout development;
* documentation may be required before deployment;
* transparency may apply during operation;
* monitoring continues after deployment;
* incident reporting can arise during operation.

Therefore, AI compliance cannot be treated as a one-time predeployment activity.

---

## 2.14 Implementation Timeline

The EU AI Act entered into force on **1 August 2024**, but its provisions do not all become applicable simultaneously.

As of September 2026, the implementation timeline is particularly important because the AI Act has entered its main application phase.

The current European Commission timeline states that:

* prohibitions, definitions and AI literacy provisions have applied since **2 February 2025**;
* governance rules and GPAI obligations have applied since **2 August 2025**;
* the general application date was **2 August 2026**;
* high-risk AI rules for certain sensitive-use cases apply from **2 December 2027**;
* high-risk AI embedded in regulated products covered by the relevant product legislation applies from **2 August 2028**.

The timeline was affected by the **AI Omnibus Regulation**, which entered into force on **27 July 2026** and introduced, among other changes, extended timelines for certain high-risk AI requirements.

For GRC professionals, this creates an important distinction between:

**Regulation adopted**

and

**Requirement currently applicable**

These are not necessarily the same date.

---

## 2.15 The Current Regulatory Position

The EU AI Act is now in an active enforcement phase.

From **2 August 2026**, the European Commission's AI Office and national authorities began enforcing the AI Act's applicable rules. New transparency requirements also began applying at that time.

This means organizations should not treat the AI Act as a future regulatory concern.

For organizations operating in the EU, AI governance should already address applicable requirements concerning:

* prohibited practices;
* AI literacy;
* governance;
* GPAI;
* transparency requirements that are currently applicable;
* applicable provider/deployer responsibilities;
* regulatory documentation;
* compliance evidence;
* incident management.

At the same time, organizations deploying high-risk systems must prepare for the future applicability dates rather than waiting until the legal deadline.

From an AI Security Governance perspective, the AI Act can be translated into several GRC layers.

### Layer 1 — Applicability

Determine whether the regulation applies.

### Layer 2 — Role

* provider;

### Layer 3 — Classification

Determine the applicable risk category.

### Layer 4 — Obligations

Identify the legal requirements.

### Layer 5 — Controls

Translate obligations into operational controls.

### Layer 6 — Evidence

Maintain evidence demonstrating implementation.

### Layer 7 — Monitoring

Monitor AI systems and regulatory developments.

### Layer 8 — Assurance

Test and independently assess compliance.

This creates the fundamental transformation:

**EU AI Act**

↓

**Legal Requirements**

↓

**GRC Obligations**

↓

**Controls**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Assurance**

## 2.17 Relationship with Other EU Laws

The EU AI Act does not exist in isolation.

AI systems can simultaneously fall under other EU legal frameworks.

Examples include:

* General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR);
* Digital Services Act;
* Digital Markets Act;
* NIS2 Directive;
* Cyber Resilience Act;
* product safety legislation;
* sector-specific financial regulation;
* medical-device legislation;
* employment law.

An AI system may therefore require multiple assessments.

For example:

**AI System**

→ AI Act assessment

→ GDPR assessment

→ Cybersecurity assessment

→ Sector-specific assessment

→ Contractual assessment

The organization should avoid treating AI Act compliance as a substitute for existing legal and regulatory obligations.

---

## 2.18 The AI Act and GDPR

The relationship between the AI Act and GDPR is particularly important.

The AI Act addresses AI-specific risks.

The GDPR governs the processing of personal data and associated rights and obligations.

An AI system processing personal data can therefore be subject to both.

For example:

**Employee AI Monitoring System**

could require:

* AI Act analysis;
* GDPR analysis;
* employment-law analysis;
* cybersecurity assessment;
* internal ethical review.

This produces a broader principle:

> **AI compliance should be integrated with privacy, security, legal and sectoral compliance rather than managed as a standalone silo.**

---

## 2.19 The AI Act and Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is embedded within the AI Act's broader governance framework, particularly for systems where security failures could create significant risks.

From an AI Security Governance perspective, this means security controls may need to address:

* unauthorized access;
* data integrity;
* model integrity;
* system resilience;
* vulnerabilities;
* malicious manipulation;
* adversarial attacks;
* supply-chain risks;
* incident response.

The exact security obligations depend on the applicable AI system category and regulatory requirements.

This creates a direct connection between:

**AI Governance**

and

**Cybersecurity Governance**.

---

The most important lesson for GRC professionals is that the EU AI Act should not be implemented as a collection of isolated legal tasks.

It should become part of an integrated management system.

A mature implementation should establish:

**AI Inventory**

↓

**Regulatory Applicability**

↓

**Risk Classification**

↓

**AI Risk Management**

↓

**Governance**

↓

**Technical and Organizational Controls**

↓

**Documentation**

↓

**Transparency**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Incident Management**

↓

**Regulatory Reporting**

↓

**Assurance**

This approach allows organizations to manage AI regulation systematically across the AI lifecycle.

The EU AI Act therefore represents more than a new compliance obligation. It establishes a foundational regulatory architecture that requires organizations to understand **what AI they operate, what role they perform, what risks the AI creates, which legal obligations apply, what controls are required, and how compliance can be demonstrated continuously**.

# Part 2 — Regulatory Requirements and Control Framework

## 2.21 Establishing an EU AI Act Compliance Framework

An organization implementing the EU AI Act should translate the regulation into a structured compliance framework.

The basic compliance chain is:

**EU AI Act**

↓

**Applicable Articles and Requirements**

↓

**Regulatory Obligations**

↓

**Control Objectives**

↓

**Controls**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Testing**

↓

**Assurance**

The organization should avoid implementing the Act as an unstructured collection of legal interpretations.

Each applicable requirement should be converted into a measurable organizational obligation with:

* a responsible owner;
* a defined control;
* implementation criteria;
* evidence requirements;
* testing procedures;
* review frequency;
* escalation requirements.

---

## 2.22 EU AI Act Applicability Assessment

The first control should be a formal applicability assessment.

For each AI system, the organization should determine:

### Organizational Scope

* Is the organization established in the EU?
* Is it acting as a provider or deployer?
* Is it an importer or distributor?
* Is it a product manufacturer incorporating AI?

### Geographic Scope

* Is the AI system placed on the EU market?
* Is it put into service in the EU?
* Is it used in the EU?
* Does the relevant activity involve persons or outputs covered by the regulation?

### Technology Scope

* Does the technology meet the definition of an AI system?
* Is it a general-purpose AI model?
* Is it integrated into another product or system?

### Use-Case Scope

* What is the intended purpose?
* What sector is involved?
* Who is affected?
* What decisions or actions are influenced?

The assessment should produce a documented applicability decision.

---

## 2.23 AI System Inventory Control

Organizations should maintain a centralized inventory of AI systems within scope.

The inventory should capture at least:

* unique AI system identifier;
* system name;
* business owner;
* technical owner;
* provider;
* model;
* model version;
* intended purpose;
* deployment location;
* users;
* affected individuals;
* data categories;
* risk classification;
* applicable AI Act requirements;
* lifecycle status;
* approval status.

The inventory should distinguish between:

**Known AI**

and

**Unregistered or Shadow AI**.

Shadow AI creates significant governance risk because the organization cannot reliably determine its regulatory exposure if it does not know which AI systems are being used.

---

## 2.24 Role Determination

The organization should formally determine its role under the AI Act for each relevant activity.

Potential roles include:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* product manufacturer;
* authorized representative;
* GPAI model provider.

The role should not be assigned merely based on contractual terminology.

It should be determined based on the organization's actual activities and the applicable legal definitions.

A role assessment should therefore be documented in the AI inventory.

---

## 2.25 Intended Purpose Control

The organization should formally document the intended purpose of each regulated AI system.

The intended-purpose record should identify:

* business objective;
* functions;
* intended users;
* affected persons;
* operating environment;
* decision-making role;
* degree of autonomy;
* expected outputs;
* permitted use;
* prohibited or unauthorized use.

This information becomes important for determining regulatory classification and applicable obligations.

Changes to intended purpose should trigger a governance review.

---

## 2.26 AI Risk Classification Control

The organization should establish a formal AI risk-classification procedure.

At a high level, the assessment should determine whether the system is:

* prohibited;
* high-risk;
* subject to specific transparency obligations;
* general-purpose AI-related;
* outside specific AI Act obligations.

The organization should not rely solely on vendor labels.

A vendor may describe a system as "low risk," while the customer's actual deployment could trigger different regulatory requirements.

Classification should therefore consider:

**Technology + Intended Purpose + Deployment Context + Organizational Role + Jurisdiction**

---

## 2.27 Prohibited-Practice Screening

Before deployment, AI systems should be screened against the prohibited practices established by the AI Act.

The screening should determine whether the proposed use falls within a prohibited category.

The control should operate as a **preventive gate**.

If a proposed use appears prohibited:

**Deployment → Blocked**

until appropriate legal and compliance review is completed.

This control should operate during:

* new-project approval;
* procurement;
* major changes;
* new geographic deployments;
* new use cases.

---

## 2.28 High-Risk AI Governance

Where an AI system is classified as high-risk, enhanced controls are required.

The organization should establish a dedicated high-risk governance workflow covering areas such as:

* risk management;
* data and data governance;
* technical documentation;
* record keeping;
* transparency;
* human oversight;
* accuracy;
* robustness;
* cybersecurity;
* quality management;
* post-market monitoring where applicable.

The precise obligations depend on the organization's role and the specific AI system.

High-risk AI should therefore receive a higher governance level than ordinary enterprise AI.

---

## 2.29 Risk Management System

For applicable high-risk AI systems, risk management should operate continuously throughout the system lifecycle.

The process should include:

**Risk Identification**

→ Identify foreseeable risks.

**Risk Analysis**

→ Evaluate likelihood and severity.

**Risk Mitigation**

→ Implement controls.

**Validation**

→ Test mitigation measures.

**Residual Risk**

→ Determine remaining exposure.

**Monitoring**

→ Monitor risks after deployment.

The risk-management process should address both:

* reasonably foreseeable misuse;
* intended use.

Risk management should also be updated when significant changes occur.

---

## 2.30 Data and Data Governance Controls

Applicable AI systems should have appropriate data governance controls.

These may include:

* data provenance;
* data quality;
* relevance;
* representativeness;
* data preparation;
* bias identification;
* data integrity;
* access controls;
* data protection;
* retention;
* lineage.

Where training, validation or testing data are relevant to regulatory requirements, the organization should maintain sufficient documentation to demonstrate how data were governed.

Data governance should be coordinated with:

* privacy;
* information security;
* records management;
* data governance;
* legal;
* model risk management.

---

## 2.31 Technical Documentation Control

Applicable AI systems require appropriate technical documentation.

The organization should establish:

* standardized documentation templates;
* mandatory fields;
* document ownership;
* approval workflow;
* version control;
* change history;
* retention requirements.

Documentation should allow regulators and authorized stakeholders to understand relevant aspects of:

* system purpose;
* architecture;
* development;
* data;
* testing;
* performance;
* risk management;
* cybersecurity;
* monitoring.

Technical documentation should be updated when material system changes occur.

---

## 2.32 Logging and Record-Keeping Controls

Organizations should establish appropriate logging and record-keeping mechanisms for applicable AI systems.

Controls may include:

* event logging;
* model version records;
* user activity;
* system changes;
* significant decisions or outputs where applicable;
* incidents;
* monitoring events;
* administrative actions.

Logs should be:

* protected from unauthorized modification;
* retained appropriately;
* searchable;
* attributable;
* time-stamped;
* linked to the relevant AI system.

Logging requirements should be determined according to the applicable AI Act obligations and other laws.

---

## 2.33 Human Oversight Controls

Where applicable, human oversight should be explicitly designed.

The organization should identify:

* who supervises the system;
* what information is provided to the human;
* what decisions the human can override;
* when intervention is mandatory;
* how the system can be stopped;
* what happens during system failure;
* what competence is required.

Human oversight should not be merely nominal.

A process where an employee is technically present but cannot realistically understand or challenge the AI output may provide weak governance.

---

## 2.34 Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity

Applicable AI systems should be designed and operated with appropriate:

* accuracy;
* robustness;
* resilience;
* cybersecurity.

Security controls should consider threats such as:

* data poisoning;
* adversarial manipulation;
* model exploitation;
* unauthorized modification;
* prompt injection;
* model extraction;
* malicious inputs;
* supply-chain compromise.

AI cybersecurity should be integrated into the organization's existing cybersecurity framework.

This allows:

**AI Act Requirements**

to connect with:

**Security Policies → Security Controls → Security Testing → Security Monitoring**

---

## 2.35 Quality Management System

Providers of applicable high-risk AI systems should establish an appropriate quality management framework.

The quality system should address relevant processes including:

* regulatory compliance;
* design;
* development;
* testing;
* data management;
* risk management;
* documentation;
* post-market monitoring;
* corrective actions;

## 2.36 Conformity Assessment Control

Where applicable, organizations should establish a conformity-assessment process before placing an AI system on the market or putting it into service.

The workflow should identify:

* applicable conformity route;
* responsible party;
* required technical documentation;
* applicable standards;
* testing requirements;
* assessment results;
* declaration requirements;
* registration requirements where applicable.

The organization should not treat conformity assessment as merely a paperwork exercise.

It should demonstrate that applicable requirements have actually been addressed.

---

## 2.37 CE Marking and Regulatory Readiness

For applicable AI systems associated with products requiring CE marking, the organization should integrate AI compliance into the broader product-conformity process.

The governance workflow should connect:

**AI System**

→ AI Act Assessment

→ Applicable Product Regulation

→ Conformity Assessment

→ Technical Documentation

→ Declaration of Conformity

→ CE Marking where applicable

This is especially important for AI embedded in regulated products.

---

## 2.38 Transparency Controls

Organizations should identify AI systems subject to specific transparency requirements.

Depending on the system and circumstances, controls may include:

* informing users that they are interacting with AI;
* communicating relevant AI-generated content information;
* labeling or marking applicable synthetic content;
* providing appropriate information to users;
* documenting transparency mechanisms.

Transparency should be tested from the user's perspective.

For example:

> Can the intended user reasonably understand that AI is involved?

A technical disclosure hidden in backend documentation may not satisfy a requirement that requires user-facing transparency.

---

## 2.39 AI Literacy Control

Organizations should establish an AI literacy program appropriate to their personnel and circumstances.

The program should identify:

* who uses AI;
* who develops AI;
* who operates AI;
* who manages AI risk;
* who supervises AI;
* what competence each role requires.

Training should address relevant topics such as:

* AI capabilities;
* AI limitations;
* risks;
* misuse;
* security;
* privacy;
* regulatory requirements;
* responsible use.

Training records should provide evidence that appropriate personnel have received relevant instruction.

---

## 2.40 GPAI Governance

Organizations interacting with general-purpose AI models should determine whether they are:

* GPAI model providers;
* downstream AI system providers;
* deployers;
* users of third-party GPAI services.

The governance process should examine:

* provider documentation;
* model capabilities;
* acceptable use;
* technical information;
* security;
* copyright-related compliance information where applicable;
* systemic-risk considerations where relevant;
* contractual responsibilities.

Organizations should avoid assuming that purchasing a GPAI service transfers all regulatory responsibility to the vendor.

## 2.41 Provider Compliance Control Framework

Organizations acting as providers may require a comprehensive compliance framework covering:

The exact control set must therefore be determined from the provider's legal role and applicable AI Act requirements.

## 2.42 Deployer Compliance Framework

Organizations acting as deployers should establish controls appropriate to their responsibilities.

* human oversight;
* monitoring;
* record keeping;
* transparency;
* data governance;
* incident escalation;
* notifying appropriate parties of relevant issues;
* suspending use when necessary.

Deployer governance should be integrated into operational processes rather than maintained solely in the compliance department.

## 2.43 Importer and Distributor Controls

Organizations acting as importers or distributors should establish controls to verify relevant compliance conditions before placing applicable AI systems on the EU market.

* required documentation;

* registration information where applicable;
* corrective-action requirements.

Supply-chain governance should clearly identify when an organization assumes an importer or distributor role.

## 2.48 Control Testing

AI Act controls should be periodically tested.

Testing methods include:

### Documentation Review
