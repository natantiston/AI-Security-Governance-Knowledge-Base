# 15.03 — EU AI Act Risk Classification

## Part 1 — Foundations

### 3.1 The Risk-Based Architecture of the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act is fundamentally structured around the principle that **AI systems should be regulated according to the risks they create**.

The Act does not treat every AI application as equally dangerous.

Instead, it establishes different regulatory requirements depending on factors such as:

* the AI system's intended purpose;
* the function performed;
* the context in which it is used;
* the potential harm;
* the people affected;
* the degree of autonomy;
* the organization's role;
* whether the system falls into a specifically regulated category.

This creates a foundational GRC principle:

> **AI risk classification determines the depth and type of regulatory governance required.**

The classification process is therefore one of the most important controls in an AI regulatory program.

---

## 3.2 Why AI Risk Classification Matters

AI classification determines what happens after an AI system enters the governance process.

A classification decision can influence:

* whether the AI use is prohibited;
* whether the system is high-risk;
* whether transparency obligations apply;
* whether GPAI requirements are relevant;
* what documentation is required;
* what risk-management processes are required;
* whether conformity assessment is necessary;
* what monitoring is required;
* what regulatory evidence must be maintained.

An incorrect classification can therefore create two opposite problems.

### Under-Classification

The organization treats a regulated AI system as low risk.

Potential consequences include:

* missing mandatory controls;
* inadequate documentation;
* insufficient oversight;
* regulatory non-compliance;
* increased harm to affected individuals.

### Over-Classification

The organization applies unnecessary controls to a low-risk system.

Potential consequences include:

* unnecessary cost;
* excessive bureaucracy;
* slower innovation;
* inefficient resource allocation.

A mature AI GRC program therefore aims for:

**Accurate + Consistent + Evidence-Based + Reproducible Classification**

---

## 3.3 The Four Broad Risk Levels

The EU AI Act is commonly represented through four broad risk categories:

1. **Unacceptable Risk**
2. **High Risk**
3. **Limited or Specific Transparency Risk**
4. **Minimal or No Risk**

These categories should not be interpreted as simply four numerical scores.

They represent different **legal treatment regimes**.

### Unacceptable Risk

Certain AI practices are prohibited.

### High Risk

Specific AI systems are subject to extensive mandatory requirements.

### Transparency Risk

Certain AI systems have specific transparency obligations.

### Minimal or No Risk

Most systems in this category are not subject to significant AI Act obligations beyond requirements that may arise from other provisions or laws, although voluntary governance may still be appropriate.

The European Commission describes this as a risk-based approach in which regulatory obligations increase according to the potential risks associated with AI use.

---

## 3.4 Classification Is a Legal Determination

AI risk classification should not be treated as merely a technical model-risk score.

An organization may already have:

* cybersecurity risk scoring;
* model risk scoring;
* privacy risk scoring;
* operational risk scoring;
* enterprise risk scoring.

These are useful, but they do not automatically determine the AI Act classification.

For example:

**Internal Risk Score = Low**

does not necessarily mean:

**EU AI Act Classification = Low/Minimal**

The AI Act uses legal criteria that must be evaluated according to the regulation.

Therefore:

> **Internal AI risk scoring and EU AI Act classification should be connected but kept conceptually distinct.**

---

## 3.5 The Role of Intended Purpose

**Intended purpose** is one of the most important concepts in AI Act classification.

The organization must determine what the AI system is intended to do and how it is expected to operate.

Relevant questions include:

* What function does it perform?
* Who uses it?
* Who is affected?
* What decision does it support?
* What action can result?
* What level of autonomy exists?
* In what environment is it deployed?
* What consequences could result from an incorrect output?

The same underlying AI technology can potentially have different regulatory implications depending on its intended use.

For example:

**AI Model**

→ Used to summarize internal meeting notes

may have relatively limited regulatory significance.

The same underlying technology:

**AI Model**

→ Used to evaluate job candidates

requires a substantially different regulatory analysis.

The technology alone does not provide the complete answer.

---

## 3.6 Classification Depends on Context

AI risk must be evaluated in context.

The following dimensions should be considered:

### Who?

Who is affected?

* employees;
* consumers;
* students;
* patients;
* applicants;
* citizens;
* customers.

### What?

What does the AI do?

* generate content;
* classify;
* recommend;
* predict;
* decide;
* identify;
* monitor.

### Where?

Where is it used?

* workplace;
* healthcare;
* education;
* financial services;
* public administration;
* consumer environment;
* critical infrastructure.

### How?

How much autonomy does the system have?

* advisory;
* decision-support;
* semi-automated;
* automated.

### Consequences?

What happens if the system is wrong?

* inconvenience;
* financial loss;
* discrimination;
* denial of opportunity;
* safety impact;
* fundamental-rights impact.

This contextual analysis is central to correct classification.

---

## 3.7 Prohibited AI Practices

The highest regulatory category concerns AI practices that are prohibited under the AI Act.

These are generally associated with unacceptable risks to individuals or society.

The regulation prohibits specified AI practices rather than establishing a general rule that every high-risk AI system is prohibited.

This distinction is critical:

**Prohibited**

means the specified practice is not permitted.

**High Risk**

means the system may be permitted but must meet extensive regulatory requirements.

Therefore:

> **High risk does not mean prohibited.**

This distinction should be explicitly incorporated into organizational classification procedures.

---

## 3.8 High-Risk AI Systems

High-risk AI represents the most significant category of regulated AI that is generally permitted subject to extensive requirements.

The Act identifies high-risk systems primarily through categories established in its legal framework, including certain AI systems:

* used as safety components of products or falling under specified regulated-product frameworks;
* listed in Annex III where they are used for specified purposes.

Examples of regulated high-risk use areas include certain applications involving:

* biometrics;
* critical infrastructure;
* education;
* employment;
* access to essential services;
* law enforcement;
* migration and border control;
* administration of justice;
* democratic processes.

However, not every AI system used in one of these broad sectors is automatically high-risk.

The organization must evaluate the specific legal criteria.

---

## 3.9 High-Risk Classification Is Not Simply Sector-Based

A common misunderstanding is:

> "AI used in healthcare is high-risk."

That is too broad.

Similarly:

> "AI used in HR is always high-risk."

That is also too broad.

The organization must determine whether the specific system and use case meet the relevant criteria.

The classification assessment should therefore evaluate:

**Sector + Function + Intended Purpose + Legal Category + Context**

rather than relying solely on industry labels.

---

## 3.10 Annex III

**Annex III** is particularly important for AI GRC because it identifies categories of high-risk AI systems based on specified intended purposes.

The relevant areas include:

1. Biometrics
2. Critical infrastructure
3. Education and vocational training
4. Employment, worker management and access to self-employment
5. Access to essential private services and essential public services and benefits
6. Law enforcement
7. Migration, asylum and border control management
8. Administration of justice and democratic processes

The exact legal classification must be determined from the applicable provisions and conditions rather than from the category name alone.

For GRC purposes, Annex III should therefore be incorporated into the classification decision tree.

---

## 3.11 Product-Related High-Risk AI

The EU AI Act also recognizes another important pathway to high-risk classification.

Certain AI systems can be high-risk because they are:

* intended to be used as safety components of products; or
* themselves products,

where the relevant products or systems are covered by specified Union harmonization legislation.

This means classification should not rely exclusively on Annex III.

The assessment should therefore include at least two major pathways:

**Path A**

Specified product-related AI systems

**Path B**

Specified AI systems listed in Annex III

This distinction is essential for organizations developing AI-enabled products.

---

## 3.12 Exceptions and Conditions

The AI Act includes conditions under which certain AI systems that fall within categories associated with high-risk use may not be classified as high-risk, depending on their specific characteristics and intended purpose.

This means classification cannot be performed using a simple:

> "Sector = High Risk"

rule.

The organization must examine the applicable legal conditions carefully.

For example, the assessment may need to determine whether the AI system:

* performs a specific function;
* materially influences a decision;
* performs a narrow procedural task;
* detects patterns without replacing human judgment;
* performs another function that affects the classification analysis.

Legal interpretation should be documented where classification is not straightforward.

---

## 3.13 The Importance of "Substantial Influence"

For some high-risk use cases, the effect that an AI system has on human decision-making can be important.

The GRC assessment should therefore ask:

> Does the AI system materially influence the outcome of a decision?

This is different from merely asking:

> Does a human remain involved?

A human may technically make the final decision while relying almost entirely on an AI recommendation.

Therefore, meaningful human involvement should be assessed in context.

---

## 3.14 Transparency-Risk AI

Certain AI systems are subject to specific transparency obligations.

These systems may include AI that:

* interacts directly with people;
* generates or manipulates certain content;
* performs other functions for which the AI Act establishes transparency requirements.

The underlying principle is:

> **People should receive appropriate information when AI involvement could affect their understanding of an interaction or content.**

Transparency requirements are not equivalent to high-risk requirements.

A system can be:

**Not High Risk**

but still:

**Subject to Transparency Obligations**

This distinction is important for compliance classification.

---

## 3.15 General-Purpose AI

General-purpose AI introduces another dimension of the AI Act.

GPAI models can support many downstream applications.

The regulatory framework therefore establishes specific obligations for providers of GPAI models.

The organization should determine:

* whether it develops a GPAI model;
* whether it provides one;
* whether it integrates a third-party GPAI model;
* whether it develops a downstream AI system;
* whether systemic-risk provisions are relevant.

GPAI classification should therefore be considered separately from the traditional prohibited/high-risk/transparency framework.

---

## 3.16 Systemic-Risk GPAI

Certain GPAI models may present systemic risk because of their capabilities, scale or other legally relevant characteristics.

These models can face enhanced obligations.

For GRC purposes, organizations should therefore distinguish:

**GPAI**

from

**GPAI with Systemic Risk**

The latter requires enhanced governance.

The classification should be monitored because model capabilities and regulatory thresholds can change.

---

## 3.17 Minimal-Risk AI

Many AI applications present relatively limited risks.

Examples may include ordinary uses such as:

* spam filtering;
* recommendation systems;
* AI-assisted productivity;
* certain game-related AI;
* routine automation.

The fact that an AI system falls outside significant mandatory AI Act obligations does not mean that no governance is necessary.

Organizations should still consider:

* security;
* privacy;
* contractual requirements;
* intellectual property;
* responsible use;
* internal AI policy.

Therefore:

**Minimal Regulatory Risk**

does not mean:

**Zero Organizational Risk**.

---

## 3.18 Classification Versus Organizational Risk

An organization should maintain separate records for:

### Legal Classification

What classification does the AI Act assign?

### Enterprise Risk Rating

What level of business risk does the organization assign?

### Security Risk

What cybersecurity exposure exists?

### Privacy Risk

What data-protection risk exists?

### Model Risk

What reliability and performance risk exists?

These can be integrated in one AI risk profile but should not be conflated.

A useful model is:

**Legal Classification**

*

**Enterprise Risk**

*

**Security Risk**

*

**Privacy Risk**

*

**Model Risk**

=

**Integrated AI Risk Profile**

---

## 3.19 Classification Evidence

Every classification decision should be supported by evidence.

Evidence should include:

* intended-purpose statement;
* system description;
* architecture;
* provider documentation;
* deployment context;
* affected-person analysis;
* applicable legal provisions;
* classification questionnaire;
* legal/compliance analysis;
* final classification;
* approving authority;
* date;
* version.

The evidence should allow an independent reviewer to understand:

> **Why was this AI system classified this way?**

---

## 3.20 Classification Is a Lifecycle Activity

Classification should not be performed once and forgotten.

Reassessment triggers should include:

* change in intended purpose;
* new users;
* new geographic market;
* new sector;
* new data;
* increased autonomy;
* model upgrade;
* new capabilities;
* integration into a regulated product;
* change in provider;
* regulatory amendment;
* new regulatory guidance.

The lifecycle is therefore:

**Initial Classification**

↓

**Deployment**

↓

**Continuous Monitoring**

↓

**Change Detection**

↓

**Reclassification**

↓

**Control Update**

↓

**Approval**

This is essential because the regulatory status of an AI system can change during its lifecycle.

---

## 3.21 Classification Governance

Classification should have clear accountability.

A typical model is:

**Business Owner**

provides intended purpose and use context.

**Technical Owner**

provides system and architecture information.

**Compliance**

performs regulatory assessment.

**Legal**

resolves significant legal interpretation.

**Security**

assesses security risk.

**Privacy**

assesses privacy implications.

**AI Governance Committee**

reviews significant or disputed classifications.

This creates separation between technical description and legal classification.

---

## 3.22 Classification Decision Tree

A simplified governance decision tree is:

**Step 1 — Is this an AI system under the Act?**

↓

If yes:

**Step 2 — Is the practice prohibited?**

↓

If no:

**Step 3 — Is it a GPAI model/use requiring specific analysis?**

↓

**Step 4 — Is it a high-risk AI system under the applicable legal criteria?**

↓

If no:

**Step 5 — Are specific transparency obligations applicable?**

↓

If no:

**Step 6 — Treat as minimal/no significant AI Act risk, while considering other legal and organizational requirements.**

This is only a conceptual framework.

Actual classification must be performed against the current legal text and applicable guidance.

---

## 3.23 Classification Governance Principle

The most important classification principle is:

> **Do not classify AI based on what the technology is called. Classify it based on what the law covers and how the AI is actually intended and used.**

Terms such as:

* chatbot;
* copilot;
* assistant;
* machine-learning model;
* foundation model;
* automation;
* decision engine;

do not by themselves establish the legal classification.

The classification must be based on the applicable legal criteria.

---

## 3.24 Classification as a GRC Control

From an AI GRC perspective, classification should be treated as a formal control.

**Control Objective:**

> Ensure every AI system is accurately classified according to applicable EU AI Act requirements before deployment and whenever material changes occur.

Control activities should include:

1. AI registration.
2. Intended-purpose documentation.
3. Role determination.
4. Classification assessment.
5. Legal/compliance review.
6. Evidence retention.
7. Approval.
8. Periodic reassessment.
9. Change-triggered reassessment.

Control effectiveness should be tested periodically.

---

## 3.25 Classification and Regulatory Proportionality

The classification system allows the organization to apply resources proportionally.

For example:

**Prohibited**

→ Legal review and deployment prevention

**High Risk**

→ Enhanced governance and extensive controls

**Transparency Risk**

→ Specific transparency controls

**Minimal Risk**

→ Simplified governance

This prevents the organization from applying the same compliance burden to every AI system.

The ultimate objective is:

> **The higher the legally relevant AI risk, the stronger and more comprehensive the governance controls should become.**

---

## 3.26 Classification as the Entry Point to AI Regulatory GRC

The classification process connects legal requirements to operational controls.

The complete chain is:

**AI Discovery**

↓

**AI System Definition**

↓

**Organizational Role**

↓

**Intended Purpose**

↓

**EU AI Act Classification**

↓

**Applicable Obligations**

↓

**Control Requirements**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Monitoring**

↓

**Reclassification**

This makes classification the central decision point between **AI inventory management** and **AI regulatory compliance**.

A reliable classification capability therefore provides the foundation for determining which EU AI Act requirements apply to each AI system and how deeply the organization must govern that system.


# Part 2 — Classification Controls and Implementation

## 3.27 Establishing an AI Act Classification Control Framework

The organization should implement a formal process for determining the regulatory classification of every AI system within the scope of the EU AI Act.

The classification framework should translate legal criteria into a controlled workflow:

**AI Discovery**

→ **AI System Qualification**

→ **Role Determination**

→ **Intended-Purpose Assessment**

→ **Prohibited-Practice Screening**

→ **High-Risk Assessment**

→ **Transparency Assessment**

→ **GPAI Assessment**

→ **Classification Decision**

→ **Approval**

→ **Evidence**

→ **Ongoing Reassessment**

The classification decision should be documented before the organization relies on the system in a regulated business process.

---

## 3.28 AI Classification Intake

Every AI system should enter the classification process through a standardized intake form.

The intake should collect:

* AI system name;
* system owner;
* business owner;
* provider;
* model;
* model version;
* intended purpose;
* business process;
* users;
* affected persons;
* geographic scope;
* sector;
* level of autonomy;
* decisions influenced;
* data processed;
* outputs generated;
* integration with other systems.

The intake should also identify whether the organization is acting as:

* provider;
* deployer;
* importer;
* distributor;
* product manufacturer;
* GPAI model provider;
* another relevant role.

Incomplete information should prevent final classification.

---

## 3.29 AI System Qualification

Before performing risk classification, the organization should determine whether the technology qualifies as an AI system under the applicable definition.

This should not be based solely on marketing terminology.

The assessment should consider the actual technical characteristics and functions of the system.

The organization should document:

**Technology Description**

*

**System Function**

*

**Autonomy**

*

**Inference Capability**

*

**Outputs**

*

**Intended Purpose**

=

**AI System Qualification Decision**

Where the answer is uncertain, the organization should escalate to Legal or Compliance.

---

## 3.30 Organizational Role Assessment

The organization should formally document its regulatory role.

A role assessment should answer:

1. Who developed the AI?
2. Who places it on the market?
3. Who puts it into service?
4. Who operates it?
5. Under whose name or trademark is it provided?
6. Who imports it?
7. Who distributes it?
8. Who integrates it into a product?
9. Who deploys it?

The result should identify the organization's applicable role.

The same organization can have different roles for different AI systems.

---

## 3.31 Intended-Purpose Questionnaire

The classification process should include a standardized intended-purpose questionnaire.

Questions should include:

* What is the AI intended to accomplish?
* What business process does it support?
* Who are its intended users?
* Who may be affected?
* Does it make decisions?
* Does it recommend decisions?
* Does it influence decisions?
* Does it interact directly with people?
* Does it evaluate people?
* Does it control or influence physical systems?
* Is it used in a regulated sector?
* Is it part of a regulated product?
* Is it used for safety-related functions?

The answers should be supported by business and technical documentation.

---

## 3.32 Prohibited-Practice Screening Control

Every applicable AI use case should pass through a prohibited-practice screening process.

The screening should compare the proposed use against the prohibited practices established by the AI Act.

A preventive control should operate as:

**Potential Prohibited Use**

→ **Immediate Compliance/Legal Review**

→ **Deployment Hold**

→ **Decision**

If the use falls within a prohibited practice, the organization should not proceed with deployment.

This control should be performed:

* during initial design;
* during procurement;
* before deployment;
* after major changes;
* when entering new markets.

---

## 3.33 High-Risk Assessment

If an AI system is not prohibited, it should be assessed against the high-risk provisions.

The assessment should examine both principal high-risk pathways:

### Path 1 — Product-Related

Is the AI system a safety component of a product, or is it itself a product, covered by relevant Union harmonization legislation specified by the AI Act?

### Path 2 — Annex III

Does the AI system fall within a listed Annex III area and meet the applicable conditions?

The classification record should identify which pathway was considered.

---

## 3.34 Annex III Classification Control

The organization should maintain an Annex III decision matrix.

The matrix should map:

**Annex III Area**

→ **Specific AI Function**

→ **Intended Purpose**

→ **Applicable Conditions**

→ **High-Risk Decision**

Examples of Annex III areas include:

* biometrics;
* critical infrastructure;
* education;
* employment;
* access to essential services;
* law enforcement;
* migration and border control;
* justice and democratic processes.

The assessment must use the current legal text and applicable guidance rather than relying on broad sector labels.

---

## 3.35 Product-Related Classification Control

Organizations developing AI-enabled products should perform a separate product-related classification assessment.

The workflow should determine:

1. What product is involved?
2. Which Union harmonization legislation applies?
3. Is the AI a safety component?
4. Is the AI itself a regulated product?
5. Does the AI Act classify the system as high-risk under the relevant provisions?
6. What conformity-assessment requirements apply?

This assessment should be coordinated with product compliance and engineering teams.

---

## 3.36 Exception Analysis

The classification process should evaluate whether an AI system falls within a relevant exception or exclusion.

The organization should document:

* relevant legal provision;
* factual circumstances;
* applicability conditions;
* evidence;
* legal interpretation;
* final decision.

The organization should not simply mark:

> "Exception applies"

without explaining why.

Exceptions should be reviewed whenever the AI system changes.

---

## 3.37 Decision Influence Assessment

For relevant use cases, the organization should assess how much influence the AI system has over a decision.

A useful scale is:

**Level 0 — No Decision Influence**

AI has no meaningful role in the decision.

**Level 1 — Informational**

AI provides information but does not directly support a consequential decision.

**Level 2 — Advisory**

AI provides a recommendation to a human.

**Level 3 — Material Influence**

The AI recommendation significantly affects the human decision.

**Level 4 — Automated Decision**

The AI effectively determines the outcome.

This internal scale does not replace the legal test.

It provides a consistent way of documenting the AI's operational influence.

---

## 3.38 Human Involvement Assessment

The classification process should document human involvement.

Questions should include:

* Is a human required to review the output?
* Can the human reject the output?
* Can the human override the system?
* Does the human have sufficient information?
* Does the human have sufficient time?
* Can the human intervene before harm occurs?
* Is the human realistically capable of challenging the AI?

A nominal human reviewer should not automatically be considered meaningful human oversight.

---

## 3.39 Sector Assessment

The organization should classify the business context.

Relevant sectors may include:

* employment;
* education;
* healthcare;
* finance;
* public administration;
* law enforcement;
* critical infrastructure;
* consumer services;
* telecommunications;
* manufacturing.

The sector should be used as a **classification input**, not as the final classification.

---

## 3.40 Affected-Person Assessment

The classification workflow should identify who may be affected.

Categories can include:

* employees;
* job applicants;
* customers;
* patients;
* students;
* consumers;
* citizens;
* vulnerable persons;
* members of the public.

The organization should also consider:

* number of affected individuals;
* sensitivity of the population;
* potential consequences;
* ability to challenge AI decisions.

This information helps determine regulatory significance and appropriate governance.

---

## 3.41 Consequence Assessment

The organization should document what happens when the AI system is wrong.

Potential consequences include:

### Low

* inconvenience;
* minor productivity loss.

### Moderate

* financial loss;
* service disruption;
* customer dissatisfaction.

### High

* employment impact;
* denial of important services;
* discrimination;
* significant financial harm.

### Severe

* safety consequences;
* fundamental-rights impact;
* widespread harm.

This internal consequence rating complements—not replaces—the legal classification.

---

Every classification should generate a formal **AI Regulatory Classification Decision Record**.

Minimum fields should include:

* AI system ID;
* classification date;
* reviewer;
* business owner;
* technical owner;
* organizational role;
* intended purpose;
* jurisdiction;
* applicable legal provisions;
* prohibited-practice result;
* high-risk assessment;
* transparency assessment;
* GPAI assessment;
* final classification;
* rationale;
* evidence;
* approval;
* review date.

This record should be retained throughout the system lifecycle.

---

## 3.43 Classification Approval Workflow

A practical approval process is:

**AI System Owner**

→ submits assessment

↓

**Technical Review**

→ validates system information

↓

**Compliance**

→ evaluates regulatory scope

↓

**Legal**

→ reviews material legal questions

↓

**Privacy/Security**

→ assess related obligations

↓

**AI Governance**

→ confirms classification

↓

**Authorized Decision Maker**

→ approves where required

The approval chain should be proportional to the risk.

---

## 3.44 Classification Evidence Requirements

Evidence should demonstrate how the classification decision was reached.

Examples include:

* architecture diagrams;
* product descriptions;
* vendor documentation;
* intended-purpose statements;
* business-process documents;
* technical specifications;
* data-flow diagrams;
* user descriptions;
* legal analysis;
* classification questionnaires;
* test results.

Evidence should be linked to the relevant decision.

---

## 3.45 Classification Quality Assurance

Classification should be subject to quality assurance.

Quality checks should verify:

* all required questions were answered;
* evidence exists;
* legal criteria were considered;
* intended purpose is documented;
* organizational role is correct;
* exceptions were assessed;
* approval was obtained;
* reassessment date exists.

A sample of classifications should be independently reviewed.

---

## 3.46 Classification Consistency Testing

Large organizations may classify similar AI systems differently across business units.

To prevent inconsistency, Compliance should periodically perform cross-system reviews.

For example:

**AI System A**

and

**AI System B**

perform similar functions.

If they receive different classifications, the organization should determine whether the difference is justified by:

* intended purpose;
* deployment context;
* affected persons;
* autonomy;
* jurisdiction;
* legal conditions.

This creates classification consistency.

---

## 3.47 Regulatory Classification Changes

A classification should be changed when the facts underlying the original decision change.

Triggers include:

* new purpose;
* new functionality;
* new users;
* new geography;
* new sector;
* increased autonomy;
* model upgrade;
* integration with a new system;
* changed data;
* new product integration;
* regulatory amendment;
* updated regulatory guidance.

The change-management system should automatically initiate reassessment when defined triggers occur.

---

## 3.48 Classification Review Frequency

Classification should be reviewed:

* when material changes occur;
* when regulatory requirements change;
* when relevant guidance changes;
* after significant incidents;
* periodically according to risk.

Higher-risk AI systems should generally receive more frequent review.

Low-risk systems may use longer review cycles where appropriate.

The review frequency should be documented.

---

## 3.49 Classification and Procurement

AI procurement should include classification controls before contract approval.

Procurement should collect:

* provider information;
* AI capabilities;
* intended purpose;
* model information;
* data processing;
* geographic scope;
* regulatory information;
* security documentation.

The AI governance team should then determine the organization's regulatory role and obligations.

This prevents organizations from purchasing AI systems that cannot be governed appropriately.

---

## 3.50 Classification and Vendor Claims

Vendor claims should be treated as evidence—not as automatic legal conclusions.

A vendor may state:

> "This AI is compliant with the EU AI Act."

The organization should ask:

* Compliant for which role?
* Under which use case?
* Under which jurisdiction?
* Which provisions?
* What evidence supports the claim?
* Which obligations remain with the customer?

The customer should perform its own applicability analysis.

---

## 3.51 Classification and Shadow AI

Unapproved AI use should be subject to retrospective classification.

When shadow AI is discovered:

**Discovery**

→ **Identify System**

→ **Identify User**

→ **Determine Purpose**

→ **Determine Data**

→ **Determine Role**

→ **Classify**

→ **Approve, Restrict or Remove**

The objective is not merely to punish unauthorized users.

The organization should determine why the AI was used outside the governance process and address the underlying process gap.

---

## 3.52 Classification Control Metrics

Organizations should monitor classification effectiveness.

Useful metrics include:

* percentage of AI systems classified;
* percentage classified before deployment;
* unclassified AI systems;
* overdue classifications;
* reclassification rate;
* classification exceptions;
* classification disputes;
* classification errors;
* average classification time.

A particularly important metric is:

> **Percentage of production AI systems with a current, approved classification.**

---

## 3.53 Classification Control Testing

Internal audit or compliance should periodically test the classification control.

Testing should include:

### Design Testing

Does the procedure address all required classification categories?

### Implementation Testing

Is the procedure embedded into AI governance?

### Operating Testing

Are classifications actually being performed?

### Accuracy Testing

Are sampled classifications legally and factually supported?

### Change Testing

Do material changes trigger reassessment?

This provides assurance that classification is not merely documented but effective.

---

## 3.54 Classification Remediation

If an incorrect classification is identified, the organization should:

1. Record the finding.
2. Determine affected systems.
3. Reassess classification.
4. Determine whether additional obligations apply.
5. Implement missing controls.
6. Assess potential regulatory exposure.
7. Determine whether notifications are required.
8. Document corrective action.
9. Validate remediation.

The organization should also perform root-cause analysis.

---

## 3.55 Classification Control Framework

The complete classification control framework can be represented as:

**1. Register AI**

↓

**2. Qualify AI System**

↓

**3. Determine Organizational Role**

↓

**4. Document Intended Purpose**

↓

**5. Screen Prohibited Practices**

↓

**6. Assess Product-Related High Risk**

↓

**7. Assess Annex III High Risk**

↓

**8. Assess Transparency Obligations**

↓

**9. Assess GPAI Relevance**

↓

**10. Determine Classification**

↓

**11. Obtain Approval**

↓

**12. Record Evidence**

↓

**13. Monitor Changes**

↓

**14. Reclassify When Necessary**

This workflow should be integrated into the enterprise AI lifecycle.

---

## 3.56 Classification as a Deployment Gate

The classification process should ultimately become a deployment control.

A production deployment should require:

**AI System Registered**

AND

**Role Determined**

AND

**Classification Approved**

AND

**Required Assessments Complete**

AND

**Required Controls Implemented**

AND

**Required Evidence Available**

→ **Deployment Permitted**

If any mandatory condition fails:

→ **Deployment Blocked or Escalated**

This makes classification a preventive regulatory control rather than an administrative exercise.

---

## 3.57 Target Control Objective

The overall control objective should be:

> **Ensure that every AI system within the scope of the EU AI Act is accurately classified according to its legal characteristics, intended purpose, deployment context and organizational role, with documented evidence, appropriate approval and mandatory reassessment when material changes occur.**

This control objective connects AI discovery to the broader EU AI Act compliance lifecycle:

**AI Inventory → Classification → Regulatory Obligations → Controls → Evidence → Monitoring → Reclassification**

A reliable classification process is therefore the mechanism through which an organization determines the appropriate level of EU AI Act governance for each AI system.

# Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Risk and Assurance

## 3.58 Classification Governance Model

EU AI Act risk classification should operate as a formal governance process rather than as a technical questionnaire completed by an AI development team.

A mature governance model establishes accountability across:

* Board and executive management;
* AI governance committee;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Enterprise risk;
* Privacy;
* Information security;
* Product management;
* Engineering;
* Data science;
* Procurement;
* Internal audit.

The governance principle is:

> **The team developing an AI system provides the facts; appropriately authorized governance functions determine and approve the regulatory classification.**

This separation helps prevent commercial or technical teams from making unsupported legal conclusions.

---

## 3.59 Classification Accountability

Every AI system should have clearly assigned classification accountability.

### Business Owner

Responsible for:

* intended business purpose;
* operational context;
* affected users;
* affected individuals;
* business decisions influenced by AI.

### Technical Owner

Responsible for:

* system architecture;
* model capabilities;
* technical behavior;
* integrations;
* autonomy;
* technical changes.

### AI Governance

Responsible for:

* classification methodology;
* governance workflow;
* classification consistency;
* escalation;
* central records.

### Compliance

Responsible for:

* regulatory applicability;
* obligation interpretation;
* regulatory mapping;
* compliance oversight.

### Legal

Responsible for:

* material legal interpretation;
* disputed classifications;
* regulatory uncertainty;
* significant regulatory exposure.

### Security

Responsible for:

* cybersecurity risk;
* technical security controls;
* relevant security characteristics.

### Privacy

Responsible for:

* personal-data implications;
* privacy risk;
* interaction with data-protection requirements.

---

## 3.60 Classification Decision Rights

The organization should formally define who can approve each classification.

For example:

| Classification                     | Minimum Governance               |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Minimal/no significant AI Act risk | Standard governance              |
| Transparency-relevant              | Compliance review                |
| Potential high-risk                | Enhanced compliance/legal review |
| High-risk                          | Formal governance approval       |
| Potentially prohibited             | Legal + Compliance escalation    |
| Disputed classification            | AI Governance Committee          |

The exact approval model should reflect the organization's legal exposure and risk appetite.

---

## 3.61 Separation of Duties

Classification should incorporate appropriate separation of duties.

The same individual should not necessarily:

* define the business purpose;
* perform the legal classification;
* approve the classification;
* approve their own exception.

For significant AI systems, an appropriate separation may be:

**Business**

→ defines purpose

**Technical**

→ defines capabilities

**Compliance/Legal**

→ evaluates regulatory criteria

**Governance**

→ approves classification

**Internal Audit**

→ independently assures the process

This reduces conflicts of interest.

---

## 3.62 Classification Governance Committee

An AI Governance Committee should review classifications when:

* the legal interpretation is complex;
* the system may be high-risk;
* the system may involve prohibited practices;
* multiple jurisdictions conflict;
* business and compliance disagree;
* the classification has significant consequences;
* a material regulatory change affects the system.

The committee should maintain decision records explaining:

* facts considered;
* legal provisions considered;
* assumptions;
* decision;
* rationale;
* dissenting opinions where relevant;
* required controls.

---

## 3.63 Classification Escalation

The organization should define explicit escalation triggers.

Examples include:

**Potential Prohibited Practice**

→ immediate Legal/Compliance escalation.

**Potential High-Risk AI**

→ enhanced governance review.

**Uncertain Legal Interpretation**

→ Legal assessment.

**Conflicting Business and Compliance Views**

→ governance committee.

**Regulatory Change**

→ mandatory reassessment.

**Material AI Incident**

→ classification review.

Escalation procedures should be documented before an incident occurs.

---

## 3.64 Classification Risk Acceptance

Classification disputes should not be resolved by informal risk acceptance.

For example, a business owner should not be able to say:

> "We believe this is low risk, so we will proceed."

If the legal classification is uncertain, the organization should follow the established legal and governance process.

The organization may accept certain **residual risks**, where legally permissible.

It should not use risk acceptance to disregard mandatory legal requirements.

---

## 3.65 Classification Quality Management

Classification quality should be managed as a measurable governance process.

Quality controls should verify:

* completeness;
* consistency;
* accuracy;
* evidence;
* legal references;
* approval;
* timeliness;
* reassessment.

The organization should periodically sample classifications to identify systematic errors.

For example, if several HR systems are incorrectly classified as low risk, the organization may have a methodological problem rather than isolated individual errors.

---

## 3.66 Classification Consistency

Organizations operating many AI systems should maintain consistency across business units.

A centralized classification methodology should establish:

* standardized questions;
* standardized terminology;
* decision criteria;
* evidence requirements;
* approval levels;
* exception procedures.

Central governance should periodically compare similar AI systems.

Differences should be justified by actual differences in:

* purpose;
* context;
* functionality;
* users;
* affected persons;
* autonomy;
* legal conditions.

---

## 3.67 Classification Methodology Governance

The classification methodology itself should be version controlled.

The organization should maintain:

* methodology version;
* effective date;
* regulatory basis;
* changes;
* approvers;
* affected systems;
* migration/reassessment requirements.

When the methodology changes because of new regulation or guidance, the organization should identify which existing classifications require review.

---

## 3.68 Regulatory Interpretation Records

When classification involves uncertainty, the organization should create a regulatory interpretation record.

The record should include:

* question;
* relevant legal provision;
* factual assumptions;
* regulatory guidance;
* legal analysis;
* interpretation;
* decision;
* responsible reviewer;
* date;
* review trigger.

This creates institutional knowledge and prevents different teams from repeatedly solving the same regulatory question independently.

---

## 3.69 AI Classification Risk Register

Classification-related risks should be included in the AI regulatory risk register.

Examples include:

### Risk 1 — Incomplete AI Inventory

Unknown AI systems cannot be classified.

### Risk 2 — Incorrect Intended Purpose

Incorrect business descriptions result in incorrect classifications.

### Risk 3 — Regulatory Misinterpretation

The organization misunderstands a legal criterion.

### Risk 4 — Stale Classification

The system changes but the classification is not updated.

### Risk 5 — Vendor Misrepresentation

The organization relies incorrectly on supplier classification.

### Risk 6 — Inconsistent Classification

Similar systems receive different classifications without justification.

These risks should have owners and mitigation plans.

---

## 3.70 Classification and Change Management

Change management should be directly connected to classification.

A material change should trigger a classification review.

Examples include:

* new functionality;
* new model;
* new model version;
* new users;
* new geographic location;
* new business process;
* new affected population;
* new data;
* increased autonomy;
* integration with another system;
* product integration.

The change workflow should ask:

> **Does this change alter the AI system's regulatory classification or obligations?**

---

## 3.71 Classification and Model Governance

Model governance should integrate with regulatory classification.

A model change should trigger an assessment of whether:

* intended purpose changed;
* capabilities changed;
* autonomy increased;
* affected persons changed;
* performance changed materially;
* new regulatory categories become relevant.

For example:

**Model v1**

may support a limited internal task.

**Model v2**

may introduce automated decision functionality.

The model change could therefore create a new regulatory assessment requirement.

---

## 3.72 Classification and Procurement Governance

Procurement should not approve significant AI acquisitions without appropriate regulatory classification.

The procurement workflow should include:

**Vendor Identification**

↓

**AI Capability Assessment**

↓

**Intended Purpose**

↓

**Organizational Role**

↓

**EU AI Act Classification**

↓

**Security/Privacy Assessment**

↓

**Contract Review**

↓

**Approval**

This prevents organizations from discovering regulatory obligations only after a contract has been signed.

---

## 3.73 Classification and Third-Party AI

Third-party AI creates a shared-responsibility challenge.

The vendor may control:

* foundation model;
* infrastructure;
* model development;
* system documentation.

The customer may control:

* deployment;
* users;
* business purpose;
* data;
* configuration;
* human oversight.

Therefore:

> **Classification should consider the customer's actual deployment, not merely the vendor's product classification.**

Vendor documentation should support—not replace—the customer's assessment.

---

## 3.74 Classification and Shadow AI Governance

Shadow AI should be incorporated into classification governance.

When unauthorized AI use is discovered:

1. Register the system.
2. Identify the user.
3. Identify the purpose.
4. Identify data processed.
5. Determine organizational role.
6. Perform classification.
7. Determine regulatory exposure.
8. Decide whether use can continue.
9. Implement controls.
10. Address the root cause.

The organization should use shadow-AI discoveries to improve governance rather than merely treating them as isolated policy violations.

---

## 3.75 Classification and Fundamental-Rights Governance

Classification governance should consider potential effects on fundamental rights.

Relevant factors include:

* discrimination;
* employment;
* access to services;
* privacy;
* autonomy;
* dignity;
* equal treatment.

For significant systems, Legal, Privacy, Compliance and relevant subject-matter experts should participate in the assessment.

This ensures that classification does not become purely technical.

---

## 3.76 Classification and Human Oversight

Human oversight should be documented where relevant.

The organization should record:

* human role;
* authority;
* qualifications;
* information available;
* ability to intervene;
* ability to override;
* escalation path.

A classification assessment should not simply state:

> "Human in the loop."

It should explain what the human actually does.

---

## 3.77 Classification Governance Metrics

Management should receive classification metrics.

Useful indicators include:

### Coverage

* percentage of AI systems classified;
* percentage classified before deployment.

### Quality

* classification error rate;
* number of classification disputes;
* number of reclassifications.

### Timeliness

* average classification time;
* overdue assessments;
* overdue reassessments.

### Risk

* number of potentially prohibited systems;
* number of high-risk systems;
* high-risk systems with unresolved gaps.

### Change

* classification changes caused by system modifications;
* classification changes caused by regulatory updates.

---

## 3.78 Classification Assurance

Assurance should evaluate whether the classification process is effective.

An assurance review should determine:

1. Are all relevant AI systems identified?
2. Are systems appropriately qualified?
3. Are organizational roles correctly identified?
4. Are intended purposes accurately documented?
5. Are prohibited practices screened?
6. Are high-risk criteria properly evaluated?
7. Are transparency obligations assessed?
8. Are GPAI considerations addressed?
9. Are decisions documented?
10. Are decisions approved?
11. Are changes triggering reassessment?
12. Is evidence sufficient?

This provides independent confidence in the classification process.

---

## 3.79 Internal Audit Testing

Internal Audit can use sampling to evaluate classification.

For each sampled AI system, auditors can trace:

**AI Inventory**

→ **Business Purpose**

→ **Technical Description**

→ **Role**

→ **Classification Questionnaire**

→ **Legal Criteria**

→ **Classification Decision**

→ **Approval**

→ **Controls**

→ **Evidence**

The audit should determine whether the conclusion is supported by both the facts and the applicable legal requirements.

---

## 3.80 Classification Findings

Typical audit findings may include:

### Finding — Missing Classification

Production AI system has no documented EU AI Act classification.

### Finding — Unsupported Classification

Classification exists but lacks evidence.

### Finding — Incorrect Classification

System was classified using an inappropriate criterion.

### Finding — Stale Classification

System changed materially without reassessment.

### Finding — Inconsistent Classification

Similar systems were classified differently without documented rationale.

### Finding — Vendor Reliance

Organization accepted vendor classification without performing its own assessment.

Each finding should have a remediation plan and owner.

---

## 3.81 Classification Remediation

Remediation should address both the affected AI system and the underlying process.

For an incorrect classification:

**Immediate**

→ Reassess system.

**Short Term**

→ Implement missing controls.

**Regulatory**

→ Determine whether additional action or notification is required.

**Systemic**

→ Correct the classification methodology.

**Preventive**

→ Add controls to prevent recurrence.

This converts individual findings into organizational learning.

## 3.82 Classification Governance Maturity

Classification maturity can be assessed across five levels.

### Level 1 — Ad Hoc

* no centralized inventory;
* informal classification;
* inconsistent decisions.

### Level 2 — Developing

* basic questionnaire;
* initial inventory;
* limited review.

### Level 3 — Defined

* formal methodology;
* defined roles;
* documented evidence;
* approval workflow.

### Level 4 — Managed

* automated classification workflows;
* continuous reassessment;
* quality assurance;
* metrics.

### Level 5 — Optimized

* integrated regulatory intelligence;
* automated change detection;
* intelligent obligation mapping;
* continuous compliance;
* predictive risk analysis.

## 3.84 Classification Governance Principle

The central governance principle is:

> **An AI system's regulatory classification must be an evidence-based, documented and accountable decision that can be independently understood and defended.**

The organization should be able to answer:

* Why was the AI system classified this way?
* Who made the determination?
* Which legal criteria were considered?
* What evidence supported it?
* What assumptions were made?
* Who approved it?
* When will it be reassessed?
* What changed since the original decision?

If these questions cannot be answered, classification governance is incomplete.
