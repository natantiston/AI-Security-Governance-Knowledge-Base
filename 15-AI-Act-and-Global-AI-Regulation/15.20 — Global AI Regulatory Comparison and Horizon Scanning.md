# 15.20 — Global AI Regulatory Comparison and Horizon Scanning — Part 1

## Part 1 — Global Regulatory Comparison, Legal Status, Scope, and Governance Foundations

1. **Global AI Regulatory Comparison** — AI regulation should be analyzed as a collection of jurisdiction-specific legal, regulatory, supervisory, standards-based, and governance regimes rather than as one globally harmonized regulatory framework.

2. **Purpose of Comparative Regulation** — Comparative regulatory analysis enables organizations to identify common obligations, jurisdiction-specific requirements, regulatory gaps, conflicting requirements, and emerging areas of convergence.

3. **Regulatory Fragmentation** — AI regulation differs materially among jurisdictions in legal basis, regulator structure, scope, terminology, enforcement mechanisms, transparency expectations, data requirements, and implementation methodology.

4. **Regulatory Convergence** — Despite differences, many jurisdictions increasingly address common themes including safety, cybersecurity, privacy, transparency, accountability, human oversight, fairness, documentation, risk management, and incident management.

5. **No Global AI Law** — There is currently no single globally applicable AI statute that establishes one uniform compliance regime for all AI developers and deployers.

6. **Jurisdictional Applicability** — An organization must determine applicable requirements based on factors such as establishment, market activity, affected persons, AI system location, data processing, service delivery, sector, and territorial rules.

7. **Regulatory Hierarchy** — Comparative analysis should distinguish binding law from regulations, regulator requirements, supervisory guidance, government policy, standards, voluntary frameworks, industry practices, and internal controls.

8. **Legal Status Classification** — Every entry in a global AI regulatory register should contain an explicit legal-status classification.

9. **Binding-Law Classification** — Enacted legislation and legally effective regulations should be classified as binding requirements within their applicable scope.

10. **Regulatory-Guidance Classification** — Regulatory guidance should be identified separately because its legal effect may differ from legislation while still influencing supervisory expectations and enforcement risk.

11. **Voluntary-Framework Classification** — Voluntary frameworks should not be represented as statutory requirements unless a law, regulation, regulator, contract, or other binding instrument expressly incorporates them.

12. **Internal-Control Classification** — An organization's own AI governance controls should be identified as internal controls even when they were designed to operationalize regulatory requirements.

13. **Regulatory Proposal Classification** — Bills, draft regulations, consultation documents, proposed rules, and policy proposals should be classified as prospective developments until they become legally effective.

14. **Enforcement-Expectation Classification** — Enforcement decisions and supervisory actions may provide evidence of how authorities interpret or apply existing requirements, but they should not automatically be treated as new legislation.

15. **Comparative Legal Register** — Maintain a global AI legal register containing jurisdiction, authority, instrument, legal status, effective date, scope, affected AI roles, obligations, penalties, enforcement authority, and implementation status.

16. **Regulatory Source Authority** — Primary legislation, official regulatory publications, government sources, regulator decisions, and authoritative standards should take precedence over secondary commentary when determining regulatory obligations.

17. **Source Validation** — Material regulatory conclusions should be validated against authoritative sources before being incorporated into the organization's compliance framework.

18. **Regulatory Currency** — AI regulatory information should include a last-reviewed date because AI regulation is changing rapidly.

19. **Regulatory Version Control** — Maintain historical versions of material regulatory requirements so the organization can reconstruct which requirements applied at a particular point in time.

20. **Effective-Date Control** — Track publication date, enactment date, effective date, transition period, compliance deadline, and enforcement date separately where applicable.

21. **Transition Management** — Regulatory transition periods should be tracked at system level where different AI systems enter scope at different times.

22. **Regulatory Horizon** — Maintain separate views for current requirements, imminent requirements, proposed requirements, regulatory consultations, and emerging regulatory signals.

23. **European Union Regulatory Model** — The EU AI Act represents a comprehensive horizontal AI regulation model combining prohibited practices, high-risk requirements, transparency obligations, GPAI requirements, governance, supervision, and penalties. The current consolidated EU AI Act is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended through July 2026.

24. **EU Risk-Based Structure** — The EU model classifies AI obligations according to regulatory risk and system characteristics rather than applying identical requirements to every AI application.

25. **EU Lifecycle Orientation** — The EU framework establishes obligations affecting different stages and roles across the AI lifecycle, including providers, deployers, importers, distributors, and GPAI providers.

26. **EU Governance Architecture** — The EU model includes Union-level and national regulatory authorities, creating a structured supervisory architecture rather than relying solely on sector regulators.

27. **EU Compliance Evidence** — Organizations subject to the EU AI Act should maintain evidence capable of demonstrating applicable classification, conformity, documentation, monitoring, transparency, incident, and governance obligations.

28. **United States Regulatory Model** — The U.S. AI regulatory environment is comparatively decentralized, combining federal statutes, sectoral regulation, agency enforcement, executive policy, state legislation, state regulators, voluntary standards, and government procurement requirements.

29. **U.S. Federal-State Interaction** — AI compliance in the United States requires consideration of both federal and state requirements and the possibility of preemption, litigation, conflicting requirements, or differing implementation approaches.

30. **U.S. Sectoral Regulation** — Organizations should assess AI applications against existing legal regimes governing areas such as consumer protection, employment, civil rights, financial services, healthcare, privacy, cybersecurity, and other regulated activities.

31. **NIST Framework Role** — NIST AI RMF provides a voluntary risk-management framework rather than a generally applicable federal AI statute. Its voluntary status should remain explicit in regulatory mapping.

32. **U.S. Standards-to-Law Mapping** — NIST AI RMF, ISO standards, industry frameworks, and other voluntary standards may be used to operationalize governance but should not automatically be described as legal obligations.

33. **United Kingdom Regulatory Model** — The UK has pursued a principles-based, regulator-led approach rather than adopting an EU-style single comprehensive AI statute. The government's five cross-sector principles address safety/security/robustness, transparency/explainability, fairness, accountability/governance, and contestability/redress.

34. **UK Five-Principle Legal Status** — The five principles were initially established on a non-statutory basis for implementation through existing regulators; organizations should therefore distinguish the principles themselves from binding obligations imposed by particular UK laws or regulators.

35. **UK Regulator Model** — UK AI governance depends substantially on existing regulators applying AI considerations within their statutory remits.

36. **UK Sectoral Assessment** — UK AI compliance should therefore identify the relevant regulator and sector-specific legal regime rather than relying solely on a generic enterprise AI policy.

37. **UK Government AI Governance** — UK public-sector AI has additional government-specific governance material, including the AI Playbook, which sets ten principles for government use of AI and is distinct from the five cross-economy regulatory principles.

38. **Singapore Regulatory Model** — Singapore has developed a practical, risk-based AI governance environment combining legislation, sectoral regulation, technical governance initiatives, testing tools, and voluntary governance frameworks.

39. **Singapore Model AI Governance Framework** — Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework provides organizational guidance for responsible AI governance and is distinct from a generally applicable AI statute.

40. **Singapore Agentic AI Governance** — In 2026, IMDA updated its Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI with practical case studies and additional practices addressing areas including multi-agent systems, third-party agents, and automation bias.

41. **Singapore Human Accountability** — The Agentic AI framework emphasizes that humans remain accountable for agentic AI and addresses risk bounding, meaningful human accountability, lifecycle technical controls, and end-user responsibility.

42. **Singapore Assurance Orientation** — Singapore's governance ecosystem places substantial emphasis on practical implementation, testing, assurance, and responsible deployment rather than relying exclusively on prescriptive statutory rules.

43. **Canada Regulatory Model** — Canadian AI governance is distributed across federal and provincial privacy law, public-sector requirements, sector-specific regulation, proposed legislation, government policy, and regulator guidance.

44. **Canada Federal-Provincial Distinction** — Canadian compliance programs must separately assess federal and provincial requirements because the applicable legal regime can vary according to organization, activity, sector, and jurisdiction.

45. **Canada Legislative Change** — Canada continues to develop and amend its digital and privacy regulatory framework. For example, the federal government introduced Bill C-36 in June 2026 to modernize private-sector privacy legislation; proposed legislation must be tracked separately from enacted requirements until its legal status changes.

46. **Canada AI Transparency Development** — Canada launched a public consultation on AI transparency in July 2026, demonstrating that AI-specific policy and regulatory development remains active. Consultation material should be treated as prospective policy development rather than automatically as binding law.

47. **Canada Public-Sector Governance** — Canadian government AI governance includes principles addressing openness, legal and ethical risk, lawful data use, output evaluation, and documentation, creating an additional governance layer for public-sector deployments.

48. **China Regulatory Model** — China has developed a comparatively prescriptive regulatory environment covering specific AI activities through rules governing algorithm recommendation, deep synthesis, generative AI services, AI-generated-content identification, and related internet information services.

49. **China Generative-AI Governance** — Organizations providing covered generative AI services must assess applicable Chinese requirements concerning service provision, content governance, data, security, identification, and regulatory filing.

50. **China Content-Identification Regime** — China's AI-generated synthetic-content identification measures provide for explicit and implicit identification mechanisms for covered AI-generated content and apply to specified internet information service providers.

51. **China Filing Environment** — China's regulatory authorities continue to operate filing and registration processes for covered generative AI services and applications. In September 2026, CAC reported additional filings and registrations completed during July and August 2026.

52. **China Regulatory Evolution** — China's AI regulatory environment continues to evolve through new rules, consultations, implementation measures, and enforcement activity.

53. **China Draft-versus-Final Control** — Proposed Chinese regulatory amendments must be distinguished from effective requirements. For example, the July 2026 draft revision concerning internet information services proposed requirements concerning training data, generated-content identification, human intervention, and user choice; draft provisions should not be treated as final law until legally effective.

54. **Comparative Regulatory Dimensions** — A global AI regulatory comparison should evaluate at least legal scope, territorial reach, AI definitions, regulated actors, risk classification, prohibited uses, transparency, privacy, human oversight, security, documentation, testing, incident reporting, registration, conformity, enforcement, and penalties.

55. **Scope Comparison** — Compare whether each jurisdiction regulates AI generally, particular AI uses, specific sectors, specific providers, specific data processing, or particular categories of AI-generated content.

56. **Actor Comparison** — Map provider, developer, deployer, user, importer, distributor, platform, model provider, application provider, and public-sector roles across jurisdictions.

57. **Lifecycle Comparison** — Determine whether regulatory requirements apply during development, training, testing, deployment, operation, modification, monitoring, incident response, or retirement.

58. **Risk Comparison** — Identify whether a jurisdiction uses formal risk categories, sector-specific risk assessments, impact assessments, principles, prohibited practices, or combinations of these approaches.

59. **Transparency Comparison** — Compare requirements concerning user disclosure, AI-generated-content identification, explanations, documentation, public information, and affected-person notification.

60. **Human-Oversight Comparison** — Compare requirements and regulatory expectations concerning human review, intervention, override authority, accountability, contestability, and autonomous action.

61. **Privacy Comparison** — Compare applicable privacy principles, lawful processing requirements, consent, purpose limitation, data minimization, individual rights, automated decision-making, and cross-border transfer requirements.

62. **Security Comparison** — Compare requirements concerning cybersecurity, model security, data security, resilience, access control, vulnerability management, incident response, and supply-chain security.

63. **Documentation Comparison** — Determine which jurisdictions require technical documentation, risk assessments, impact assessments, records, filings, testing evidence, or other compliance documentation.

64. **Testing Comparison** — Compare requirements for validation, performance testing, security testing, red teaming, conformity assessment, assurance, and ongoing monitoring.

65. **Incident Comparison** — Determine whether incidents trigger notification, reporting, corrective action, suspension, recall, disclosure, or other regulatory obligations.

66. **Registration Comparison** — Identify jurisdictions and AI activities requiring registration, filing, certification, listing, or other regulatory submission.

67. **Conformity Comparison** — Determine whether systems require formal conformity assessment, third-party assessment, self-assessment, certification, or risk-based internal assurance.

68. **Enforcement Comparison** — Compare regulatory authorities, investigation powers, administrative penalties, corrective measures, service restrictions, criminal consequences where applicable, and private enforcement mechanisms.

69. **Penalty Comparison** — Compare penalties using the legally applicable methodology for each jurisdiction rather than attempting to convert every jurisdiction into one numerical risk score.

70. **Regulatory Philosophy Comparison** — Comparative analysis should identify whether a jurisdiction primarily uses comprehensive horizontal legislation, sectoral regulation, principles-based regulation, voluntary governance, activity-specific rules, or a hybrid model.

71. **Regulatory Centralization Comparison** — Determine whether AI oversight is concentrated in a central AI authority, distributed among existing regulators, divided between federal and state authorities, or structured through multiple specialized agencies.

72. **Regulatory Coordination Comparison** — Assess how authorities coordinate AI regulation across privacy, cybersecurity, consumer protection, employment, financial services, healthcare, competition, and other domains.

73. **Standards Role Comparison** — Identify whether technical standards, conformity standards, industry frameworks, or government frameworks are incorporated into or referenced by binding requirements.

74. **Voluntary-to-Binding Boundary** — Maintain a clear distinction between voluntary governance frameworks and binding legal obligations even where voluntary frameworks strongly influence market or supervisory expectations.

75. **Regulatory Maturity Comparison** — Internal maturity models may compare the organization's capability to manage different jurisdictions, but maturity scores are internal governance tools and are not legal classifications.

76. **Global Control Baseline** — Establish a common global AI control baseline addressing governance, risk, privacy, security, transparency, human oversight, documentation, monitoring, incidents, suppliers, and evidence.

77. **Jurisdictional Overlay** — Apply jurisdiction-specific regulatory overlays to the global baseline rather than assuming one global control set satisfies every jurisdiction.

78. **Control Equivalence** — Where substantially equivalent requirements exist across jurisdictions, map them to a common enterprise control while retaining the individual legal sources.

79. **Control Divergence** — Where requirements differ materially, implement jurisdiction-specific controls or technical configurations.

80. **Regulatory Conflict Management** — Establish a formal process for resolving situations in which compliance with one jurisdiction's requirement may conflict with another jurisdiction's legal, privacy, security, localization, or operational requirement.

81. **Regulatory Extraterritoriality Assessment** — Assess whether a jurisdiction's requirements may apply to organizations or activities outside its physical territory.

82. **Market-Access Assessment** — Consider whether offering an AI system to users in a jurisdiction creates regulatory exposure even when the provider is established elsewhere.

83. **Affected-Person Assessment** — Consider the location and status of affected persons when determining regulatory applicability.

84. **Data-Location Assessment** — Consider where AI training, inference, storage, logging, monitoring, and backup data are processed.

85. **Cloud-Jurisdiction Assessment** — Identify the jurisdictions implicated by cloud infrastructure, model hosting, APIs, subprocessors, and supporting services.

86. **Supplier-Jurisdiction Assessment** — Determine whether third-party model providers, data suppliers, cloud providers, or AI platforms create additional regulatory exposure.

87. **Regulatory Dependency Mapping** — Map dependencies among AI regulation, privacy law, cybersecurity law, employment law, consumer protection, intellectual property, sector regulation, and contractual requirements.

88. **Global Regulatory Risk Register** — Maintain a risk register capturing regulatory applicability uncertainty, control gaps, conflicting obligations, emerging regulation, enforcement exposure, evidence weaknesses, and implementation deadlines.

89. **Regulatory Ownership** — Assign accountable owners for each jurisdiction and regulatory domain.

90. **Three-Lines Integration** — Integrate global AI regulatory management with the organization's Three Lines model, maintaining appropriate separation among operational ownership, risk/compliance oversight, and independent assurance.

91. **Legal Function Role** — Legal should provide or validate legal interpretation for material regulatory questions, particularly where obligations are ambiguous, conflicting, novel, or enforcement-sensitive.

92. **Compliance Function Role** — Compliance should coordinate regulatory inventories, obligation mapping, monitoring, control oversight, evidence, regulatory change, and reporting.

93. **Technical Function Role** — Engineering, security, data, and AI teams should implement technical and operational controls corresponding to applicable requirements.

94. **Business Owner Role** — Business owners remain accountable for the lawful and governed use of AI within their operational domain.

95. **Executive Oversight** — Executive management should oversee material global AI regulatory exposure, significant compliance gaps, regulatory incidents, and major changes in regulatory strategy.

96. **Board Oversight** — Where AI regulatory exposure is material to organizational strategy, risk, customers, employees, or legal obligations, appropriate board or committee oversight should be established.

97. **Regulatory Decision Rights** — Define who can approve AI deployment, accept residual regulatory risk, authorize exceptions, suspend systems, approve remediation, and respond to material regulatory inquiries.

98. **Regulatory Stop Authority** — Establish explicit authority to suspend or prevent AI deployment when a mandatory legal requirement has not been satisfied or material regulatory exposure cannot be adequately controlled.

99. **Regulatory Evidence Architecture** — Design the compliance environment so that each material regulatory requirement can be traced to an accountable owner, implemented control, affected AI system, evidence source, testing activity, and assurance result.

100. **Global AI Regulatory Governance Principle** — Treat global AI compliance as a continuously changing, jurisdiction-specific governance discipline in which **legal requirements, regulatory expectations, voluntary frameworks, and internal controls remain explicitly separated while being integrated into one enterprise AI risk and compliance architecture**.


# 15.20 — Global AI Regulatory Comparison and Horizon Scanning — Part 2

## Part 2 — Regulatory Comparison Controls, Implementation Requirements, and Operational Management

101. **Global Regulatory Applicability Control** — Establish a documented process for determining which AI regulations, regulatory authorities, sector requirements, privacy regimes, and jurisdictional rules apply to each AI system.

102. **AI Regulatory Scope Record** — For every material AI system, record the jurisdictions, markets, users, affected persons, business activities, AI roles, data categories, and regulatory instruments considered during applicability assessment.

103. **Territorial Applicability Assessment** — Assess territorial scope using legally relevant connecting factors rather than assuming that regulatory obligations apply solely according to the organization's place of incorporation.

104. **Market-Availability Assessment** — Determine whether making an AI system available in a jurisdiction creates regulatory exposure even where development or hosting occurs elsewhere.

105. **Affected-Person Location Control** — Record the locations and relevant characteristics of persons affected by consequential AI processing when these factors affect legal applicability.

106. **Data-Processing Jurisdiction Control** — Identify jurisdictions involved in collection, training, inference, storage, logging, monitoring, backup, and deletion of AI-related data.

107. **AI Supply-Chain Jurisdiction Control** — Identify the jurisdictions associated with model providers, cloud providers, data suppliers, API providers, subprocessors, and other material AI dependencies.

108. **Regulatory Role Classification** — Determine whether the organization acts as an AI provider, deployer, importer, distributor, platform operator, model provider, application provider, data controller, processor, employer, or another regulated role.

109. **Multiple-Role Assessment** — Where an organization performs multiple AI-related roles, assess the obligations associated with each role independently.

110. **System-Level Regulatory Classification** — Regulatory classification should be performed at AI-system or use-case level where the legal regime depends on intended purpose, functionality, users, context, or deployment.

111. **Model-Level Assessment** — Separately assess foundation models and general-purpose AI models where applicable regulatory requirements attach to the model provider rather than only to downstream applications.

112. **Application-Level Assessment** — Assess downstream AI applications independently because a compliant underlying model does not automatically make every application compliant.

113. **Agentic-System Assessment** — AI agents should receive additional assessment for autonomy, tool access, data access, delegation, external actions, identity, authorization, and human intervention.

114. **Embedded-AI Assessment** — Identify AI functionality embedded within conventional software, cloud platforms, enterprise applications, devices, and third-party services.

115. **Shadow-AI Assessment** — Include unauthorized AI applications, browser tools, personal accounts, unmanaged APIs, and embedded AI services within regulatory discovery processes.

116. **AI Inventory Reconciliation** — Reconcile the regulatory inventory against procurement records, cloud assets, application inventories, identity systems, data inventories, and security tooling.

117. **Regulatory Inventory Ownership** — Assign an accountable owner for the completeness and accuracy of the AI regulatory inventory.

118. **Inventory Review Frequency** — Review inventory records according to AI risk, regulatory volatility, business change, and system criticality.

119. **Regulatory Obligation Extraction** — Extract actionable obligations from applicable legislation, regulations, regulatory guidance, and other authoritative instruments.

120. **Obligation Atomicity** — Where practical, decompose complex regulatory requirements into discrete obligations that can be assigned, controlled, tested, and evidenced.

121. **Obligation Source Reference** — Every material obligation should retain a reference to the authoritative source from which it was derived.

122. **Obligation Legal-Status Field** — Each obligation should identify whether it is binding law, regulatory requirement, guidance, voluntary practice, proposed requirement, or internal control.

123. **Obligation Effective-Date Field** — Each obligation should contain the applicable effective date and transition requirements.

124. **Obligation Jurisdiction Field** — Each obligation should identify its geographic and organizational scope.

125. **Obligation Owner** — Assign an accountable owner for implementing each material regulatory obligation.

126. **Control Mapping** — Map each obligation to one or more internal controls.

127. **Evidence Mapping** — Map each control to the evidence required to demonstrate operation.

128. **Testing Mapping** — Map each material control to its testing methodology, frequency, tester, and acceptance criteria.

129. **Assurance Mapping** — Map material controls to management assurance, compliance testing, Internal Audit, or other independent assurance activities as appropriate.

130. **Regulatory Crosswalk Control** — Maintain a crosswalk linking common regulatory themes across jurisdictions while preserving each jurisdiction's original legal source.

131. **Crosswalk Non-Equivalence Warning** — A common control mapping must not be interpreted as evidence that two jurisdictions impose identical legal obligations.

132. **Common-Control Identification** — Identify requirements that can be addressed through common enterprise controls such as identity management, cybersecurity, privacy, human oversight, documentation, incident response, and supplier governance.

133. **Jurisdiction-Specific Overlay** — Add jurisdiction-specific requirements where common controls do not provide complete coverage.

134. **Control Coverage Analysis** — Periodically identify regulatory obligations with no mapped control or with controls that have insufficient scope.

135. **Control Duplication Analysis** — Identify unnecessarily duplicated controls across jurisdictions and consolidate them where legally and operationally appropriate.

136. **Regulatory Control Conflict Assessment** — Analyze whether different jurisdictions require materially different treatment of the same AI process.

137. **Legal Conflict Escalation** — Escalate material conflicts between legal requirements to qualified legal counsel or the organization's designated legal authority.

138. **Technical Conflict Resolution** — Where regulatory differences can be addressed through technical configuration, implement jurisdiction-specific configurations with appropriate change control.

139. **Data-Flow Segmentation** — Use geographic or logical segmentation where different regulatory regimes require different data handling.

140. **Model Deployment Segmentation** — Where appropriate, maintain separate model configurations or deployment environments for jurisdictions with materially different regulatory requirements.

141. **Regional Policy Enforcement** — Enforce jurisdiction-specific AI policies using identity, network, application, data, and configuration controls where technically feasible.

142. **Geofencing Assessment** — Evaluate geographic access controls where regulatory scope requires restricting availability or functionality by location.

143. **Localization Control** — Where legally required, implement appropriate data, infrastructure, record, or operational localization mechanisms.

144. **Cross-Border Transfer Control** — Assess and control international transfers of AI-related personal, confidential, regulated, or restricted information.

145. **International Data-Transfer Register** — Maintain a register of material AI-related cross-border data transfers and their applicable legal mechanisms.

146. **Transfer-Impact Assessment** — Conduct appropriate impact assessments where applicable privacy or data-transfer requirements warrant them.

147. **Third-Country Supplier Assessment** — Assess third-country suppliers for regulatory, privacy, security, operational, and geopolitical risks.

148. **Regulatory Sovereignty Risk** — Identify dependencies on foreign jurisdictions whose laws may affect access to AI infrastructure, data, models, services, or evidence.

149. **Regulatory Concentration Control** — Monitor concentration of AI services within a single jurisdiction, supplier, cloud provider, or regulatory regime.

150. **Global Minimum-Control Baseline** — Establish minimum enterprise AI controls that apply regardless of jurisdiction, subject to documented legal and operational exceptions.

151. **Baseline Control Domains** — The global baseline should generally address governance, risk management, security, privacy, transparency, human oversight, documentation, testing, monitoring, incidents, suppliers, and evidence.

152. **Legal-Requirement Overlay** — Apply mandatory jurisdiction-specific requirements on top of the global baseline.

153. **Higher-Standard Strategy** — Where legally permissible, an organization may adopt a common higher control standard to simplify global operations.

154. **Higher-Standard Legal Boundary** — A voluntary enterprise standard must not be described as a universal legal requirement merely because it exceeds a minimum legal obligation.

155. **Local-Control Exception** — Document circumstances in which a jurisdiction requires a control that cannot be implemented through the global baseline.

156. **Exception Approval** — Assign appropriate authority for approving jurisdiction-specific regulatory exceptions.

157. **Exception Expiration** — Regulatory exceptions should contain a review or expiration date.

158. **Compensating Control** — Where a standard control cannot be implemented, establish and document an appropriate compensating control where legally permissible.

159. **Regulatory Control Effectiveness** — Test whether controls actually mitigate the mapped regulatory risk rather than merely verifying that policies exist.

160. **Regulatory Design Review** — Evaluate whether regulatory controls are appropriately designed for the applicable AI system and jurisdiction.

161. **Regulatory Operating Review** — Evaluate whether controls operate consistently over the relevant period.

162. **Control Evidence Quality** — Evidence should be complete, accurate, attributable, time-relevant, protected from unauthorized alteration, and retrievable.

163. **Evidence Freshness** — Establish validity periods for assessments, certifications, testing, approvals, risk decisions, supplier evidence, and other recurring records.

164. **Evidence Provenance** — Record the source, owner, creation date, system, version, and applicable control for material regulatory evidence.

165. **Evidence Chain of Custody** — Maintain appropriate chain-of-custody controls for evidence used in investigations, litigation, or regulatory proceedings.

166. **Regulatory Evidence Repository** — Maintain a controlled repository for regulatory obligations, mappings, decisions, assessments, evidence, findings, and correspondence.

167. **Evidence Access Control** — Restrict regulatory evidence according to confidentiality, privacy, legal, security, and role-based access requirements.

168. **Evidence Retention Control** — Retain evidence according to applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, audit, and records-management requirements.

169. **Evidence Disposal Control** — Dispose of records securely when authorized and when retention requirements have expired.

170. **Legal-Hold Preservation** — Suspend normal disposal processes when a legal or regulatory hold applies.

171. **Regulatory Evidence Recovery** — Periodically test recovery of critical regulatory records and evidence.

172. **Regulatory Evidence Searchability** — Ensure material evidence can be retrieved efficiently by AI system, jurisdiction, requirement, control, owner, date, and regulatory event.

173. **Regulatory Audit Trail** — Maintain records of changes to regulatory obligations, mappings, applicability decisions, controls, exceptions, and evidence.

174. **Regulatory Change Detection** — Monitor authoritative sources for legislative, regulatory, supervisory, policy, and standards developments.

175. **Multi-Source Monitoring** — Use multiple authoritative sources where regulatory responsibility is distributed across governments, regulators, agencies, and sector authorities.

176. **Source Reliability Control** — Establish a hierarchy of trusted regulatory sources.

177. **Secondary-Source Validation** — Use secondary legal analysis as supporting information but validate material conclusions against authoritative sources.

178. **Regulatory Alert Triage** — Classify regulatory alerts by jurisdiction, legal status, subject, effective date, materiality, and affected AI systems.

179. **Regulatory Alert False-Positive Management** — Establish procedures for rejecting irrelevant regulatory alerts without losing auditability.

180. **Regulatory Alert False-Negative Testing** — Periodically evaluate monitoring coverage for missed material regulatory developments.

181. **Regulatory Change Record** — Each material regulatory change should record source, publication date, legal status, effective date, impacted jurisdictions, affected systems, and accountable owner.

182. **Impact Assessment Trigger** — Material regulatory changes should trigger a documented impact assessment.

183. **System Impact Mapping** — Identify which AI systems, models, applications, data flows, suppliers, policies, controls, and contracts are affected.

184. **Control Impact Mapping** — Determine which controls require creation, modification, testing, or retirement following regulatory change.

185. **Policy Impact Mapping** — Determine whether policies, standards, procedures, contracts, notices, or user communications require revision.

186. **Technical Impact Mapping** — Determine whether model configurations, access controls, data architecture, logging, monitoring, or deployment restrictions require changes.

187. **Training Impact Mapping** — Determine whether employees or contractors require updated regulatory training.

188. **Change Implementation Plan** — Define actions, owners, deadlines, dependencies, evidence, and validation criteria for material regulatory changes.

189. **Regulatory Change Closure** — Do not close a material regulatory change until implementation and evidence requirements have been validated.

190. **Regulatory Change Auditability** — Preserve historical change records so the organization can demonstrate when and how it responded.

191. **Horizon-Scanning Function** — Establish a formal horizon-scanning process for future AI legislation, regulations, consultations, standards, enforcement trends, and policy developments.

192. **Horizon-Scanning Time Horizons** — Maintain near-term, medium-term, and longer-term regulatory outlooks.

193. **Near-Term Horizon** — Track requirements likely to become effective within the organization's operational planning period.

194. **Medium-Term Horizon** — Track proposed legislation, consultations, regulatory initiatives, and standards that may require material preparation.

195. **Long-Term Horizon** — Track emerging regulatory concepts and policy trends that could materially influence future AI governance.

196. **Emerging-Regulation Register** — Maintain a separate register for regulatory developments that are not yet binding.

197. **Proposal Status Control** — Update proposal records when consultations close, bills change, regulations are enacted, provisions are withdrawn, or effective dates are established.

198. **Consultation Monitoring** — Monitor regulatory consultations relevant to AI systems, data, cybersecurity, privacy, automated decision-making, transparency, and sector regulation.

199. **Consultation Response Governance** — Where the organization submits consultation responses, establish appropriate legal, policy, technical, and executive review.

200. **Consultation Evidence** — Retain submitted consultation responses and the internal analysis supporting material positions.

201. **Regulatory Scenario Planning** — Develop scenarios for material proposed AI regulatory changes.

202. **Scenario Assumption Register** — Document assumptions underlying regulatory scenarios and distinguish assumptions from confirmed legal requirements.

203. **Scenario Control Design** — Where appropriate, design controls that can be activated quickly if an anticipated regulatory requirement becomes effective.

204. **Regulatory Readiness Assessment** — Assess readiness for significant forthcoming requirements before their effective dates.

205. **Transition-Period Control** — Track systems benefiting from transitional arrangements and verify that the relevant conditions remain satisfied.

206. **Regulatory Deadline Calendar** — Maintain a centralized calendar of effective dates, filing deadlines, reporting deadlines, assessments, renewals, and regulatory commitments.

207. **Deadline Escalation** — Escalate approaching or overdue material regulatory deadlines.

208. **Regulatory Change Prioritization** — Prioritize changes using legal applicability, potential impact, urgency, affected population, implementation complexity, and regulatory exposure.

209. **No Legal Priority Substitution** — Internal prioritization must not be used to disregard a mandatory legal deadline.

210. **Regulatory Change Dependency Management** — Track dependencies among legal changes, technology projects, procurement, contracts, data architecture, security controls, and organizational processes.

211. **Regulatory Change Testing** — Test material changes before declaring the affected controls operational.

212. **Post-Implementation Review** — Review significant regulatory changes after implementation to verify that intended controls operate as expected.

213. **Regulatory Change Lessons Learned** — Capture lessons from delayed, incomplete, or failed regulatory-change implementations.

214. **Regulatory Knowledge Maintenance** — Keep the regulatory knowledge base current, versioned, attributable, and subject to quality control.

215. **Obsolete-Requirement Management** — Archive superseded requirements while preserving historical versions required for audit, legal, or regulatory purposes.

216. **Regulatory Definition Management** — Maintain jurisdiction-specific legal definitions rather than assuming that common terminology has identical legal meaning.

217. **Terminology Crosswalk** — Provide internal mappings for terms such as provider, deployer, automated decision system, high-risk AI, foundation model, agent, synthetic content, and AI-generated content.

218. **Definition Conflict Control** — Escalate materially different legal definitions where they affect regulatory applicability.

219. **Regulatory Interpretation Repository** — Store approved interpretations of ambiguous or complex regulatory requirements.

220. **Interpretation Approval** — Material interpretations should receive appropriate legal or compliance review before becoming enterprise control requirements.

221. **Interpretation Scope** — Identify the jurisdiction, business activity, AI systems, facts, and assumptions to which each interpretation applies.

222. **Interpretation Review Date** — Assign review dates to interpretations that depend on evolving law, guidance, or regulatory practice.

223. **Interpretation Change Trigger** — Reassess interpretations following material legislative, judicial, regulatory, or factual changes.

224. **Regulatory Enforcement Monitoring** — Monitor relevant enforcement actions, regulatory investigations, supervisory findings, and official decisions.

225. **Enforcement Applicability Assessment** — Determine whether an enforcement development applies to the organization's facts, jurisdiction, legal regime, or AI use case.

226. **Enforcement-Lesson Classification** — Classify enforcement findings as directly applicable legal requirements, interpretations of existing law, regulator expectations, factual findings, or lessons for internal risk management.

227. **No Automatic Obligation Creation** — An enforcement action should not automatically be converted into a universal legal obligation without a legal basis.

228. **Enforcement Trend Analysis** — Analyze recurring enforcement themes to identify areas where existing controls may require strengthening.
