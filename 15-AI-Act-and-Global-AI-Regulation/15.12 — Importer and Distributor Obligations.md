# 15.12 — Importer and Distributor Obligations

## Part 1 — Foundations, Role Determination, and Regulatory Responsibility

### 1. Importer and Distributor Obligations as AI Supply-Chain Controls

Importer and distributor obligations establish an important layer of AI regulatory governance between the provider and the final market or user.

An organization may not develop an AI system and may not operate it as a deployer, but it can still have regulatory responsibilities because it:

* imports an AI system into the European Union;
* makes an AI system available on the EU market;
* distributes an AI system;
* supplies an AI system through commercial channels; or
* participates in the AI supply chain between the provider and the deployer.

Importer and distributor governance therefore extends the AI Act control environment beyond the provider and deployer.

### 2. Importer Definition

Under the EU AI Act, an **importer** is a natural or legal person located or established in the Union that places on the Union market an AI system bearing the name or trademark of a provider established outside the Union.

The concept is particularly relevant when an organization in the EU introduces an AI system supplied by a provider established outside the EU.

The importer should therefore understand:

* who the provider is;
* where the provider is established;
* who places the system on the EU market;
* which entity bears the provider name or trademark;
* whether the organization is acting as importer;
* whether another organization already performs the importer role; and
* what obligations apply to the specific AI system.

### 3. Distributor Definition

A **distributor** is a natural or legal person in the supply chain, other than the provider or importer, that makes an AI system available on the Union market.

The distributor can therefore operate downstream from both the provider and importer.

Examples can include organizations that:

* resell AI-enabled products;
* distribute AI systems through commercial channels;
* supply AI-enabled devices;
* make AI systems available to customers;
* distribute software containing AI functionality; or
* operate as intermediaries in the supply chain.

### 4. Provider, Importer, Distributor and Deployer Roles

The AI supply chain may contain several legally distinct roles.

A simplified model is:

**Provider → Importer → Distributor → Deployer → User**

However, the actual supply chain may contain multiple organizations and overlapping roles.

For example:

**Non-EU Provider → EU Importer → EU Distributor → Enterprise Customer → Deployer**

Each role should be separately assessed.

### 5. Role Determination Is Entity- and System-Specific

An organization should not be classified globally as simply an "importer" or "distributor."

The determination should be performed for each relevant AI system and supply-chain transaction.

The same organization could be:

* an importer for one AI product;
* a distributor for another;
* a deployer for a third; and
* a provider for an internally developed system.

Role determination should therefore be maintained in the AI governance inventory.

### 6. Importer Status and Provider Location

Importer status is particularly relevant when the provider is established outside the European Union.

The organization should establish:

* provider legal entity;
* provider establishment location;
* Union representative where applicable;
* importer identity;
* distribution chain;
* market-placement arrangements; and
* contractual responsibilities.

The provider's physical location, legal establishment and contractual structure should be verified rather than inferred from a product website or sales representative.

### 7. Distributor Status

A distributor should understand whether it is merely making an AI system available on the market or whether its activities create another regulatory role.

Relevant questions include:

* Who is the provider?
* Is an importer involved?
* Has the AI system already been placed on the EU market?
* Has the distributor modified the system?
* Has the distributor changed the system's name or trademark?
* Has the distributor altered its intended purpose?
* Is the distributor acting under its own name?
* Does the distributor become responsible for the system under another provision?

### 8. AI-IMP-01 — Importer Role Determination

Organizations potentially acting as importers should establish a formal importer-role assessment.

The assessment should document:

* AI system;
* provider;
* provider establishment;
* importer entity;
* market-placement activity;
* supply-chain relationship;
* contractual structure;
* applicable obligations;
* responsible owner; and
* approval.

### 9. AI-DIS-01 — Distributor Role Determination

Organizations potentially acting as distributors should establish a corresponding distributor-role assessment.

The assessment should establish:

* system identity;
* provider;
* importer, if applicable;
* supply-chain position;
* distribution activity;
* market;
* intended purpose;
* regulatory classification;
* required verification; and
* responsible owner.

### 10. Supply-Chain Visibility

Importer and distributor compliance requires sufficient visibility into the AI supply chain.

The organization should understand:

* provider;
* manufacturer where relevant;
* importer;
* distributors;
* authorized representatives;
* downstream customers;
* deployers;
* critical subcontractors; and
* relevant technical dependencies.

A lack of supply-chain visibility can make regulatory verification difficult.

### 11. Provider Due Diligence

Importers and distributors should obtain sufficient information about the provider and AI system before making the system available on the Union market.

Due diligence should consider:

* provider identity;
* provider establishment;
* regulatory status;
* AI classification;
* documentation;
* conformity;
* CE marking where applicable;
* registration where applicable;
* instructions;
* contact information;
* known risks;
* corrective actions; and
* incidents.

### 12. AI-IMP-02 — Provider Verification

The importer should verify that the provider has fulfilled applicable obligations before placing the AI system on the Union market.

For high-risk AI systems, the AI Act specifies particular checks for importers, including verification concerning conformity assessment, technical documentation, CE marking, registration and required provider information.

The verification should be documented rather than treated as an informal procurement activity.

### 13. AI-DIS-02 — Distributor Verification

A distributor should perform the checks applicable to its role before making an AI system available on the Union market.

The distributor should verify, where applicable:

* required conformity indications;
* CE marking;
* required documentation;
* instructions;
* provider identification;
* importer identification;
* storage and transport conditions; and
* other applicable regulatory information.

### 14. High-Risk AI Systems

Importer and distributor obligations become particularly important when the AI system is classified as high-risk.

High-risk systems may involve areas such as:

* employment;
* education;
* access to essential services;
* law enforcement;
* migration;
* justice;
* critical infrastructure; and
* biometric applications.

The organization should first establish whether the system is legally high-risk before determining the applicable importer or distributor controls.

### 15. High-Risk Verification Principle

An importer or distributor should not rely solely on a supplier's statement that an AI system is compliant.

Appropriate evidence should be obtained.

Evidence may include:

* EU declaration of conformity;
* technical documentation availability;
* CE marking;
* registration information;
* provider identity;
* instructions for use;
* conformity-assessment evidence;
* quality-management information where appropriate; and
* relevant post-market information.

### 16. AI-IMP-03 — High-Risk AI Verification

Before placing a high-risk AI system on the Union market, the importer should perform the checks required by the AI Act.

The importer should verify, as applicable, that:

* the appropriate conformity assessment has been carried out;
* the provider has prepared the required technical documentation;
* the appropriate CE marking is present;
* the provider has identified the required information;
* the system has been registered where required; and
* the provider has complied with applicable requirements.

### 17. AI-DIS-03 — High-Risk Distribution Verification

Before making a high-risk AI system available on the Union market, the distributor should perform the checks required by its role.

The distributor should verify relevant:

* conformity indicators;
* documentation;
* instructions;
* provider information;
* importer information;
* storage conditions; and
* other required markings or information.

### 18. Do Not Make a Non-Compliant System Available

Importers and distributors should not make an AI system available on the Union market when required compliance conditions are not satisfied.

Where a system appears not to comply, the organization should:

1. suspend market availability;
2. investigate;
3. notify the appropriate party;
4. document the issue;
5. determine whether authorities must be informed; and
6. resume availability only when appropriate conditions have been satisfied.

### 19. AI-IMP-04 — Non-Compliance Blocking

The importer should establish a control capable of preventing market placement when mandatory verification has failed.

Blocking conditions may include:

* missing conformity assessment;
* missing CE marking where required;
* missing documentation;
* incomplete provider identification;
* missing registration where required;
* suspected prohibited use;
* known serious non-compliance; or
* unresolved regulatory concerns.

### 20. AI-DIS-04 — Distribution Blocking

Distributors should have equivalent controls preventing distribution when required compliance conditions are not met.

The control should integrate with:

* order management;
* inventory management;
* product management;
* warehouse systems;
* sales systems; and
* customer fulfillment.

### 21. Importer Identification

Importers have identification responsibilities for relevant AI systems.

The importer should ensure that its identity and required contact information are appropriately associated with the AI system where required.

The organization should maintain evidence demonstrating that the required information was applied correctly.

### 22. Distributor Identification

Distributors should maintain sufficient information to identify:

* provider;
* importer;
* AI system;
* relevant model or product;
* version where available;
* supply-chain transaction;
* customer; and
* date of availability.

This supports traceability and regulatory investigation.

### 23. Traceability Through the Supply Chain

Importer and distributor controls should provide sufficient traceability to determine:

* where the AI system came from;
* who supplied it;
* when it entered the Union market;
* who received it;
* which version was supplied;
* which documentation accompanied it; and
* whether subsequent corrective action affected the system.

### 24. AI-IMP-05 — Supply-Chain Traceability

Importers should maintain records sufficient to trace the relevant AI system through the supply chain.

Records may include:

* purchase orders;
* invoices;
* provider records;
* shipment records;
* system identifiers;
* versions;
* conformity documentation;
* customer information;
* distribution dates; and
* corrective-action records.

### 25. AI-DIS-05 — Distribution Traceability

Distributors should maintain records sufficient to identify the source and recipient of relevant AI systems.

Traceability should support:

* product recall;
* corrective action;
* incident investigation;
* regulatory requests;
* customer notification; and
* market-surveillance activities.

### 26. Documentation Availability

Importers and distributors should understand which documents must accompany or be available for the AI system.

Depending on the applicable system and role, documentation may include:

* instructions for use;
* declaration of conformity;
* technical documentation;
* identification information;
* CE marking;
* registration information; and
* relevant safety or compliance information.

### 27. AI-IMP-06 — Documentation Verification

Importers should verify the availability and consistency of required documentation before market placement.

The verification should identify:

* document;
* source;
* version;
* date;
* applicable AI system;
* completeness;
* discrepancies; and
* verification result.

### 28. AI-DIS-06 — Distributor Documentation Control

Distributors should ensure that required documentation is available to customers and authorities as applicable.

Documentation should remain associated with the correct product and version.

### 29. Instructions for Use

Instructions are particularly important for AI systems that require controlled operation.

The importer and distributor should verify that required instructions are:

* available;
* understandable;
* appropriately localized where necessary;
* associated with the correct system;
* current; and
* supplied through the appropriate channel.

### 30. Storage and Transport Conditions

Where applicable, importers and distributors should ensure that storage and transport conditions do not compromise AI-system compliance.

This is particularly relevant for AI systems incorporated into physical products.

Controls may address:

* environmental conditions;
* hardware integrity;
* packaging;
* tamper protection;
* software integrity;
* configuration;
* firmware;
* secure delivery; and
* chain of custody.

### 31. Software Supply-Chain Integrity

For software-based AI systems, distribution controls should protect against unauthorized modification.

Controls may include:

* cryptographic signatures;
* secure repositories;
* hash verification;
* secure download channels;
* access controls;
* release verification;
* version control; and
* integrity monitoring.

### 32. AI-IMP-07 — Product Integrity Verification

Importers should verify the integrity of the AI system before market placement.

The verification may include:

* version verification;
* package integrity;
* signature validation;
* configuration;
* security controls;
* documentation;
* conformity information; and
* identification.

### 33. AI-DIS-07 — Distribution Integrity Control

Distributors should protect AI systems from unauthorized modification while under their control.

The control should address:

* storage;
* access;
* transmission;
* packaging;
* software distribution;
* configuration; and
* customer delivery.

### 34. Modification of an AI System

Importers and distributors should understand the regulatory implications of modifying an AI system.

A material modification may alter:

* functionality;
* intended purpose;
* risk;
* performance;
* security;
* regulatory classification; or
* the legal role of the organization.

An organization should therefore not assume that a modification is operationally insignificant.

### 35. AI-IMP-08 — Modification Assessment

Before modifying an imported AI system, the organization should assess whether the modification could create additional regulatory responsibilities.

The assessment should consider:

* technical change;
* intended-purpose change;
* provider identity;
* branding;
* conformity;
* risk classification;
* documentation;
* security; and
* downstream obligations.

### 36. AI-DIS-08 — Distributor Modification Control

Distributors should establish controls preventing unauthorized modification of AI systems.

If a distributor modifies an AI system, Legal, Compliance and AI Governance should determine whether the modification changes the organization's regulatory role.

### 37. Own Name or Trademark

Use of an organization's own name or trademark can have significant regulatory implications.

Organizations should carefully assess situations where they:

* rebrand an AI product;
* sell it under a private label;
* place their own trademark on the system;
* represent the AI system as their own product; or
* combine third-party AI functionality into their own product.

Such activities may affect provider responsibilities.

### 38. AI-IMP-09 — Rebranding Assessment

Before rebranding an AI system, the organization should perform a formal regulatory assessment.

The assessment should consider:

* provider role;
* importer role;
* distributor role;
* own-name or trademark implications;
* conformity;
* technical documentation;
* registration;
* liability;
* contracts; and
* post-market responsibilities.

### 39. AI Supply-Chain Contracts

Contracts should clearly allocate relevant responsibilities among:

* provider;
* authorized representative;
* importer;
* distributor;
* integrator;
* deployer; and
* other supply-chain participants.

Contractual allocation does not necessarily eliminate statutory obligations imposed directly by law.

### 40. AI-IMP-10 — Importer Contract Control

Importer agreements should address, as applicable:

* provider representations;
* conformity;
* documentation;
* registration;
* regulatory cooperation;
* incident notification;
* corrective action;
* product withdrawal;
* recall;
* supply continuity;
* audit rights; and
* indemnification.

### 41. AI-DIS-09 — Distributor Contract Control

Distributor agreements should address:

* product compliance;
* required documentation;
* provider and importer information;
* storage;
* transport;
* modification restrictions;
* incident escalation;
* corrective action;
* withdrawal;
* recall; and
* regulatory cooperation.

### 42. Non-EU Provider Governance

When the provider is established outside the Union, importer governance should verify the relationship between the provider, any authorized representative and the EU importer.

The importer should understand:

* who represents the provider in the Union;
* who maintains regulatory documentation;
* who communicates with authorities;
* who handles incidents;
* who supports corrective action; and
* who retains required evidence.

### 43. Authorized Representative Coordination

Where an authorized representative is required or appointed, the importer should maintain appropriate coordination.

The organization should identify:

* representative;
* mandate;
* scope;
* contact information;
* regulatory responsibilities;
* documentation access; and
* escalation procedures.

### 44. Market Surveillance

Importers and distributors form part of the broader market-surveillance ecosystem.

They should be prepared to cooperate with competent authorities and provide relevant information or documentation when legally required.

The European Commission describes market-surveillance authorities as having powers to investigate compliance and take corrective measures, while providers, importers and distributors have defined responsibilities within the AI Act supply chain.

### 45. AI-IMP-11 — Market-Surveillance Cooperation

Importers should establish procedures for responding to market-surveillance requests.

The procedure should define:

* responsible contact;
* evidence sources;
* response authority;
* legal review;
* deadlines;
* submission process;
* evidence retention; and
* follow-up.

### 46. AI-DIS-10 — Distributor Market-Surveillance Cooperation

Distributors should maintain sufficient information to respond to competent-authority requests.

The distributor should be able to identify:

* supplier;
* provider;
* importer;
* product;
* version;
* customers where legally permissible;
* distribution date; and
* relevant documentation.

### 47. Corrective Measures

When an AI system is determined to be non-compliant, the importer or distributor may need to support corrective action.

Corrective measures can include:

* bringing the system into compliance;
* preventing further availability;
* withdrawal;
* recall;
* customer notification;
* replacement;
* technical correction; or
* cooperation with authorities.

### 48. AI-IMP-12 — Corrective-Action Coordination

Importers should maintain a formal process for coordinating provider corrective actions.

The process should track:

* affected systems;
* versions;
* customers;
* issue;
* corrective measure;
* responsible party;
* deadline;
* evidence; and
* closure.

### 49. AI-DIS-11 — Distributor Corrective Action

Distributors should be capable of executing relevant corrective measures.

The process should cover:

* identification of affected inventory;
* suspension of sales;
* customer notification;
* product retrieval;
* replacement;
* provider coordination;
* documentation; and
* regulatory communication.

### 50. Serious Incidents and Safety Concerns

Importers and distributors should have mechanisms for identifying and escalating serious incidents or significant compliance concerns involving AI systems.

Potential triggers include:

* serious harm;
* major security compromise;
* significant non-compliance;
* unsafe behavior;
* regulatory intervention;
* widespread product defect; or
* provider notification.

### 51. AI-IMP-13 — Incident Escalation

Importer incident procedures should establish escalation to:

* provider;
* authorized representative where applicable;
* distributor;
* deployer;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Cybersecurity;
* AI Governance; and
* competent authorities where required.

### 52. AI-DIS-12 — Distributor Incident Escalation

Distributors should promptly escalate material AI incidents and compliance concerns through defined supply-chain channels.

The organization should preserve relevant information and avoid uncontrolled modification or destruction of evidence.

### 53. Recall and Withdrawal Readiness

Organizations distributing or importing AI systems should be capable of supporting withdrawal or recall where required.

Readiness should include:

* affected-product identification;
* customer traceability;
* inventory control;
* communication;
* provider coordination;
* technical remediation;
* replacement;
* evidence preservation; and
* regulatory cooperation.

### 54. AI-IMP-14 — Importer Recall Control

The importer should maintain a controlled recall or withdrawal process appropriate to its role.

The process should identify:

* trigger;
* authority;
* affected systems;
* supply-chain partners;
* customers;
* communications;
* remediation;
* evidence; and
* closure criteria.

### 55. Importer and Distributor Compliance Gate

Before an organization imports or distributes a material AI system, it should establish that:

* its regulatory role is documented;
* the provider and supply chain are identified;
* the AI system classification is established;
* required conformity information is verified;
* required documentation is available;
* required identification and markings are verified;
* modifications are controlled;
* storage and distribution conditions are appropriate;
* traceability is established;
* incident escalation is operational;
* corrective-action procedures exist;
* withdrawal and recall procedures are available; and
* regulatory cooperation responsibilities are understood.

The control objective is to ensure that AI systems entering or moving through the Union market are subject to appropriate regulatory verification and supply-chain accountability before they reach downstream users.

# Part 2 — Importer and Distributor Compliance Controls, Operational Requirements, and Implementation

### 56. Importer and Distributor Compliance Assessment

An importer or distributor should establish a documented compliance assessment before making a material AI system available on the Union market.

The assessment should determine:

* organizational role;
* provider identity;
* provider location;
* importer identity;
* AI system identity;
* regulatory classification;
* conformity status;
* required documentation;
* marking requirements;
* registration status where applicable;
* supply-chain traceability;
* storage and transport conditions;
* modification status;
* incident arrangements; and
* corrective-action responsibilities.

### 57. AI-IMP-15 — Importer Obligation Matrix

The importer should maintain an obligation matrix connecting applicable legal requirements to operational controls.

The matrix should identify:

* legal requirement;
* applicability;
* verification activity;
* control owner;
* evidence;
* frequency;
* exception;
* remediation; and
* accountable management.

### 58. AI-DIS-13 — Distributor Obligation Matrix

The distributor should maintain a corresponding matrix for its applicable obligations.

The matrix should identify:

* AI system;
* provider;
* importer;
* distribution activity;
* applicable requirements;
* verification;
* evidence;
* responsible person;
* exception; and
* corrective action.

### 59. AI-IMP-16 — Provider Compliance Verification

The importer should establish a formal process for verifying relevant provider compliance before market placement.

For applicable high-risk AI systems, verification should cover the requirements assigned to importers by the AI Act, including relevant conformity assessment, technical documentation, CE marking, registration and provider information.

### 60. AI-DIS-14 — Distributor Compliance Verification

The distributor should verify applicable compliance indicators before making an AI system available.

The verification should consider:

* CE marking where required;
* documentation;
* instructions;
* provider information;
* importer information;
* product identification;
* conformity information; and
* applicable storage or transport conditions.

### 61. AI-IMP-17 — Provider Identity Verification

The importer should verify the legal identity of the provider.

Verification may include:

* legal name;
* registered address;
* country of establishment;
* corporate registration;
* contractual entity;
* trademark owner; and
* authorized representative where applicable.

The objective is to prevent uncertainty concerning which entity is legally responsible for the AI system.

### 62. AI-IMP-18 — Provider Establishment Verification

The importer should establish whether the provider is established inside or outside the Union.

This determination is important because importer responsibilities are particularly relevant where an AI system is placed on the Union market by an EU-established entity on behalf of a provider established outside the Union.

### 63. AI-IMP-19 — Authorized Representative Verification

Where an authorized representative is applicable, the importer should verify the representative's identity and mandate.

The importer should maintain:

* representative identity;
* contact information;
* mandate scope;
* effective date;
* relevant responsibilities; and
* termination or replacement information.

### 64. AI-IMP-20 — AI System Identity Verification

The importer should establish the exact identity of the AI system being imported.

Identification should include, where available:

* product name;
* model;
* version;
* unique identifier;
* provider;
* hardware or software platform;
* intended purpose; and
* relevant product family.

### 65. AI-DIS-15 — Product Identity Verification

The distributor should verify that the AI system received corresponds to the product and version documented by the provider or importer.

Differences should be investigated before distribution.

### 66. AI-IMP-21 — Classification Verification

The importer should verify the AI system's regulatory classification.

The assessment should determine whether the system is:

* prohibited;
* high-risk;
* subject to transparency obligations;
* GPAI-related;
* minimal-risk; or
* subject to other applicable regulatory requirements.

The importer should not rely exclusively on informal supplier descriptions.

### 67. AI-DIS-16 — Classification Awareness

Distributors should maintain sufficient classification information to understand the regulatory controls applicable to the products they distribute.

The distributor does not necessarily perform the provider's full classification assessment, but it should verify the information relevant to its own legal responsibilities.

### 68. AI-IMP-22 — High-Risk System Verification

Where a system is high-risk, the importer should perform the specific verification required by the AI Act before placing the system on the Union market.

Evidence should be linked to the exact system and version.

### 69. AI-DIS-17 — High-Risk Distribution Verification

Where a high-risk system is distributed, the distributor should perform the applicable checks before making it available.

The checks should be recorded and retained as compliance evidence.

### 70. AI-IMP-23 — Conformity Assessment Verification

The importer should verify that the appropriate conformity assessment procedure has been carried out where required.

The importer should confirm:

* assessment completed;
* appropriate procedure used;
* relevant documentation available;
* applicable system identified; and
* evidence consistent with the product being imported.

### 71. AI-DIS-18 — Conformity Evidence Verification

The distributor should verify the presence of required conformity indicators and supporting information within the scope of its obligations.

Discrepancies should prevent normal distribution until appropriately resolved.

### 72. AI-IMP-24 — EU Declaration of Conformity Verification

Where required, the importer should verify the availability of the EU declaration of conformity.

Verification should confirm that the declaration corresponds to:

* the provider;
* the AI system;
* the relevant version or product;
* applicable legislation; and
* the appropriate conformity process.

### 73. AI-DIS-19 — Declaration Availability

Distributors should ensure that required declaration or conformity information is available through the appropriate supply-chain process.

The distributor should maintain evidence demonstrating its verification.

### 74. AI-IMP-25 — CE Marking Verification

Where CE marking is required, the importer should verify its presence and appropriate application.

The check should consider:

* visibility;
* legibility;
* permanence where applicable;
* association with the correct product; and
* consistency with conformity documentation.

### 75. AI-DIS-20 — CE Marking Distribution Control

The distributor should verify required CE marking before making the system available.

A missing, altered or apparently incorrect CE marking should trigger investigation.

### 76. AI-IMP-26 — Registration Verification

Where the AI Act requires registration, the importer should verify the relevant registration status before placing the system on the Union market.

The registration information should be associated with the correct AI system.

### 77. AI-DIS-21 — Registration Awareness

Distributors should maintain sufficient information to determine whether the AI system has the relevant registration status where this forms part of their applicable verification responsibilities.

### 78. AI-IMP-27 — Technical Documentation Availability

The importer should verify that required technical documentation has been prepared and is available as required.

The importer should not necessarily recreate the provider's technical documentation, but should establish that the required documentation exists and can be made available through the applicable regulatory process.

### 79. AI-DIS-22 — Documentation Availability Control

Distributors should verify that required documentation accompanies or is made available with the AI system as required.

The documentation should correspond to the correct product and version.

### 80. AI-IMP-28 — Instructions Verification

The importer should verify that instructions for use are available where required.

The review should consider:

* completeness;
* language;
* version;
* accessibility;
* consistency;
* intended purpose;
* limitations; and
* human-oversight information where relevant.

### 81. AI-DIS-23 — Instructions Distribution Control

The distributor should ensure that applicable instructions are supplied through the required distribution channel.

Missing or incorrect instructions should trigger a distribution hold where appropriate.

### 82. AI-IMP-29 — Provider Contact Information

The importer should verify that the provider's required contact information is available.

Information should be sufficiently reliable to support:

* customer communication;
* incident escalation;
* regulatory requests;
* corrective action; and
* market-surveillance activities.

### 83. AI-DIS-24 — Supply-Chain Contact Information

Distributors should maintain reliable contact information for relevant:

* providers;
* importers;
* authorized representatives;
* technical contacts; and
* regulatory contacts.

### 84. AI-IMP-30 — Importer Identification Control

The importer should ensure that its required identification information is appropriately associated with the AI system.

The information should remain accurate when organizational details change.

### 85. AI-DIS-25 — Distributor Identification Control

The distributor should maintain sufficient records identifying itself and relevant upstream supply-chain participants.

### 86. AI-IMP-31 — Supply-Chain Traceability

The importer should maintain traceability from the provider through importation and onward distribution.

Traceability records may include:

* purchase orders;
* shipment records;
* product identifiers;
* model versions;
* quantities;
* dates;
* warehouse;
* recipient;
* documentation;
* conformity records; and
* corrective actions.

### 87. AI-DIS-26 — Customer Traceability

The distributor should maintain sufficient downstream records to identify customers or recipients where legally and operationally appropriate.

Traceability supports:

* corrective action;
* withdrawal;
* recall;
* incident investigation;
* customer notification; and
* regulatory cooperation.

### 88. AI-IMP-32 — Batch and Version Traceability

Where AI systems are supplied in versions or batches, the importer should be able to identify the affected version or batch.

This is particularly important when a compliance problem affects only a particular release.

### 89. AI-DIS-27 — Version-Controlled Distribution

Distributors should prevent accidental distribution of superseded or withdrawn versions.

Inventory systems should be capable of identifying:

* approved versions;
* withdrawn versions;
* recalled versions;
* blocked versions; and
* current versions.

### 90. AI-IMP-33 — Storage Control

Imported AI systems should be stored under conditions that do not compromise their compliance or integrity.

Where applicable, controls should address:

* environmental conditions;
* physical security;
* access;
* software integrity;
* hardware integrity;
* configuration; and
* tamper protection.

### 91. AI-DIS-28 — Distribution Storage Control

Distributors should maintain suitable storage conditions for AI systems under their control.

Storage conditions should be periodically reviewed for compliance with provider requirements.

### 92. AI-IMP-34 — Transport Integrity

Where physical AI products are transported, the importer should establish appropriate chain-of-custody controls.

Controls may include:

* secure packaging;
* tamper evidence;
* shipment tracking;
* authorized carriers;
* delivery confirmation; and

# Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Risk Management, and Assurance

## Governance and Accountability

136. **Governance of Importer and Distributor Obligations**
     Importer and distributor compliance should be governed as an integrated AI supply-chain responsibility rather than treated solely as a logistics or procurement activity. The governance framework should establish accountability for verifying that AI systems placed on the Union market or made available within the Union comply with applicable requirements, while clearly separating statutory responsibilities from internal control responsibilities.

137. **AI Supply-Chain Governance Framework**
     The organization should maintain a documented governance framework covering providers, authorized representatives, importers, distributors, deployers, product owners, procurement, legal, compliance, information security, privacy, quality, regulatory affairs, and internal audit. The framework should define how responsibilities are assigned, escalated, monitored, and evidenced.

138. **Importer Accountability**
     Where an organization acts as an importer, it should establish explicit accountability for importer-specific compliance activities. Management should ensure that responsible personnel have sufficient authority, competence, resources, and access to provider documentation to perform required verification before the relevant AI system is placed on the Union market.

139. **Distributor Accountability**
     Where an organization acts as a distributor, accountability should cover verification activities performed before making an AI system available, preservation of compliance evidence, distribution controls, identification of non-compliance, escalation, and cooperation with providers and market-surveillance authorities.

140. **Role-Specific RACI Model**
     A RACI matrix should identify who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed for importer and distributor obligations. The matrix should distinguish at minimum:

* provider;
* authorized representative;
* importer;
* distributor;
* deployer;
* product owner;
* procurement;
* legal/compliance;
* information security;
* privacy;
* quality;
* regulatory affairs;
* incident management; and
* internal audit.

141. **System-Specific Role Assignment**
     The organization should not assume that a single legal entity has the same AI Act role for every AI system. Role determination should be performed for each relevant system, transaction, product configuration, and supply-chain arrangement.

142. **AI Supply-Chain Governance Committee**
     Organizations with significant AI portfolios should consider establishing an AI supply-chain governance committee or assigning these responsibilities to an existing AI governance, technology risk, product compliance, or risk committee. The committee should oversee material importer and distributor risks, significant supplier issues, regulatory developments, and unresolved compliance exceptions.

143. **Executive Oversight**
     Senior management should receive sufficient information to understand the organization's exposure arising from imported and distributed AI systems. Reporting should include material compliance gaps, high-risk AI systems, supplier dependencies, significant incidents, corrective actions, recalls, withdrawals, regulatory inquiries, and overdue remediation.

144. **Board-Level Oversight**
     Where AI supply-chain risk is material to the organization, the board or an appropriate board committee should receive periodic reporting on regulatory compliance, systemic supplier risks, significant AI incidents, and the effectiveness of importer and distributor controls.

145. **Three Lines Model**
     Importer and distributor governance should align with the Three Lines Model:

* **First Line:** procurement, product, operations, supply-chain, and business teams execute controls.
* **Second Line:** legal, compliance, risk, security, privacy, and quality functions establish requirements and provide oversight.
* **Third Line:** internal audit independently evaluates governance, risk management, and control effectiveness.

146. **Segregation of Duties**
     Critical compliance decisions should be subject to appropriate segregation of duties. Where practicable, the individual approving an AI system for market availability should not be the sole individual responsible for supplier selection, technical validation, or commercial performance.

147. **Management Accountability**
     Management should remain accountable for the effectiveness of importer and distributor controls even when operational activities are outsourced to logistics providers, consultants, managed-service providers, or other third parties.

148. **Outsourcing Governance**
     Outsourcing verification, documentation management, distribution, or monitoring does not automatically transfer the organization's legal responsibilities. Contracts should specify the activities performed by the service provider, required evidence, service levels, escalation requirements, audit rights, and regulatory cooperation obligations.

149. **Policy Framework**
     The organization should maintain policies covering:

* AI supply-chain governance;
* importer and distributor compliance;
* AI classification;
* provider due diligence;
* conformity and documentation verification;
* product and software integrity;
* change management;
* incident escalation;
* withdrawal and recall;
* regulatory cooperation; and
* evidence retention.

150. **Procedure Framework**
     Policies should be supported by operational procedures defining exactly how systems are reviewed, approved, blocked, distributed, monitored, escalated, withdrawn, recalled, and retired.

151. **Control Ownership**
     Every material importer and distributor control should have an identified control owner. Control ownership should include responsibility for execution, evidence generation, exception handling, remediation, and periodic review.

152. **Control Design Authority**
     The compliance or risk function should establish minimum control requirements, while operational functions should implement controls in the systems and workflows through which AI products are acquired, imported, distributed, or made available.

153. **Risk Appetite**
     The organization should establish AI supply-chain risk appetite appropriate to its business model. Risk appetite should define acceptable levels of:

* supplier compliance uncertainty;
* documentation deficiencies;
* unresolved regulatory exceptions;
* supplier concentration;
* product integrity risk;
* cybersecurity exposure;
* privacy risk;
* operational dependency; and
* regulatory non-compliance.

154. **Risk Acceptance Authority**
     Exceptions to mandatory controls should not be accepted informally. The organization should define which risks may be accepted internally and which conditions require escalation to legal, compliance, senior management, or another authorized governance body.

155. **No Internal Override of Mandatory Law**
     Internal risk acceptance should not be interpreted as permission to disregard mandatory statutory requirements. A business decision to accept commercial or operational risk cannot, by itself, authorize conduct prohibited by applicable law.

## Risk Management

156. **AI Supply-Chain Risk Assessment**
     Each material importer and distributor relationship should be subject to documented risk assessment considering the AI system, provider, intended purpose, jurisdiction, regulatory classification, distribution model, technical dependencies, and potential impact on individuals and organizations.

157. **Importer Risk Register**
     The importer should maintain an importer-specific risk register where appropriate. Risks may include:

* provider non-compliance;
* incomplete technical documentation;
* invalid conformity evidence;
* incorrect classification;
* missing registration;
* inadequate instructions;
* product modification;
* cybersecurity compromise;
* software integrity failure;
* supplier concentration;
* regulatory uncertainty; and
* inability to execute corrective action.

158. **Distributor Risk Register**
     The distributor should maintain appropriate risks relating to the systems it makes available. Particular attention should be given to:

* receiving non-compliant products;
* distributing products after a compliance concern arises;
* inadequate traceability;
* unauthorized modification;
* storage or transport degradation;
* supplier communication failures;
* ineffective withdrawal procedures; and
* inability to identify affected customers.

159. **Risk Scoring**
     AI supply-chain risks should be evaluated using consistent criteria such as likelihood, impact, detectability, regulatory significance, affected population, duration, and reversibility.

160. **High-Risk AI Supply-Chain Risk**
     High-risk AI systems should receive enhanced governance because failures in conformity, documentation, human oversight, cybersecurity, or other regulated controls can create significant legal, operational, financial, safety, or fundamental-rights consequences.

161. **Risk Treatment**
     Risk treatment should identify one or more of the following responses:

* avoid;
* reduce;
* transfer;
* accept where legally permissible; or
* terminate the relationship or availability of the affected system.

162. **Risk Escalation Thresholds**
     Defined thresholds should trigger escalation. Examples include suspected prohibited practices, high-risk compliance deficiencies, missing conformity evidence, serious incidents, material cybersecurity compromise, inability to trace affected systems, or regulatory requests.

163. **Residual Risk**
     After controls are implemented, residual risk should be documented and reviewed by an appropriately authorized person. Residual risk should be distinguished from unresolved compliance obligations.

164. **Supplier Concentration Risk**
     Organizations should assess dependence on individual AI providers, model providers, distributors, cloud platforms, or technology ecosystems. Concentration risk can affect the organization's ability to replace a non-compliant system or execute a withdrawal or recall.

165. **Fourth-Party Risk**
     Importer and distributor risk assessments should consider material dependencies below the immediate supplier where those dependencies can affect compliance, security, availability, traceability, or product integrity.

166. **Geographic Supply-Chain Risk**
     Organizations should evaluate jurisdictions involved in development, hosting, manufacturing, distribution, support, and software delivery where jurisdictional differences may affect regulatory compliance, data handling, security, or enforcement.

167. **Provider Due-Diligence Risk**
     Provider due diligence should consider the provider's legal identity, regulatory responsibilities, compliance maturity, documentation quality, change-management practices, incident history, security posture, and ability to provide timely regulatory cooperation.

168. **Authorized Representative Risk**
     Where an authorized representative is involved, the organization should establish mechanisms to verify that responsibilities, communications, documentation access, and regulatory interactions are clearly defined.

169. **Contractual Risk Controls**
     Supplier contracts should establish appropriate obligations concerning:

* regulatory compliance;
* documentation;
* conformity evidence;
* system identification;
* changes;
* incidents;
* cybersecurity;
* corrective actions;
* withdrawal;
* recall;
* regulatory cooperation;
* audit rights; and
* record retention.

170. **Supplier Risk Reviews**
     High-impact providers and suppliers should be subject to periodic reassessment. Reviews should consider changes in the AI system, ownership, legal status, regulatory classification, provider controls, incident history, and market conditions.

171. **Risk-Based Review Frequency**
     Review frequency should be proportional to risk. A critical high-risk AI system may require more frequent assessment than a low-impact system with limited regulatory exposure.

172. **Risk Trigger Events**
     Unscheduled reassessment should occur following material events such as:

* provider changes;
* model or system changes;
* significant software updates;
* changes in intended purpose;
* regulatory changes;
* serious incidents;
* security breaches;
* product recalls;
* enforcement actions; or
* material supplier-control failures.

173. **Risk Dependencies**
     Importer and distributor risk assessments should recognize that compliance depends on upstream provider controls. A weak provider control environment can increase downstream importer or distributor risk even when downstream procedures are well designed.

174. **Risk Aggregation**
     Organizations with multiple AI suppliers should aggregate individual risks to identify portfolio-level exposure. A large number of individually moderate risks may create a significant aggregate compliance or operational risk.

175. **Risk Reporting**
     Risk reporting should distinguish between:

* open compliance issues;
* accepted residual risks;
* control deficiencies;
* supplier risks;
* incidents;
* regulatory matters; and
* remediation activities.

176. **Risk Monitoring**
     Risk registers should be periodically refreshed using operational events, control-testing results, provider notifications, incidents, audit findings, regulatory developments, and market-surveillance information.

177. **Risk-Based Control Prioritization**
     Control resources should prioritize systems and relationships where non-compliance could produce significant safety, fundamental-rights, financial, operational, cybersecurity, or reputational consequences.

## Regulatory and Legal Governance

178. **Legal Interpretation Governance**
     Legal or compliance functions should establish a controlled process for interpreting AI regulatory requirements affecting importer and distributor activities. Interpretations should be documented where they materially influence classification, role determination, market availability, or control design.

179. **Regulatory Obligation Register**
     The organization should maintain a regulatory obligation register identifying applicable requirements, affected entities, responsible functions, control mappings, evidence requirements, effective dates, and implementation status.

180. **Legal Requirement Versus Internal Control**
     The organization should clearly distinguish:

* obligations imposed directly by law;
* contractual requirements;
* internal policies;
* risk-management controls; and
* recommended practices.

This distinction prevents internal procedures from being incorrectly represented as statutory requirements.

181. **Regulatory Change Management**
     A formal regulatory change process should monitor amendments, guidance, standards, implementing measures, enforcement developments, and authoritative interpretations that could change importer or distributor responsibilities.

182. **Effective-Date Management**
     Regulatory requirements should be mapped to their applicable effective dates. Compliance systems should not rely solely on publication dates because different provisions may become applicable at different times.

183. **Regulatory Horizon Scanning**
     Compliance teams should periodically scan relevant European Union regulatory developments and authoritative guidance affecting AI systems, product compliance, cybersecurity, data protection, consumer protection, product liability, and digital regulation.

184. **Regulatory Impact Assessment**
     When a regulatory change is identified, the organization should assess:

* affected AI systems;
* affected suppliers;
* affected importer/distributor roles;
* policy changes;
* control changes;
* documentation changes;
* training requirements;
* technology changes; and
* evidence requirements.

185. **Regulatory Interpretation Records**
     Material interpretations should be retained with their supporting legal analysis, assumptions, responsible reviewer, approval date, and subsequent updates.

186. **Legal Escalation**
     Ambiguous or high-impact regulatory questions should be escalated to qualified legal or compliance personnel rather than resolved solely through operational judgment.

187. **Cross-Regulatory Governance**
     Importer and distributor governance should account for overlapping requirements where applicable, including product safety, cybersecurity, privacy, consumer protection, intellectual property, sector-specific regulation, and contractual obligations.

188. **Regulatory Conflict Management**
     Where requirements appear to conflict, the organization should document the conflict, obtain appropriate legal advice, identify the applicable hierarchy of obligations, and implement an approved resolution.

189. **Regulatory Evidence**
     The organization should retain evidence demonstrating how regulatory obligations were interpreted and translated into operational controls. This supports audits, regulatory inquiries, and future reassessment.

190. **Compliance Obligation Ownership**
     Each material regulatory obligation should have a designated owner responsible for monitoring changes, maintaining the associated control, and ensuring that evidence remains current.

## Supply-Chain and Provider Governance

191. **Provider Governance Framework**
     Providers should be governed through a lifecycle that includes onboarding, compliance assessment, contracting, monitoring, change management, incident management, remediation, and exit.

192. **Provider Onboarding Gate**
     An AI provider should not be approved solely on commercial or technical suitability where regulatory obligations apply. Compliance requirements should form part of the onboarding decision.

193. **Provider Compliance Scorecard**
     Organizations may use scorecards to assess providers against criteria such as:

* legal identity;
* regulatory role;
* AI classification;
* documentation;
* conformity evidence;
* security;
* privacy;
* incident management;
* change management;
* transparency;
* auditability; and
* corrective-action capability.

194. **Provider Performance Monitoring**
     Provider performance should be monitored against agreed compliance and operational requirements. Repeated failures should trigger escalation or reassessment.

195. **Provider Change Governance**
     Material changes by providers should be communicated through a controlled process. The organization should assess whether the change affects role determination, classification, conformity, intended purpose, security, documentation, or distribution authorization.

196. **Material Change Definition**
     The organization should establish criteria for material changes, including significant software modifications, new functionality, changed intended purpose, changes affecting regulated characteristics, and modifications that may alter risk.

197. **Provider Notification Obligations**
     Contracts should require timely notification of material compliance events, security incidents, serious incidents, regulatory actions, product recalls, changes in legal identity, and significant system changes where appropriate.

198. **Provider Evidence Access**
     The organization should maintain appropriate access to documentation needed to demonstrate compliance. Evidence access should be tested rather than assumed.

199. **Supplier Audit Rights**
     Contracts should provide appropriate audit or assurance mechanisms proportionate to risk, including document review, independent assurance reports, control attestations, or direct audit rights where justified.

200. **Supplier Remediation Governance**
     Material provider deficiencies should be assigned owners, deadlines, remediation requirements, validation criteria, and escalation paths.

201. **Supplier Suspension**
     The organization should establish criteria for temporarily suspending acquisition, importation, or distribution where a material compliance concern cannot be adequately controlled.

202. **Supplier Termination**
     Where a provider cannot demonstrate required compliance or repeatedly fails remediation, termination or replacement should be considered subject to legal, contractual, operational, and continuity assessment.

203. **Supplier Exit Planning**
     Critical AI providers should have exit strategies addressing data, configurations, integrations, licenses, documentation, replacement technology, customer impact, and continuity.

204. **Supply-Chain Traceability Governance**
     Governance should ensure that the organization can identify relevant provider, importer, distributor, product, system, version, transaction, and customer relationships to the extent required by applicable obligations and risk.

205. **Distribution Network Governance**
     Where multiple distributors are involved, responsibility for each stage of the distribution chain should be documented. Interfaces between organizations should not create unidentified compliance gaps.

206. **Downstream Communication Governance**
     Material compliance information should be communicated to relevant downstream parties in a controlled and traceable manner.

207. **Compliance Information Consistency**
     Information relating to system identity, classification, instructions, conformity, intended purpose, and restrictions should remain consistent across procurement, product catalogs, contracts, technical systems, and distribution records.

208. **Supply-Chain Exception Governance**
     Exceptions affecting supplier compliance or distribution should be recorded, risk-assessed, approved by the appropriate authority, and subject to expiration or remediation.

209. **Supply-Chain Governance Metrics**
     Management should monitor metrics such as:

* percentage of suppliers with completed compliance reviews;
* overdue supplier assessments;
* documentation completeness;
* open supplier findings;
* material provider changes;
* unresolved compliance exceptions;
* incidents by provider;
* corrective-action aging; and
* supplier concentration.

210. **Supply-Chain Governance Assurance**
     Management should periodically assess whether importer and distributor governance operates as designed and whether responsibilities remain appropriate as AI systems, providers, distribution models, and regulations change.

## Assurance and Control Effectiveness

211. **Control Assurance Framework**
     Importer and distributor controls should be subject to a formal assurance framework covering control design, implementation, operating effectiveness, evidence quality, exceptions, remediation, and continuous monitoring.

212. **Control Design Assessment**
     Control owners should periodically determine whether each material control is appropriately designed to address the underlying regulatory, operational, security, privacy, and supply-chain risk.

213. **Control Operating Effectiveness**
     A properly designed control is insufficient if it is not consistently executed. Testing should verify that required importer and distributor controls actually operate during the relevant period.

214. **Evidence Quality Assessment**
     Control evidence should be evaluated for:

* authenticity;
* completeness;
* accuracy;
* timeliness;
* traceability;
* retention;
* approval; and
* linkage to the specific AI system or transaction.

215. **Exception Management**
     Control exceptions should be formally recorded and classified according to severity. Exceptions should have an owner, remediation deadline, risk assessment, and escalation path.

216. **Management Assurance**
     Management should periodically attest, where appropriate, that material importer and distributor controls are implemented and operating effectively. Such attestations should be supported by objective evidence.

217. **Second-Line Compliance Review**
     Compliance or risk functions should independently review first-line control execution, challenge deficiencies, assess regulatory interpretation, and monitor remediation.

218. **Information Security Assurance**
     Security functions should evaluate controls relevant to software integrity, unauthorized modification, access control, supply-chain compromise, vulnerability management, and incident response.

219. **Privacy Assurance**
     Where AI systems process personal data, privacy functions should assess applicable privacy requirements and verify that importer and distributor activities do not undermine established data-protection controls.

220. **Quality Assurance**
     For AI systems integrated into regulated products or quality-controlled environments, quality functions should verify that applicable quality and product-compliance processes incorporate importer and distributor responsibilities.

221. **Internal Audit**
     Internal Audit should independently assess the governance, risk management, and control framework based on a risk-based audit plan. Internal Audit should not assume responsibility for operating first- or second-line controls.

222. **Audit Scope**
     An audit may examine:

* role determination;
* supplier due diligence;
* classification verification;
* conformity evidence;
* documentation;
* traceability;
* modification controls;
* incident management;
* corrective action;
* withdrawal and recall;
* regulatory cooperation;
* evidence retention; and
* management oversight.

223. **Audit Sampling**
     Where the organization handles multiple AI systems, audit sampling should be risk-based and should include higher-risk systems, material suppliers, recently changed systems, and systems associated with previous control failures.

224. **Audit Evidence**
     Audit evidence should allow the auditor to reconstruct how an AI system entered the supply chain, which controls were performed, who approved relevant actions, what evidence was reviewed, and how exceptions were resolved.

225. **Assurance Over Automated Controls**
     Automated compliance controls should themselves be tested. Organizations should not assume that an automated workflow is effective merely because it technically executes.

226. **GRC Platform Assurance**
     Where importer and distributor compliance is managed through a GRC platform, organizations should validate data integrity, workflow configuration, role permissions, control mappings, evidence retention, and reporting logic.

227. **Key Performance Indicators**
     KPIs should measure operational execution, such as:

* review completion rates;
* supplier onboarding cycle time;
* evidence completion;
* distribution hold duration;
* corrective-action closure;
* incident response time; and
* regulatory request response time.

228. **Key Risk Indicators**
     KRIs should identify deteriorating risk, including:

* increasing documentation gaps;
* repeated provider deficiencies;
* overdue remediation;
* unexplained system changes;
* increasing incidents;
* supplier concentration;
* traceability failures; and
* unresolved compliance exceptions.

229. **Control Failure Analysis**
     Material control failures should undergo root-cause analysis rather than being treated solely as individual operational mistakes.

230. **Corrective Action Governance**
     Corrective actions should identify the underlying failure, affected systems, responsible owner, target date, required evidence, validation method, and residual risk.

231. **Remediation Validation**
     A corrective action should not automatically be considered closed when management reports completion. Appropriate validation should confirm that the underlying deficiency has actually been addressed.

232. **Independent Validation**
     Material or high-risk remediation should receive independent validation from compliance, risk, quality, security, or another appropriately independent function.

233. **Assurance Reporting**
     Assurance results should be reported to appropriate management and governance bodies, including significant deficiencies, overdue actions, repeat findings, systemic issues, and emerging risks.

234. **Repeat Finding Management**
     Repeated audit or control findings should be treated as evidence of potential systemic weakness and should receive elevated management attention.

235. **Regulatory Examination Readiness**
     The organization should maintain the capability to produce coherent evidence demonstrating how importer and distributor responsibilities were determined, implemented, monitored, and remediated.

236. **Regulatory Request Management**
     Regulatory requests should be assigned to accountable owners, tracked through controlled workflows, supported by verified evidence, and reviewed before submission where appropriate.

237. **Evidence Chain of Custody**
     Evidence relating to significant compliance matters, incidents, withdrawals, recalls, or regulatory investigations should be preserved with sufficient metadata to establish its provenance and integrity.

238. **Record Retention Governance**
     Retention periods should be established based on applicable legal requirements, contractual requirements, organizational policy, and the risk associated with the relevant evidence.

239. **Evidence Destruction Controls**
     Records subject to litigation holds, regulatory investigations, audits, or other preservation requirements should not be destroyed under ordinary retention processes.

240. **Assurance Independence**
     The level of independence applied to assurance should be proportionate to risk. Critical compliance assertions should receive greater independence than routine operational self-checks.

241. **Management Review Frequency**
     Management review frequency should be risk-based and should increase when the organization experiences significant supplier changes, regulatory developments, incidents, or control deficiencies.

242. **Compliance Dashboard**
     A management dashboard should provide a consolidated view of importer and distributor compliance status, including:

* systems under management;
* supplier status;
* high-risk systems;
* documentation completeness;
* open issues;
* incidents;
* corrective actions;
* withdrawals and recalls;
* regulatory requests; and
* assurance findings.

243. **Portfolio-Level Assurance**
     Organizations with substantial AI portfolios should assess whether control effectiveness is consistent across business units, geographic locations, suppliers, and distribution channels.

244. **Cross-Functional Assurance**
     Complex AI supply chains should be reviewed through coordinated assurance involving legal, compliance, security, privacy, quality, procurement, operations, and audit functions where appropriate.

245. **Control Maturity Assessment**
     The organization may assess importer and distributor control maturity using levels such as:

* ad hoc;
* repeatable;
* defined;
* managed; and
* optimized.

246. **Maturity Criteria**
     Maturity assessment should consider governance, role determination, automation, evidence quality, monitoring, supplier management, regulatory change management, assurance, and continuous improvement.

247. **Control Automation**
     Where appropriate, organizations should automate verification of system identity, version, supplier status, documentation expiry, approval status, distribution holds, and regulatory control requirements.

248. **Human Review of Automated Decisions**
     Automated compliance decisions that can materially affect market availability, distribution, suspension, or regulatory reporting should have appropriate human review and escalation mechanisms.

249. **Continuous Assurance**
     Continuous assurance should use control telemetry, supplier updates, system changes, incidents, compliance events, and GRC data to identify emerging deficiencies between formal assessment cycles.

250. **Assurance Feedback Loop**
     Findings from audits, incidents, regulatory interactions, supplier assessments, and control testing should feed back into policies, procedures, risk assessments, supplier requirements, training, and control design.

251. **Governance Training**
     Personnel responsible for importer and distributor governance should receive role-appropriate training covering applicable AI regulatory obligations, internal controls, escalation requirements, evidence standards, and their specific accountability.

252. **Competence of Control Owners**
     Control owners should possess sufficient knowledge to recognize compliance failures and understand when issues require escalation to legal, compliance, security, privacy, quality, or senior management.

253. **Governance Knowledge Management**
     Interpretations, procedures, control decisions, lessons learned, audit findings, and regulatory communications should be maintained in a controlled knowledge repository.

254. **Decision Traceability**
     Material governance decisions should be traceable to the relevant AI system, regulatory requirement, risk assessment, decision-maker, supporting evidence, and approval.

255. **Compliance Decision Records**
     For significant importer or distributor decisions, the organization should retain a decision record explaining the basis for approving, restricting, suspending, withdrawing, or recalling an AI system.

256. **Exception Expiration**
     Internal compliance exceptions should have defined expiration dates or review triggers. Permanent exceptions should be subject to elevated governance approval.

257. **Escalation Effectiveness**
     Organizations should test whether personnel can identify and escalate significant importer or distributor compliance issues within required timeframes.

258. **Incident-to-Governance Feedback**
     Serious incidents and significant compliance events should be reviewed for governance implications, including whether supplier controls, role assignments, risk appetite, or oversight mechanisms require modification.

259. **Regulatory-to-Control Mapping**
     Material regulatory requirements should remain mapped to operational controls and evidence. This mapping should be updated when laws, guidance, system characteristics, or organizational responsibilities change.

260. **Control Rationalization**
     Organizations should periodically identify duplicate, ineffective, obsolete, or conflicting controls and rationalize them without weakening regulatory coverage.

261. **Control Dependency Mapping**
     Critical importer and distributor controls should be mapped to dependent systems, people, suppliers, data, applications, and processes so that changes in dependencies can trigger reassessment.

262. **Critical Control Identification**
     The organization should identify controls whose failure could prevent lawful market placement or availability, effective incident response, withdrawal, recall, or regulatory cooperation.

263. **Critical Control Monitoring**
     Critical controls should receive enhanced monitoring and escalation. Examples include conformity verification, system identification, traceability, modification control, distribution holds, incident escalation, and recall execution.

264. **Governance Committee Reporting**
     Material control failures and unresolved regulatory risks should be escalated to the appropriate governance committee according to predefined thresholds.

265. **Executive Escalation**
     Issues that could materially affect legal compliance, customer safety, fundamental rights, business continuity, or organizational reputation should be escalated to senior management.

266. **Regulatory Escalation**
     Where applicable law requires notification or cooperation with market-surveillance authorities or other competent authorities, internal governance should ensure that escalation decisions are made promptly and by appropriately authorized personnel.

267. **Corrective-Action Governance Board**
     For significant supplier or AI-system compliance failures, organizations may establish a corrective-action review mechanism involving compliance, legal, product, security, quality, procurement, and executive stakeholders.

268. **Recall Governance**
     Recall decisions should be governed through a controlled process addressing legal assessment, affected products and customers, communications, logistics, evidence, regulatory interaction, and verification of recall effectiveness.

269. **Withdrawal Governance**
     Withdrawal decisions should establish when an AI system must cease being made available, how affected inventory or digital availability is controlled, who is responsible, and how completion is evidenced.

270. **Distribution Hold Governance**
     Distribution holds should be capable of being applied rapidly when material compliance uncertainty or safety concerns arise. The hold mechanism should cover both physical products and relevant digital distribution channels.

271. **Digital Distribution Governance**
     For software-based AI systems, governance should address marketplaces, application repositories, APIs, cloud deployment, software updates, license activation, and other mechanisms through which an AI system may be made available.

272. **Version Governance**
     Governance should ensure that compliance evidence corresponds to the actual version being imported or distributed. A valid assessment for one version should not automatically be treated as evidence for a materially different version.

273. **Configuration Governance**
     Where AI functionality depends on configurable parameters, integrations, or deployment configurations, governance should define when configuration changes require reassessment.

274. **Change Approval Governance**
     Material changes should be subject to documented approval before the changed AI system is made available where applicable controls require such approval.

275. **Emergency Change Governance**
     Emergency changes should have controlled authorization, documented rationale, post-change review, and retrospective compliance validation.

276. **Business Continuity Governance**
     Continuity planning should consider the consequences of provider suspension, regulatory restrictions, product withdrawal, supplier failure, cyber incidents, or loss of critical AI functionality.

277. **Alternative Supplier Governance**
     For critical AI systems, organizations should assess whether alternative providers or technologies are available and sufficiently mature to support continuity if the primary supply chain becomes unavailable.

278. **Exit Risk Governance**
     Exit planning should consider technical portability, contractual restrictions, data dependencies, interoperability, replacement timelines, customer impact, and regulatory implications.

279. **Crisis Governance**
     Organizations should define governance arrangements for major AI supply-chain events, including who has authority to suspend distribution, initiate recall, notify regulators, communicate with customers, and approve recovery.

280. **Crisis Decision Authority**
     Crisis procedures should identify decision-makers and deputies so that urgent compliance decisions are not delayed by unclear authority.

281. **Tabletop Exercises**
     High-risk AI supply chains should periodically conduct tabletop exercises involving scenarios such as provider non-compliance, serious incidents, cybersecurity compromise, unauthorized modification, regulatory intervention, withdrawal, and recall.

282. **Exercise Evidence**
     Exercise results should document participants, scenario, decisions, response times, control failures, lessons learned, and remediation actions.

283. **Lessons-Learned Management**
     Lessons from incidents, audits, exercises, recalls, and supplier failures should be incorporated into governance and control improvements.

284. **Stakeholder Communication Governance**
     Communications concerning material compliance failures should be coordinated among legal, compliance, communications, security, product, customer support, and executive stakeholders as appropriate.

285. **Customer Communication Controls**
     Where customers or downstream users may be affected by non-compliance, withdrawal, recall, or material system changes, communication should be accurate, controlled, timely, and traceable.

286. **Public Statements and Regulatory Claims**
     Organizations should ensure that public statements concerning AI compliance, conformity, safety, or regulatory status are supported by appropriate evidence and approved through the relevant governance process.

287. **Evidence-Based Compliance Claims**
     Marketing, procurement, sales, and product teams should not make unsupported claims that an AI system is “EU AI Act compliant,” “certified,” “safe,” or otherwise compliant unless the claim is substantiated and legally appropriate.

288. **Governance of Compliance Representations**
     Supplier representations and contractual compliance statements should be reviewed and validated against available evidence rather than accepted without challenge.

289. **Third-Party Assurance**
     Independent certifications, attestations, assessment reports, or other third-party assurance may support governance but should not automatically replace the organization's own assessment of its responsibilities.

290. **Assurance Reliance**
     Where the organization relies on third-party assurance, it should evaluate the scope, period, methodology, provider independence, exceptions, and applicability to the specific AI system and supply-chain relationship.

291. **Management Information Quality**
     Governance decisions depend on accurate information. AI inventory, supplier records, classification, version data, documentation status, incident information, and distribution records should be subject to data-quality controls.

292. **Governance Data Ownership**
     Each critical governance data element should have an identified owner responsible for accuracy, completeness, update frequency, and correction.

293. **Control Data Reconciliation**
     Where the same information exists across procurement, inventory, product, GRC, security, and distribution systems, reconciliation controls should identify inconsistencies.

294. **Governance Record Integrity**
     Compliance records should be protected against unauthorized modification and should preserve sufficient audit history to reconstruct material decisions.

295. **Access Control for Compliance Records**
     Access to regulatory evidence, supplier documentation, incident records, and governance decisions should be restricted according to role and business need.

296. **Governance Monitoring**
     Management should monitor whether importer and distributor governance remains aligned with changes in AI systems, organizational structure, supplier relationships, technology architecture, and regulatory requirements.

297. **Periodic Governance Review**
     The governance framework should be reviewed periodically and following material events to determine whether roles, responsibilities, policies, controls, committees, escalation paths, and assurance activities remain appropriate.

298. **Governance Effectiveness Review**
     Effectiveness should be evaluated using evidence rather than merely confirming that policies and committees exist. The review should consider actual control performance, incidents, audit results, remediation, supplier behavior, and regulatory interactions.

299. **Continuous Governance Improvement**
     Governance deficiencies should result in documented improvement actions with accountable owners, deadlines, validation criteria, and appropriate management oversight.

300. **Lifecycle Governance Assurance**
     Importer and distributor governance should remain active throughout the AI system lifecycle, from supplier selection and acquisition through importation, distribution, modification, monitoring, incident response, withdrawal, recall, replacement, and retirement.
