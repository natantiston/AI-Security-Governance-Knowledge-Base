# 15.01 — Global AI Regulation Landscape

## Part 1 — Foundations

### 1.1 The Emergence of AI Regulation

Artificial intelligence has evolved from a specialized technology into a general-purpose capability embedded in business processes, products, public services, cybersecurity operations, financial systems, healthcare, education, employment, communications, and critical infrastructure.

The increasing deployment of AI creates governance challenges that traditional information-security, privacy, and technology-risk frameworks do not fully address. AI systems can influence decisions, generate content, classify individuals, make recommendations, automate actions, process sensitive information, and interact with other systems with varying degrees of human oversight.

As a result, governments and regulators have increasingly moved from voluntary AI principles toward formal regulatory frameworks.

The global AI regulatory environment is not represented by one universal law. Instead, it consists of multiple regulatory approaches operating simultaneously:

* Comprehensive AI-specific legislation
* Sector-specific AI regulation
* Existing privacy and data-protection laws applied to AI
* Consumer-protection and product-safety laws
* Anti-discrimination and employment laws
* Cybersecurity requirements
* Government procurement requirements
* Voluntary standards and codes of practice
* Executive or governmental policy frameworks
* International agreements and regulatory cooperation

For an AI Security Governance function, this creates a fundamental requirement:

> **An organization must determine which AI regulations, standards, laws, and regulatory expectations apply to each AI system based on jurisdiction, role, purpose, sector, technology, and risk.**

The European Union's AI Act is particularly significant because it establishes a comprehensive, risk-based legal framework rather than regulating AI through a single sector or technology. The regulation entered into force on 1 August 2024, with obligations becoming applicable progressively.

---

### 1.2 From AI Ethics to AI Regulation

The development of AI governance can broadly be viewed as a progression:

**AI Ethics → AI Principles → AI Governance → AI Risk Management → AI Regulation → AI Regulatory Compliance**

Early AI governance discussions focused heavily on principles such as:

* Fairness
* Transparency
* Accountability
* Privacy
* Human oversight
* Safety
* Explainability
* Non-discrimination

These principles remain important, but regulatory frameworks increasingly convert them into enforceable organizational obligations.

For example, a principle such as:

> "AI systems should be transparent."

may become a regulatory requirement requiring an organization to:

* inform users that they are interacting with AI;
* disclose certain AI-generated or manipulated content;
* maintain technical documentation;
* provide information to downstream users;
* maintain records and logs;
* conduct specified assessments;
* demonstrate compliance to regulators.

The distinction is important for GRC professionals.

**Ethics asks:**

> What should we do?

**Governance asks:**

> What policies, responsibilities, processes, and controls should we establish?

**Risk management asks:**

> What could go wrong, and how should we reduce the risk?

**Regulation asks:**

> What are we legally required to do?

**Compliance asks:**

> Can we demonstrate that we actually fulfilled those legal and governance obligations?

AI Security Governance therefore operates at the intersection of all five.

---

### 1.3 The Global Regulatory Landscape Is Fragmented

AI regulation is developing through different legal philosophies.

Some jurisdictions favor comprehensive AI-specific legislation. Others primarily rely on existing laws, sector regulators, voluntary frameworks, standards, or government policies.

Consequently, two organizations deploying essentially the same AI technology may face substantially different obligations depending on where they:

* develop the system;
* provide the system;
* deploy the system;
* sell the system;
* operate the system;
* process personal information;
* make decisions affecting individuals;
* serve customers;
* conduct business;
* place AI products on a market.

This creates a **jurisdictional compliance problem**.

An organization cannot simply ask:

> "Is our AI system compliant?"

It must ask:

> "Compliant with which legal and regulatory requirements, in which jurisdictions, for which AI system, performing which purpose, and under which organizational role?"

This distinction is fundamental to AI regulatory GRC.

---

### 1.4 The EU AI Act as a Major Global Reference Point

The EU AI Act represents one of the most important developments in AI regulation because it establishes a harmonized, risk-based framework covering different categories of AI risk.

The framework distinguishes, among other categories:

1. **Prohibited or unacceptable-risk AI practices**
2. **High-risk AI systems**
3. **AI systems subject to specific transparency obligations**
4. **Minimal- or no-risk AI systems**

The European Commission describes the framework as using four broad levels of risk, with obligations increasing according to the risk associated with the AI use.

This creates an important GRC principle:

> **AI regulation should be proportional to the potential harm created by the AI system and its intended use.**

The regulatory assessment therefore cannot focus exclusively on the underlying model.

For example, the same foundation model could potentially be used for:

* generating marketing text;
* assisting a software developer;
* answering customer questions;
* supporting medical decisions;
* evaluating job applicants;
* assisting law enforcement.

The underlying model may be similar, but the regulatory risk can be substantially different because the **intended purpose and deployment context** are different.

---

### 1.5 AI Regulation Is Becoming Lifecycle-Based

Modern AI regulation increasingly addresses the AI lifecycle rather than only the moment when a system is released.

A simplified AI regulatory lifecycle is:

**Strategy → Design → Development → Data → Testing → Validation → Deployment → Operation → Monitoring → Incident Management → Modification → Retirement**

Regulatory requirements can potentially arise at multiple stages.

#### Strategy

The organization determines:

* why AI is being used;
* which jurisdictions are involved;
* which regulatory requirements apply;
* whether the proposed use is permissible.

#### Design

The organization determines:

* intended purpose;
* users;
* affected individuals;
* human oversight;
* security architecture;
* data requirements;
* risk controls.

#### Development

The organization may need to address:

* data governance;
* documentation;
* testing;
* cybersecurity;
* model evaluation;
* technical controls.

#### Deployment

The organization may need to establish:

* transparency mechanisms;
* user information;
* access controls;
* human oversight;
* operational monitoring.

#### Operation

The organization must monitor:

* performance;
* security;
* incidents;
* misuse;
* changes in risk;
* regulatory developments.

#### Retirement

The organization must address:

* data retention;
* system decommissioning;
* records;
* contractual obligations;
* regulatory evidence;
* downstream dependencies.

This lifecycle perspective is critical because AI compliance cannot be reduced to completing a questionnaire immediately before production deployment.

---

### 1.6 AI Regulation Extends Beyond AI Developers

A common misconception is that AI regulation primarily affects companies that develop AI models.

In reality, regulatory obligations can affect different participants in the AI ecosystem.

Depending on the applicable regulation, organizations may operate as:

* AI model providers;
* AI system providers;
* deployers;
* users;
* importers;
* distributors;
* product manufacturers;
* technology suppliers;
* integrators;
* downstream providers;
* public authorities;
* third-party assessors.

The EU AI Act explicitly distinguishes different roles and assigns obligations according to those roles.

This means that an organization using a commercially purchased AI service cannot automatically assume:

> "The vendor is responsible for AI compliance."

The vendor may have provider obligations, while the customer may have deployer obligations.

A mature AI GRC program therefore establishes **role-based accountability**.

---

### 1.7 Extraterritorial and Cross-Border Effects

AI systems frequently cross national boundaries.

A single AI service may involve:

**Model Developer → Cloud Provider → AI Platform → Enterprise → Employee → Customer**

with infrastructure and data distributed across several countries.

This creates regulatory questions such as:

* Where is the AI system developed?
* Where is the provider established?
* Where is the system placed on the market?
* Where is it deployed?
* Where are affected individuals located?
* Where is personal data processed?
* Which sector is involved?
* Which legal entity is responsible?
* Does the regulation have extraterritorial reach?

Therefore, geographic location alone is insufficient for determining AI regulatory applicability.

The organization should instead establish an **AI regulatory applicability model**.

A basic model can be expressed as:

**Regulatory Applicability = Jurisdiction + Role + AI Purpose + Sector + Risk + Data + Market Activity**

This becomes one of the foundations of an AI Regulatory GRC capability.

---

### 1.8 The Importance of Intended Purpose

One of the most important concepts in AI regulation is **intended purpose**.

The regulatory classification of an AI system may depend not merely on what the technology can technically do, but on what it is intended to do and how it is actually deployed.

The European Commission's AI Act guidance emphasizes that classification depends on factors including the AI system's function, intended purpose, and the specific context and modalities of its use.

For example, an AI model capable of analyzing resumes does not automatically mean every use of that model has the same regulatory classification.

The GRC analysis must examine:

* What is the system intended to accomplish?
* Who uses it?
* Who is affected?
* What decisions does it influence?
* Does it make or recommend decisions?
* What degree of autonomy exists?
* What consequences can result from an incorrect output?
* Is a human decision-maker involved?
* Is the AI used in a regulated sector?
* Is the use explicitly covered by applicable legislation?

This is why AI inventory and AI use-case registration are foundational GRC controls.

---

### 1.9 Regulatory Risk Versus Technical Risk

AI regulatory risk is not identical to AI technical risk.

A technically secure AI system can still create regulatory exposure.

For example:

**Technical assessment:**

> The model is secure, encrypted, highly available, and protected against unauthorized access.

But a regulatory assessment might identify:

> The organization failed to provide required transparency information.

Similarly:

**Technical assessment:**

> The model performs with 95% accuracy.

But a regulatory assessment might identify:

> The model's use creates unacceptable discrimination or violates a prohibited-use restriction.

Therefore, AI governance should maintain separate but interconnected risk dimensions:

| Risk Dimension    | Core Question                                                    |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Security Risk     | Can the AI system be attacked or compromised?                    |
| Privacy Risk      | Does the system improperly process personal data?                |
| Safety Risk       | Can the system cause physical or operational harm?               |
| Model Risk        | Can the model produce unreliable or inappropriate results?       |
| Ethical Risk      | Does the system conflict with organizational principles?         |
| Regulatory Risk   | Does the system violate applicable law or regulation?            |
| Legal Risk        | Could the organization face litigation or liability?             |
| Reputational Risk | Could deployment damage trust or reputation?                     |
| Operational Risk  | Can the organization operate and control the system effectively? |

A mature AI GRC program integrates these risk dimensions rather than treating them as independent silos.

---

### 1.10 The AI Regulatory Landscape Is Continuously Changing

AI regulation is not static.

Legislation, implementing rules, regulatory guidance, technical standards, codes of practice, enforcement interpretations, and national implementing measures continue to evolve.

This is particularly important for organizations implementing the EU AI Act.

For example, as of 2026, the EU AI Act's implementation timeline has already evolved through the EU's AI Omnibus changes. Most AI Act rules began applying on 2 August 2026, while certain high-risk requirements have been delayed to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028 depending on the category of system.

The European Commission also published guidance on Article 50 transparency obligations in July 2026, shortly before those obligations became applicable.

This demonstrates an important governance principle:

> **AI compliance is a continuous monitoring function, not a one-time certification exercise.**

Organizations therefore need regulatory horizon scanning capable of identifying:

* new legislation;
* amendments;
* regulatory guidance;
* enforcement actions;
* standards;
* codes of practice;
* supervisory interpretations;
* implementation deadlines;
* sector-specific requirements;
* emerging regulatory expectations.

---

### 1.11 The Role of International Standards

Legislation is only one component of the AI governance ecosystem.

International standards and frameworks can provide organizations with practical mechanisms for implementing regulatory expectations.

Important examples include:

* ISO/IEC 42001 — AI management systems
* ISO/IEC 23894 — AI risk management
* ISO/IEC 27001 — information security management
* ISO/IEC 27701 — privacy information management
* NIST AI Risk Management Framework
* NIST Cybersecurity Framework
* OECD AI Principles
* UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI
* Relevant sector-specific standards and technical standards

These frameworks do not automatically replace legal requirements.

Instead:

**Regulation defines obligations.**

**Standards provide implementation mechanisms.**

**GRC translates both into organizational controls.**

This distinction is essential.

An organization should never conclude:

> "We are ISO/IEC 42001 certified, therefore we are legally compliant with every AI regulation."

Certification can provide evidence of management-system maturity, but legal compliance must still be assessed against the applicable legislation.

---

### 1.12 AI Regulatory GRC as the Organizational Capability

The ultimate objective of this chapter is not simply to understand individual AI laws.

The objective is to establish an **AI Regulatory GRC capability** capable of continuously answering five questions:

### 1. What applies?

Identify the laws, regulations, standards, contractual requirements, and regulatory expectations applicable to the organization.

### 2. What must we do?

Translate legal requirements into organizational obligations.

### 3. Who is responsible?

Assign responsibilities to:

* Board and executives;
* AI governance committees;
* Legal;
* Compliance;
* Privacy;
* Information security;
* Risk management;
* Procurement;
* Engineering;
* Data science;
* AI product teams;
* Business owners;
* Internal audit.

### 4. How do we prove compliance?

Maintain evidence such as:

* AI inventories;
* risk assessments;
* impact assessments;
* technical documentation;
* model documentation;
* testing records;
* training records;
* transparency notices;
* approvals;
* monitoring records;
* incident reports;
* supplier assessments;
* contracts;
* audit results.

### 5. How do we know when requirements change?

Establish continuous:

**Regulatory Monitoring → Impact Analysis → Control Update → Implementation → Evidence → Assurance**

This closed-loop capability forms the foundation of AI regulatory GRC.

---

### 1.13 The Regulatory Mindset for AI Security Governance

AI Security Governance professionals should therefore approach regulation differently from traditional compliance programs.

Traditional compliance may ask:

> "Which controls do we have?"

AI regulatory governance must first ask:

> "What AI are we operating, what does it do, who is affected, where is it used, what legal role do we occupy, and which regulatory obligations apply?"

The correct sequence is:

**AI Discovery → AI Classification → Regulatory Applicability → Risk Assessment → Obligations → Controls → Evidence → Monitoring → Assurance**

This sequence prevents a common GRC failure: implementing generic AI controls without first determining which legal obligations actually apply.

The global AI regulatory landscape is therefore best understood not as a collection of unrelated laws, but as an evolving ecosystem in which **jurisdiction, AI lifecycle, organizational role, intended purpose, risk, data, sector, and regulatory obligations intersect**.


# Part 2 — Regulatory Applicability and Control Framework

## 2.1 Establishing an AI Regulatory Applicability Framework

An AI regulatory GRC program should translate the global regulatory landscape into a repeatable process for determining which requirements apply to each AI system.

The organization should avoid managing AI regulation as a generic legal checklist.

Instead, each AI use case should pass through a structured applicability assessment:

**AI System Identification → Organizational Role → Jurisdiction → Intended Purpose → Sector → Risk → Data → Regulatory Scope → Applicable Obligations**

The output should be an **AI Regulatory Applicability Record**.

A minimum record should contain:

| Field                  | Purpose                                                |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| AI System ID           | Unique identifier                                      |
| AI System Name         | Business/system name                                   |
| Business Owner         | Accountable business owner                             |
| Technical Owner        | Responsible technical team                             |
| Provider/Developer     | Internal or external provider                          |
| Organizational Role    | Provider, deployer, importer, distributor, etc.        |
| Intended Purpose       | Authorized purpose of the system                       |
| Use Case               | Specific business application                          |
| Jurisdictions          | Countries/regions involved                             |
| Sector                 | Financial, healthcare, employment, public sector, etc. |
| Affected Persons       | Employees, customers, citizens, applicants, etc.       |
| Data Types             | Personal, sensitive, confidential, public, etc.        |
| Risk Classification    | Applicable AI risk category                            |
| Applicable Laws        | Relevant legislation                                   |
| Applicable Standards   | Relevant standards/frameworks                          |
| Regulatory Obligations | Requirements derived from applicable law               |
| Control Status         | Implementation status                                  |
| Evidence Location      | Compliance evidence                                    |
| Review Date            | Next regulatory review                                 |

This record becomes the foundation for subsequent control implementation.

---

## 2.2 AI Regulatory Scoping

Regulatory scoping should occur before an AI system is approved for production.

The organization should establish a formal set of scoping questions.

### Organizational Scope

Determine:

* Which legal entity operates the system?
* Which entity provides the AI?
* Which entity deploys it?
* Is the organization acting as a provider, deployer, importer, distributor, manufacturer, or another role?
* Are third parties involved?

### Geographic Scope

Determine:

* Where is the provider established?
* Where is the deployer established?
* Where is the AI system placed on the market?
* Where is the system used?
* Where are affected individuals located?
* Where is data processed?
* Where are model infrastructure and cloud services located?

### Functional Scope

Determine:

* What does the AI system do?
* What decisions does it influence?
* Does it generate content?
* Does it classify individuals?
* Does it make recommendations?
* Does it perform biometric functions?
* Does it interact with people?
* Does it control physical processes?
* Does it operate autonomously?

### Risk Scope

Determine:

* What harm could occur?
* Who could be affected?
* How severe could the harm be?
* How likely is the harm?
* Can the AI system materially affect rights, safety, employment, access to services, or other important outcomes?

## 2.3 Regulatory Obligation Mapping

Once applicability is established, legal requirements should be translated into specific obligations.

For example:

**Regulatory Requirement**

> Appropriate transparency must be provided.

should become measurable organizational obligations such as:

* Identify systems subject to transparency requirements.
* Determine the required disclosure.
* Define the responsible owner.
* Implement disclosure mechanisms.
* Validate disclosure during testing.
* Monitor changes to applicable requirements.
* Retain evidence demonstrating implementation.

This creates a chain:

**Law → Obligation → Control → Procedure → Evidence → Assurance**

This chain should be maintained for significant AI regulatory requirements.

## 2.4 Regulatory Requirement Register

Organizations should maintain an **AI Regulatory Requirement Register**.

A simplified structure is:

| ID         | Regulation        | Article/Section      | Obligation  | Applicability | Control       | Owner      | Evidence    | Status      |
| ---------- | ----------------- | -------------------- | ----------- | ------------- | ------------- | ---------- | ----------- | ----------- |
| AI-REG-001 | EU AI Act         | Applicable provision | Requirement | AI System A   | AI-CTRL-001   | Compliance | Assessment  | Implemented |
| AI-REG-002 | Privacy law       | Applicable provision | Requirement | AI System A   | PRIV-CTRL-004 | Privacy    | DPIA        | Implemented |
| AI-REG-003 | Sector regulation | Applicable provision | Requirement | AI System B   | SEC-CTRL-008  | Security   | Test report | Partial     |

The register should distinguish between:

* **Legal obligation**
* **Regulatory guidance**
* **Standard**
* **Contractual requirement**
* **Internal policy requirement**
* **Recommended practice**

These categories should never be silently treated as equivalent.

## 2.6 AI Regulatory Control Objectives

Each regulatory requirement should be converted into a control objective.

For example:

**Requirement:** The organization must maintain appropriate documentation.

**Control Objective:**

> Ensure required AI system documentation is complete, accurate, current, approved, and retrievable throughout the applicable AI lifecycle.

The corresponding controls might include:

1. Documentation templates.
2. Mandatory documentation fields.
3. Technical-owner responsibility.
4. Compliance review.
5. Version control.
6. Periodic review.
7. Change-triggered updates.
8. Evidence retention.

This allows internal audit and compliance teams to test controls objectively.

## 2.7 Preventive, Detective and Corrective Controls

AI regulatory controls should be classified according to when they operate.

### Preventive Controls

Prevent regulatory violations before deployment.

Examples:

* prohibited-use screening;
* regulatory applicability assessment;
* AI risk classification;
* approval gates;
* supplier due diligence;
* mandatory legal review;
* access restrictions.

### Detective Controls

Identify regulatory problems after implementation.

Examples:

* compliance monitoring;
* model monitoring;
* transparency testing;
* audit reviews;
* regulatory change detection;
* incident detection;
* periodic control testing.

### Corrective Controls

Respond to identified compliance failures.

Examples:

* remediation plans;
* system suspension;
* model rollback;
* policy updates;
* user notification;
* incident reporting;
* regulator engagement;
* corrective training.

A mature AI GRC program should maintain all three categories.

## 2.8 AI Regulatory Approval Gates

AI systems should pass defined governance gates before progressing through the lifecycle.

A practical model is:

**Gate 1 — Registration**

The AI use case is registered in the AI inventory.

**Gate 2 — Regulatory Screening**

Applicable jurisdictions and laws are identified.

**Gate 3 — Risk Classification**

The AI system is classified according to applicable regulatory and organizational criteria.

**Gate 4 — Risk Assessment**

AI, security, privacy, legal, operational, and other risks are assessed.

**Gate 5 — Control Design**

Required controls are identified.

**Gate 6 — Control Validation**

Controls are tested.

**Gate 7 — Approval**

Authorized stakeholders approve deployment.

**Gate 8 — Production Monitoring**

The system enters continuous monitoring.

**Gate 9 — Change Review**

Material changes trigger reassessment.

This prevents AI systems from bypassing governance simply because they are acquired from a third-party provider.

## 2.10 Regulatory Crosswalks

A crosswalk maps regulatory requirements to existing organizational controls.

For example:

**EU AI Act Requirement**

↓

**AI Regulatory Obligation**

↓

**Existing ISO/IEC 27001 Control**

↓

**Existing Privacy Control**

↓

**AI-Specific Control**

↓

**Evidence**

This allows organizations to avoid creating redundant control environments.

For example, an organization may already have:

* information-security policies;
* supplier risk management;
* incident management;
* access control;
* records management;
* change management;
* employee training.

These existing controls can potentially support AI regulatory compliance.

However, the organization should perform a **control adequacy assessment** rather than assuming existing controls automatically satisfy AI-specific requirements.

## 2.11 Control Inheritance

AI systems frequently depend on infrastructure and services controlled by other teams or vendors.

For example:

**AI Application**

→ Cloud Platform

→ Identity Provider

→ Data Platform

→ Foundation Model Provider

→ Security Monitoring Platform

Some regulatory controls may therefore be inherited.

The organization should document:

* inherited control;
* control owner;
* service provider;
* scope;
* assurance evidence;
* contractual basis;
* limitations;
* residual responsibility.

A cloud provider's security certification does not eliminate the organization's responsibility for controls that remain under its own operational control.

The same principle applies to AI providers.

## 2.14 AI Regulatory Control Testing

Internal compliance testing should determine whether controls are:

### Designed

Does the control address the applicable requirement?

### Implemented

Has the control actually been deployed?

### Operating

Does the control operate as intended?

### Effective

Does it reduce the relevant regulatory risk?

For example:

> Policy states that AI systems must undergo regulatory classification.

This demonstrates **design**.

An AI inventory showing completed classifications demonstrates **implementation**.

Sampling AI projects and verifying that classifications were performed demonstrates **operation**.

Testing whether classifications correctly identified applicable regulatory obligations demonstrates **effectiveness**.

## 2.15 Regulatory Change Management

AI regulation requires a formal regulatory-change process.

A regulatory change workflow should be:

**Monitor → Identify → Validate → Assess → Map → Approve → Implement → Test → Evidence → Communicate**

When a new regulation or regulatory interpretation is identified, the organization should determine:

1. What changed?
2. When does it become effective?
3. Which legal entities are affected?
4. Which AI systems are affected?
5. Which controls are affected?
6. Which policies must change?
7. Which contracts must change?
8. Which technical systems must change?
9. Which employees need training?
10. What evidence must be generated?

The result should be a controlled remediation plan.

## 2.16 Regulatory Horizon Scanning

Horizon scanning should identify regulatory developments before they become mandatory.

Sources may include:

* government regulators;
* official legislative publications;
* supervisory authorities;
* regulatory consultations;
* official guidance;
* recognized standards bodies;
* industry regulators;
* international organizations.

Horizon scanning should classify developments as:

**Watch → Emerging → Confirmed → Effective → Enforcement**

This allows organizations to prioritize regulatory developments according to their impact.

## 2.17 Regulatory Impact Assessment

Not every regulatory development requires the same response.

A regulatory impact assessment should consider:

**Impact = Scope × Regulatory Significance × Control Gap × Implementation Effort × Deadline**

A high-impact change might:

* affect many AI systems;
* create new legal obligations;
* require substantial technical changes;
* require new documentation;
* require contractual amendments;
* have a short implementation deadline.

Such changes should be escalated to senior governance bodies.

---

## 2.18 Regulatory Exceptions

Organizations may encounter situations where full compliance cannot immediately be achieved.

Exceptions should never be informal.

An AI regulatory exception process should document:

* requirement;
* affected system;
* reason for exception;
* risk;
* compensating controls;
* responsible owner;
* legal/compliance assessment;
* expiration date;
* remediation plan;
* approval authority.

Exceptions should be:

**Time-bound → Risk-assessed → Approved → Monitored → Closed**

Permanent exceptions should be treated as a governance concern.

# Part 3 — Governance, Accountability, Risk and Assurance

## 3.1 AI Regulatory Governance Model

AI regulatory compliance should be governed as an enterprise capability rather than treated solely as a legal or technical function.

A mature governance model establishes clear accountability across:

* Board of directors
* Executive management
* AI governance committee
* Legal
* Regulatory compliance
* Enterprise risk management
* Information security
* Privacy
* Internal audit
* Procurement
* Data governance
* AI engineering
* Model risk management
* Business owners
* Human resources
* Third-party risk management

The governance model should establish three fundamental principles:

1. **Accountability cannot be delegated away.**
2. **Regulatory responsibility must follow the organization's actual role.**
3. **AI decisions must be traceable to an accountable authority.**

Technology teams may operate an AI system, but governance must determine whether the organization is permitted to use it and under what conditions.

---

## 3.2 Board-Level Accountability

The board does not need to manage individual AI models, but it should provide oversight of material AI regulatory risk.

Board responsibilities may include:

* approving AI governance principles;
* understanding significant AI regulatory exposure;
* approving AI risk appetite;
* overseeing material regulatory risks;
* reviewing significant AI incidents;
* ensuring adequate resources;
* challenging management on unresolved compliance deficiencies;
* ensuring regulatory obligations are incorporated into enterprise governance.

Board reporting should focus on business and regulatory consequences rather than technical model metrics alone.

Examples include:

> How many material AI systems operate within the organization?

> Which systems present significant regulatory exposure?

> Are any prohibited or potentially prohibited uses under consideration?

> How many high-risk systems have unresolved control deficiencies?

> Are regulatory deadlines being met?

> Have any AI incidents required regulatory notification?

---

## 3.3 Executive Management

Executive management converts board expectations into operational accountability.

Typical executive responsibilities include:

* establishing the AI governance program;
* approving policies;
* assigning accountable executives;
* allocating resources;
* ensuring regulatory compliance;
* establishing escalation mechanisms;
* approving significant risk acceptance;
* ensuring business units comply with AI governance requirements.

The executive sponsor should have sufficient authority to resolve conflicts between:

**Business Speed ↔ AI Risk ↔ Regulatory Compliance**

AI governance becomes ineffective when business teams can bypass compliance simply because deployment is commercially urgent.

---

## 3.4 AI Governance Committee

A cross-functional AI Governance Committee can provide centralized oversight.

Typical membership may include:

| Function       | Primary Contribution        |
| -------------- | --------------------------- |
| Legal          | Legal interpretation        |
| Compliance     | Regulatory obligations      |
| Security       | AI security and cyber risk  |
| Privacy        | Data protection             |
| Risk           | Enterprise risk             |
| Engineering    | Technical feasibility       |
| Data Science   | Model risk                  |
| Procurement    | Supplier risk               |
| Business       | Business purpose and impact |
| HR             | Workforce implications      |
| Internal Audit | Independent assurance       |

The committee should not necessarily approve every low-risk AI use case.

A risk-based governance model should allow routine low-risk uses to follow streamlined workflows while escalating significant AI systems for deeper review.

---

## 3.5 Three Lines Model

AI regulatory governance can be integrated with the Three Lines Model.

### First Line — Business and Technology

Responsible for:

* identifying AI systems;
* following approved procedures;
* implementing controls;
* managing operational risks;
* maintaining documentation;
* monitoring systems;
* escalating incidents.

### Second Line — Risk, Compliance, Privacy and Security

Responsible for:

* establishing frameworks;
* advising first-line teams;
* reviewing risk;
* monitoring compliance;
* challenging control effectiveness;
* maintaining regulatory interpretations;
* reporting significant risks.

### Third Line — Internal Audit

Responsible for independent assurance over:

* AI governance;
* regulatory compliance;
* control design;
* control effectiveness;
* risk management;
* evidence quality;
* remediation.

Internal audit should remain independent from operational AI ownership.

## 3.7 AI Regulatory Risk Appetite

AI governance should define how much regulatory risk the organization is willing to accept.

Risk appetite should establish boundaries such as:

* prohibited uses are not permitted;
* material regulatory violations require escalation;
* unresolved critical findings prevent deployment;
* high-risk AI requires enhanced governance;
* exceptions require documented approval;
* regulatory reporting deadlines cannot be intentionally bypassed.

Risk appetite should be translated into decision rules.

For example:

**Critical regulatory risk → No deployment**

**High regulatory risk → Executive approval**

**Moderate risk → Standard governance**

**Low risk → Simplified governance**

The thresholds should be formally defined rather than decided informally by project teams.

---

## 3.8 Regulatory Risk Assessment

AI regulatory risk assessments should evaluate more than whether a legal requirement exists.

The assessment should consider:

### Likelihood

How likely is a regulatory failure?

### Impact

What would happen if the organization failed?

Potential impacts include:

* regulatory penalties;
* enforcement action;
* litigation;
* suspension of AI deployment;
* contractual consequences;
* reputational damage;
* customer harm;
* operational disruption.

### Exposure

How many people, systems, countries, or business processes are affected?

### Control Strength

How effective are existing controls?

### Detectability

How quickly would a regulatory violation be identified?

The result should be a documented residual-risk assessment.

| Regulatory change missed    | Weak horizon scanning    | Deadline failure          | Medium     | Regulatory monitoring    | Low           | Compliance    |

The register should link risks to:

* AI systems;
* regulations;
* controls;
* findings;
* remediation actions.

---

## 3.10 AI Regulatory Exceptions and Risk Acceptance

AI regulatory risk acceptance must be carefully controlled.

An exception should never be used to override a mandatory legal prohibition simply because the business considers the risk acceptable.

Where an actual legal requirement permits discretion, the organization may establish a formal exception process.

Each exception should include:

* requirement;
* affected AI system;
* reason;
* risk analysis;
* compensating controls;
* legal assessment;
* approval authority;
* expiration date;
* remediation plan.

The organization should distinguish:

**Risk acceptance**

from

**Regulatory non-compliance.**

Management may accept certain business risks, but it cannot necessarily accept a violation of mandatory law.

## 3.13 Regulatory Ownership by Jurisdiction

Multinational organizations should establish jurisdictional ownership.

For example:

**Global AI Governance**

establishes the baseline.

**Regional Compliance**

interprets regional requirements.

**Local Legal**

addresses country-specific requirements.

**Business Units**

implement controls.

This can be represented as:

**Global Baseline → Regional Overlay → Country Requirements → Business Implementation**

The objective is to avoid two common failures:

### Failure 1 — Fragmentation

Every country creates an independent AI governance program.

### Failure 2 — Over-centralization

A global policy ignores local legal requirements.

A federated governance model usually provides better scalability.

## 3.14 Regulatory Control Ownership

Every significant AI regulatory control should have an identified control owner.

A control owner should be responsible for:

* control design;
* implementation;
* operating effectiveness;
* evidence;
* testing;
* remediation.

For example:

**Control:** AI systems must undergo regulatory classification.

**Control Owner:** AI Governance Office.

**Process Owner:** AI Product Management.

**Evidence Owner:** AI System Owner.

**Assurance Owner:** Compliance/Internal Audit.

This separation prevents ambiguity.

## 3.15 Regulatory Evidence and Auditability

AI regulatory governance must be auditable.

An auditor should be able to trace:

**AI System**

→ **Regulatory Classification**

→ **Applicable Requirements**

→ **Risk Assessment**

→ **Controls**

→ **Control Evidence**

→ **Approval**

→ **Monitoring**

→ **Incidents**

→ **Remediation**

This creates an **AI regulatory audit trail**.

Without traceability, an organization may have controls but still be unable to demonstrate compliance effectively.

## 3.16 Regulatory Assurance

Assurance should occur at multiple levels.

### Management Assurance

Business owners attest that required controls are operating.

### Compliance Assurance

Compliance evaluates adherence to regulatory requirements.

### Security Assurance

Security evaluates technical and cyber controls.

### Privacy Assurance

Privacy evaluates data-protection requirements.

### Internal Audit

Internal Audit independently evaluates governance and control effectiveness.

### External Assurance

Where appropriate, external assessors may provide independent assurance.

The objective is not simply to obtain a certification.

The objective is to provide reasonable confidence that regulatory requirements are being identified, controlled, monitored, and evidenced.

Critical findings should have defined escalation requirements.

## 3.18 Root-Cause Analysis of Regulatory Failures

Possible root causes include:

* poor regulatory monitoring;
* inadequate training;
* weak procurement processes;
* insufficient technical controls;
* missing governance gates;
* undocumented AI use;
* uncontrolled shadow AI;
* ineffective change management.

For significant incidents, organizations should perform structured root-cause analysis and identify systemic corrective actions.

**Change Detection**

↓

↓

**Human Validation**

↓

**AI-Assisted Impact Analysis**

↓

**Affected AI Assets Identified**

↓

**Affected Controls Identified**

↓

**Tasks Created**

↓

## 4.19 Global Regulatory Conflict Management

Multinational AI systems can encounter conflicting regulatory requirements.

For example, one jurisdiction may impose:

* localization requirements;

while another requires:

* cross-border data transfer.

Another jurisdiction may require:

* specific transparency;

while a contractual or security requirement may restrict certain disclosures.

The architecture should therefore support:

**Global Baseline Controls**

plus

**Jurisdiction-Specific Overlays**

plus

**Conflict Identification**

plus

**Legal Resolution**

A jurisdictional control model can be represented as:

**Global Control**

→ EU Overlay

→ U.S. Overlay

→ UK Overlay

→ Canada Overlay

→ Singapore Overlay

→ China Overlay

This avoids duplicating the entire control framework for every country.

## 4.20 Regulatory Compliance by Design

AI regulatory governance should ultimately become embedded into the enterprise architecture.

Instead of:

> Build AI → Deploy AI → Ask Compliance

the target model is:

> Identify Regulation → Design Requirements → Build Controls → Test → Deploy → Monitor

This is **Regulatory Compliance by Design**.

The mature architecture therefore connects regulatory requirements directly to technology and operational processes.

A complete model is:

**Global Regulations**

↓

**Regulatory Intelligence**

↓

**Legal Obligations**

↓

**GRC Control Library**

↓

**AI Inventory**

↓

**AI Risk Classification**

↓

**AI System Architecture**

↓

**Preventive and Detective Controls**

↓

**Evidence**

↓

**Continuous Monitoring**

↓

**Audit and Assurance**

↓

**Regulatory Change**

↓

**Updated Requirements**

The resulting system creates a continuous regulatory feedback loop:

**Regulation → Governance → Architecture → Operation → Evidence → Assurance → Regulatory Change → Governance**

This establishes the technical and operational foundation for managing AI regulatory compliance across jurisdictions and throughout the AI lifecycle.
